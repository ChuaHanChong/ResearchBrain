# VIEW: Visual Imitation Learning with Waypoints

> [!summary] Summary
> VIEW is a human-to-robot visual imitation learning algorithm that extracts a condensed waypoint prior from a single human video, scores robot rollouts with an agent-agnostic object-tracking reward, and explores around waypoints via a grasp/task-split quality-diversity search. It learns manipulation tasks in under 30 minutes with fewer than 20 real-world rollouts, beating prior state-of-the-art.

## Problem

- Prior human-to-robot imitation methods need many real-world robot rollouts to converge, making single-video learning slow and sample-inefficient in practice.
- Existing pipelines like WHIRL depend on GPU-heavy video inpainting to remove humans/robots from frames before computing agent-agnostic rewards, adding cost and latency.
- Unified exploration over the full trajectory ignores that grasping needs sparse-reward search while manipulation needs dense-reward search, limiting scalability to long or multi-waypoint tasks.

## Method

- **VIEW**'s condensed prior extraction: ==100DOH== hand detection plus ==MANO== refinement yields a hand trajectory that ==SQUISHE== compresses from hundreds of frames to 3-4 informative waypoints.
- Agent-agnostic reward: object trajectories are extracted from both human and robot videos via hand-contact anchor boxes, and reward is negative pixel-space ==MSE== between corresponding object waypoints, avoiding inpainting.
- Grasp/task ==quality-diversity== exploration: waypoints are split into a grasp phase using ==CVT==-based high-level sampling plus ==Bayesian-optimization== low-level exploitation, and a dense-reward task phase optimized in parallel per waypoint, with a residual network to transfer noise corrections across tasks.

## Results

- Real-world UR10 experiments show VIEW reaching over **80%** success on uncluttered tasks and about **100%** on cluttered move tasks, versus WHIRL's under **50%** within **100** rollouts.
- Simulation ablations show VIEW's grasp/task split hits at least **92%** success on push versus roughly **70%** for unified Bayesian optimization, which nearly fails at **0%** on the four-waypoint move task.
- Adding the residual network cuts rollouts needed for new tasks by over **40%** on average, though it can hurt performance when a new demonstration falls outside the training distribution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compressing a demonstration to a handful of intent-capturing waypoints, rather than reasoning over every frame, is sufficient for efficient human-to-robot transfer.
> - Splitting exploration by reward structure (sparse for grasping, dense for manipulation) rather than optimizing the whole trajectory jointly is key to scaling with waypoint count.
> - Agent-agnostic, object-centric pixel comparison can replace expensive video inpainting for cross-embodiment reward computation, but residual transfer models need distribution-matched training data to help rather than hurt.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively researching methods for robots to acquire complex manipulation skills. Two prominent paradigms for robot learning are Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). IL typically requires numerous demonstrations, often involving kinesthetic guidance or teleoperation of the robot, which can be laborious. RL, conversely, demands extensive environmental interactions and often struggles with the definition of appropriate reward functions, particularly in unstructured settings.

Visual Imitation Learning (VIL) offers an alternative by enabling robots to learn from video demonstrations. A significant challenge in VIL, especially when demonstrations involve humans, is bridging the "embodiment gap" – the morphological and kinematic differences between human demonstrators and robotic learners. Human hand movements may not directly translate to a robot's end-effector. Existing approaches to address this include using specialized tools for demonstrations (e.g., reacher-grabbers), employing camera angles to minimize morphological discrepancies, or constructing large datasets of robot-specific demonstrations.

Another line of research focuses on learning directly from human video demonstrations. Some methods attempt to translate human videos into robot-applicable formats using techniques like cycle consistency networks or by extracting key points, but these often require vast datasets of both human and robot videos. Other approaches leverage neural networks to learn reward functions from human videos for reinforcement learning. While promising, many of these human-to-robot VIL methods still necessitate a substantial number of real-world robot rollouts for effective task learning. For instance, methods like WHIRL, which is a state-of-the-art approach, can be computationally intensive due to requirements like video inpainting and may struggle with long-horizon tasks or require many rollouts.

This work contributes to the landscape of human-to-robot VIL by focusing on sample efficiency and adaptability. It aims to reduce the number of robot interactions required to learn a task from a single human video demonstration, directly addressing the limitations of high data requirements and the embodiment gap. By introducing a waypoint-centric approach, the authors propose a method that distills relevant information from high-dimensional video data, thereby making VIL more practical for real-world robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to learn manipulation tasks efficiently and effectively from single, arbitrarily long human video demonstrations. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Information Overload in VIL**: Video demonstrations, composed of thousands of image frames and numerous pixels, contain a high volume of data. For a robot, discerning actionable information from this raw visual input is challenging. The authors hypothesize that not all video data is equally important; instead, learning can be streamlined by focusing on critical "waypoints" that capture the essential aspects of a task, such as grasping locations or object movement phases. This selective focus is intended to alleviate the computational burden associated with high-dimensional video data.

2. **Bridging the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap**: Human demonstrations often feature hands manipulating objects, while robots use grippers or other end-effectors. These morphological differences mean that directly mimicking human actions is often ineffective. The research aims to develop a method that can process human-centric demonstrations and adapt them to the robot's morphology, ensuring successful task execution despite these inherent disparities.

3. **Enhancing Sample Efficiency**: Traditional IL often requires many demonstrations, and RL demands extensive interaction with the environment. The motivation is to significantly reduce the number of robot rollouts (real-world trials) needed for a robot to learn a task. This efficiency is crucial for deploying robotic systems in practical scenarios, where each real-world trial can be time-consuming, resource-intensive, or prone to wear and tear.

4. **Enabling Learning from Single, Long-Horizon Demonstrations**: The research seeks to empower robots to learn from a single video demonstration, encompassing tasks that may involve multiple objects or sequential subtasks (long-horizon tasks). This contrasts with approaches that might require multiple short demonstrations or extensive pre-training. The ability to learn from a single, comprehensive demonstration simplifies the human teaching process.

5. **Facilitating Few-Shot Adaptation**: As robots learn more tasks, they accumulate knowledge about their own morphology, sensor noise, and the environment. A further objective is to leverage this accumulated experience to accelerate learning on new, unseen tasks (few-shot learning). This involves modeling systematic errors between initial video-derived trajectories and successful robot trajectories to improve future prior extractions.

In summary, the core motivation is to make human-to-robot VIL more practical and efficient by enabling robots to quickly learn a diverse range of manipulation tasks from minimal human input—specifically, a single video demonstration—while robustly handling the complexities of visual data and morphological differences.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Visual Imitation lEarning with Waypoints (VIEW), consists of a multi-pronged approach to enhance sample efficiency in human-to-robot VIL. It operates in distinct phases: prior extraction, agent-agnostic reward generation, sample-efficient exploration, and an optional residual network for few-shot adaptation.

#### 3.1. Prior Extraction

The process begins by processing a single RGB-D video demonstration of a human performing a task. This phase is divided into two sub-components:

1. **Hand Trajectory Extraction**: The 100 Days of Hands (100DOH) model is used to identify the human hand's location and contact information in each video frame. The MANO hand model further refines these bounding box coordinates to pinpoint the human wrist position in 2D, which is then converted to 3D world coordinates using depth information. To address redundancy in the extracted trajectory, the Spatial Quality Simplification Heuristic - Extended (SQUISHE) algorithm is applied. SQUISHE compresses the trajectory by prioritizing key waypoints that represent significant changes in hand direction or object interaction, reducing the number of points while preserving the essential shape of the trajectory.

2. **Object Trajectory Extraction**: Utilizing the hand-object contact information from 100DOH, the system identifies frames where interaction occurs. Anchor boxes and an object detector (Mask R-CNN) are employed to pinpoint the primary object of interest. Once identified, its 2D pixel coordinates are tracked across the video and converted to 3D world coordinates using depth data, creating a trajectory for the manipulated object. This entire process distills the demonstration into a condensed human hand trajectory (waypoints), an identified target object (tag), and the corresponding object trajectory.

#### 3.2. Agent-Agnostic Rewards

To provide feedback for the robot's learning, an agent-agnostic reward function is designed. This reward mechanism compares the movement of the target object during the robot's interactions with its movement in the human demonstration. Object trajectories are extracted from both the human's video and the robot's rollout video using the same procedure as in prior extraction. The negative Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the corresponding waypoints of the human's object trajectory (in pixel space) and the robot's object trajectory (also in pixel space) serves as the reward. This pixel-based comparison aims to minimize transformation errors and focuses solely on the object's behavior, making it independent of the agent performing the task.

#### 3.3. Exploration for Iterative Improvement

The initial trajectory extracted from the human video often requires refinement due to morphological differences and sensor noise. The exploration phase iteratively improves this trajectory by dividing the task into two sequential sub-phases: grasp exploration and task exploration.

1. **Correcting the Grasp Waypoint**: This phase focuses on identifying an effective grasp location for the robot. The initial human grasp waypoint (ω_h_grasp) serves as a prior.
 - **Restricting the Exploration Space**: A refined bounding box (B) is defined around both the human grasp waypoint (ω_h_grasp) and the estimated object location at the time of grasp (ω_o_grasp). This bounding box is biased towards the object, creating a more compact and relevant search region compared to a naive, centrally-located box.
 - **Rewards for Grasp Exploration**: A grasp is considered successful if the robot can pick up and move the object. The system assesses success by checking if the object remains near the robot's end-effector at the subsequent waypoint (ω_h_grasp+1). Rewards are sparse and can vary (increase or decrease) near successful grasp locations.
 - **Grasp Exploration Strategy (Quality-Diversity - QD)**: Due to sparse and locally varying rewards, a QD approach is used, combining high-level and low-level search:
 - **High-Level Grasp Exploration**: The bounding box B is discretized into regions using Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT) and k-means clustering. High-level waypoints are sampled from unexplored regions by maximizing distance to previously visited waypoints and applying a regularization constraint to ensure uniform spacing, optimizing for diversity and coverage.
 - **Low-Level Grasp Exploitation**: Regions showing significant reward changes (indicating proximity to a successful grasp) are prioritized for refinement. A waypoint is sampled from visited regions with a probability proportional to the magnitude of its reward change. Within this refined local region, Bayesian Optimization (BO) is used to find the optimal grasp location.
 - **Trade-off**: A probability `p_explore` balances exploration of new regions (high-level search) and exploitation of promising regions (low-level search), adapting based on the variance of rewards in visited waypoints.

2. **Correcting the Task Waypoints**: Once a successful grasp is established, the robot focuses on replicating the object manipulation demonstrated by the human. This phase has denser rewards, as any object movement leads to a change in reward. Bounding boxes are defined around each waypoint in the human's task trajectory, and separate instances of Bayesian Optimization (BO) are run in parallel for each waypoint to iteratively refine the robot's trajectory to match the human's demonstrated object movement.

#### 3.4. Residual Network

To enable few-shot learning, a residual network is trained to model systematic errors between the initially extracted human trajectory (ξ_h) and the empirically successful robot trajectory (ξ_*). This error, referred to as η_static, accounts for consistent inaccuracies from sensors, model misalignment, and morphological differences. The residual network (Φ) is a multi-layer perceptron trained on a dataset of previously solved tasks (D = (ξ_h, ξ_*)) to estimate this noise. When a new task video is provided, the robot's initial prior (ξ_h_k+1) is adjusted by adding the output of the residual network (Φ(ξ_h_k+1)), aiming to produce a more accurate starting trajectory and reduce subsequent exploration time.

#### 3.5. Incorporating Multi-Object Scenarios

VIEW is designed to scale to multi-object scenarios by segmenting long demonstrations into distinct sub-trajectories based on changes in hand-object contact information. Each sub-trajectory, representing an interaction with a single object, is then solved sequentially using the grasp and task exploration phases described above. This modular approach allows the robot to handle complex, multi-step tasks by breaking them down into manageable subtasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation studies and real-world robot experiments to validate the efficacy of VIEW and its individual components.

#### 4.1. Simulations

An ablation study in a Pybullet environment with a FrankaEmika robot arm investigated the impact of noise, trajectory compression, and exploration strategies on tasks like picking, pushing, and moving a cup.

- **Impact of Noise**: VIEW demonstrated robustness to noise in the extracted prior. Success rates were nearly 100% for noise variances between 0.05m and 0.15m. Beyond 0.2m, success rates decreased, and more exploration rollouts were required for convergence. Tasks with more waypoints (e.g., "move") generally required more rollouts. This indicates that VIEW's exploration can effectively overcome inaccuracies in the initial prior.

- **Impact of Trajectory Compression**: The proposed SQUISHE compression algorithm significantly outperformed simple fixed-rate downsampling. While increasing the number of waypoints (higher sampling rate) generally increased the number of rollouts needed for convergence, fixed-rate sampling often missed critical waypoints (e.g., the grasp point), leading to lower success rates compared to SQUISHE, which intelligently retains essential features.

- **Impact of Exploration Approach**: Separating exploration into distinct grasping and manipulation phases (VIEW's approach) yielded substantially higher success rates and fewer rollouts compared to a unified optimization approach (Bayesian Optimization across the entire trajectory). The unified approach struggled, especially with tasks involving more waypoints (e.g., "move"), where its success rate approached zero. This highlights the importance of VIEW's segmented exploration for scaling to more complex tasks.

- **Impact of Residual Network**: Integrating the residual network enabled few-shot learning on new tasks with unseen object locations. It reduced the number of rollouts required for the robot to learn each task by over 40% on average, often achieving success in fewer than 10 trials. The residual network demonstrated its ability to denoise distorted priors and accelerate learning, though performance decreased if the initial hand trajectory was outside the distribution of the training data for the residual.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

Real-world experiments were conducted on a Universal Robots UR10 manipulator using human video demonstrations for "push," "pick," and "move" tasks in both uncluttered and cluttered environments.

- **Uncluttered Tasks**:
 - **Prior (Baseline)**: Directly replaying the trajectory extracted from the human video was consistently unsuccessful, failing to grasp or manipulate the object in any trial due to a typical 10-15 cm distortion from the correct pick location.
 - **WHIRL (Baseline)**: WHIRL, adapted with VIEW's object-centric reward model, showed some success in grasping but struggled with task manipulation within 100 rollouts (approx. 45 minutes). For "push" and "move," its full task success rate was limited.
 - **ours-BO (Ablation)**: This variant, using Bayesian Optimization for both grasp and task exploration without the QD algorithm for grasp, performed worse than full VIEW, and in some cases, worse than WHIRL, indicating the criticality of the QD approach for effective grasp learning.
 - **VIEW (Ours)**: VIEW achieved high success rates (80-100%) across all uncluttered tasks, significantly outperforming baselines. It learned to replicate human demonstrations in under 30 minutes and fewer than 20 real-world rollouts.

- **Cluttered Tasks**:
 - **Baselines**: Prior, WHIRL, and ours-BO all exhibited limited success (<50%) in cluttered environments. WHIRL's design, even with the adapted reward, struggled with the complexity of avoiding extraneous items and consistently re-grasping.
 - **VIEW (Ours)**: VIEW demonstrated robust performance in cluttered settings, achieving nearly 100% success in grasping and manipulating the correct object, matching the human's demonstration. It consistently solved these tasks in under 30 minutes.

- **Learning from Multiple Tasks (Residual)**: For cluttered tasks, the "ours-residual" variant (full VIEW with the residual network) reduced the number of interactions needed to learn the task by approximately 25% for 3 out of 4 demonstrations. This indicates VIEW's capacity to leverage prior experience for accelerated learning. However, for one demonstration, the residual negatively impacted learning, which the authors attributed to the initial trajectory being outside the residual network's training data distribution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper introduces VIEW, an algorithm that addresses critical limitations in human-to-robot Visual Imitation Learning (VIL), particularly regarding sample efficiency and the embodiment gap.

1. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency**: A primary impact is the significant reduction in the number of real-world robot rollouts and training time required to learn manipulation tasks. VIEW enables robots to learn from a single video demonstration in under 30 minutes with fewer than 20 real-world trials, a substantial improvement over previous state-of-the-art methods that may require hours or many more rollouts. This efficiency makes VIL more practical for rapid deployment in dynamic environments and reduces the cost and wear associated with extensive robot interaction.

2. **Robustness to Embodiment Differences and Noise**: The methodology explicitly addresses the morphological disparities between humans and robots and accounts for sensor noise in video data. By focusing on waypoints and iteratively refining trajectories through exploration, VIEW can successfully translate human intentions into robot actions, even when a direct kinematic mapping is infeasible or the initial visual prior is imperfect.

3. **Scalability to Complex and Long-Horizon Tasks**: VIEW's modular design, including trajectory compression and the segmentation of tasks into grasp and manipulation phases, allows it to handle arbitrarily long video demonstrations and tasks involving multiple objects. This capability is crucial for learning more complex, sequential real-world tasks beyond simple pick-and-place operations.

4. **Few-Shot Learning Capabilities**: The integration of a residual network demonstrates a pathway towards few-shot learning, where robots can leverage past experiences to accelerate learning on new, similar tasks. This mechanism enables the robot to systematically correct for consistent errors in prior extraction, improving adaptability over time.

5. **Foundation for Future Research**: The approach of extracting concise, high-level waypoints and object trajectories can serve as a translation layer, generating state-action pairs from video demonstrations. This can be valuable for downstream policy learning or behavior cloning frameworks, potentially enabling more adaptable policies that respond to changes in the world state.

Despite limitations such as the current inability to incorporate orientation, dependence on a fixed camera pose, and environmental consistency, VIEW represents a step toward one-shot visual imitation learning. The work provides a foundation for developing robotic systems that can quickly acquire new skills from human demonstrations with minimal human intervention, thereby broadening the applicability of robotics in various unstructured environments.
