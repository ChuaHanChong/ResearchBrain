# Flexible Locomotion Learning with Diffusion Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The Diffusion-MPC framework developed by researchers at Harvard University introduces a method for controlling quadrupedal robots, reinterpreting generative diffusion models as implicit dynamics priors for Model Predictive Control. This approach enables flexible, real-time locomotion adaptable to new tasks and environments at test time, demonstrating a 20% reduction in energy consumption in real-world locomotion and up to a 50% improvement in dynamic balancing success rates under external pushes.

## Problem

- Legged locomotion control requires systems that are robust in complex, contact-rich dynamics and adaptable to evolving task requirements and environmental conditions.
- Traditional Model Predictive Control (MPC) relies on accurate dynamics models, which are difficult to obtain for complex robots, and often requires computationally intensive, handcrafted designs.
- Model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) typically learns fixed policies, limiting the ability to synthesize diverse behaviors or adapt to novel objectives at test time without extensive retraining.

## Method

- The Diffusion-==MPC== framework employs a learned generative diffusion model to implicitly capture system dynamics and generate state-action sequences, effectively serving as a data-driven prior for MPC.
- Reward-based planning and constraint projection are integrated directly into the diffusion model's reverse sampling process, allowing for on-the-fly adaptation to new reward functions, compositional objectives, and physical limits without retraining.
- An interactive online training algorithm finetunes the diffusion planner by executing it in the environment, filtering and reweighting collected trajectories by their realized returns, enhancing robustness and bridging the sim-to-real gap.

## Results

- Diffusion-MPC demonstrated successful adaptation on a Unitree Go2 robot to novel tasks, including a **20%** reduction in energy consumption during locomotion, precise joint position regulation for distinct poses, and adaptive height changes from initial states not seen in training.
- Interactive finetuning significantly enhanced real-world performance, achieving **100%** success rates for various commanded speeds (**0.5**, **0.7**, **1.0** m/s) compared to 0-**20%** for untuned policies, and improved dynamic balancing success under external pushes by up to **50%**.
- Real-time operation at 50 Hz with a 21 ms latency was achieved on onboard robot hardware through optimized asynchronous planning and early-step caching techniques, enabling practical deployment and zero-shot transfer to challenging real-world terrains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generative diffusion models can implicitly capture complex system dynamics and serve as effective priors for MPC-like planning, enabling flexible, data-driven control.
> - Integrating reward-based planning and constraint projection during the diffusion model's reverse sampling process facilitates highly adaptable behavior synthesis at test time, responding to diverse objectives and physical limitations.
> - Interactive online training through reward-weighted denoising adaptation is critical for robustifying learned planners in real-world environments, overcoming limitations of purely offline demonstration data and improving stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged locomotion in robotics presents a significant challenge, requiring control systems that are both robust in complex, contact-rich dynamics and adaptable to evolving task requirements and environmental conditions. The broader research landscape for locomotion control is primarily dominated by two paradigms: Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Model-Free Reinforcement Learning (RL).

Classical MPC approaches are recognized for their ability to achieve agile and versatile maneuvers by explicitly encoding objectives and constraints within an optimization framework. This inherent flexibility allows for adaptation to new scenarios. However, traditional MPC methods often depend on highly accurate dynamics models, which are difficult to obtain for complex, contact-rich locomotion. They also tend to be computationally intensive, frequently necessitating simplifying assumptions and handcrafted designs that limit their accuracy and generalizability in real-world settings.

In contrast, model-free RL methods have demonstrated impressive results in generating robust policies for various behaviors, including traversing complex terrains and achieving extreme motions. These methods often leverage massively parallel simulation training. A key limitation of RL, however, is that it typically learns a fixed policy network, which can make it challenging to synthesize diverse behaviors or adapt to novel objectives at test time without extensive retraining. While some RL approaches allow for a family of behaviors, these behaviors typically need to be predefined during training through specific reward designs and incorporated as network inputs, limiting on-the-fly flexibility.

Recent advancements in generative models, particularly diffusion models, have opened new avenues for decision-making and control. These models can learn expressive priors over trajectories from large datasets. Some works have explored diffusion models as action generators or for specific behaviors with pre-defined skill vectors, while others have considered them as generative priors for planning in simpler settings.

This paper addresses the gap between the flexibility of MPC and the efficiency and robustness of RL by proposing a framework that leverages the learned generative capabilities of diffusion models within an MPC-like planning scheme. It aims to overcome the rigidities of fixed RL policies and the modeling complexities of classical MPC, particularly for flexible, test-time adaptation in legged locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a control framework for legged robots that enables flexible and adaptable locomotion, particularly at test time, without requiring retraining for every new task or environmental condition. The authors aim to achieve controllers that are robust, compatible with task and safety considerations, and capable of synthesizing diverse behaviors on the fly.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current locomotion control paradigms:
- **Adaptability Gap:** Existing model-free RL methods often produce fixed policies that are difficult to adapt to new behaviors, task objectives, or environmental changes (e.g., new terrains, altered physical limits) at test time. Robots operating autonomously in unpredictable environments require controllers that can integrate new constraints and adapt to novel objectives without prior training on those specific conditions.
- **Dynamics Modeling Challenge in MPC:** While classical MPC offers a natural mechanism for adaptation by incorporating objectives and constraints, its reliance on highly accurate dynamics models is a significant hurdle for complex, contact-rich locomotion. Obtaining these models is often difficult, and practical implementations frequently require substantial simplifications and handcrafted designs, which can limit performance and generalizability.
- **Limitations of Imitation Learning:** Directly training generative planners on demonstrations can lead to issues such as insufficient data coverage for critical objectives (e.g., energy efficiency, smoothness), limited data quality, inconsistencies, and the sim-to-real gap. Policies learned purely through imitation may also drift off-distribution during real-world deployment.

To address these motivations, the authors propose **Diffusion-MPC**, which aims to:
1. **Reinterpret Diffusion Models as Generative Priors for MPC:** Leverage the expressive power of learned generative diffusion models to implicitly capture system dynamics and generate state-action trajectories. This allows for model-based planning without explicitly handcrafting dynamics models.
2. **Enable Flexible Behavior Synthesis at Test Time:** Integrate reward-based planning and constraint projection directly into the diffusion model's reverse sampling process. This allows for adapting trajectories to new reward specifications, combining multiple reward and constraint terms, and satisfying physical limits without retraining the core model.
3. **Enhance Robustness and Alignment with Task Objectives:** Introduce an interactive online training algorithm. This procedure involves executing the reward-and-constraint planner in the environment, filtering and reweighting collected trajectories by their realized returns, and then updating the diffusion model. This addresses the limitations of purely imitative pretraining and improves robustness in real-world deployment.
4. **Achieve Real-Time Deployment:** Design practical techniques for real-time operation on onboard hardware, such as asynchronous execution and early-step caching, to meet the high control rate requirements of legged locomotion.

In summary, the key objective is to combine the flexibility of MPC with the data-driven learning capabilities of diffusion models and interactive training, thereby enabling robust, adaptable, and real-time locomotion control for quadrupedal robots in dynamic and unpredictable environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Diffusion-MPC framework integrates generative diffusion models with Model Predictive Control principles to achieve flexible locomotion.

#### 3.1. A. Diffusion-MPC Formulation

The core of the methodology involves reinterpreting diffusion models as generative priors for model predictive control. Instead of an explicit dynamics model, a diffusion model learns to jointly represent state transitions and action proposals as state-action sequences ($\tau$) over a finite horizon $H$.
- **Trajectory Diffusion:** The diffusion model learns to map Gaussian noise to structured state-action trajectories by reversing a forward noising process. It models the probability distribution $p_\theta(\tau)$ of desired trajectories.
- **Flexible Behavior Synthesis Through Sampling:** During the reverse (denoising) process, the model iteratively refines a candidate trajectory. At each step $k$, a reward planning update and a constraint projection are applied:
 - **Reward Planning:** The denoised trajectory is adjusted using a gradient-based update: $\tau^{(k-1)} \leftarrow \tau^{(k-1)} + \eta_k \Sigma_k \nabla_\tau R(\tau^{(k-1)})$. This steers trajectories toward higher reward regions, where $R(\tau)$ is a differentiable trajectory-level reward function.
 - **Compositional Rewards:** The reward function $R(\tau)$ can combine a learned neural component ($R_{nn}$) for complex behaviors and an analytic component ($R_{an}$) for hand-specified objectives. This allows for flexible task definition at test time.
 - **Constraint Projection:** After the reward planning step, the trajectory is projected onto a feasible set $C$ using an operator $\Pi_C(\tau^{(k-1)})$, ensuring physical limits and safety constraints are met (e.g., joint limits).
 - **Initial State Enforcement:** The initial state of the trajectory ($s_0$) is fixed to match the current observation, maintaining consistency with the robot's real-time state.
 - **Candidate Ranking:** To improve robustness and explore diverse rollouts, multiple trajectories ($N$ candidates) are sampled in parallel. The best trajectory, as scored by the reward function $R(\tau)$, is selected for execution.
- **Joint State-Action Modeling:** Unlike action-only diffusion approaches, Diffusion-MPC jointly models state and action distributions. This allows for direct application of reward shaping and constraint injection to the evolving state sequence at every denoising step, implicitly respecting system dynamics without needing a separate dynamics model.

#### 3.2. B. Planner Learning with Environment Interaction

To overcome the limitations of offline demonstration data (e.g., lack of coverage, quality, sim-to-real gap), the authors introduce an interactive online training procedure:
- **Reward-Weighted Denoising Adaptation:** The diffusion planner is deployed in the environment to collect new trajectories. These trajectories are then filtered and reweighted based on their *realized returns* from the environment ($R_r(\tau)$), similar to reward-weighted regression.
- **Filtering and Reweighting:** Trajectories are weighted exponentially by their returns ($w(R_r(\tau)) = \exp(R_r(\tau)/T)$). Only the top-K highest-return trajectories are retained for updates, with others discarded (assigned zero weight). The weights are then normalized.
- **Objective Function:** The diffusion model is finetuned using a weighted denoising objective: $\mathcal{L}_{RWD}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\bar{w}(R_r(\tau)) \|\tau - \tau_\theta(\tau^{(k)}, k)\|_2^2]$. This biases updates towards trajectories that yield higher environmental rewards.
- **Replay Buffer:** A replay buffer interleaves new on-policy planner rollouts with previously collected trajectories, maintaining data diversity while focusing learning on reward-favored regions.
This interactive approach is designed to be stable, off-policy, and sample-efficient, avoiding high-variance policy gradients and critic bootstrapping.

#### 3.3. C. Real-time Planning for Deployment

To ensure real-time operation on quadrupedal robots, several practical techniques are implemented:
- **Asynchronous Planning:** An asynchronous pipeline with a planning horizon $H$ and replan margin $D$ is used. The controller executes actions from a current plan. When execution reaches $H-D$ steps, a replanning cycle is triggered using the latest observation. The robot continues executing $D$ buffered actions from the old plan until the new plan is ready and time-aligned. This maintains a high control rate while preserving closed-loop feedback. (e.g., $H=11, D=3$).
- **Caching Early Denoising Steps:** Successive plans often share similar early denoising steps. To reduce computational cost, the existing plan is shifted across time steps and reused as initialization for the next planning window, up to $m$ steps. This warm-starting strategy saves computation while preserving solution quality.
- **Sampler Choice and Step Budget:** DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models) is used for higher quality sampling during inference, typically with a budget of 10 denoising steps.

#### 3.4. D. Experiment Setup and Adaptation Tasks

- **Simulator and Robot:** Isaacgym is used for pretraining and interactive training. The framework is deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadrupedal robot with an Intel NUC 12 PRO onboard computer. Control frequency is 50 Hz.
- **Observation Space:** The robot's observation includes base angular velocity, projected gravity, command vector, joint positions and velocities, and the previous action.
- **Low-Level Control:** PD control (Kp=40.0, Kd=1.0) is used for joint torques.
- **Adaptation Tasks:** The framework is evaluated on diverse adaptation tasks, each defined by specific reward models ($R_{nn}$ or $R_{an}$) and/or constraint projections:
 - **Base Height Variation:** A neural network reward model predicts reward for desired height profiles.
 - **Joint Limit Restriction:** Analytic rewards penalize deviations from a target posture and violations of joint ranges, complemented by projection.
 - **Energy Saving:** A neural network reward model predicts energy cost, and the planner minimizes time-integrated mechanical power.
 - **Joint Acceleration/Velocity Regularization:** Analytic terms penalize high joint rates, with projection for enforcing limits.
 - **Balancing:** Analytic rewards align the body-frame gravity vector with a desired direction and regularize its temporal smoothness.
- **Planner Learning Process:** A two-stage process is used: initial pretraining on 4,000 trajectories from a PPO-trained demonstrator policy in a static environment, followed by finetuning with 1,500 additional environment interactions per environment using the interactive training procedure and domain randomization (varying base mass, mass position, friction, initial joint positions, motor strength, proprioception latency).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the capabilities of Diffusion-MPC in both simulation and real-world deployment, highlighting its adaptation ability, the effectiveness of interactive learning, and real-time performance.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments

1. **Adaptation Tasks:**
 - **Impact of Planning Components (Table II):** Evaluation across 1,000 environments over 3,000 simulation steps revealed that increasing the number of candidate trajectories, enabling reward-based planning, and using constraint projection consistently improved task adherence (reduced penalties).
 - **Candidate Number:** Increasing candidates (C1 to C100) generally reduced penalties, indicating improved robustness through exploration of diverse trajectory proposals.
 - **Reward Planning (R✓):** Activating reward planning significantly reduced penalties across all task categories (Joint Vel, Acc, Pos, Balancing, Energy) compared to no reward planning (R✗), demonstrating its effectiveness in semantically aligning trajectories with objectives.
 - **Constraint Projection (C✓):** Constraint projection was crucial for feasibility-dominated tasks like joint position adaptation, ensuring safe joint configurations. The non-zero penalties in these cases indicate successful generalization to regions beyond the demonstration distribution, finding feasible actions for novel objectives.
 - **Velocity Tracking:** The framework maintained high linear and angular velocity tracking (above 97% of baseline), demonstrating that adaptation did not excessively compromise nominal locomotion quality.
2. **Interactive Learning Experiments:**
 - **Finetuning Effectiveness (Table III):** Comparing a planner initialized on offline data with one subsequently finetuned via interactive training showed significant improvements. Finetuning substantially increased stability and velocity-tracking accuracy, particularly at higher commanded speeds (e.g., 0.7 m/s and 1.0 m/s forward velocity), where stability improved from 23.1% to 90.5% and 18.4% to 98.8%, respectively. Velocity deviation ($E_v$) also decreased.

#### 4.2. B. Real-world Experiments

1. **Adaptation Tasks:**
 - **Energy Saving (Fig. 2):** In a long-distance locomotion task (0.6 m/s), the energy-saving reward led to a 20% reduction in overall energy consumption (State of Charge, SoC) compared to no planning. This was achieved through smoother action patterns and reduced foot lifting height.
 - **Joint Position Regulation (Fig. 2, Fig. 3):** The robot successfully adapted to distinct, novel reference joint poses (negative and positive calf targets), which differed from the training data. The negative target resulted in an upward tilt with front-leg propulsion, while the positive target caused a forward shift with increased calf-thigh angles and front-calf use for balance. This demonstrated flexible adaptation to novel kinematic preferences.
 - **Height Variation (Fig. 4):** The robot transitioned from an initial base height of 25 cm to a desired 18 cm solely through test-time reward planning. Notably, the training dataset did not contain demonstrations of height *transitions*, indicating successful generalization.
 - **Dynamic Balancing (Fig. 5, Table IV):** Under external lateral pushes from a pendulum, incorporating balancing rewards and constraint planning significantly improved the robot's ability to withstand disturbances and recover balance. Success rates for medium and hard impacts increased from 0.8 to 1.0 and 0.6 to 0.9, respectively.
2. **Locomotion Performance:**
 - **Zero-Shot Deployment (Fig. 6):** Diffusion-MPC demonstrated successful zero-shot transfer and stable locomotion on challenging real-world terrains, including soft uneven grass and a grassy slope, without environment-specific retraining. This was facilitated by neural network-based foot-lifting rewards and adaptive joint regularization for slopes.
3. **Interactive Learning (Real-world):**
 - **Finetuning on Hardware (Fig. 7, Table V):** The finetuned policy showed substantial gains in real-world success rates (100% at 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0 m/s) compared to the untuned version (20%, 10%, 0% at respective speeds). The untuned policy performed worse than in simulation, potentially due to reduced responsiveness from the replan margin.
 - **Learning from Scratch (Fig. 8):** The research also demonstrated that an interactive diffusion planner could be learned effectively from scratch, without reliance on initial demonstration data, and successfully deployed in the real world.
4. **Deployment Ablation (Table VI):**
 - **Real-time Performance:** The optimized asynchronous planning strategy (D=3, m=7, with cache refresh) achieved real-time operation at 50 Hz with a low latency of 21 ms.
 - **Impact of Caching and Resetting:** Aggressive caching without refresh (configs B, D) could degrade motion quality for omni-directional transitions. Cache resetting (config E) was crucial for robust transitions between different locomotion patterns, maintaining high motion quality for forward, turning, and backward movements. DDIM sampling degraded motion quality compared to cached DDPM steps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Diffusion-MPC, a novel framework that significantly advances the state of legged locomotion control by addressing the critical need for robust and adaptable controllers that can operate in unpredictable environments.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Bridging the Gap between RL and MPC:** Diffusion-MPC effectively combines the strengths of model-free reinforcement learning (data-driven policy learning) and model predictive control (explicit objective and constraint handling). By using a learned generative diffusion model as an implicit dynamics prior, it overcomes the limitations of classical MPC's reliance on accurate, handcrafted models and the rigidity of fixed policies from traditional RL.
- **Flexible Test-Time Adaptation:** A key contribution is the ability to adapt robot behavior to novel reward specifications, complex constraints, new terrains, and altered physical limits at test time, without requiring extensive retraining. This is achieved through reward-based planning, constraint projection, and compositional reward functions, enabling a diverse range of behaviors from a single learned model. This capability is crucial for robots operating in dynamic and unstructured real-world settings where unforeseen conditions are common.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Performance:** The proposed interactive online training algorithm, leveraging reward-weighted denoising, allows the diffusion planner to finetune itself by interacting with the environment. This significantly improves robustness, particularly in challenging scenarios and at higher commanded speeds, and bridges the sim-to-real gap, leading to more reliable real-world deployment. The demonstration of learning from scratch without offline demonstration data further expands its applicability.
- **Practical Real-time Deployment:** The development of practical techniques such as asynchronous planning and early-step caching ensures that Diffusion-MPC can operate in real-time on onboard robot hardware, meeting the high control rate requirements for dynamic legged locomotion. This moves the approach beyond theoretical proposals into tangible, deployable solutions.
- **Skill Compositionality:** The framework inherently supports the combination of multiple reward and constraint terms, enabling the synthesis of complex behaviors from simpler, constituent skills. This modularity enhances the utility and generalizability of the learned controller.

The potential impact extends to various applications where adaptable legged robots are needed, such as exploration in hazardous environments, search and rescue operations, logistics, and assistive robotics. By providing a practical path toward adaptable, general-purpose embodied control, Diffusion-MPC lays groundwork for future developments in autonomous robotics that can seamlessly integrate diverse sensory inputs (e.g., LiDAR, vision, natural language) and handle more complex, task-dependent constraints, ultimately leading to more intelligent and versatile robotic agents.
