# Groupwise Agentic Grading and Advantage Redistribution for Code Agent RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Gagar is a reinforcement learning method for code agents. For each rollout group, a trained grader agent inspects every attempt together in one shared workspace and ranks the test-passing patches on quality. Lower-ranked passes are downweighted, then all positive advantages are rescaled so their sum is unchanged. On MiMo-V2.6-Flash this beats binary-reward GRPO, uses fewer turns and tokens, and trains more stably.

## Problem

- With binary test rewards, GRPO gives every test-passing trajectory the same advantage, so clean targeted fixes and bloated out-of-scope patches are reinforced equally.
- Text-only judges that score one candidate at a time miss repository context, execution evidence and cross-candidate comparison, which are needed to spot unnecessary complexity or weak strategies.
- Simply downweighting lower-quality passes removes positive credit but leaves negative advantages unchanged, tilting updates toward penalties and destabilizing training.

## Method

- **Groupwise agentic grader**: an ==SFT==-trained MiMo-V2.6-Pro reads trajectories, patches, repository and test logs in a shared workspace, runs targeted checks, and must cite evidence for any negative judgment.
- Five-criterion tiered ranking: approach, precision, minimality, side effects and codebase consistency are scored into T1/T2/T3 tiers and mapped to discount factors; confirmed leaked-answer hacks get zero reward.
- ==Sum-preserving advantage redistribution==: one common factor rescales the quality-weighted positive advantages back to their original sum, keeps the quality ratios between passes, and leaves failed-trajectory advantages untouched.

## Results

- In code-only RL, the baseline fell from **58.5% to 50.2%** on DeepSWE by step 28, while Gagar reached **62.2%** (**+12.1 points**) and later peaked at **63.4%**.
- At step 28, Gagar cut DeepSWE mean turns from **132.3 to 111.6** and tokens from **191.9k to 172.9k**; SWE-bench Pro rose to **62.5%** versus a ~59% baseline plateau.
- Downweighting without redistribution raised entropy from **0.359 to 0.905** and fell to **56.2%** on DeepSWE; blinded quality win rate was **69.8%**, and mixed-task Pro scored **71.9%** on DeepSWE.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When adding quality preferences on top of verifiable rewards, move credit among the successes instead of shrinking it, so positive and negative updates stay balanced.
> - Comparing candidates for the same task, with the ability to run code, exposes quality differences that grading each output alone in text misses.
> - Rewarding precise, minimal implementations also curbs the runaway growth of trajectory length in long-horizon agent RL, so solution quality and efficiency improve together.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In repository-level coding, an agent changes code and runs tests. Executable tests tell a training system whether an agent's patch passes. They do not distinguish a focused fix from a passing patch that makes unnecessary or risky changes. That distinction matters to developers because it can affect review, rework, and maintainability after the tests pass. GAGAR has an agentic grader compare the attempts for the same task. It then changes how much training credit each passing attempt receives. In controlled code-only training with MiMo-V2.6-Flash, later training maintained stronger DeepSWE test performance with fewer measured turns and tokens.

### 2. Comparing attempts, preserving credit

A rollout group is several attempts at one task. Each trajectory's "advantage" is its training weight relative to the other trajectories in its group. Binary test outcomes give every passing trajectory the same positive group-relative advantage, so they do not distinguish implementation quality. GAGAR operates on groups containing both passing and failing trajectories.

GAGAR's SFT-trained MiMo-V2.6-Pro grader jointly examines the task, repository, submitted patches, complete trajectories, and test outputs. It can inspect code and run targeted checks, and it ranks only passing candidates. The ranking uses five dimensions:

- suitability of approach
- implementation precision
- minimality of changes
- avoidance of unintended side effects
- consistency with codebase conventions

Ties are allowed when the evidence does not justify a distinction.

After ranking, quality-based factors discount lower-ranked passing trajectories. A common rescaling then restores the original positive-advantage total across passing trajectories and retains the relative ratios produced by the quality factors. In the idealized formulation, failed-trajectory advantages stay unchanged. The deployed implementation caps rescaling at 1.5. Exact preservation holds only when that cap is inactive. When the cap is active, the original positive sum and failed advantages need not be preserved. The paper does not report how often the cap is active.

The proposed process. Passing and failing trajectories, patches, the repository, and tests feed "Agentic Inspection." Confirmed hacks are assigned zero reward and excluded from ranking. Valid passing patches are ranked, and the ranks become quality weights. Rank-based redistribution restores the original positive sum and leaves failed advantages unchanged before the RL policy update.

### 3. What the controlled results show

The controlled test used code-only RL with MiMo-V2.6-Flash, initialized from a pre-RL SFT checkpoint. The benchmark was DeepSWE v1.1, a long-horizon software-development benchmark. Each evaluation uses three samples per task, and the mean pass rate is reported as avg@3.

The binary-reward baseline's pass rate fell from 58.5% at step 20 to 50.2% at step 28. The authors stopped the baseline at that step. At step 28, GAGAR reached 62.2%, and it continued to a reported DeepSWE peak of 63.4% at step 44. At step 28, GAGAR also used 111.6 versus 132.3 mean turns, which is 15.6% lower. It generated 172.9k versus 191.9k mean tokens, which is 9.9% lower. These are evaluation-turn and token-length comparisons. They do not measure elapsed training time, total attempts, or wall-clock speed.

### 4. Why restoration matters

The downweighting-only ablation tests GAGAR's explanation. That run discounts lower-quality passing trajectories but neither restores the removed positive credit nor re-centers the resulting advantages. The ablation therefore removes restoration and centering together. It does not isolate either one as the sole cause.

Without redistribution, policy entropy rose from 0.359 at step 1 to 0.905 at step 30. With the full method, it rose from 0.358 to 0.513. The step-28 DeepSWE results show the same gap:

- **Downweighting only:** 56.2% avg@3, 143.5 turns, and 236.0k tokens.
- **With redistribution:** 62.2% avg@3, 111.6 turns, and 172.9k tokens.

These results are consistent with the authors' interpretation that discarding positive credit while leaving negative advantages unchanged disrupts credit balance and accompanies unstable training. They do not establish that credit balance is the only cause of instability.

### 5. Keep the learning signal

In GAGAR, test success remains the gate for learning. Comparisons among passing implementations then decide where the passing credit goes. The proposed conservation rule connects a finer preference for implementation quality to preserving the group's successful-training signal. In the deployed implementation, exact preservation holds only when the rescaling cap is inactive.
