# Learning Neural Constitutive Laws From Motion Observations for Generalizable PDE Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> A hybrid framework, Neural Constitutive Laws (NCLaw), learns material-specific constitutive models from motion observations by integrating neural networks into a differentiable physics simulator that explicitly enforces fundamental physical laws. This approach demonstrates robust generalization capabilities across diverse materials and complex scenarios, outperforming purely data-driven methods by orders of magnitude in unseen conditions and requiring less training data.

## Problem

- End-to-end neural network models for physical simulation often fail to guarantee physical correctness and generalizability due to implicitly learning fundamental PDEs.
- Modeling complex, highly nonlinear material-specific constitutive laws is challenging for traditional methods and purely data-driven approaches.
- Direct learning of constitutive laws is hampered by the difficulty of acquiring labeled stress-strain data.

- A hybrid approach (**NCLaw**) integrates neural networks to model material-specific constitutive laws within a differentiable ==PDE== simulator based on the ==Material Point Method== (==MPM==).
- Fundamental physical laws are explicitly enforced by the differentiable simulator, while complex constitutive relationships are learned by neural networks.
- Neural network architectures are designed with physics-aware inductive biases, including rotation equivariance and undeformed state equilibrium, to ensure physical consistency.

## Results

- NCLaw achieved reconstruction losses typically below 1e-3 and generally outperformed spline, neural, and GNN baselines across various elastoplastic materials and fluids.
- The framework demonstrated robust generalization to unseen initial/boundary conditions, complex geometries (up to **1M** particles), extended time horizons, and multi-physics scenarios, outperforming baselines by orders of magnitude.
- It successfully learned constitutive laws from real-world 2D video observations of dough, and ablation studies confirmed the critical importance of physics-aware network architectural priors for stability and convergence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Disentangling fundamental physical laws (enforced by the simulator) from material-specific constitutive laws (learned by NNs) is crucial for generalizability and physical consistency.
> - Neural networks, equipped with physics-aware architectural priors like rotation equivariance, can effectively learn diverse and complex constitutive behaviors.
> - Learning constitutive laws can be achieved from readily available motion observations by embedding neural networks within a differentiable physics simulator, bypassing the need for direct stress-strain measurements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges at the intersection of classical physics simulations and machine learning, specifically concerning the modeling of Partial Differential Equation (PDE)-governed dynamical systems. These systems are fundamental across various scientific and engineering disciplines. Traditionally, such systems are solved computationally using numerical methods like the Finite Element Method (FEM).

Recently, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a tool to advance PDE modeling. Existing ML approaches can broadly be categorized into two groups:
1. **Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs):** These methods assume the underlying PDE is known and leverage this knowledge in their loss functions to guide neural network solutions. While ensuring physical consistency, they still learn the entire solution space.
2. **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and similar end-to-end models:** These approaches learn the dynamics entirely from data, often without explicit knowledge of the underlying PDEs. While flexible, they may struggle with physical correctness and generalizability, particularly when dealing with out-of-distribution scenarios, as they implicitly learn both the governing equations and material properties.

The presented research positions itself by advocating for a hybrid approach. It argues that universally accepted physical laws, which form the core of many PDEs (e.g., conservation of mass and momentum in elastodynamics), should be explicitly enforced rather than learned. This ensures physical correctness by design. Conversely, the paper identifies constitutive laws—relationships describing material-specific behaviors like stress-strain in solids or shear stress-rate in fluids—as particularly suitable candidates for data-driven learning. These laws are often complex, highly nonlinear, and traditionally designed by domain experts through various data-fitting tools (e.g., high-order polynomials, splines). The authors note that neural networks have shown promise in this area due to their high learning capacity and versatility, potentially alleviating the need for manual function form design.

Within this context, the paper tackles two primary challenges associated with learning constitutive laws via neural networks:
1. **Enforcing Physical Priors:** Ensuring that neural constitutive laws adhere to fundamental physical principles, such as frame indifference (rotation equivariance).
2. **Data Acquisition:** Overcoming the difficulty of obtaining labeled stress-strain pairs, which are often impractical or impossible to measure directly.

This work contributes to the growing field of physics-informed machine learning by providing a framework that integrates data-driven constitutive models within a classical, differentiable PDE simulation pipeline, aiming to achieve both physical fidelity and broad generalizability from limited data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a hybrid neural network (NN) and Partial Differential Equation (PDE) approach, termed "Neural Constitutive Laws" (NCLaw), for learning generalizable PDE dynamics directly from motion observations. This framework aims to predict material behavior in scenarios beyond the training data by accurately modeling underlying constitutive laws.

The key motivations driving this research are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Limitations of End-to-End Neural Network Models:** Many existing NN approaches for physical simulation learn an end-to-end model that implicitly encompasses both the fundamental governing PDEs (e.g., elastodynamics) and the material-specific constitutive models. The authors argue that without explicit enforcement of PDE knowledge, these approaches frequently cannot guarantee physical correctness. This deficiency limits their generalizability, especially when applied to scenarios that deviate significantly from the training data. The motivation is to overcome these limitations by explicitly incorporating known physical principles.

- **Leveraging Known Physical Laws:** The paper posits that core governing PDEs are often well-established and widely accepted within scientific communities. Instead of attempting to learn these fundamental laws from data, which can lead to physically inconsistent predictions, they should be explicitly enforced. By doing so, the framework can leverage decades of established physical understanding to ensure that the simulated dynamics remain physically plausible and correct.

- **Identifying Constitutive Laws as Optimal Targets for Learning:** While fundamental PDEs are known, the specific constitutive laws that describe the behavior of particular materials (e.g., how a material deforms under stress, or flows under shear) are often complex, highly nonlinear, and challenging to model accurately with explicit mathematical forms. These relationships are inherently data-fitting in nature. Therefore, the authors motivate that constitutive models represent a particularly suitable component of a physical system to be learned using the data-fitting capabilities of neural networks. A single, expressive neural network architecture could potentially capture diverse behaviors ranging from elastic solids to fluids, replacing the need for bespoke, expert-designed models for each material.

- **Overcoming Data Scarcity Challenges for Constitutive Modeling:** A significant challenge in learning constitutive laws directly is the difficulty of obtaining labeled datasets, such as precise stress-strain pairs. Such data is often impossible to measure in situ (e.g., in biological tissues) or requires specialized and expensive equipment. The motivation here is to enable learning from more readily available data: motion observations (kinematic information, like particle positions). By embedding the neural constitutive model within a differentiable simulator, the system can be trained by comparing simulated motion with observed motion, circumventing the need for direct stress or strain measurements.

- **Guaranteeing Physical Consistency and Robustness through Network Architecture:** When learning constitutive laws with neural networks, it is crucial that these learned models adhere to fundamental physical priors, such as rotation equivariance (frame indifference) and undeformed state equilibrium (maintaining rest shape under zero load). Without these guarantees, the learned models might exhibit unphysical behaviors. The motivation is to design a network architecture that intrinsically bakes in these priors as inductive biases, thereby ensuring physical consistency and improving the robustness and generalizability of the learned models without relying on extensive data augmentation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed "Neural Constitutive Laws" (NCLaw), integrates neural networks into a differentiable physics simulation framework to learn material-specific constitutive models from motion observations. The approach is hybrid, leveraging explicit PDE knowledge for the overall dynamics while using neural networks for the difficult-to-model constitutive relationships.

#### 3.1. Background and PDE Foundation

The work assumes that materials adhere to the elastodynamic equation (ρ₀¨ϕ = ∇ · P + ρ₀b), where ϕ is the deformation map, ρ₀ is the initial density, P is the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress, ¨ϕ is acceleration, and b is the body force. For this PDE to be well-defined, constitutive relationships must be provided. These include:
- **Elastic Constitutive Law (Eμ):** Defines the relationship between the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress (P) and the elastic deformation gradient (F_e).
- **Plastic Constitutive Law (Pμ):** Defines an inequality yield constraint (f_Y(P) < 0) and a flow rule for when this constraint is violated, typically projecting a trial elastic deformation gradient onto an admissible elastic region.

#### 3.2. Discretization and Differentiable Simulation

To numerically solve the elastodynamic equation, it is discretized spatially and temporally. The Material Point Method (MPM) is chosen for its versatility in handling various materials. MPM discretizes the domain with material points, and the continuous PDE is transformed into a discrete dynamical system: s_{n+1} = M_μ(s_n), where s_n represents the state vector (positions, velocities, elastic deformation gradients of all material points) at time step n, and μ encodes system parameters, including those of the constitutive laws.

A key aspect of the methodology is implementing this MPM-based simulation in a differentiable manner. This allows for gradient-based optimization, where gradients of a loss function can be backpropagated through the simulation steps to update model parameters.

#### 3.3. Neural Constitutive Laws (NCLaw)

The core of NCLaw involves replacing the classical, expert-designed elastic (E_μ) and plastic (P_μ) constitutive laws within the differentiable MPM simulator with neural network-based models: E_{θe} and P_{θp}, where θ_e and θ_p are the neural network weights.

- **Hybrid NN-PDE Time-Stepping:** In the modified time-stepping algorithm, the neural elastic law E_{θe} computes stress from the elastic deformation gradient. The system then updates the state (positions, velocities, trial elastic deformation gradient) by solving the governing PDE using the standard MPM time integration scheme (I). Finally, the neural plastic law P_{θp} processes the trial elastic deformation gradient to obtain the new elastic deformation gradient, projecting it onto the plastic yield constraint if necessary.
- **Modularity:** The crucial distinction is that only the constitutive laws are learned by neural networks, while the widely accepted PDE-based time integration scheme (I), which embodies fundamental physical laws like conservation of mass and momentum, remains unchanged and is not learned.
- **General Representation:** Unlike prior work that might assume specific backbone forms (e.g., neo-Hookean elasticity), NCLaw employs neural networks to represent the entire constitutive law without such assumptions, providing greater expressiveness and versatility.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

The neural networks are trained by minimizing a loss function (L) that quantifies the difference between the simulated particle positions ({x_n}) and the ground-truth observed particle positions ({x_gt,n}) over a trajectory.
min_{θe, θp} L({x_gt,n}^T_{n=0}, {x_n}^T_{n=0})
This approach uses easily measurable motion observation data for supervision, circumventing the need for direct stress-strain measurements.

#### 3.5. Physics-Aware Network Architecture

To ensure physical consistency and robustness, the neural networks are designed with specific inductive biases:

- **Rotation Equivariance (Frame Indifference):** The properties of materials should be invariant under rotations. To achieve this for square matrix inputs like deformation gradients, the neural networks are fed with a selected set of rotation invariants:
 - Principal stretches (singular values Σ) obtained from Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the deformation gradient F_e = UΣVᵀ.
 - The right Cauchy-Green tensor (F_eᵀF_e).
 - The determinant of the elastic deformation gradient (det(F_e)).
 The rotation matrix R (from F_e = RS via polar decomposition) is extracted from the deformation gradient. The network's output, which is rotation-invariant, is then multiplied by R to produce a rotation-equivariant stress (P) or updated deformation gradient (F_e,new), ensuring it transforms correctly under rotations.

- **Undeformed State Equilibrium (Rest Shape Preservation):** To ensure that materials maintain their rest shape under zero external load, two architectural choices are made:
 - Input invariants are normalized such that they are zero when the material is in its undeformed state.
 - All bias layers are removed from the neural networks.
 These measures ensure that when zero loads are applied (inputs are zero), the elasticity network generates zero stress, and the plasticity network preserves the trial elastic deformation gradient, thus maintaining equilibrium.

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

The differentiable MPM simulator is implemented on GPUs using Warp. The neural networks are 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with 64 neurons per layer, using GELU activation and no normalization layers. Training employs the Adam optimizer with specific learning rates for elasticity and plasticity, a cosine annealing scheduler for learning rates, and gradient clipping. A "teacher-forcing" scheme, restarting from ground-truth positions periodically, is also used. The networks are implemented in PyTorch, which is integrated with Warp for efficient GPU memory sharing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the reconstruction, generalization, and practical applicability of the NCLaw framework.

#### 4.1. Reconstruction Performance

NCLaw was trained on a single motion trajectory for four diverse elastoplastic materials: JELL-O (purely elastic), SAND (Drucker-Prager plasticity), PLASTICINE (von Mises plasticity), and WATER (weakly compressible fluid).
- NCLaw achieved low reconstruction losses (typically below 1e-3) and generally outperformed baseline methods (spline, neural, gnn). It ranked first or second in most environments.
- The `neural` baseline performed well on JELL-O (purely elastic), which lacks plasticity, but struggled with other materials. The `spline` method consistently yielded poor results. `gnn` showed acceptable, but not optimal, performance across environments.
- NCLaw's performance was comparable to "oracle" methods (`labeled` and `sys-id`) that had access to ground-truth stress-strain data or assumed known constitutive models, respectively. This indicated NCLaw's ability to learn complex constitutive behaviors from motion observations alone.

#### 4.2. Generalization Performance (One-Shot)

After training on a single, limited trajectory (1k particles, 1k time steps), NCLaw was evaluated on out-of-distribution generalization tasks:
- **Extended Time Horizon:** NCLaw maintained accuracy even when the simulation duration was doubled.
- **Unseen Initial/Boundary Conditions:** The method generalized to different initial velocities and boundary conditions (e.g., inclined planes).
- **Challenging Geometries:** NCLaw accurately simulated complex geometries (e.g., armadillo, goldfish, bunny, cow) and scaled to larger particle counts (up to ~30k), significantly exceeding the training data's particle count.
- **Qualitative Comparison:** NCLaw visually matched ground-truth simulations across all generalization tasks. In contrast, `neural` failed for plasticity-heavy materials, and `gnn` often produced visually implausible results, suggesting its strong reliance on extensive training data for generalization, unlike NCLaw.
- **Quantitative Comparison:** NCLaw consistently ranked first or second in generalization tasks, outperforming baselines by orders of magnitude, highlighting its efficiency in learning generalizable dynamics from minimal data.

#### 4.3. Extreme Generalization

NCLaw's robustness was tested under extreme conditions:
- **Million-Particle Simulation:** It successfully simulated a complex water dragon geometry with over 1 million particles, demonstrating scalability and accuracy, despite being trained on only 1k particles.
- **Vigorous Collisions:** The model generalized well to scenarios involving multiple rubber toys colliding vigorously with each other and the floor, a condition not present in the training data. This highlighted the benefit of disentangling constitutive laws from the main simulation framework.

#### 4.4. Multi-Physics Generalization

The framework demonstrated generalization to multi-physics environments:
- **Coupled Systems:** NCLaw accurately simulated a rubber duck (JELL-O) falling into a swimming pool (WATER), involving over 100k particles and material coupling, with negligible visual differences from the ground truth.
- **Phase Transition:** It accurately modeled a "melting" ice cream (PLASTICINE gradually transitioning to SAND), showcasing stability and accuracy even with time-varying material properties.

#### 4.5. Real-World Experiment

NCLaw was applied to a real-world scenario of dough deformation:
- **Learning from Video:** It successfully learned the complex elastic and plastic constitutive laws of real-world dough from 2D video observations, which were used to track particle-level deformations (with regularization for the depth dimension in 3D).
- **Reconstruction and Generalization:** NCLaw accurately reconstructed the observed dough trajectory and generalized to an unseen star-shaped dough geometry, capturing its non-trivial deformation behaviors.

#### 4.6. Ablation Study

An ablation study on PLASTICINE demonstrated the importance of the physics-aware network architecture:
- **Rotation Equivariance:** Removing rotation equivariance led to sub-optimal convergence.
- **Undeformed State Equilibrium:** Removing this prior resulted in unstable constitutive laws, causing the simulation to blow up, indicating its critical role in model stability.

#### 4.7. Auxiliary Results

- **Runtime Comparison:** NCLaw exhibited comparable runtime performance to `gnn` despite `gnn` using larger time steps, due to GPU optimization.
- **Data Efficiency:** NCLaw outperformed `gnn` by orders of magnitude even when `gnn` was trained with 100 times more data, underscoring NCLaw's data efficiency due to its physics-informed design.
- **Parameter Identification:** While not as accurate as the `labeled` oracle in directly identifying stress tensor values (P), NCLaw achieved comparable accuracy for updated elastic deformation gradients (F_e,new) and produced precise, generalizable kinematics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The NCLaw framework presents a significant advancement in the field of scientific machine learning and physical simulation, with several implications and potential impacts:

- **Hybrid ML-PDE Paradigm:** The work establishes a robust hybrid approach that combines the strengths of traditional PDE-based physical simulators (guaranteed physical correctness, stability, and adherence to known laws) with the data-driven expressiveness of neural networks (for complex, unmodeled, or unknown components). This 'best-of-both-worlds' solution offers a pathway to overcome the limitations of purely data-driven black-box models that may violate physical principles, and purely analytical models that struggle with complex material behaviors.

- **Enhanced Generalizability and Robustness:** By explicitly enforcing fundamental PDE constraints and incorporating physics-aware inductive biases (rotation equivariance, undeformed state equilibrium) into the network architecture, NCLaw achieves orders-of-magnitude higher accuracy and significantly improved generalization capabilities compared to purely neural network approaches. This 'one-shot' generalization to unseen geometries, initial/boundary conditions, extended temporal ranges, and even multi-physics systems, from minimal training data, is a critical step towards deploying ML models in diverse and unpredictable real-world scenarios.

- **Data Efficiency:** The ability to learn complex constitutive laws from a single motion trajectory, without requiring explicit stress-strain measurements or vast amounts of simulation rollouts, substantially reduces the data requirements. This is particularly impactful for applications where data acquisition is expensive, time-consuming, or physically impossible (e.g., in vivo biological material characterization, novel material discovery).

- **Versatile Material Modeling:** NCLaw demonstrates that a single, generic neural network architecture can capture a wide range of diverse material behaviors, from elastic solids and granular media to plastic materials and fluids. This eliminates the need for expert-designed, case-by-case constitutive models, accelerating the process of material characterization and simulation.

- **Applications in Engineering and Robotics:** The framework's ability to learn from real-world video data (e.g., dough) opens avenues for data-driven material characterization in various fields. This could have implications for:
 - **Soft Robotics:** Enabling more accurate modeling and control of soft robots, which rely on complex and often uncharacterized materials.
 - **Material Science:** Facilitating the discovery and characterization of new materials with intricate mechanical properties.
 - **Medical Imaging:** Estimating mechanical properties of biological tissues where direct measurement is challenging.
 - **Computer Graphics and Animation:** Generating more realistic and physically plausible simulations of deformable objects.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work lays the groundwork for several promising future directions, including extending NCLaw to other physical phenomena (e.g., heterogeneity, hysteresis, hardening/softening), incorporating advanced thermodynamic principles, generalizing to other PDE types (e.g., Navier-Stokes for turbulence modeling), integrating with different discretization schemes (e.g., FEM), and addressing challenges like initial condition uncertainties and learning from partial observations or pixel-level video data.

In summary, NCLaw represents a robust and data-efficient method for learning complex physical dynamics, offering a significant step towards generalizable, physics-informed machine learning solutions for scientific and engineering problems.
