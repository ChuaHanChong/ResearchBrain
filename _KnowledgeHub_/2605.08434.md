# Failing Forward: Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL) enhances Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by integrating online-generated failure trajectories as adaptive negative guidance during action generation. This framework improves robotic task success rates and robustness across various manipulation tasks, particularly for diffusion and flow-based VLA policies.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are brittle due to training primarily on success-only trajectories, leading to compounding errors and unrecoverable failures in out-of-distribution scenarios.
- Current methods for learning from failure often require handcrafted failure modes or real-time human intervention, limiting scalability and generalization.
- Diffusion- and flow-based VLA policies lack explicit mechanisms to actively avoid failure-prone actions during generation, as they are trained solely on successful demonstrations.

## Method

- Introduces **Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL)**, an end-to-end framework that uses ==online-generated failure trajectories== to provide ==adaptive negative guidance== during VLA action generation.
- Employs a ==Dual Action Generator (DAG)== architecture with a shared VLM backbone and parallel generators for successful and failed action distributions.
- Implements an ==adaptive failure-informed sampling== mechanism, dynamically adjusting guidance strength based on the ==cosine similarity== between success and failure noise predictions, repelling from failure modes.

## Results

- AFIL consistently achieved the highest success rates on both short- and long-horizon in-domain tasks, demonstrating significant gains over static failure-informed guidance, particularly a **+12.0%** improvement on one long-horizon task.
- The framework outperformed all compared methods (e.g., Diffusion Policy, Octo, OpenVLA) across all four challenging LIBERO task suites, increasing average success rate from "π₀.₅"'s **96.9%** to **98.4%**.
- AFIL significantly enhanced robustness in out-of-domain tasks involving unseen objects and cluttered scenes, with improvements like **62.7%** success for "Lift Cylinder" compared to **14.7%** for the success-only baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adapting the negative guidance strength, based on the cosine similarity between predicted success and failure actions, effectively steers the policy away from emerging failure modes without overly repelling it from valid actions.
> - Collecting failure data through online inference from a pre-trained policy captures a richer, more realistic distribution of errors than handcrafted taxonomies, improving generalizability.
> - The Dual Action Generator architecture, by sharing a VLM backbone, allows for efficient learning of both success and failure distributions with minimal additional computational overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation presents challenges in perception, long-horizon reasoning, and precise control under uncertainty. Traditional learning-based policies, such as behavioral cloning and reinforcement learning, have found success in structured environments but often require extensive data, task-specific reward engineering, and careful environment setup, which limits their scalability and generalization.

Recently, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a promising paradigm for robotics. Systems like RT-2, PaLM-E, Octo, and OpenVLA leverage large-scale pre-trained vision-language representations to enable embodied decision-making. These models aim for increased generality and scalability by grounding robotic actions in semantic understanding derived from diverse data.

Despite their potential, existing VLA models frequently exhibit brittleness during deployment. This limitation stems from their reliance on training data primarily composed of success-only, teleoperated trajectories collected in controlled environments. Such data can lead policies to overfit to narrow behavioral manifolds, lacking the corrective signals necessary for recovering from suboptimal or unforeseen states. Consequently, minor execution errors can rapidly compound into unrecoverable failures, particularly when the robot encounters out-of-distribution scenarios.

Recognizing this issue, some research has explored integrating failure data into policy learning to enhance robustness. However, these efforts often depend on handcrafted or pre-defined failure modes, which necessitate domain expertise and may not encompass the full spectrum of realistic deployment-time failures. Furthermore, recovery mechanisms in these approaches often rely on task-specific heuristics or real-time human intervention, which restricts scalability and generalization to novel tasks or robot embodiments.

Many advanced VLA policies employ diffusion-based or flow-based generative architectures due to their capability to model multimodal action distributions and capture complex perception-control dependencies. However, since these models are typically trained solely on successful demonstrations, they lack explicit mechanisms to actively avoid actions that might lead to failure, even if such failure-prone actions are proximate in the action space. In related fields like image generation, techniques such as classifier guidance and negative prompting effectively steer generative processes away from undesirable attributes. Direct application of these negative guidance strategies to VLAs is non-trivial, given the sequential nature of robotic actions, the requirement for temporal consistency, and the computational cost associated with integrating separate models for desired and undesired distributions within large VLM backbones.

This paper addresses the aforementioned limitations by proposing Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL). AFIL positions itself within the evolving landscape of VLA models by specifically tackling the brittleness and generalization issues arising from success-only training. It leverages insights from learning from failure and guided generative models, adapting them for efficient and scalable robotic manipulation. By introducing an adaptive, negative guidance mechanism that leverages online-generated failure trajectories, AFIL aims to provide VLA policies with explicit signals to avoid failure-prone actions, thereby improving robustness and long-horizon task completion without requiring extensive manual supervision or predefined failure categories.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL), an end-to-end framework designed to enhance the robustness and task success rates of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly those based on diffusion and flow architectures. This is achieved by leveraging failure trajectories as adaptive negative guidance during action generation.

The motivation for AFIL arises from several critical limitations observed in the current state of VLA models for robotic manipulation:

1. **Brittleness of Success-Only VLA Policies:** Existing VLA models are predominantly trained using behavioral cloning on datasets composed entirely of successful, often teleoperated, demonstrations. While effective for learning desired behaviors, this approach results in policies that are brittle and lack corrective capabilities. When a robot encounters minor execution errors or deviates slightly from the training manifold, these errors can compound rapidly, leading to unrecoverable "out-of-distribution" failures. The absence of explicit training signals for recovery or error avoidance renders these policies fragile in dynamic and uncertain real-world environments.

2. **Limitations of Current Failure Learning Approaches:** Although the value of learning from failure data to improve robustness is acknowledged in recent literature, existing methodologies often pose significant practical challenges:
 - **Reliance on Handcrafted Failure Modes:** Many approaches require manual specification or predefined taxonomies of failure modes (e.g., "incomplete grasp," "slip," "translation failure"). This necessitates considerable domain expertise and may not adequately capture the diverse, stochastic, and emergent nature of real-world failures, thereby limiting generalizability.
 - **Dependence on Human-in-the-Loop Intervention:** Some methods require real-time human feedback or physical intervention for failure correction. While effective in specific scenarios, this approach is not scalable for autonomous long-horizon deployment and increases operational costs.
 - **Task-Specific Recovery Heuristics:** Recovery strategies are frequently tied to specific tasks, which hinders their transferability across different manipulation challenges or robot embodiments.

3. **Inefficiency of Conventional Generative VLA Sampling:** Diffusion- and flow-based models are effective at modeling complex, multimodal action distributions. However, when trained solely on successful trajectories, sampling from these learned distributions might still yield actions that are close to failure-prone regions. The policy lacks an explicit mechanism to "repel" itself from actions associated with undesirable outcomes, making robust deployment challenging in states where success and failure actions might initially be ambiguous or only subtly diverge.

4. **Challenges in Applying Negative Guidance to VLAs:** Concepts like negative prompting, which use negative conditions to steer sample generation away from undesirable attributes in models like Text-to-Image generators, offer a conceptual parallel. However, directly applying such guidance to VLAs is complex due to:
 - **Sequential and Temporally Consistent Action Generation:** Robotic control requires producing a sequence of actions that are temporally coherent and responsive to evolving observations, unlike static image generation.
 - **Computational Overhead:** Conventional negative guidance often involves separate models for desired and undesired distributions, which can be computationally prohibitive when applied to VLA systems built upon large vision-language model (VLM) backbones.

AFIL is motivated to overcome these challenges by proposing an efficient, unified, and adaptive failure-informed guidance mechanism specifically tailored for diffusion- and flow-based VLA policies. The core idea is to equip VLA models with the ability to "fail forward" by learning from and actively avoiding failure states, thereby fostering more robust, generalizable, and autonomous robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL) framework is an end-to-end approach designed to improve the robustness of diffusion- and flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by integrating adaptive negative guidance derived from failure trajectories. The method comprises a Dual Action Generator (DAG) architecture for learning success and failure behaviors, followed by an adaptive failure-informed sampling mechanism during inference.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

- **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models:** The objective is to learn policies, π_θ(a_t | o_t, ℓ), that generate continuous robot actions (a_t:t+H) conditioned on visual observations (I_t), proprioceptive states (x_t), and language instructions (ℓ). VLA models typically consist of a large-scale vision-language model (VLM) backbone, Φ_VLM(I_t, ℓ), followed by a specialized action generation module, G. This work focuses on generative VLA models (diffusion- and flow-based) for their ability to model multimodal action distributions.
- **Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) and Negative Prompting (NP):** CFG in generative models improves conditional generation by combining conditional and unconditional score estimates. Negative Prompting extends this by repelling samples from undesirable regions, effectively guiding generation away from negative conditions. AFIL draws inspiration from these concepts but adapts them for the sequential action generation in robotics.

#### 3.2. Dual Action Generator (DAG) Architecture

Conventional VLA models typically train a single policy on success-only trajectories. AFIL extends this by introducing two parallel action generators that share a common VLM backbone, allowing explicit modeling of both successful and failed action distributions.

- **Components:**
 - **Shared VLM Backbone (Φ_VLM):** This component processes input images (I_t) and language instructions (ℓ) to extract multimodal features (h). This shared backbone ensures efficient failure-aware policy learning with limited additional parameter overhead, as the computationally intensive vision-language processing is done once.
 - **Success Action Generator (G_succ):** Trained exclusively on successful robot demonstrations. It learns the distribution p_θs(a | o, ℓ) corresponding to desired, task-achieving actions.
 - **Failure Action Generator (G_fail):** Trained exclusively on failure trajectories. It learns the distribution p_θf(a | o, ℓ) associated with unsuccessful or error-prone outcomes.

- **Failure Data Generation:** Unlike approaches that rely on handcrafted failure taxonomies, AFIL acquires failure data through online inference. A pre-trained VLA policy is executed to generate rollouts. Any trajectories that do not lead to task success are labeled as failure trajectories and collected. This method aims to:
 - Capture a richer and more realistic distribution of failures, including emergent and compounded errors that might be difficult to foresee manually.
 - Eliminate the need for human annotation or the design of specific perturbations.
 - Optionally, collected failure trajectories can be "corrected" via motion-planner-based replanning to generate augmented success data for training.

- **Training Objectives:** Both G_succ and G_fail are trained on their respective datasets (D_s for success, D_f for failure) using standard generative objectives.
 - For diffusion-based policies, each generator predicts noise ϵ_θ(a_η, o, ℓ, η) at diffusion step η, minimizing the L2 norm of the difference between predicted and ground-truth noise.
 - For flow-based policies, the action head predicts a velocity field v_θ(a_η, o, ℓ, η) to match the ground-truth velocity between noisy and target actions.
 The core VLA training objective remains unchanged, applied distinctly to parameterize success and failure action heads.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Failure-Informed Sampling

During inference, merely sampling from the success generator (G_succ) can still lead to actions that drift into failure-prone regions. AFIL addresses this by employing an adaptive failure-informed sampling mechanism, analogous to Negative Prompting, but specifically tailored for sequential robotic actions.

- **Guided Action Generation:**
 - Let ϵ_succ(a_η) be the noise predicted by G_succ and ϵ_fail(a_η) be the noise predicted by G_fail at diffusion time step η (conditioning on o, ℓ, and η is implicit for brevity).
 - The failure-informed (FI) guided noise prediction (ϵ*_FI) is formulated as:
 ϵ*_FI = ϵ_succ(a_η, ∅) - λ_η(a_η) (ϵ_fail(a_η) - ϵ_succ(a_η, ∅))
 This can be approximated as:
 ϵ*_FI = ϵ_succ(a_η, ∅) - ˆλ_η(a_η) ϵ_fail(a_η)
 - This formulation approximates a score combination: ∇_a log p_succ(a_η) - ˆλ_η(a_η) ∇_a log p_fail(a_η), effectively repelling the sampling process from the failure distribution while attracting it to the success distribution.

- **Adaptive Guidance Scale (ˆλ_η(a_η)):** A key innovation is the adaptive modulation of guidance strength, which varies based on the current action (a_η) and diffusion step (η). The guidance scale ˆλ_η(a_η) is determined by the cosine similarity between the noise predictions from the success and failure generators:
 ˆλ_η(a_η) = α(1 - cos(ϵ_succ, ϵ_fail))
 where α is a global guidance scale hyperparameter.

 - **Rationale:**
 - This quantity intuitively captures how distinguishable successful and failed behaviors are in the local state.
 - When success and failure predictions are highly aligned (high cosine similarity, e.g., during initial neutral movements where both trajectories might be similar), the guidance strength ˆλ_η(a_η) remains small, preventing unnecessary repulsion.
 - Conversely, when success and failure predictions diverge (low cosine similarity, indicating a clear emergence of a failure mode), ˆλ_η(a_η) increases, allowing the policy to actively steer away from failure-prone actions and bias sampling towards corrective behaviors.
 - Cosine distance is chosen over other divergence metrics (e.g., MMD, JSD) due to its computational efficiency for real-time VLA applications and its ability to capture the "semantic intent" or angular orientation of actions, which is often more relevant in robotics than absolute coordinate differences. This allows for nuanced guidance that adapts to the stage of the task.

The AFIL methodology thus provides a unified, efficient, and dynamic mechanism to improve VLA policy robustness by learning from failure and intelligently guiding action generation away from undesirable outcomes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL) framework was conducted across various robotic manipulation tasks in simulated environments, using a π₀.₅ VLA backbone. The experiments assessed AFIL's effectiveness, efficiency, and generality in improving task success rates and robustness under different conditions.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Setup

- **Base VLA Architecture:** All experiments were built upon the π₀.₅ architecture, a flow-matching-based VLA model, highlighting AFIL's compatibility with existing generative VLA designs.
- **Environments:**
 - **ManiSkill:** A Franka Emika Panda 7-DoF robot arm was used for both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluations.
 - *In-domain tasks:* Included three short-horizon tasks ("stack red cube on green cube," "lift the peg upright," "pull cube to the red and white target") and two long-horizon tasks ("place bowl on the plate and spoon on the bowl," "place can on the plate and banana on the plate").
 - *Out-of-domain (OOD) tasks:* Assessed generalization to unseen objects and cluttered scenes with novel geometries and textures. These included two short-horizon tasks ("Lift Cylinder," "Stack Cubes" with distractors) and one long-horizon task ("Fruit Sorting").
 - *Data Collection (ManiSkill):* The training dataset consisted of 1500 success trajectories, 1000 failure-corrected trajectories (obtained by replanning failures), and 1500 raw failure trajectories.
 - *Evaluation Protocol (ManiSkill):* In-domain tasks were evaluated over 50 initialization configurations, with 3 runs each (total 150 rollouts). OOD tasks used 25 configurations with 3 runs each (total 75 rollouts). Success rates were reported.
 - **LIBERO Benchmark:** This benchmark consists of four task suites (LIBERO-Spatial, -Object, -Goal, and -Long/LIBERO-10), each with 10 tasks and 50 human teleoperated demonstrations.
 - *Failure Data (LIBERO):* Collected from rollouts of a pre-trained π₀.₅ policy fine-tuned on LIBERO.
 - *Evaluation Protocol (LIBERO):* Average success rates were reported over 3 random seeds, with 500 rollouts per seed (10 tasks × 50 rollouts per task), following standard benchmark procedures.

#### 4.2. In-Domain Evaluation (ManiSkill)

- **Ablation Study (Table 1):**
 - **Success-Only:** The baseline policy trained solely on successful demonstrations.
 - **Success + Failure Correction:** Incorporating motion-planner-corrected failure data alongside success data showed initial performance improvements, particularly for less complex tasks.
 - **DAG-VLA + Static-FIL (λ=0.05):** Training with both success and failure (corrected and raw) data, and applying failure-informed sampling with a fixed guidance strength, further improved results over failure correction alone.
 - **DAG-VLA + Adaptive-FIL (α=1.0):** The proposed AFIL method, which employs adaptive guidance during inference.
- **Key Finding:** Adaptive-FIL consistently achieved the highest success rates across all short- and long-horizon in-domain tasks. Notably, it demonstrated significant gains on long-horizon tasks (e.g., +7.4% and +12.0% over Static-FIL), indicating that dynamically adjusting failure guidance is beneficial for tasks prone to compounding errors.

- **Ablation Study on Guidance Scale α (Table 2):**
 - An ablation study investigated the impact of the global guidance scale α (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0) for adaptive guidance on two representative tasks.
 - **Key Finding:** Performance was optimal within a moderate range (α = 0.5 to 1.0). Higher guidance scales (e.g., α = 2.0, 5.0) led to a decrease in success rates, suggesting that excessively strong negative guidance can be detrimental, potentially by overly repelling the policy from valid, albeit initially ambiguous, action regions.

#### 4.3. Benchmark Evaluation (LIBERO)

- **Comparison with Baselines (Table 3):** AFIL (DAG-VLA π₀.₅ + AFIL) was compared against Diffusion Policy, Octo, OpenVLA, CoT-VLA, and the strong π₀.₅ baseline.
- **Key Finding:** AFIL consistently outperformed all compared methods across all four LIBERO task suites.
 - It achieved notable improvements, particularly on the challenging LIBERO-10 (long-horizon) suite, increasing the success rate from π₀.₅'s 92.4% to 95.8%.
 - The average performance across all suites improved from 96.9% (π₀.₅) to 98.4% (AFIL), demonstrating that AFIL provides benefits even to robust, pre-trained VLA policies.

#### 4.4. Out-of-Domain (OOD) Evaluation (ManiSkill)

- **Comparison (Table 4):** Performance of success-only π₀.₅, π₀.₅ with failure correction, and DAG-VLA π₀.₅ + AFIL was compared on OOD tasks involving unseen objects and cluttered scenes.
- **Key Finding:**
 - The success-only π₀.₅ policy showed substantial degradation in performance under distribution shifts (e.g., 14.7% for "Lift Cylinder").
 - Incorporating failure correction significantly improved OOD performance (e.g., 53.3% for "Lift Cylinder").
 - DAG-VLA with AFIL achieved the highest success rates across all OOD tasks (e.g., 62.7% for "Lift Cylinder," 64.0% for "Fruit Sorting"), demonstrating enhanced robustness to visual, geometric, and task-level distribution shifts.

In summary, the results indicate that AFIL consistently improves the success rates and robustness of VLA policies. The adaptive nature of the failure guidance mechanism is particularly effective for long-horizon tasks and out-of-domain generalization, where dynamic adjustments to error avoidance are crucial.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL) presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The demonstrated improvements in robustness and task success rates have several implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization for VLA Models:** AFIL directly addresses a critical weakness of current VLA systems: their brittleness and poor generalization when encountering minor execution errors or out-of-distribution states. By providing VLA policies with an explicit mechanism to steer away from failure-prone actions, AFIL significantly improves performance across in-domain, out-of-domain, short-horizon, and long-horizon tasks. This makes VLA models more reliable and capable of operating in less structured and more unpredictable real-world environments.

2. **Efficient and Scalable Failure Learning:** The proposed framework avoids the limitations of prior approaches to learning from failure. It does not require:
 - **Handcrafted Failure Modes:** By leveraging online-generated failure rollouts from a pre-trained VLA, AFIL eliminates the need for manual design or annotation of specific error categories, which often requires extensive domain expertise and may not capture the full diversity of real-world failures. This reduces engineering effort and improves the realism of learned failure distributions.
 - **Human-in-the-Loop Intervention:** The adaptive guidance mechanism operates autonomously during inference, reducing reliance on continuous human supervision or real-time physical corrections. This promotes greater autonomy and scalability for robotic systems.
 - **Separate Large-Scale Models:** The Dual Action Generator (DAG) architecture shares a common vision-language backbone, introducing only moderate parameter overhead. This efficient design makes integrating failure-aware learning practical for VLA models that typically rely on large foundation models.

3. **Adaptive and Nuanced Control:** The core concept of adaptive guidance, where the repulsion strength from failure modes is dynamically adjusted based on the distance between success and failure distributions, represents a nuanced approach to control. This prevents overly aggressive guidance in situations where success and failure trajectories might initially align (e.g., early task stages) and amplifies corrective guidance when clear failure modes emerge. This adaptive nature is particularly beneficial for long-horizon tasks where errors can compound, enabling the policy to make context-aware decisions.

4. **Broader Applicability and Future Research:** AFIL is described as model-agnostic, having been successfully integrated with both diffusion- and flow-based VLA policies (specifically, the π₀.₅ architecture). This suggests its potential applicability to a wide range of current and future generative VLA architectures, offering a generalizable solution for improving robustness. The work also opens avenues for further research into richer representations of failure, alternative divergence metrics for adaptive guidance, and its application to even more complex, unstructured real-world settings.

5. **Accelerated Real-World Deployment:** By making VLA policies more robust and generalizable, AFIL contributes to accelerating the deployment of autonomous robots in practical applications. Industries such as logistics, manufacturing, healthcare, and domestic service could benefit from robots that are more resilient to unexpected events and can recover from errors without constant human oversight. The ability of robots to "fail forward" and learn from past mistakes is a crucial step towards developing truly intelligent and capable embodied AI systems.

In summary, AFIL offers a principled, efficient, and effective framework for equipping VLA models with the ability to learn from and avoid failure, marking a step towards more robust, autonomous, and widely applicable robotic manipulation.
