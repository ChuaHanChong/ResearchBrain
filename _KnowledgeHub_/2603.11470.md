# NFPO: Stabilized Policy Optimization of Normalizing Flow for Robotic Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Zhejiang University and Westlake University developed NFPO, a method that integrates Normalizing Flows into the on-policy PPO framework for robotic policy learning. NFPO achieves stable multi-modal policy learning by normalizing internal scale network outputs, demonstrates competitive or superior performance against Gaussian PPO in high-dimensional tasks, and successfully transfers to real-world humanoid robots for stable control.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning policies for robotic control predominantly use restricted multivariate Gaussian distributions, which limit their ability to model complex, multi-modal action distributions.
- Integrating modern multi-modal generative networks into on-policy Policy Optimization frameworks for learning control policies from scratch presents significant training and numerical instability challenges.
- Existing multi-modal DRL approaches often focus on off-policy methods, whereas on-policy methods are often preferred in robotics for their computational efficiency and robustness in simulation-rich environments.

## Method

- **NFPO** replaces the standard ==multivariate Gaussian policy parameterization== in **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** with ==Normalizing Flows (NFs)==, specifically using the ==RealNVP== architecture.
- Training instability, common with naive NF integration, is addressed by ==normalizing the scale-network output== `s(x)` in RealNVP coupling layers using a ==scaled hyperbolic tangent== (`l · tanh(s_θ(x))`) to bound values.
- Ablation studies determined that the final NFPO configuration ==omits the entropy loss term== and ==does not add action noise== during training, as these were found to be unhelpful or detrimental to performance and stability.

## Results

- NFPO achieved competitive or superior performance compared to state-of-the-art Gaussian PPO baselines across various high-dimensional locomotion and manipulation tasks, particularly in challenging environments like `G1JoyStickRoughTerrain`.
- The method successfully demonstrated multi-modal behaviors in sparse-reward environments (e.g., Gridworld, UR10 reaching) by generating diverse action distributions and distinct trajectories to targets, unlike unimodal PPO policies.
- Policies trained with NFPO were successfully transferred to a real-world Unitree G1 humanoid robot, enabling stable and agile **29-DoF** whole-body control for joystick-based locomotion and motion tracking.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Naive integration of Normalizing Flows into online Reinforcement Learning causes significant training instability due to issues like overfitting to high-advantage samples, exponentially growing internal values, and unbounded network outputs.
> - Constraining the output of the scale network within RealNVP via normalization (e.g., `tanh` or `clip`) is crucial for achieving stable and robust Normalizing Flow-based policy optimization.
> - Unlike standard PPO, an explicit entropy regularization term and adding action noise during training were found to be unnecessary or even detrimental to NFPO's performance and stability across various robotic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated significant advancements, leading to its widespread application, particularly in robotic control. The decreasing "Sim-to-Real" gap has enabled policies trained in parallel simulation environments to be transferred to real-world robotic systems, mitigating the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection. This approach has facilitated achievements in various robotic tasks, including locomotion for bipedal, quadruped, and humanoid robots.

Despite these successes, a limitation in DRL-based robotic policy learning is the prevalent use of multivariate Gaussian distributions with diagonal covariance matrices for policy parameterization. This standard parameterization is inherently restricted in its ability to model complex, multi-modal action distributions. This contrasts with supervised-learning-based robotic policy learning (e.g., Behavior Cloning), where modern network architectures like Diffusion Policies and Transformers, capable of multi-modal modeling, are commonly adopted.

Recent research has begun exploring the integration of multi-modal modeling networks, such as diffusion models, into DRL. However, most of these efforts have focused on off-policy methods like Soft Actor Critic (SAC), which prioritize sample efficiency. In robotic learning, particularly with abundant simulation resources, computational efficiency, memory consumption, and robustness across diverse simulator and reward function settings are often considered more critical. Under these criteria, on-policy methods, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), are frequently favored and have achieved numerous successes. The integration of modern multi-modal networks into the on-policy Policy Optimization paradigm, for learning control policies purely from scratch, has remained an open challenge.

This paper addresses this specific gap by proposing the use of Normalizing Flows (NFs) as the policy parameterization within an on-policy framework. Normalizing Flows are generative models known for their ability to model complex multi-modal distributions, their closed-form log-probability calculation, and their relatively low computational and memory overheads, making them potential candidates for robotic policy learning. While NFs have been explored in DRL, their stable integration into an on-policy setting is an area this work specifically investigates.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to design and implement a novel method that integrates modern multi-modal modeling networks into the on-policy Policy Optimization paradigm for robotic control, learning policies purely from scratch. This aims to overcome the limitations of the conventional multivariate Gaussian policy parameterization in DRL-based robotic policy learning, which lacks the expressiveness to model complex, multi-modal action distributions.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several key observations and requirements within the domain of robotic learning:
1. **Addressing Multi-modality Limitations**: Traditional DRL policies, typically parameterized as Gaussian distributions, struggle to represent scenarios where multiple distinct optimal actions exist for a given state. This can limit the diversity and robustness of learned robotic behaviors. The authors seek a policy parameterization that can naturally capture such multi-modal distributions.
2. **Suitability for On-Policy DRL in Robotics**: For robotic applications, where massive samples can be generated in simulation, computational efficiency, memory consumption, and robustness to various simulator and reward function settings are often prioritized over sample efficiency. On-policy methods like PPO align well with these priorities. The motivation is to find a multi-modal policy that is compatible with and performs robustly in this favored on-policy setting.
3. **Leveraging Normalizing Flows**: Normalizing Flows are identified as a promising candidate for multi-modal policy parameterization due to their inherent ability to model complex distributions, their closed-form log probability calculation (critical for PPO's objective), and their relatively low computational and memory overhead compared to other generative models like diffusion models, which often require sampling-based approximations for log probabilities.
4. **Overcoming Training Instability**: A key motivation is to address the significant training and numerical instability observed when naively combining Normalizing Flows with online Reinforcement Learning algorithms, particularly Policy Optimization. The authors aim to provide a detailed analysis of these instabilities and develop effective solutions to ensure stable and robust learning.
5. **Practical Applicability**: The ultimate motivation is to develop a method that is not only theoretically sound but also practically viable for real-world robotic systems. This includes ensuring computational and memory efficiency, robustness across various tasks, and ease of integration into existing training pipelines with minimal code changes, culminating in successful transfer to physical robots.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Integrate Normalizing Flows into the PPO framework for multi-modal robotic policy learning.
- Analyze and resolve the training instability encountered when combining NFs with online RL.
- Achieve a policy capable of competitive performance against state-of-the-art Gaussian PPO implementations in diverse simulation environments.
- Demonstrate that the learned policies can exhibit multi-modal behaviors.
- Validate the method's ability to transfer learned policies to real-world robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on replacing the standard multivariate Gaussian policy parameterization in Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Normalizing Flows (NFs) and subsequently addressing the training instabilities that arise.

**Reinforcement Learning and PPO Background:**
The work is framed within the standard Reinforcement Learning paradigm, where an agent learns a policy $\pi$ to maximize expected cumulative rewards in a Markovian Decision Process (MDP). PPO is chosen as the on-policy algorithm, which optimizes policies using an advantage-based clip objective. A key requirement for PPO is the efficient calculation of the log probability of an action given a state, $\log \pi(a|s)$.

**Normalizing Flow (NF) Policy Parameterization:**
Normalizing Flow is adopted for policy parameterization. An NF defines a bijective mapping $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^D$ between a complex data distribution $p(x)$ and a simple prior distribution $q(z)$ (typically a standard Normal distribution). The key properties of NFs relevant to this work are:
- **Bijectivity**: Ensures that sampling from $p(x)$ can be done by sampling $z \sim q(z)$ and applying $x = f_\theta^{-1}(z)$, and inference (calculating $z$ from $x$) by $z = f_\theta(x)$.
- **Closed-form Log Probability**: The log probability of $p(x)$ can be computed using the change-of-variables formula: $\log p(x) = \log q(f_\theta(x)) + \log |\det(df_\theta(x)/dx)|$. This is crucial for PPO's objective.
- **RealNVP Architecture**: The RealNVP (Real-valued Non-Volume Preserving) architecture is employed, which uses coupling layers. In a coupling layer, the input dimensions are partitioned into two sets, $d$ and $\setminus d$. One set of dimensions ($d$) is passed through unchanged, while the other set ($\setminus d$) is transformed by an affine coupling layer:
 $f_\theta(x)_d = x_d$
 $f_\theta(x)_{\setminus d} = x_{\setminus d} \odot \exp(s_\theta(x_d)) + t_\theta(x_d)$
 Here, $s_\theta$ and $t_\theta$ are neural networks. The determinant of the Jacobian for this transformation simplifies to $\exp(\sum s_\theta(x_d))$, making its calculation efficient. Multiple coupling layers are stacked, typically alternating the partitioned dimensions, to ensure all input dimensions influence each other.

**Integration into PPO:**
Integrating NF into PPO involves replacing the Gaussian policy with an NF. Specifically, during policy evaluation, $\log \pi(a|s)$ is computed using the NF's log probability formula. For action sampling, a latent variable $z$ is sampled from the prior $q(z)$, and then the action $a$ is generated via $a = f_\theta^{-1}(z)$. All other components of the PPO algorithm (e.g., advantage estimation, value function updates) remain largely unchanged.

**Addressing Training Instability:**
Naive integration of NFs into online RL caused significant instability, manifesting as numerical overflows and training crashes. The authors identified three primary reasons:
1. **Overfitting**: Expressive multi-modal policies can overfit to specific high-advantage samples, leading to a sudden increase in their log-probabilities, potentially at the expense of stability.
2. **Exponential Values**: The $\exp(s_\theta(x_d))$ term in RealNVP can grow exponentially large if $s_\theta(x_d)$ values are unbounded, causing numerical instability.
3. **Unbounded Output**: Unlike Gaussian log-probabilities, the neural network outputs within NF can be unbounded, contributing to numerical issues.

**Proposed Solution: Normalization of the Scale Network Output $s(x)$:**
To mitigate these instabilities, the core idea is to constrain the output of the $s_\theta(x_d)$ network, which directly influences the exponential term in RealNVP's Jacobian determinant. Several normalization techniques were tested:
- **`no_s`**: Omitting $s(x)$ entirely (i.e., fixing it to 0 or 1), effectively reducing the expressiveness to NICE (Non-linear Independent Components Estimation).
- **`s_clip`**: Applying a hard clipping function, `clip(s_θ(x), -l, l)`, to bound the output within a range $[-l, l]$.
- **`s_tanh`**: Using a scaled hyperbolic tangent function, `l * tanh(s_θ(x))`, to smoothly bound the output within $[-l, l]$.
- **`s_asymmetric`**: An advanced technique from prior literature.

**Experimental Evaluation of Normalization Techniques:**
Experiments conducted in the Unitree RL Gym's `g1` environment demonstrated that `s_clip` and `s_tanh` were the most effective in stabilizing training, while `s_none` (naive NF) and `no_s` performed poorly. `s_tanh` was ultimately chosen for its robustness to the hyperparameter `l` and its smoother gradient properties compared to hard clipping (as detailed in Appendix A.2). A default value of $l=0.5$ was found to perform well across various tasks.

**Additional Ablations and Final NFPO Configuration:**
Further ablations were conducted on two other factors:
- **Entropy Term**: Unlike traditional PPO where an entropy loss encourages exploration and prevents mode collapse, adding an entropy term to NFPO did not significantly improve performance and could even slow down learning in some tasks. Consequently, the final NFPO configuration *removed the entropy loss*.
- **Action Noise**: Inspired by prior works on NFs, adding Gaussian noise to actions during training and removing it during sampling was tested. This approach, however, *decreased* RL performance and caused training instability in challenging environments. Therefore, the final NFPO configuration *does not add action noise* during training.

The final NFPO method consists of a 4-layer RealNVP network with `l * tanh(s_θ_j(a_d_j))` for the scale component in its coupling layers, with $l=0.5$, and without an explicit entropy loss term or action noise during training. This configuration, developed through systematic analysis and experimentation, forms the basis for subsequent performance evaluations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from extensive experiments conducted across multiple simulation environments (IsaacGym, Mujoco Playground, IsaacLab) and real-world robotic platforms.

#### 4.1. Influence of Various Factors on NFPO Performance

- **Normalization Techniques (tanh vs. clip for $s(x)$):** Both `s_clip` and `s_tanh` effectively stabilized training given appropriate `l` values. However, `s_tanh` demonstrated greater robustness to variations in the hyperparameter `l` across different tasks, leading to its selection for the final NFPO design. The value $l=0.5$ was identified as a suitable default.
- **Entropy Term:** Contrary to its role in standard PPO, adding an entropy loss term did not significantly improve NFPO's performance and, in some challenging tasks like `G1JoyStickRoughTerrain`, it could slow down the learning process. This led to the decision to *omit the entropy loss* in the final NFPO configuration.
- **Action Noise During Training:** Introducing Gaussian noise to actions during training, followed by deterministic action sampling, was found to *decrease* RL performance across tasks. In challenging environments, it even triggered training instability and early termination. Consequently, the final NFPO configuration *does not incorporate action noise* during training.
- **Final NFPO Design:** Based on these ablations, NFPO was configured as a 4-layer RealNVP network utilizing `tanh` transformations for its scale components ($s(x)$), with $l=0.5$, and without an entropy loss term or action noise. This setup was consistently used across all subsequent experiments without task-specific tuning.
- **Ablation on Layers and Hidden Dimensions (Appendix A.4):** A minimum of 3 layers was necessary for sufficient expressiveness in the odd-even masking strategy. Performance generally converged for 4, 6, and 8 layers. For hidden dimensions, 512 showed the best performance on harder tasks, while 64 was the worst.

#### 4.2. Performance Comparison with State-of-the-Art PPO Implementations

- NFPO was compared against RSL-RL's PPO implementation (PPO-adaptive and PPO-fixed) in Unitree RL Gym (locomotion tasks) and Mujoco Playground (manipulation tasks).
- **Competitive or Stronger Performance:** NFPO consistently achieved competitive or superior performance compared to the PPO baselines.
- **High-Dimensional Control:** In high-dimensional locomotion tasks like `g1-joystick`, `h1-joystick`, and `G1JoyStickRoughTerrain`, NFPO demonstrated stronger convergence, which the authors attribute to its multi-modal modeling capabilities.
- **Complex Terrains:** On `Go1JoystickRoughTerrain`, NFPO exhibited less performance fluctuation, indicating better adaptation to complex environments.
- **Simpler Tasks:** For simpler tasks such as `go2-joystick`, NFPO achieved performance comparable to PPO.
- **Manipulation Tasks:** In manipulation tasks like `AlohaHandOver` and `PandaOpenCabinet`, results were mixed, suggesting that these tasks may require more careful hyperparameter tuning for all methods.
- **Computation Efficiency:** On the Unitree Gym's g1 environment, NFPO incurred approximately a 19% increase in wall-clock time compared to PPO.
- **Classic Mujoco Tasks (Appendix A.3):** NFPO achieved similar performance to PPO on simpler environments (Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d) and superior performance on complex environments like Humanoid.

#### 4.3. Demonstration of Multi-Modal Behaviors

- **Gridworld Environment:** In a sparse reward gridworld, NFPO generated diverse actions that led to multiple distinct trajectories towards the reward region, while PPO converged to a single shortest path, not exhibiting multi-modal behaviors.
- **Isaac-Reach-UR10-v0:** When trained in a sparse-reward UR10 reaching task, NFPO generated diverse end-effector trajectories to the target, whereas PPO produced more fixed and consistent trajectories, further illustrating NFPO's ability to learn multi-modal policies.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Other Multi-modal Policy Learning Methods

- **On-policy Diffusion-based Methods (e.g., FPO):** Compared to FPO on 7 Mujoco Playground tasks. NFPO successfully performed 5 out of 7 tasks, while FPO successfully performed 2 out of 7. This suggests NFPO's improved learning stability and robustness in these robotic tasks under a consistent configuration.
- **Off-policy Normalizing Flows-based Methods (e.g., Meow):** Despite extensive hyperparameter tuning, Meow did not exhibit meaningful learning performance in Unitree RL Gym's g1 and go2 environments. It consumed substantial GPU memory and failed to learn effective locomotion or command following, although episode length increased in some cases.
- **Overall:** The results indicate that NFPO offers better learning stability and robustness across various robotic learning tasks and environments compared to evaluated contemporary multi-modal policy methods, especially when considering practical constraints like computational efficiency and a generalized configuration.

#### 4.5. Real-World Transferability

- **Unitree G1 Robot:** Policies trained with NFPO were successfully transferred to a real-world Unitree G1 humanoid robot.
- **Hierarchical Control:** A common hierarchical structure was used, with NFPO providing high-level action targets (50Hz) and a low-level PD controller generating joint torques (200Hz).
- **Stable and Agile Control:** The policy enabled the G1 robot to perform stable and agile 29-DoF whole-body control for joystick-based locomotion and motion tracking (e.g., dancing).
- **Deterministic Deployment (Appendix A.6):** Similar to PPO practice, deploying the deterministic version of NFPO (using the mode of the base Gaussian distribution) generated more stable and smooth real-world motions compared to its stochastic counterpart.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces NFPO, a method for integrating Normalizing Flows (NFs) into the on-policy Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) framework, specifically tailored for robotic policy learning. This work carries several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Bridging a Critical Gap in DRL for Robotics**: NFPO addresses the long-standing challenge of equipping on-policy DRL agents with the capability to model multi-modal action distributions. Previous standard DRL approaches with Gaussian policies were limited to unimodal distributions, hindering the learning of diverse or ambiguous optimal strategies inherent in complex robotic tasks. By enabling multi-modal policies within the PPO framework, NFPO expands the representational power of DRL for robotics.

2. **Enhanced Policy Expressiveness and Robustness**: The ability of NFPO to learn multi-modal behaviors, as demonstrated in gridworld and UR10 reaching tasks, suggests that robots can acquire more versatile and robust control policies. In real-world scenarios, where environmental conditions or task goals might necessitate multiple distinct but equally effective actions, multi-modal policies can potentially lead to more adaptive and resilient robotic systems.

3. **Practicality for Real-World Robotic Deployment**: NFPO is designed with practical considerations for roboticists in mind. Its focus on computational and memory efficiency, robustness across diverse simulation settings, and minimal code changes to existing PPO pipelines makes it a viable candidate for integration into current robotic development workflows. The successful transfer of NFPO-trained policies to a real Unitree G1 robot for complex locomotion and motion tracking tasks directly validates its real-world applicability and robustness, a critical hurdle for DRL methods.

4. **Competitive Performance with SOTA Baselines**: The empirical results show that NFPO achieves performance competitive with, and in several challenging high-dimensional control tasks, superior to, state-of-the-art Gaussian PPO implementations. This indicates that the added complexity of multi-modal modeling does not compromise core performance and, in fact, can offer advantages in specific scenarios, without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning for each task.

5. **Foundation for Future Research in Policy Analysis**: The inherent properties of Normalizing Flows, such as their closed-form log probability calculation, offer avenues for deeper analysis and interpretation of learned policies. This could lead to a better understanding of *why* certain actions are chosen, how policies adapt to different states, and how to debug or refine learned behaviors. The authors explicitly highlight this as a future research direction, suggesting potential for advancements in explainable AI for robot control.

6. **Advancing Multi-modal DRL Research**: By providing a stable and effective method for integrating NFs into on-policy RL, NFPO contributes to the broader field of DRL. It demonstrates that the training instability commonly associated with combining expressive generative models with online RL can be systematically analyzed and addressed through targeted architectural and regularization techniques. This could inspire further research into other generative models within the on-policy paradigm.

In conclusion, NFPO represents a step toward unlocking the full potential of multi-modal policy learning in DRL-based robotics. Its stability, performance, and demonstrated real-world transferability position it as a significant contribution, paving the way for more sophisticated and adaptive robotic behaviors.
