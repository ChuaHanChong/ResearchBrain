# LAP: Language-Action Pre-Training Enables Zero-shot Cross-Embodiment Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Princeton University and Physical Intelligence introduced Language-Action Pre-training (LAP), a method that represents low-level robot actions in natural language, enabling zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer with a 2x relative improvement over prior Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The LAP-3B model also achieves faster and more data-efficient fine-tuning for new real-world tasks, requiring approximately 2.5x fewer demonstrations.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle with zero-shot transfer to previously unseen robot embodiments, requiring costly and time-consuming per-embodiment fine-tuning.
- The ==distributional mismatch== between VLM pre-training (language) and typical robotic action representations (raw continuous values or arbitrary tokens) degrades the VLM's generalization capabilities for motor control.
- The immense diversity of robot embodiments makes it impractical to collect exhaustive training data for every possible configuration, necessitating policies that generalize from limited embodiment diversity.

## Method

- Introduced Language-Action Pre-training (**LAP**), which deterministically parses continuous low-level robot actions into structured natural language descriptions, called ==language-actions==.
- Trained a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone (PaliGemma-3B) to auto-regressively predict these language-action tokens as a conditional language modeling task.
- Combined the VLM with a knowledge-insulated ==diffusion==-based action expert responsible for high-frequency continuous action execution during real-time robot control.

## Results

- LAP-3B achieved an average zero-shot success rate exceeding **50%** across three previously unseen robot embodiments and six manipulation tasks, demonstrating a **2x** relative improvement over prior VLAs.
- The method showed significantly faster fine-tuning, requiring approximately **2.5x** fewer real-world demonstrations to reach comparable performance on challenging new tasks.
- LAP models exhibited favorable scaling with increasing model size (Gemma3 backbones), consistently showing monotonic performance improvement where baselines saturated or degraded early.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representing robot actions in natural language mitigates the distributional mismatch, allowing VLMs to better leverage their vast pre-trained semantic and visual knowledge for robust control.
> - Language-action supervision promotes the learning of more transferable, embodiment-agnostic control representations, which is crucial for achieving true zero-shot generalization across diverse robot hardware.
> - The language-action format naturally supports co-training with Vision-Question-Answering (VQA) tasks, further enhancing cross-embodiment performance and accelerating fine-tuning for new tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work operates within the rapidly evolving landscape of **generalist robot policies**, specifically focusing on **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**. The overarching goal in this field is to develop foundation models for robotics that can understand natural language instructions, perceive diverse environments, and execute complex tasks across various robot embodiments.

Traditionally, robotics research often involved training robot policies for specific tasks on particular hardware configurations. However, recent advancements, largely driven by the success of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) in other domains, have spurred the development of VLAs. These models leverage the powerful visual and semantic priors learned by VLMs from internet-scale data to achieve strong generalization to new tasks and environments (e.g.,).

Despite remarkable progress, a critical bottleneck for existing VLAs is their inability to perform **zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer**. Even after large-scale multi-embodiment pre-training on datasets like Open X-Embodiment, state-of-the-art VLAs typically remain tightly coupled to their training embodiments. This means deploying a VLA on a new robot, even one with minor differences like an altered gripper or camera placement, usually requires costly and time-consuming per-embodiment fine-tuning. This requirement fundamentally undermines the vision of a single, broadly deployable "robot foundation model."

The paper situates itself by categorizing existing approaches to address embodiment variation:
- **Unified State/Action Spaces:** Many methods attempt to abstract away embodiment differences by defining unified action spaces (e.g., end-effector pose) or using embodiment-specific projectors/heads. While these approaches facilitate learning across multiple robots, they generally still necessitate adaptation for *unseen* embodiments.
- **Embodiment Conditioning:** Another line of work explicitly conditions policies on robot features, whether inferred from kinematics, provided as soft prompts (X-VLA), or through dedicated training stages (DexVLA). Some, like RDT2, report zero-shot transfer but under strong assumptions about hardware alignment (e.g., identical grippers and wrist cameras), which limits true generalization.
- **Action Representations:** This is the most direct lineage for LAP. Prior VLAs have explored different ways to represent actions:
 - **High-level language/code:** Treating actions as abstract sub-tasks (e.g.,), requiring separate low-level controllers.
 - **Visual cues:** Using 2D keypoints or visual traces as intermediate representations.
 - **Arbitrary/Non-linguistic tokens:** Encoding continuous actions into discrete, often out-of-vocabulary tokens (e.g., FAST, RT-2). The key critique here, which LAP addresses, is that this can introduce "distributional mismatch" and degrade the VLM's powerful pre-trained semantic knowledge.
 - **Direct Natural Language/String tokens:** More recent work has begun representing actions directly in natural language or as raw string tokens, aiming for improved semantic grounding. LAP builds on this emerging paradigm, critically demonstrating that this approach can scale to large robot datasets and enable *true* zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, which prior methods in this category (like VLA-0) often struggled with at scale (Table 1).

LAP's central contribution is to propose a novel action representation strategy that sidesteps the limitations of previous methods by directly aligning robot actions with the natural language domain in which VLMs excel. It focuses on achieving *true* zero-shot transfer without requiring specific embodiment-conditioning mechanisms or matched hardware interfaces, making it a significant advancement towards building truly generalist and readily deployable robot foundation models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a **generalist robot policy capable of zero-shot deployment on previously unseen robot embodiments without any per-embodiment adaptation.** This is presented as a "long-standing goal in robotics" that current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have not yet adequately addressed.

The motivation stems from several critical observations:

1. **Limitation of Current VLAs:** Despite advancements in leveraging pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and large-scale multi-embodiment training, state-of-the-art VLAs still exhibit poor zero-shot transferability to novel robots. Even minor differences in hardware (e.g., gripper design, camera placement) necessitate costly fine-tuning, undermining the promise of a single, universal foundation model for robotics.
2. **Vast and Expensive Embodiment Space:** The space of possible robot embodiments is enormous and inherently expensive to cover exhaustively with training data. While task and environment diversity can be scaled using in-the-wild data and internet-scale videos, embodiment diversity is constrained by hardware availability and data collection logistics. Therefore, achieving zero-shot transfer requires extracting maximal generalization from *limited* embodiment diversity.
3. **Distributional Mismatch in Action Representation:** The authors hypothesize that the difficulty in cross-embodiment transfer lies in *how VLMs are adapted for motor control*. Standard VLA training often fine-tunes VLMs to predict raw actions (continuous joint or end-effector commands) or abstract, non-linguistic tokens. Such representations lack inherent semantic structure across embodiments and induce a "distributional mismatch" with the VLM's pre-training data (which is language-centric). This mismatch can degrade the VLM's valuable pre-trained knowledge, causing embodiment-specific action learning to override generalizable features.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives for the Language-Action Pre-training (LAP) approach are:
- **Mitigate Distributional Mismatch:** Develop an action representation that aligns naturally with the VLM's input-output distribution to preserve its semantic and visual generalization capabilities.
- **Enable Zero-Shot Transfer:** Achieve substantial zero-shot generalization to novel robot embodiments, moving beyond the need for extensive fine-tuning.
- **No Learned Tokenizer or Costly Annotation:** Design an action representation that can be derived deterministically from raw actions without requiring a separate learned tokenizer or manual data annotation.
- **No Embodiment-Specific Architectural Design:** Avoid introducing specialized architectural components or conditioning mechanisms for different embodiments, relying instead on the inherent generalizability of the chosen representation.
- **Efficient Adaptation:** Facilitate faster and more data-efficient fine-tuning for new embodiments and more complex tasks when some adaptation is still desired.
- **Favorable Scaling:** Ensure the approach scales effectively with increasing model size, benefiting from larger VLM backbones.
- **Unified Language-Action Format:** Create an action representation that naturally unifies action prediction with other Vision-Question-Answering (VQA) objectives, enabling synergistic co-training.

In essence, the paper aims to fundamentally rethink the interface between perception, language, and action in VLAs, believing that a semantically rich, language-based action representation holds the key to unlocking true cross-embodiment generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the LAP methodology is a novel approach to represent low-level robot actions directly in natural language, dubbed "language-actions," which are then used to supervise a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone.

#### 3.1. Language-Action Pre-training (LAP) Formulation

LAP reframes the action learning problem as a conditional language modeling task. Given an observation (multi-view RGB images and proprioceptive state) and a natural language task instruction, the VLM is trained to auto-regressively predict a sequence of language-action tokens. This is achieved using a standard cross-entropy loss, similar to behavioral cloning for continuous actions but applied to linguistic outputs.

#### 3.2. Language-Action Dataset Curation

A crucial aspect is generating language-actions at scale without manual annotation. This is done by deterministically parsing continuous end-effector delta motions from existing robotic datasets into structured natural language descriptions.
- **Coordinate System:** A fixed, common robotics convention is adopted (+x forward, +y left, +z up) for translational and rotational components (Euler angles).
- **Reference Frames:** To promote embodiment-agnostic reasoning, language-actions are expressed in randomized reference frames (50% robot base frame, 50% end-effector frame), with the chosen frame included in the model prompt.
- **Structured Templates:** Language-actions follow a small set of natural language templates using verbs like "move," "tilt," and "rotate," followed by direction and discretized magnitude (e.g., "move forward 5 cm", "tilt forward 45 degrees"). Zero-valued movements are omitted.
- **Temporal Abstraction:** Instead of generating actions at every timestep, a single language-action describes the net displacement over an "action chunk" (a sequence of H timesteps). This provides a lower-frequency, more abstract supervision signal, easier for VLMs to model while still capturing motor-level effects.

This deterministic parsing avoids learned tokenizers (like FAST) and costly human annotation, ensuring the action supervision remains within the VLM's natural language pre-training distribution, thereby mitigating the distributional mismatch.

#### 3.3. LAP-3B Model Architecture and Training

To ensure efficient real-time robot control, LAP is instantiated in **LAP-3B**, a hybrid "Mixture-of-Transformers" architecture inspired by π0.5.
- **VLM Backbone:** The backbone is initialized with PaliGemma-3B, a powerful VLM, and is trained to predict the discrete language-action tokens using the cross-entropy loss.
- **Lightweight Action Expert:** A diffusion-based (flow-matching) action expert predicts continuous action chunks. This expert is responsible for high-frequency motor execution at inference.
- **Communication and Knowledge Insulation:** The VLM and action expert interact via cross-attention. Crucially, "knowledge insulation" is applied by blocking gradients from the action expert propagating back to the VLM backbone. This preserves the VLM's pre-trained representations and stabilizes joint training.
- **Inference:** For real-time control (25 Hz), only the action expert is rolled out.

The overall training objective combines the flow-matching loss for the action expert and the cross-entropy loss for the VLM, with a weighting factor (λ) to balance their convergence rates.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Training Data:** LAP-3B is trained on a large mixture of open-sourced robot datasets, including Open X-Embodiment (OXE) and MolmoAct, with a higher proportion given to the DROID dataset. Idle segments and trajectories lacking task instructions are filtered.
- **Proprioceptive State:** Cartesian end-effector pose (position, 6D rotation representation, binary gripper state) is discretized into 255 bins and appended to the prompt as text tokens.
- **Normalization:** States and actions are normalized using global quantiles from the full dataset.
- **Compute:** Training uses a global batch size of 2048 across 64 TPU v6e chips for 15,000 gradient steps (approx. 10 wall-clock hours), corresponding to roughly 0.65 epochs over the dataset.
- **Image Processing:** Images are resized to 224x224, and standard augmentations (random crop, rotation, color jitter) are applied during training.

#### 3.5. Co-training with VQA Objectives

The language-action format naturally supports co-training with auxiliary Visual-Question-Answering (VQA) tasks. For example, a "motion prediction" VQA task can ask the model to describe the robot's movement between two consecutive images using a language-action, further strengthening the VLM's semantic grounding and multi-modal understanding.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Strategy

The methodology is rigorously evaluated across four robot embodiments (one seen, three unseen: Custom Franka, YAM, Kinova) and ten real-world manipulation tasks (Pick and Place, Sort, Tissue, Put Towel, Pour for zero-shot; Hang Tape on Rack, Fold Towel for fine-tuning). Performance is compared against both publicly available VLA checkpoints and controlled baselines with identical architectures but different action representations (FAST tokens, no VLM action supervision, digit tokens). Metrics include zero-shot success rates (with statistically valid confidence intervals), fine-tuning data and compute efficiency, t-SNE visualizations of learned representations, action prediction error on unseen data, and scaling behavior with model size. Over 1300 real-robot evaluation trials were conducted.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that Language-Action Pre-training (LAP) offers significant advancements in cross-embodiment generalization for VLAs.

#### 4.1. Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Generalization

- **Breakthrough Performance:** LAP-3B achieves an average zero-shot success rate exceeding 50% across three previously unseen robot embodiments (Custom Franka, YAM, Kinova) and six diverse manipulation tasks. This represents an approximate **2× relative improvement (and 30% absolute improvement)** over the strongest prior VLAs and baselines.
- **First Substantial Zero-Shot Success:** The authors highlight that LAP-3B is, to their knowledge, the first VLA system to achieve substantial zero-shot transfer to novel *real robot embodiments* without any embodiment-specific fine-tuning.
- **Failure of Prior VLAs:** In stark contrast, all tested open-sourced VLAs, including powerful models like π0.5-DROID (which performs well in-distribution), consistently collapse to near-zero success rates on unseen robots. This confirms the hypothesis that existing VLA training recipes lead to tight coupling with training embodiments.
- **Strong In-Distribution Performance:** On the seen DROID embodiment, LAP-3B achieves performance comparable to π0.5-DROID, which was explicitly fine-tuned on the DROID dataset, but LAP-3B does so without any DROID-specific adaptation.
- **Robustness of Language-Actions:** Replicated baselines with alternative action representations (e.g., FAST tokens, digit tokens) perform significantly worse, with the VLA-0-replicated model collapsing due to overfitting to trivial action predictions at large scale. This underscores the superiority of the language-action representation in promoting robust learning.

#### 4.2. Fine-Tuning Efficiency on New Embodiments

- **Faster Convergence in Simulation:** On the LIBERO simulation benchmark, LAP-3B converges substantially faster, reaching near-optimal performance (96.8%) within six epochs—a fraction of the training steps required by baselines and even an oracle model.
- **Data Efficiency in Real World:** For challenging real-robot tasks (Hang Tape on Rack, Fold Towel and Place in Basket), LAP-3B achieves strong performance with significantly fewer demonstrations. For instance, on the YAM robot, it reaches ~50% task progress with only 20 demonstrations, approximately 2.5× fewer than baselines. This demonstrates improved sample efficiency for adaptation.

#### 4.3. Analysis of LAP’s Cross-Embodiment Generalization

- **Transferable Representations:** T-SNE visualizations of learned embodiment features show that LAP-3B produces substantial overlap between training and unseen embodiment representations. This indicates that LAP-3B learns more *transferable, embodiment-agnostic control representations*, a key factor explaining its strong zero-shot performance. Baselines exhibit limited alignment.
- **Lower Action Prediction Error:** LAP-3B consistently achieves lower action prediction error on held-out datasets from unseen robot embodiments during pre-training, compared to baselines. This further confirms that language-action supervision leads to more generalizable control representations and contributes to stable training dynamics.

#### 4.4. Co-Training with Vision-Language Datasets

- **Performance Gains:** Integrating a motion-prediction VQA task (which naturally uses the language-action format) further improves LAP-3B's cross-embodiment performance and spatial generalization across robots like Custom Franka and YAM.
- **Accelerated Fine-Tuning:** Co-training also leads to checkpoints that adapt more easily to new tasks, resulting in faster fine-tuning with fewer gradient steps and higher final convergence performance.

#### 4.5. Scaling with Model Size

- **Favorable Scaling:** LAP models, when scaled up with Gemma3 backbones (4B, 12B, 27B parameters), consistently achieve lower validation loss and show monotonic performance improvement with increased model capacity.
- **Baseline Saturation:** In contrast, the π0.5-replicated baseline exhibits early saturation and even degradation at larger scales, suggesting that its action representation is not as amenable to benefiting from increased model capacity. This reinforces that language-action supervision remains effective and increasingly advantageous in the large-model regime.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Language-Action Pre-training (LAP) and its instantiation in LAP-3B represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic learning, particularly for the development of generalist robot policies and foundation models.

#### 5.1. Enabling True Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Transfer

The most profound impact of this work is the demonstration of substantial zero-shot generalization to previously unseen robot embodiments. This capability addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics, moving beyond the costly and time-consuming per-embodiment adaptation required by most existing VLAs. By allowing a single policy to control diverse robots out-of-the-box, LAP-3B accelerates robot deployment in new environments and configurations, dramatically lowering the barrier to entry for robotics applications. This is a critical step towards the realization of truly "generalist" robots that can operate flexibly in unstructured real-world settings.

#### 5.2. Bridging the Gap Towards Robot Foundation Models

LAP's core idea—representing low-level actions in natural language—is a crucial conceptual breakthrough. It directly aligns robot control signals with the input-output distribution of powerful Vision-Language Models (VLMs), mitigating the "distributional mismatch" that previously hampered transfer. This semantic unification means that robot interaction data can be more seamlessly integrated with existing internet-scale VLM corpora. The long-term implication is the possibility of training VLMs *explicitly for robotics*, leading to foundation models that not only reason and communicate in natural language but also *act* within the same unified representation space. Such models could unlock unprecedented levels of intelligence and adaptability in robotic systems.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Data and Compute Efficiency

The findings on efficient fine-tuning are highly practical. LAP-3B's ability to adapt faster and with significantly fewer demonstrations (e.g., 2.5× less data in real-world tasks) means that deploying new skills or adapting to new embodiments becomes substantially less resource-intensive. This reduces the need for massive, specialized datasets for every new robot or task, making robot learning more accessible and scalable.

#### 5.4. Robustness, Generalization, and Scaling

The empirical evidence (t-SNE visualizations, lower prediction errors, favorable scaling laws) strongly supports that language-actions foster more transferable, embodiment-agnostic representations. This inherent robustness makes the models more reliable under embodiment shifts and indicates that the benefits of LAP will amplify with larger models and datasets. The ability of LAP to scale favorably, unlike baselines that saturate or degrade, positions it as a promising architecture for future, even larger, robot foundation models.

#### 5.5. Broader Implications and Future Directions

LAP opens up exciting avenues for future research:
- **Heterogeneous Robot Systems:** The principles of language-actions can be extended to more complex and diverse robotic systems, such as bimanual robots (e.g., by describing coordinated dual-arm actions).
- **Leveraging Less Precise Data:** Language-actions, being less sensitive to noise than raw continuous actions, could enable the use of richer, but less precise, data sources like human pose tracking, Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) data, or even internet videos, thereby democratizing robot learning data.
- **Hierarchical Control:** While LAP addresses low-level control, integrating it with hierarchical or multi-scale action representations could extend its benefits to tasks requiring extremely high precision or fast reactive control.

In conclusion, LAP-3B is a landmark contribution that moves the field significantly closer to the vision of truly generalist robots. By innovatively leveraging the power of natural language for robot control, it establishes a new paradigm for cross-embodiment generalization, promising to accelerate the development and deployment of intelligent robotic systems in the real world.
