# PHASER: Phase-Aware and Semantic Experience Replay for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A new continual learning framework, PHASER, addresses catastrophic forgetting in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation by employing phase-aware memory allocation and multi-modal interference routing. This framework achieved up to a 31% increase in Average Success Rate over standard experience replay and reduced Negative Backward Transfer, further enhanced by an automated phase discovery pipeline for autonomous operation.

## Problem

- Standard experience replay (ER) in VLA models suffers from "phase starvation," where brief, critical sub-skills within manipulation tasks are under-represented due to uniform frame-level sampling.
- Traditional ER allocates replay budget uniformly across historical tasks, failing to account for varying interference risks and non-uniform forgetting during new skill acquisition.
- Manual annotation of sub-skill temporal boundaries for every new task makes lifelong adaptation of VLA models impractical.

## Method

- A phase-centric capacity allocation mechanism assigns a fixed replay budget to each identified sub-skill phase, regardless of its duration, preventing "phase starvation" of critical actions.
- A multi-modal interference-aware routing strategy dynamically prioritizes historical phases for replay based on their risk of forgetting, using tri-modal prototypes (language, visual, kinematic).
- An automated pipeline (Auto-PC) for discovering and semantically verifying sub-skill phase boundaries, combining unsupervised change-point detection with VLM-based verification.

## Results

- PHASER increased Average Success Rate by up to **31%** over standard Experience Replay and reduced Negative Backward Transfer, achieving **87.8%** ASR on LIBERO-Goal and **85.8%** on LIBERO-Long for OpenVLA-OFT-7B.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of both phase-centric capacity allocation and multi-modal interference routing; routing without guaranteed phase capacity led to catastrophic degradation.
- The automated phase boundary sourcing (Auto-PC) pipeline achieved performance comparable to human-annotated PHASER, enabling autonomous and scalable continual learning for VLA models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Success in sequential manipulation tasks is upper-bounded by the weakest sub-skill phase, indicating that uniform frame-level replay is insufficient and domain-specific structural priors are essential.
> - Effective continual learning requires both guaranteeing minimum replay capacity for all critical sub-skill phases and dynamically prioritizing replay based on multi-modal interference risk.
> - Automated phase boundary discovery can achieve performance comparable to human annotations, enabling practical and scalable lifelong learning for VLA models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated capabilities in language-conditioned robotic manipulation. The deployment of these models in dynamic, open-ended environments necessitates continuous skill acquisition. However, this process frequently leads to catastrophic forgetting, where the acquisition of new skills degrades previously learned behaviors. Catastrophic forgetting is a recognized challenge in the field of continual learning.

Experience replay (ER) is a common strategy to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. It involves storing a subset of past experiences and replaying them alongside new data during training. Standard ER methods typically employ uniform sampling from a global buffer. This paper identifies two specific limitations of this standard approach within the context of robotic manipulation:
1. **Phase Starvation**: Manipulation trajectories are often composed of distinct sub-skills or "phases" (e.g., approach, grasp, transport) that vary significantly in duration. Uniform sampling allocates replay memory proportionally to phase length, leading to an under-representation of brief but causally critical sub-skills.
2. **Non-uniform Forgetting**: Standard ER treats all historical tasks identically, failing to account for varying degrees of forgetting across different tasks. Some tasks may share perceptual contexts but demand divergent actions, posing a higher risk of interference and forgetting.

The current work, PHASER, positions itself within the data-driven (replay-based) paradigm of continual learning for VLAs. It aims to refine the experience replay mechanism by incorporating structural priors derived from the temporal and semantic characteristics of manipulation tasks. While other continual learning approaches for VLAs exist, such as architecture-based methods (e.g., dynamic adapters, skill banks) and regularization-based strategies, PHASER is designed as an architecture-agnostic, plug-and-play replay scheme. This approach differentiates it from methods that rely on architectural modifications or explicit representation learning for sub-skill structures, by focusing on a more effective allocation and retrieval of replay experiences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a continual learning framework for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that specifically addresses the limitations of standard experience replay (ER) in the context of robotic manipulation, thereby improving the retention of previously learned skills while acquiring new ones.

This objective is driven by several detailed motivations:

- **Addressing Catastrophic Forgetting in VLA Models**: VLA models are designed to operate in open-ended environments, requiring them to continually learn new manipulation skills. This sequential learning inherently leads to catastrophic forgetting, where knowledge of older tasks is overwritten by new task learning. Mitigating this forgetting is fundamental for the long-term adaptability and utility of robotic systems.
- **Overcoming Intra-Task Phase Starvation**: Robotic manipulation tasks are not monolithic; they are typically composed of a sequence of distinct sub-skills or "phases" (e.g., approaching an object, grasping it, transporting it, placing it). These phases naturally vary in temporal duration. Standard ER, which samples frames uniformly, allocates replay buffer capacity in proportion to a phase's length. This leads to a critical issue termed "phase starvation," where brief but causally essential sub-skills (like a precise grasp or contact transition) receive disproportionately few replay frames. Insufficient rehearsal of these critical sub-skills can severely degrade end-to-end task success, as task success is often a multiplicative product of the success of its constituent phases. The motivation is to ensure that all critical sub-skills, regardless of their temporal length, receive adequate memory support.
- **Mitigating Inter-Task Budget Misallocation and Non-Uniform Forgetting**: Beyond individual phases, different historical tasks pose varying degrees of forgetting risk when a new task is learned. Standard ER's uniform allocation of replay budget across all past tasks overlooks this non-uniformity. Certain historical tasks might share similar perceptual contexts (e.g., same objects, similar environment) with the current task but require entirely different physical actions. Such overlaps can lead to high interference, making these tasks more susceptible to forgetting. The motivation is to intelligently prioritize replay experiences from historical tasks that are at a higher risk of being overwritten, thus optimizing the limited replay budget.
- **Enabling Autonomous Lifelong Adaptation**: The proposed solutions, particularly phase-centric memory allocation, rely on defining temporal boundaries for sub-skills. Traditionally, such boundaries might require intensive manual annotation. For VLA models to adapt autonomously and continuously in real-world, open-ended scenarios, manual supervision for phase decomposition is impractical. Therefore, a key motivation is to automate the process of extracting and labeling sub-skill boundaries without human intervention, thereby enabling fully autonomous lifelong learning.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more intelligent and context-aware experience replay strategies that better align with the structured, sequential nature of robotic manipulation tasks, ultimately leading to more robust and autonomously adaptable VLA models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PHASER is introduced as an architecture-agnostic continual learning framework designed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in VLA models by optimizing experience replay. The methodology is structured around reinterpreting VLA continual learning through a Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) lens and implementing two core data-side principles: intra-task phase-centric capacity allocation and inter-task multi-modal interference routing. Additionally, an automated pipeline, Auto-PC, is developed for phase boundary sourcing.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and SMDP Perspective

The paper frames VLA continual learning as an agent sequentially learning tasks {T₁..., T_N}, each defined by a language instruction and expert trajectories. By modeling each trajectory as an SMDP consisting of sub-skill phases (ϕ_p), the work highlights that task success is bounded by the weakest phase. Uniform frame-level replay is shown to populate the buffer proportionally to each phase's temporal footprint, leading to "phase starvation" for short, critical sub-skills.

#### 3.2. Intra-Task: Phase-Centric Capacity Allocation

To counter phase starvation, PHASER fundamentally shifts memory allocation from the overall task to individual physical phases.
- **Allocation Rule**: Each identified phase ϕ_p is assigned a fixed, equal budget of K frames, decoupling its memory support from its temporal duration. This ensures that brief, causally critical actions receive adequate learning support.
- **Buffer Population**: Phase buffers are populated by two steps:
 1. **Temporal Subsampling**: Frames are subsampled with a stride (∆=3) to remove near-identical consecutive frames and reduce redundancy.
 2. **Reservoir Sampling**: Within the defined phase boundaries, frames are sampled up to the capacity K using reservoir sampling.

#### 3.3. Inter-Task: Multi-Modal Interference-Aware Routing

To address non-uniform inter-task forgetting, PHASER employs a multi-modal interference routing strategy.
- **Tri-Modal Prototypes**: Historical phases are characterized by tri-modal prototypes P_i = (e_L_i, e_V_i, e_A_i), which are a language embedding, a mean visual feature, and a normalized kinematic signature (action embedding).
- **Priority Scoring**: The interference priority (U_i,k) between a historical phase ϕ_i and the current training task T_k is computed as:
 `U_i,k = S_ctx_i,k ⋅ (D_A_i,k - γ(1 - D_A_i,k))`
 - `S_ctx_i,k`: Quantifies perceptual context overlap, calculated as a weighted sum of cosine similarities between language embeddings and visual embeddings (α cos(e_L_i, e_L_k) + (1−α) cos(e_V_i, e_V_k)). This identifies phases sharing similar visual and linguistic contexts.
 - `D_A_i,k`: Represents action divergence, calculated as 1/2 (1 - cos(e_A_i, e_A_k)). High action divergence indicates that similar contexts require different actions.
 - **Intuition**: Phases that share high perceptual context with the current task but demand divergent physical actions are considered at highest risk of forgetting, and thus prioritized for replay. Default values for α and γ are 0.5.
- **Boltzmann Sampling**: Replay weights (p_i) are derived from a Boltzmann distribution: `p_i ∝ exp(U_i,k/τ)` with τ = 0.25. This ensures that phases with higher interference scores are sampled with greater probability. This calculation is performed only once per task transition using cached prototypes, incurring marginal computational overhead during training.

#### 3.4. Automated Phase Boundary Sourcing (Auto-PC)

To enable autonomous lifelong learning without manual phase annotation, PHASER integrates Auto-PC (Phase Candidates).
- **Pipeline**: Auto-PC combines two stages:
 1. **Unsupervised Action-Signal Change-Point Detection**: Analyzes low-level action signals (e.g., changes in joint velocities, gripper state) to propose candidate temporal boundaries.
 2. **VLM-Based Semantic Verification**: A Vision-Language Model (VLM) processes a subsampled video of the trajectory to semantically verify, refine, and label these candidate boundaries. This step ensures the detected boundaries correspond to meaningful sub-skills.
- **Benefit**: This pipeline provides a robust, zero-overhead, and automated method for obtaining phase definitions, replacing manual human annotation.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Environments**: Evaluations were conducted on the LIBERO benchmark, specifically LIBERO-Goal (10 single-stage tasks for skill diversity) and LIBERO-Long (10 compositional multi-stage tasks for structural retention).
- **Model Architectures**: PHASER was tested on three VLA backbones: OpenVLA-OFT-7B (Llama-2 VLM + OFT action head), QwenGR00T-3B (Qwen2.5-VL-3B + GR00T DiT-B regression head), and QwenOFT-3B (Qwen2.5-VL-3B + DiT-B denoising diffusion head). All utilized rank-32 LoRA for fine-tuning.
- **Baselines**: Compared against Sequential FT (no replay), standard ER (uniform reservoir replay), MIR (interference-aware sample prioritization), and iCaRL (exemplar herding + feature distillation). All replay methods operated under identical per-task memory budgets.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Average Success Rate (ASR↑) over 50 zero-shot rollouts per task, and Negative Backward Transfer (NBT↓) calculated from a 10x10 task-by-checkpoint matrix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PHASER across multiple VLA backbones and LIBERO suites yielded several findings:

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Comparison with Baselines

- PHASER demonstrated substantial improvements in continual learning performance, increasing the Average Success Rate (ASR) by up to 31% over matched-budget standard Experience Replay (ER).
- It achieved an 87.8% final ASR on the LIBERO-Goal CL setting with OpenVLA-OFT-7B, outperforming ER (77.6%), MIR (81.6%), and iCaRL (50.2%).
- On LIBERO-Long, PHASER reached 85.8% ASR with OpenVLA-OFT-7B, compared to ER's 54.6%. Similar significant gains were observed with QwenGR00T-3B (48.6% vs. 31.4% for ER) and QwenOFT-3B (51.6% vs. 33.0% for ER).
- The performance lift from ER to PHASER was particularly pronounced on LIBERO-Long, which involves longer-horizon tasks. This is consistent with PHASER’s phase-centric capacity allocation protecting brief, critical sub-skills that uniform replay typically under-supports.
- The gains were observed across different VLA backbones (7B and 3B parameter counts) and action head types (deterministic vs. stochastic policies), indicating the framework's architecture-agnostic nature.

#### 4.2. Decomposition and Causal Verification

- **Routing Requires Capacity Floor**: An ablation study showed that augmenting ER with multi-modal routing alone, without the phase-centric capacity floor, could lead to catastrophic degradation under strict memory constraints (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT-7B on LIBERO-Long, where ASR dropped from 54.6% (ER) to 23.4% (ER + routing)). This was attributed to Boltzmann routing over-concentrating the limited budget, leading to the eviction of vulnerable short phases.
- **Capacity Floor Resolves Degradation**: Integrating the phase-centric capacity floor completely resolved this degradation, demonstrating its role in guaranteeing foundational kinematic stability and enabling the routing module to safely reallocate residual budget.
- **Phase-Localized Instability**: Analysis of per-phase action loss on QwenGR00T-3B x LIBERO-Long revealed that forgetting under uniform ER was characterized by heavy-tailed dispersion, with high mean and variance across phase-length buckets. PHASER, in contrast, maintained action loss tightly clustered across all length buckets, preventing single high-variance phases from causing task failure.
- **Zero-Budget Causal Control**: A control experiment where memory for an initial phase (ϕ0) was completely removed and redistributed to other phases led to a collapse of overall ASR to 16.0% (from 48.6% for full PHASER, and below ER's 31.4%). The unprotected ϕ0 exhibited a high, heavy-tailed loss (0.172 ± 0.131), while adequately buffered phases remained stable (0.055 ± 0.021). This provides evidence that the per-phase capacity floor is a critical mechanism against phase-localized catastrophic forgetting.

#### 4.3. Robustness to Phase Abstractions and Task Ordering

- **Automated Phase Sourcing (Auto-PC)**: Auto-PC, the automated pipeline for phase boundary detection, achieved performance comparable to human-annotated PHASER on LIBERO-Long. This suggests that PHASER's efficacy relies on a structurally sound decomposition rather than precise human annotations. While one outlier case (OpenVLA-OFT-7B on LIBERO-Goal) showed a larger performance gap (87.8% vs. 63.6%), Auto-PC still outperformed ER significantly.
- **Task Order Invariance**: PHASER's performance margin over ER was maintained or improved under reverse and shuffled task orderings. This indicates that PHASER's dynamic multi-modal routing adapts to varying interference risks at each task boundary, unlike ER's static uniform buffer.

#### 4.4. Capacity Scaling and Compute-Memory Efficiency

- **Capacity Scaling**: PHASER scaled monotonically with memory capacity on QwenGR00T-3B x LIBERO-Long, consistently exploiting available memory to achieve high retention in high-capacity regimes. Other baselines, such as MIR, exhibited diminishing returns at larger capacities.
- **Efficiency**: PHASER achieved substantial ASR gains with only marginal computational overhead compared to vanilla ER. This positions it as a lightweight, drop-in replacement without significant increases in wall-clock training time or peak GPU memory, as confirmed by a Pareto frontier analysis across backbones.

#### 4.5. Hyperparameter Sensitivity

- The tri-modal priority score parameters (context blend α and redundancy penalty γ) showed robustness, with an optimal range around α=0.5 and γ=0.5. Both language-only (α=1) and vision-only (α=0) context similarities, as well as disabling the redundancy penalty (γ=0), resulted in suboptimal performance.
- The Boltzmann temperature (τ) also demonstrated robustness over a range (0.25 to 1.0), with degradation occurring at very low temperatures (τ ≤ 0.10).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper introduces PHASER, a continual learning framework that addresses specific limitations of traditional experience replay (ER) in the context of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation. The significance and potential impact of this work can be considered in several areas:

#### 5.1. Enhanced VLA Adaptability and Robustness

PHASER directly tackles two core challenges in continual learning for VLA models: phase starvation and inter-task budget misallocation. By ensuring that brief but causally critical sub-skills are adequately supported in memory (phase-centric capacity allocation) and by prioritizing replay of historical phases most at risk of forgetting (multi-modal interference routing), PHASER significantly improves the retention of learned behaviors. This enhanced robustness to catastrophic forgetting is crucial for VLA models to continually acquire new skills in open-ended, dynamic environments without sacrificing previously mastered capabilities.

#### 5.2. Generalizability Across Diverse VLA Architectures

The framework’s architecture-agnostic design is a notable feature. Its effectiveness was demonstrated across three distinct VLA backbones (OpenVLA-OFT-7B, QwenGR00T-3B, QwenOFT-3B), spanning different parameter scales and action head types (regression vs. diffusion policies). This broad applicability suggests that PHASER can be integrated with various existing and future VLA models, making it a versatile tool for the continual learning community in robotics.

#### 5.3. Practicality and Autonomous Lifelong Learning

PHASER operates as a drop-in replacement for uniform ER, offering substantial performance gains with only marginal computational overhead. This low computational cost makes it practical for real-world deployment where computational resources might be constrained. Furthermore, the introduction of Auto-PC, an automated pipeline for phase boundary detection, removes the reliance on manual supervision for segmenting manipulation trajectories. This is a critical step towards enabling fully autonomous, lifelong adaptation for robotic systems, as it allows robots to continually learn and refine skills without human intervention in defining task sub-components.

#### 5.4. Advancing Experience Replay Strategies

The work offers a structural reinterpretation of replay buffer management. By shifting the atomic unit of memory from individual frames to semantically meaningful phases, PHASER moves beyond simple sample-level selection. This paradigm shift could inspire future research in more structured and context-aware memory management techniques for continual learning, particularly in domains where temporal or hierarchical structures are inherent in the data. The use of tri-modal prototypes for interference routing also highlights the benefit of leveraging multi-modal information (language, vision, action) to inform replay decisions.

#### 5.5. Foundation for Future Research

PHASER provides a strong foundation for future exploration. The paper identifies opportunities for further improvement, such as upgrading intra-phase sampling to more sophisticated keyframe extraction methods (e.g., semantic-logical dependency scoring) to maximize the informativeness of stored frames. It also complements other continual learning paradigms (architectural and regularization-based), suggesting potential for synergistic integration to achieve even greater retention capabilities.

In summary, PHASER represents a contribution to the field of continual robot learning by providing a robust, efficient, and broadly applicable method for mitigating catastrophic forgetting in VLA models. Its impact lies in enabling more capable and autonomously adaptable robotic systems that can continuously learn and operate effectively in complex, evolving environments.
