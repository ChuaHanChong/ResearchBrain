# Motion Primitive Discovery in a Humanoid Robot via Self-Organising Maps for Phase Recognition

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Comenius University Bratislava developed a biologically inspired, two-level architecture for motor primitive discovery and online phase recognition in a humanoid robot. This system achieved 93.9% phase recognition accuracy using only learned motor primitives and up to 97.3% with full contextual information on a simulated NICO humanoid.

## Problem

- Interpreting human motor behavior and inferring intentions in human-robot interaction (HRI) is challenging.
- Current end-to-end action classification models often lack the explicit two-level structure (primitives and sequences) for action understanding suggested by biological evidence.
- Developing computational models that effectively represent and utilize proprioceptive signals, a less understood sense in cognitive neuroscience, is crucial for robust movement control.

## Method

- A **two-level, Mirror Neuron System-inspired** computational architecture was proposed, integrating ==Self-Organising Maps (SOMs)== for primitive discovery and an ==Echo State Network (ESN)== for online phase recognition.
- Two separate SOMs (==A-SOM== for arm kinematics, ==H-SOM== for hand kinematics) were trained on simulated humanoid proprioceptive data to discover reusable motion primitives without supervision.
- An ESN was used to integrate the temporal sequence of these SOM-discovered primitive descriptors, along with optional contextual information, to predict the current movement phase online.

## Results

- The ESN achieved a phase recognition accuracy (PRA) of **93.9%** using only the SOM-based primitive descriptors, which further increased to **94.9%** with the addition of full contextual information.
- The A-SOM demonstrated a phase purity of **73.9%** with coherent topographical organization for arm movements, while the H-SOM achieved a high grasp purity of **97.2%**, effectively separating hand configurations.
- Increasing the ESN's reservoir capacity was the dominant factor for improving PRA, achieving up to **96.7%** with primitives alone at **N=1000**, and overall **97.3%** with full context, demonstrating that context largely compensates for limited temporal memory.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - SOM-discovered motor primitives, when temporally integrated by an ESN, are sufficient to support high online phase recognition accuracy, capturing a dominant share of phase-discriminative information.
> - Arm and hand kinematics encode distinct and complementary aspects of motor behavior, with A-SOM capturing arm transport dynamics and H-SOM effectively encoding hand configurations and grasp types.
> - Contextual signals (e.g., action identity, object distance, contact) refine phase recognition accuracy but play a secondary role compared to the information inherent in primitive sequences, diminishing in value with increased ESN reservoir capacity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of understanding the computational basis of action recognition, a critical area in social cognition and human-robot interaction (HRI). The ability for a robot to interpret human motor behavior and infer intentions is fundamental for effective collaboration and adaptation.

The paper draws inspiration from the Mirror Neuron System (MNS), a biological model where neurons activate both during action execution and observation, suggesting action understanding is grounded in an internal motor vocabulary rather than abstract perceptual classification. Neurophysiological studies indicate that parietal mirror neurons encode both observed actions and their goals, promoting the development of computational systems that can distinguish actions at the level of motor primitives and goal-directed sequences.

Current approaches often involve training recurrent or transformer architectures end-to-end on action labels, directly encoding observed motion from kinematic or visual signals. However, these methods do not consistently emphasize the biological insight that action understanding may operate simultaneously across two levels: a repertoire of elementary motion primitives and the goal-directed sequences they form. This research specifically aims to address this by explicitly modeling action understanding at these two levels, consistent with MNS-inspired computational models that utilize Self-Organising Maps (SOMs) for integrating motion and context inputs, leading to the emergence of goal-specific neuron pools. Previous work in this domain has also explored topographic mapping as an organizing principle for MNS models.

This study distinguishes itself by focusing on the topographic representation of proprioceptive signals, which describe the time-varying state of moving arm and hand. Proprioception, while fundamental for movement control, is a less understood sense in cognitive neuroscience. By adopting a dual-level architecture with SOMs for primitive discovery and an Echo State Network (ESN) for temporal integration and phase recognition, the research positions itself within the field of biologically inspired robotics, seeking to create robust, interpretable models for robot action understanding that align with established principles of cortical organization and MNS function.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose and evaluate a two-level, MNS-inspired computational architecture for motor primitive discovery and online phase recognition in a humanoid robot. This architecture aims to enable a robot to interpret ongoing movements at a fundamental level, contributing to more adaptive and collaborative human-robot interaction.

The motivation for this work is multifaceted:

- **Biological Inspiration and Action Understanding:** The Mirror Neuron System (MNS) provides a compelling biological model for action recognition, suggesting that understanding observed actions is rooted in an internal motor vocabulary. This motivates the development of computational systems that can similarly decompose and interpret actions. The authors seek to build a system that can distinguish actions based on motor primitives and their goal-directed sequences, rather than relying solely on abstract perceptual classification.
- **Addressing Limitations of Current Models:** While modern deep learning approaches can classify actions, they do not always explicitly model the two-level structure of action understanding (primitives and sequences) suggested by biological evidence. This research aims to incorporate this two-level emphasis, moving beyond end-to-end classification to a more structured, interpretable representation.
- **Proprioceptive Signal Processing:** The study specifically focuses on learning from proprioceptive signals (kinematics of arm and hand), acknowledging their fundamental role in movement control and the relative lack of understanding of this sense in cognitive neuroscience. The use of cortical topography as an organizing principle for these signals is a key design choice.
- **Dual-Level Architecture for Primitive Discovery and Temporal Integration:**
 - **Level 1 (Motion Primitive Discovery):** The motivation for using two separate Self-Organising Maps (SOMs) for arm (A-SOM) and hand (H-SOM) kinematics is grounded in the observation that motion and context inputs to parietal mirror neurons originate from distinct neural pathways. This dual-map design reflects the belief that arm movement and hand shaping provide complementary information during action execution. SOMs are chosen because they can discover reusable motion primitives (compact patterns of movement) without supervision, organizing similar movements into prototype nodes that form a discrete vocabulary.
 - **Level 2 (Online Phase Recognition):** An Echo State Network (ESN) is employed to encode the temporal ordering of the primitives discovered by the SOMs. The ESN's ability to integrate temporal sequences into a reservoir state allows for the inference of movement phases. A key motivation here is to extend previous ESN-based approaches to arm trajectory prediction to the more complex task of *online* phase recognition, where the system must identify the *current* movement phase from the sensory-motor stream available up to that timestep, rather than classifying a complete action after observing the entire sequence.
- **Core Research Question:** The central objective is to quantify the sufficiency of the SOM-based primitive representation for online phase recognition. Specifically, the research questions are:
 1. How accurately can the ESN identify the current movement phase using only SOM activation trajectories (i.e., learned motor primitives)?
 2. What is the additional contribution of contextual information (e.g., action identity, object distance, contact state) beyond the primitive signal to online phase recognition accuracy?

The study explicitly states that its objective is to test the sufficiency of the SOM representation, not to establish its superiority over direct-kinematics ESNs or contemporary sequence-learning models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a two-level architecture consisting of Self-Organising Maps (SOMs) for motor primitive discovery and an Echo State Network (ESN) for online movement phase recognition. The overall processing pipeline comprised motion data collection, feature selection, windowing and normalization, SOM training, temporal integration via ESN, and online phase recognition.

#### 3.1. Data Collection in Simulation

Data was generated using a physics-based Unity simulation environment featuring the NICO humanoid robot. The robot performed seven motor actions: Pick, Eat, Place, Push, Tap, Point, and Wave. These actions involved interaction with a library of 14 physically diverse objects (spheres, cubes, cylinders, discs, irregular shapes, varying sizes). Object positions were randomized within the robot's reachable workspace to ensure diverse motion trajectories. Object-manipulation actions (Pick, Eat, Place) utilized either a Power or Precision grasp, while other actions employed dedicated hand configurations.
Arm movements were controlled by a Barracuda ONNX Inverse Kinematics (IK) network, mapping Cartesian poses to 6 joint angles. The 13 finger joints of the hand were controlled by a low-dimensional synergy controller based on four scalar parameters (thumb rotation, thumb curl, index closure, finger closure), designed to mimic human motor neuroscience.
Motion data was logged at 60 Hz (approximately 16.7 ms per frame), capturing 108 simultaneous variables per frame. The complete dataset comprised 4,200 trials. The 108 recorded variables were categorized into nine functional groups (e.g., metadata, arm joint angles/velocities, TCP positions/velocities, palm pose, finger joints, aperture, object state). Actions were defined as complete goal-directed trials, while each action was decomposed into a sequence of named movement phases, resulting in 27 distinct phase labels across all actions. Motion primitives were defined as compact patterns discovered by the SOMs, reusable across actions and phases.

#### 3.2. Feature Analysis and Selection

A three-stage pipeline was applied to the 108 candidate features to reduce redundancy and confirm feature independence:
1. **Initial Exclusion:** 51 columns were removed due to being metadata, trial labels (non-kinematic), workspace-dependent absolute joint positions, or duplicated TCPs. This left 57 candidate kinematic features.
2. **Correlation Analysis:** Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between all pairs of the remaining 57 features across all 1,284,794 frames. Absolute correlation values $|r_{ij}|$ were used to create a symmetric similarity matrix R.
3. **Clustering and Selection:** The similarity matrix R was converted to a distance matrix $d_{ij} = 1 - r_{ij}$. Hierarchical agglomerative clustering with Ward’s minimum-variance linkage was applied to this distance matrix. A dendrogram was used to visualize the merge tree. Features that branched at low distances (high correlation) were considered redundant. By cutting the dendrogram at a threshold of $\tau = 0.7$, 22 non-redundant features were selected.
 These 22 features were then divided for the two SOMs:
 - **A-SOM (Arm SOM):** 13 features (3 TCP velocities, 6 arm joint velocities, 4 palm orientation quaternion components).
 - **H-SOM (Hand SOM):** 9 features (2 aperture and velocity, 3 thumb and middle finger positions, 4 finger joint velocities).

#### 3.3. Windowing and Normalization

Motion logs were segmented into overlapping windows of 30 frames (0.5 seconds at 60 Hz) with a stride of 15 frames (50% overlap). Each window was represented by its per-feature mean. Phase and action labels were assigned from the central frame of each window. To prevent bias, actions were stratified to ensure an equal number of windows per action, resulting in 8,789 windows per action (61,523 total). Each feature was independently normalized using z-score standardization, calculated from the training set statistics, yielding vectors $\hat{x}(t)$. Normalization was applied independently for A-SOM and H-SOM inputs.

#### 3.4. The SOM Models (Level 1)

Two separate SOMs were trained: A-SOM for arm kinematics and H-SOM for hand kinematics.
- **Architecture and Training:** Each SOM comprised a grid of $R \times C = K$ neurons, learning a weight matrix $W \in R^{K \times d}$ (where $d$ is the input dimension, $d_A=13$ for A-SOM, $d_H=9$ for H-SOM). For an input vector $\hat{x}(t)$, the best matching unit (BMU) $i^*$ was identified as the neuron with the minimum Euclidean distance between its weight vector and the input. Weights were updated using the Kohonen rule, incorporating a learning rate $\alpha(n)$ and a Gaussian neighborhood function $h_{i,i^*}(t)$ that decreased over time.
- **Configuration:** A-SOM used a 35x35 grid of neurons. H-SOM used an 18x18 grid. These sizes were determined through grid search, with A-SOM's size chosen for improved mean phase purity and H-SOM's size reflecting the lower intrinsic dimensionality of the hand synergy controller.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Map organization was quantified by measuring the purity of phase, action, and grasp labels. For each active neuron, the dominant class label was identified, and purity was the proportion of samples belonging to that dominant label, averaged across active neurons. Neuron utilization was the percentage of neurons that became BMUs for at least one training sample.

#### 3.5. Phase Recognition using the ESN (Level 2)

The ESN integrated the temporal sequence of primitive descriptors (BMU weight vectors) from the SOMs to predict the current movement phase.
- **Dataset and Split:** The original 4,200 simulated trials were subsampled at a stride of 5 (83 ms per step). A stratified 80:20 split by action created 3,360 training trials and 840 test trials. After a warm-up period, the training set contained 190,603 labeled timesteps.
- **Input Representation:** At each timestep $t$, the BMU weight vectors from A-SOM ($b_A(t) \in R^{13}$) and H-SOM ($b_H(t) \in R^9$) were concatenated to form the primitive descriptor. Four cumulative context configurations were evaluated:
 1. `ctx-no` (22D): Primitives only, $u(t) = [b_A(t), b_H(t)]$.
 2. `ctx-act` (29D): + 7-dimensional one-hot action identity $a$.
 3. `ctx-dst` (30D): + normalized palm-object distance $d(t) \in R$.
 4. `ctx-all` (31D): + binary object contact flag $c(t) \in \{0, 1\}$.
- **Target:** The ESN readout target $y(t)$ was a 27-dimensional one-hot encoding of the ground-truth phase $\phi(t)$.
- **Reservoir:** An ESN with $N=300$ randomly connected neurons was used. The state update employed a leaky integrator: $\tilde{x}(t) = \text{tanh}(W_{res}x(t-1) + W_{in}u(t))$ and $x(t) = (1-\alpha)x(t-1) + \alpha\tilde{x}(t)$, with a leaking rate $\alpha=0.50$. $W_{in}$ and $W_{res}$ were randomly initialized (spectral radius $\rho=0.99$) and fixed. The reservoir state was reset for each trial, and the first 5 steps were used as warm-up.
- **Readout Training:** Readout weights $W_{out}$ were computed analytically using ridge regression ($W_{out} = (X^T X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^T Y$, with $\lambda=10^{-4}$), based on reservoir states $X$ and target phases $Y$ collected during a single pass through training trials.
- **Inference and Evaluation:** The predicted phase $\hat{\phi}(t)$ at test time was the argmax of $W_{out}^T x(t)$. Phase recognition accuracy (PRA) was defined as the fraction of post-warm-up test steps where the predicted phase matched the ground-truth phase.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The study also explored the impact of various reservoir configurations (number of neurons N, spectral radius $\rho$, leaking rate $\alpha$, ridge regression $\lambda$) on PRA.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded specific results regarding the efficacy of the dual-SOM architecture for motor primitive discovery and the subsequent online phase recognition using an ESN.

#### 4.1. SOM Model Results (Level 1)

- **A-SOM (Arm SOM):**
 - Achieved 90.0% neuron utilization, with unused neurons primarily located at the map periphery.
 - Exhibited a phase purity of 73.9%, demonstrating strong topographical organization. Large, coherent zones for phases such as 'lift', 'wave', and 'preshape' emerged without explicit supervision. The map showed a directional reaching topology where approach phases occupied one half and terminal phases the other.
 - Action purity was 66.9%, and grasp purity was 66.4%. These lower purities indicated that arm kinematics alone were insufficient to fully distinguish actions sharing similar reach trajectories or to encode specific grip types, suggesting a role for other kinematic information.
- **H-SOM (Hand SOM):**
 - Achieved 51.5% neuron utilization, which was lower than A-SOM. This was attributed to the hand synergy controller producing discrete, non-overlapping configuration spaces, leading to dead neurons acting as boundaries between isolated action islands.
 - Demonstrated a high grasp purity of 97.2%. Actions like Point, Tap, Wave, and Push formed perfectly pure, isolated clusters. Power and Precision grasps separated into distinct zones, with impurities restricted to a narrow transition band. This result indicates that the H-SOM effectively captured hand configuration.
 - Action purity was 72.9%, and phase purity was 76.1%, which exceeded A-SOM's phase purity. This confirmed that finger-level signals provided complementary discriminative structure compared to arm-level primitives.
- **Complementarity:** The results confirmed that arm and hand kinematics encoded distinct and complementary aspects of motor behavior, supporting the dual-SOM design. A-SOM primarily captured the spatial and temporal structure of arm transport, while H-SOM accurately encoded hand configuration.
- **BMU Trajectories:** Visualization of BMU trajectories on the A-SOM for a single trial demonstrated smooth paths, indicating that the SOM preserved the temporal structure of the executed action and that consecutive phases occupied coherent regions of the map.

#### 4.2. ESN Phase Recognition Results (Level 2)

- **Phase Recognition Accuracy (PRA):**
 - **Primitive Descriptor Alone (ctx-no):** The ESN achieved a PRA of 93.9% using only the SOM activation trajectories (concatenated BMU weight vectors) as input. This indicates that the learned motor primitives contained a dominant share of the phase-discriminative information, even without external contextual cues.
 - **Contextual Information Contribution:**
 - Adding action identity (`ctx-act`) improved PRA to 94.6% (+0.7 percentage points (pp) over `ctx-no`).
 - Adding normalized palm-object distance (`ctx-dst`) resulted in 94.6% PRA (+0.8 pp over `ctx-no`).
 - The full context configuration (`ctx-all`), including action identity, object distance, and object contact flag, achieved 94.9% PRA (+1.0 pp over `ctx-no`).
 - These results demonstrate that while contextual signals do provide a refinement, they play a secondary role compared to the information inherent in the temporal integration of primitive descriptors by the ESN.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:**
 - Reservoir capacity ($N$) was identified as the dominant factor influencing accuracy. PRA generally increased monotonically with $N$. For example, at $N=300$, the base PRA was 93.9%, but at $N=1000$, it reached 96.7% for `ctx-no`.
 - The gain provided by contextual features diminished as reservoir capacity increased. At $N=30$, full context (ctx-all) contributed +4.1 pp, while at $N=1000$, it contributed only +0.60 pp. This suggests that contextual features can compensate for limited reservoir memory rather than providing entirely independent discriminative signals.
 - The best overall result achieved was 97.3% PRA (for $N=1000, \rho=0.90, \alpha=0.10$ with `ctx-all`).
- **Single-Trial Analysis (Eat Action Example):**
 - For a representative 'Eat' trial, the `ctx-no` configuration achieved 98.4% accuracy (63 out of 64 steps), with a single error occurring at the first 'move-to-grasp' timestep. This error was attributed to the reservoir requiring an additional step to transition from a sustained 'preshape' phase.
 - The `ctx-all` configuration achieved 100% accuracy for the same trial. This improvement was attributed to the contextual information (action one-hot and palm-to-object distance) providing a sufficient signal to detect the phase transition one step earlier.
 - The analysis also highlighted that neither A-SOM nor H-SOM provided unambiguous information throughout the entire trial independently. For instance, an A-SOM BMU might show a mixed distribution of phases (e.g., 35% 'move-to-grasp', 23% 'preshape'). However, the ESN resolved this ambiguity through the temporal integration of preceding reservoir states and the simultaneous integration of complementary A-SOM and H-SOM descriptors (e.g., H-SOM indicating finger opening when A-SOM was ambiguous).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of action recognition in both social cognition and human-robot interaction by proposing and evaluating a biologically inspired, two-level architecture.

#### 5.1. Core Contributions and Scientific Significance

- **Validation of MNS-Inspired Architecture:** The work successfully integrates established Self-Organising Maps (SOMs) and Echo State Networks (ESNs) within an architecture inspired by the Mirror Neuron System (MNS). This integration provides a computational framework for both motor primitive representation and online phase recognition.
- **Support for Computational Hypothesis:** The results support the computational hypothesis that self-organized motor representations, when temporally integrated, can effectively support accurate online recognition of ongoing movement phases. The high phase recognition accuracy achieved by the ESN, even with only primitive descriptors, indicates the robustness of this approach.
- **Demonstration of Complementary Kinematic Signals:** The dual-SOM design validates the concept that arm and hand kinematics encode complementary aspects of motor behavior. The A-SOM captures the spatial and temporal dynamics of arm transport, while the H-SOM effectively captures hand configurations and grasp types. This suggests that separate primitive spaces for different body parts are beneficial for comprehensive action representation.
- **Quantification of Contextual Information Role:** The study quantifies the contribution of contextual information (e.g., action identity, object distance, contact state) to phase recognition. The finding that these contextual cues provide refinement rather than being the dominant source of phase-discriminative structure is significant. It implies that the intrinsic dynamics of motor primitives, as captured by the SOMs and integrated by the ESN, hold most of the essential information for recognizing movement phases. The diminishing gain from context with increased reservoir capacity further suggests that context can compensate for limitations in temporal memory.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Advancement in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** By enabling a robot to accurately and online recognize ongoing movement phases, this work lays a foundation for more sophisticated and intuitive human-robot interaction. Robots equipped with such capabilities could interpret human partners' motor behaviors, anticipate their intentions, adapt proactively, and collaborate more effectively. This moves beyond simple action classification to a deeper, phase-level understanding of movement.
- **Biological Plausibility for AI/Robotics:** The MNS-inspired approach offers a biologically plausible computational model for action understanding, which could inform the design of more human-like and intelligent robotic systems. It provides a structured, interpretable approach compared to opaque end-to-end learning methods.
- **Basis for Future Predictive and Generative Models:** The authors outline future work to extend this model from recognition to prediction (anticipating the next SOM activation) and eventually to closed-loop generation of imagined motor trajectories. This could lead to robots capable of not only understanding observed actions but also predicting and even mimicking them, enhancing their learning and adaptation capabilities.
- **Insights into Proprioception:** By explicitly modeling and leveraging proprioceptive signals, the research contributes to the broader understanding of this fundamental sense in cognitive neuroscience and its computational representation.

#### 5.3. Current Limitations and Future Directions

A current limitation noted by the authors is that the model is trained and evaluated on the NICO robot's own actions. Extending its capabilities to cross-embodiment observation, where a robot observes human actions and maps them to its own motor vocabulary, will require further research into mirroring mechanisms for handling viewpoint, scale, and embodiment differences. Future work will also focus on transitioning from phase recognition to prediction, by training the ESN to anticipate subsequent SOM activations, which could then enable primitive prediction and closed-loop generation of motor trajectories.
