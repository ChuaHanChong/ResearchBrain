# Learning Smooth State-Dependent Traversability from Dense Point Clouds

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northeastern University and DEVCOM Army Research Laboratory introduced SPARTA, a method that learns state-dependent terrain traversability from dense point clouds by modeling angle-dependent risk as a smooth analytical function. This approach achieved a 91% success rate in challenging simulated boulder fields and enabled safer robot navigation on hardware by improving generalization and computational efficiency.

## Problem

- Autonomous mobile robots struggle to accurately assess terrain traversability in off-road environments due to the state-dependent nature of risk, particularly concerning the robot's angle of approach.
- Existing learning methods that directly encode the angle of approach face issues like demanding large datasets, wrap-around discontinuities, and computational inefficiency during real-time planning.
- Coarser terrain representations (e.g., elevation maps) lose fine geometric details crucial for risk assessment, and many prior works do not adequately capture aleatoric uncertainty in traversability.

## Method

- **SPARTA** predicts angle-dependent risk as a categorical distribution over discrete bins, where the concentration parameters are represented as smooth analytical functions of the approach angle.
- It leverages the periodic nature of angles by decomposing the risk functions into ==Fourier basis functions== on the 1-Sphere, outputting Fourier coefficients from a neural network that processes dense point clouds.
- The framework integrates with a ==Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)== planner, utilizing ==Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR)== at a specified alpha level as a robust, worst-case risk metric for motion optimization.

## Results

- **SPARTA** achieved a lower test EMD^2 loss (**0.09**) compared to the AngleInput baseline (**0.15**), demonstrating improved generalization to unseen angles of approach.
- The model exhibited an order-of-magnitude faster inference runtime for querying risk during planning compared to baselines, maintaining constant speed with increasing model complexity.
- In simulation, **SPARTA** achieved a **91%** success rate in challenging boulder fields and consistently chose safe paths on a physical robot, outperforming baselines which often chose risky trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Imposing geometric structure by representing angle-dependent risk with Fourier basis functions significantly improves model generalization to unseen angles and naturally handles angular periodicity.
> - Predicting an analytical function's coefficients (rather than direct angle predictions) allows for an order-of-magnitude faster risk query during planning, enhancing computational efficiency for real-time applications.
> - Modeling risk probabilistically via categorical distributions and integrating CVaR into the planning objective provides a robust framework for safety-critical tasks by accounting for aleatoric uncertainty and worst-case scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Autonomous mobile robots operating in off-road environments encounter complex challenges, particularly in accurately assessing terrain traversability. This field is critical for applications ranging from environmental exploration to defense operations. Existing methods for traversability estimation typically rely on either handcrafted heuristics or learned patterns from sensor data.

**Current Limitations:**
- **State-Dependency:** A significant challenge in off-road autonomy is that terrain traversability often depends on the robot's state, such as its speed and, crucially, its angle of approach. Traditional methods, including those based on elevation maps, often struggle to capture these nuanced, state-dependent interactions due to their inability to fully account for fine geometric details or to efficiently model the continuous nature of angle dependency.
- **Data Representation:** While point clouds capture rich geometric details, which are often lost in simplified representations like elevation maps (as illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper), existing learning-based approaches that directly input the angle of approach into a neural network face practical difficulties. These include demanding large, diverse training datasets, struggling with wrap-around discontinuities (where 0 and 2π are the same angle), and being computationally inefficient during planning due to repeated model inference for different angles.
- **Aleatoric Uncertainty:** Many prior works provide point estimates of risk, which may not adequately capture the inherent aleatoric uncertainty in real-world traversability, limiting the robustness of planning decisions.

**SPARTA's Position and Contribution:**
This work, introducing SPARTA (Smooth Point-cloud Approach-angle Reasoning for Terrain Assessment), addresses these limitations by specifically focusing on learning angle-of-approach-conditioned traversability from dense point clouds. SPARTA distinguishes itself by:
1. **Explicitly Modeling Angle-Dependency:** Unlike many previous methods that either ignore angle-dependency or encode it naively, SPARTA explicitly models the continuous nature of the approach angle.
2. **Geometric Structure Imposition:** It leverages the geometric structure of the 1-Sphere (the unit circle for angles) by representing the angle-dependent risk as a smooth analytical function composed of Fourier basis functions. This design choice aims to mitigate the issues of data sparsity and wrap-around discontinuities encountered by direct angle encoding.
3. **Computational Efficiency for Planning:** By predicting a reusable analytical function, SPARTA aims to reduce the computational overhead during real-time planning, as the function can be queried multiple times without repeated neural network inference.
4. **Robust Risk Assessment:** It models risk as a categorical distribution and incorporates Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) into the planning objective, providing a principled approach to account for aleatoric uncertainty and worst-case scenarios.
5. **Utilizing Dense Point Clouds:** By using dense point clouds as input, SPARTA retains fine geometric details crucial for distinguishing traversable terrain features from environmental noise.

The paper aims to extend the capabilities of traversability estimation methods by providing a more granular and accurate prediction of risk, particularly for variables like potential vehicle damage, which prior works often simplify through handcrafted rules.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a learning-based method capable of accurately estimating the traversability of terrain, specifically conditioned on the vehicle's angle of approach, using dense point cloud data. This method is named SPARTA (Smooth Point-cloud Approach-angle Reasoning for Terrain Assessment).

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several key challenges in off-road autonomy:

1. **State-Dependent Risk:** A fundamental issue in complex off-road environments (e.g., dense forests, boulder fields) is that the risk associated with traversing a particular terrain patch is not static but varies significantly with the robot's state. The angle of approach is highlighted as a critical state variable influencing traversability, as some obstacles are only navigable from specific orientations. Existing approaches often rely on hand-crafted heuristics or simplified models that struggle to capture this complex, angle-dependent interaction accurately.
2. **Computational and Data Inefficiency:** Learning state-dependent traversability by directly encoding the angle of approach as an input to a neural network presents practical limitations. Such models typically demand a large and diverse training dataset to generalize across various angles and are computationally inefficient during motion planning. Each query for a different angle requires a full neural network inference, which can be prohibitive for real-time planning algorithms like MPPI that generate numerous rollouts.
3. **Information Loss in Terrain Representation:** Representations such as elevation maps can lead to information loss, making it difficult to differentiate between traversable terrain and environmental noise (e.g., leaves) or to discern subtle geometric features crucial for accurate risk assessment. Dense point clouds, conversely, retain these fine geometric details.
4. **Modeling Aleatoric Uncertainty:** The inherent unpredictability and noise in real-world systems (aleatoric uncertainty) necessitate a robust method for risk assessment beyond single scalar predictions. A probabilistic representation of risk is required to inform risk-aware planning.

To address these motivations, SPARTA proposes a method that incorporates several underlying principles:
- **Impose Geometric Structure:** The approach explicitly leverages the cyclical nature of approach angles by modeling them on the 1-Sphere.
- **Smooth Analytical Function:** Rather than point predictions for specific angles, the model learns parameters for a smooth analytical function that can predict risk for *any* angle of approach.
- **Fourier Basis Functions:** These functions are chosen to compose the analytical function due to their properties of periodicity and smoothness, which are expected to improve generalization, especially with limited data, and avoid wrap-around discontinuities.
- **Query Efficiency:** Once the Fourier coefficients for a terrain patch are computed, the analytical function can be queried efficiently with minimal computational overhead for subsequent planning steps, eliminating the need for repeated neural network inference.
- **Probabilistic Risk:** Risk is modeled as a categorical distribution, providing a more comprehensive representation of uncertainty compared to scalar values.

The paper focuses on vehicle damage (specifically, tire deformation) as the primary risk variable to demonstrate the framework's effectiveness, a metric often overlooked or simplified in prior research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPARTA (Smooth Point-cloud Approach-angle Reasoning for Terrain Assessment) is designed to predict angle-dependent traversal risk from dense point clouds. The methodology integrates geometric structure, probabilistic risk modeling, and computational efficiency into a unified framework.

#### 3.1. Risk Modeling with Categorical Distributions

The method addresses aleatoric uncertainty by modeling risk as a random variable, specifically a categorical distribution with B discrete bins. This approach allows for approximating arbitrary distributions without assuming a specific parametric form. Given a terrain patch (point cloud Q) and an angle of approach (ϕ ∈ [0, 2π]), the goal is to predict p(γ_ϕ), the probability mass function (PMF) of a risk-related variable (γ_ϕ). This PMF is parameterized by non-negative concentration parameters ¯γ_ϕ = [γ_1_ϕ..., γ_B_ϕ], which are normalized to sum to 1.

#### 3.2. Leveraging the 1-Sphere with Fourier Basis Functions

The core novelty of SPARTA lies in representing the risk concentration parameters (γ_i_ϕ) as smooth functions of the approach angle (ϕ).
- **Angle Representation:** The angles [0, 2π] are mapped to the 1-sphere (S1), recognizing their periodic nature.
- **Fourier Series:** Any square-integrable real-valued function over S1 can be decomposed into a linear combination of Fourier basis functions {1} ∪ {cos(kϕ), sin(kϕ) : k ∈ [1, n]}. SPARTA utilizes this to define each concentration parameter γ_i_ϕ as:
 γ_i_ϕ = σ( (a_0_i / 2) + Σ(k=1 to n) [a_k_i cos(kϕ) + b_k_i sin(kϕ)] ),
 where σ(·) is the sigmoid function, ensuring non-negativity.
- **Network Output:** The neural network Γ_θ takes the input point cloud Q and predicts the Fourier coefficients F_i = [a_0_i, a_1_i, b_1_i..., a_n_i, b_n_i] for each of the B risk bins.
- **Advantages:** This representation naturally handles periodicity, avoiding wrap-around discontinuities that plague direct angle encoding. It also offers computational efficiency; once coefficients are computed, querying the function for any angle involves a fast, low-dimensional dot product and sigmoid operation.

#### 3.3. Imposed Smoothness and Generalization

The use of Fourier basis functions imposes smoothness on the predicted risk functions. The paper provides a theoretical analysis demonstrating that each γ_i_ϕ is Lipschitz continuous with respect to ϕ. An upper bound on the Lipschitz constant L_i is derived:
L_i ≤ (1/4) Σ(k=1 to n) k * sqrt((a_k_i)^2 + (b_k_i)^2).
This bounded Lipschitz constant is hypothesized to improve model generalization to unseen angles of approach, as smaller Lipschitz constants are associated with better generalization in deep learning. This allows the network to concentrate on extracting meaningful features from the point cloud rather than learning the angular structure implicitly.

#### 3.4. Neural Network Architecture

All learned models (SPARTA, AngleInput, AngleFree) use B=8 bins for categorical distributions.
- **Point Pillars Encoder:** The raw point cloud Q (m points in R^(m×3)) is normalized (x,y in [-0.5, 0.5], z in [0, 0.5]) and discretized into 0.1m pillars. Points within each pillar are augmented with relative coordinates (x_c, y_c, z_c) to the pillar's mean and offsets (x_p, y_p) from the pillar center. Up to 32 points per pillar are sampled to generate a 32-dimensional feature vector, resulting in a 10x10x32 pseudo-image tensor.
- **SPARTA's Backbone and Decoder:**
 - A two-layer convolutional backbone (input channels, output channels, kernel size, stride, padding) processes the pseudo-image. Batch normalization and ReLU activation are used.
 - Max pooling reduces the 3x3x256 output tensor to a 256-dimensional point cloud feature.
 - A multilayer perceptron (MLP) decoder (one hidden layer with 512 neurons, layer normalization, ReLU) transforms the 256-dimensional feature into a 56-dimensional output vector, representing the 8 sets of Fourier coefficients (F_1..., F_8) for n=3 (2*3+1=7 coefficients per bin, 7*8=56).

#### 3.5. Training

- **Dataset:** Consists of { (Q_k, y_k, ϕ_k) }, where Q_k is the point cloud, y_k is the ground truth (empirical) risk PMF, and ϕ_k is the angle of approach.
- **Loss Function:** The squared Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD^2) is used to minimize the difference between the predicted and ground truth PMFs: L_EMD^2(p(ϕ_k), y_k) = ||cs(p(ϕ_k)) - cs(y_k)||^2_2, where cs(·) is the cumulative sum operator.

#### 3.6. Integration with Motion Planner

- **Planner:** A Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planner is used for control sequence optimization.
- **Risk Metric:** The Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) at level α ∈ (CVaR_α(γ_ϕ)) is adopted as the worst-case risk metric. This represents the expectation of the α portion of the distribution's right tail, making it suitable for safety-critical tasks.
- **Planning Objective:** The MPPI minimizes a cost function comprising a task-related term (e.g., goal-reaching) and a risk cost. The risk cost (CVaR_α(γ_ϕ_t)) is scaled by the current vehicle velocity (v_t) to penalize high-speed traversal of risky terrain. The planning problem dynamically updates Fourier coefficients, concentration parameters, and risk PMF at each timestep.

#### 3.7. Data Collection (for Experiments)

- **Simulator:** High-fidelity simulator BeamNG.tech was used.
- **Risk Variable:** Tire deformation extent was used as a surrogate metric for vehicle damage, calculated as d_w = (r_w - r_inner) / (r_outer - r_inner).
- **Procedure:** A 1x1m patch was selected, and non-overlapping obstacles (max elevation 0.5m) were randomly placed. The vehicle was positioned for single-wheel contact. Deformation of contacting tire nodes was recorded. Samples below a threshold (0.2) were discarded, and the rest were binned into 8-bin histograms to form empirical risk PMFs (y).
- **Dataset Size:** 24,000 samples were collected, augmented with 8 random rotations to 216,000 samples. Random perturbations were added to point clouds for robustness. 90% for training, 10% for testing.

#### 3.8. Baselines

- **AngleInput:** Shares SPARTA's encoder architecture but includes an additional MLP encoder for the angle of approach, fused with point cloud features to predict concentration parameters directly.
- **AngleFree:** Uses the same convolutional backbone but predicts concentration parameters without modeling angle dependency.
- **Elev (Elevation Baseline):** A heuristic-based method that selects paths with the lowest maximum elevation under the vehicle's wheel footprint, inspired by prior work on stepping difficulty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the performance of SPARTA in terms of generalization, inference efficiency, and planning effectiveness in both simulated and hardware environments.

#### 4.1. Model Generalization Performance

- SPARTA (our model) achieved a lower test EMD^2 loss (0.09) compared to the AngleInput baseline (0.15).
- The AngleInput baseline exhibited overfitting to the training data, while SPARTA's test loss continued to decrease, supporting the hypothesis that imposing smoothness via Fourier basis functions aids generalization to unseen angles. This suggests that the geometric prior allows SPARTA to more effectively learn features from the point cloud.

#### 4.2. Inference Efficiency

- SPARTA demonstrated an order-of-magnitude faster inference runtime for querying risk compared to AngleInput.
- Crucially, SPARTA's runtime remained approximately constant even with increasing model complexity (e.g., higher Fourier frequencies), as it only requires a low-dimensional dot product and sigmoid computation during planning queries. In contrast, AngleInput's runtime scaled with model complexity due to repeated full neural network inference.

#### 4.3. Planning in Simulation

Experiments were conducted in two primary simulation environments: a challenging 40m boulder field and a controlled narrow passage with mixed obstacles.
- **Boulder Field Task:**
 - **Success Rate:** SPARTA achieved a 91% success rate (reaching the goal without vehicle damage), outperforming AngleInput (85%), AngleFree (73%), and the Elevation (Elev) baseline (73%).
 - **Velocity:** AngleFree and Elev exhibited higher average vehicle velocities (3.2 m/s) compared to SPARTA (2.6 m/s). This was attributed to their inability to differentiate safe from risky obstacles, leading to a degenerate risk cost that effectively prioritized speed. Reducing their speed limit did not substantially improve their success rates, indicating fundamental limitations in their risk estimation.
- **Narrow Passage Task (Qualitative Analysis):**
 - SPARTA correctly identified risky obstacles and preemptively decelerated, resulting in the lowest maximum tire force (1426 N).
 - AngleFree and Elev failed to differentiate between safe and risky obstacles of similar elevation. They drove at a constant, higher velocity, leading to significantly higher maximum tire forces (2937 N and 4367 N, respectively), indicating a higher probability of vehicle damage. This highlights that AngleFree and Elev would require overly conservative speed limits to guarantee safety, which is empirically difficult to determine.

#### 4.4. Planning on Hardware

- SPARTA was evaluated on a custom-built wheeled robot navigating an environment with a physical obstacle and a virtually placed risky obstacle.
- In 5 trials, SPARTA consistently chose the safe path.
- The Elev baseline, however, chose the risky path in 3 out of 5 trials, which would likely have caused vehicle damage due to sharp obstacle edges.
- This demonstrates SPARTA's ability to generalize to real-world settings and enable safe, smooth traversal.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Ablating CVaR:** Comparing CVaR (α=0.9) to using the mean (α=0) of the predicted distribution.
 - Using CVaR resulted in a higher success rate for SPARTA (95% vs. 89%), emphasizing its importance for safety-critical tasks by incorporating worst-case risk.
- **Ablating Maximum Fourier Frequency (n):**
 - n=1 resulted in a lower success rate (91%) compared to n=3 (95%), suggesting insufficient expressivity.
 - n=5 achieved a similar success rate (94%) as n=3 but might introduce overly abrupt changes due to higher Lipschitz bounds.
 - n=3 was chosen as an optimal balance between expressivity and generalization.

#### 4.6. Generalization to Other Problem Settings

- The framework was shown to be applicable to other risk variables, such as linear traction, which also exhibits angle-dependency.
- An example demonstrated that SPARTA could distinguish an obstacle traversable in the forward direction but risky in reverse, while an angle-agnostic model would be overly conservative.
- The authors suggest the framework can be extended to any variables exhibiting smooth, continuous behaviors dependent on Lie group structures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents SPARTA, a method that addresses key limitations in off-road autonomy by learning smooth state-dependent traversability from dense point clouds. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Robustness:** By explicitly modeling the angle-of-approach dependency of terrain traversability and incorporating Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) for risk-aware planning, SPARTA enables robots to make more informed and safer decisions. This has the potential to significantly reduce vehicle damage and increase mission success rates in challenging, geometrically complex off-road environments.
2. **Improved Generalization and Data Efficiency:** The novel use of Fourier basis functions to represent angle-dependent risk as a smooth, analytical function provides a strong geometric prior. This approach naturally handles periodicity and avoids wrap-around discontinuities, which are common issues when dealing with angular variables. This inherent smoothness contributes to better generalization performance, particularly when training data may be limited or sparse for all possible angles, thereby improving data efficiency.
3. **Increased Computational Efficiency for Planning:** A critical bottleneck in real-time autonomous navigation is the computational cost of repeated model inferences during planning. SPARTA mitigates this by allowing the prediction of Fourier coefficients once per terrain patch, enabling subsequent queries for any angle of approach with minimal computational overhead. This efficiency is crucial for integrating into high-frequency, iterative planners like MPPI.
4. **Leveraging High-Fidelity Data:** By utilizing dense point clouds, SPARTA retains fine geometric details that are often lost in coarser representations like elevation maps. This allows for a more accurate distinction between traversable terrain features and environmental noise, leading to more precise risk assessments.
5. **Broad Applicability and Extensibility:** While demonstrated with tire deformation as a surrogate for vehicle damage, the underlying framework is generalizable. The authors highlight its applicability to other risk-related variables (e.g., linear traction, wheel force, acceleration, rollover risk) and other problems exhibiting smooth, continuous behaviors dependent on Lie group structures. This extensibility suggests SPARTA could serve as a foundational method for various state-dependent problems in robotics.
6. **Advancement in Geometric Deep Learning for Robotics:** The work contributes to the growing field of geometric deep learning by demonstrating how imposing intrinsic geometric structures (like the 1-Sphere and Fourier basis functions) can lead to more effective and efficient learning for robotics problems.
7. **Practical Relevance:** The validation of SPARTA in both high-fidelity simulation and on physical hardware demonstrates its potential for real-world deployment. Given the affiliation with DEVCOM Army Research Laboratory, this research has direct implications for military and defense applications, where autonomous systems operate in unstructured and hazardous off-road terrains.
