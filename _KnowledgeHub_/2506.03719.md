# On the Closed-Form of Flow Matching: Generalization Does Not Arise from Target Stochasticity

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Inria and Mila demonstrate that generalization in flow matching models for high-dimensional data does not arise from target stochasticity, but rather from the network's approximation failures, particularly at early generative time steps. Their Empirical Flow Matching method, which uses a more deterministic target, yields consistent performance improvements on image generation benchmarks.

## Problem

- The discrepancy between the theoretical tendency of flow matching models to memorize training data and their practical ability to generalize.
- Investigating whether the stochastic nature of the training targets in flow matching models is a critical factor for generalization, as previously hypothesized.
- Understanding the fundamental mechanisms underpinning generalization in high-dimensional deep generative models.

## Method

- Utilize the closed-form expression of the ==optimal velocity field== in conditional flow matching to analyze its properties and the network's approximation error.
- Propose "Empirical Flow Matching (EFM)," a Monte Carlo approximation of the optimal velocity field, designed to reduce target stochasticity and serve as a more stable regression target.
- Implement a "hybrid model" approach to experimentally identify specific time intervals during the generative process where generalization capabilities are determined.

## Results

- Target stochasticity was found to be negligible in high-dimensional real datasets, refuting the "generalization through variance" hypothesis.
- Generalization directly correlated with the neural network's failure to perfectly approximate the optimal closed-form velocity field, especially at early time steps.
- The proposed Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) method, using a more deterministic target, demonstrated improved performance with lower FID scores on CIFAR-10, CelebA, and FMNIST image generation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "stochastic target" of vanilla Conditional Flow Matching is effectively non-stochastic in high-dimensional data settings for most of the generative process.
> - Generalization in flow matching models correlates with the neural network's inability to perfectly approximate the theoretically memorizing optimal velocity field.
> - Approximation failures occurring at early time steps (e.g., t < 0.2) are critical for fostering generalization and breaking memorization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper is situated within the rapidly evolving field of deep generative models, specifically focusing on diffusion models and flow matching techniques. These models have achieved remarkable success in synthesizing high-quality, realistic data across diverse modalities like images, audio, and video, often indistinguishable from real content. Despite their empirical success, the fundamental mechanisms underpinning their generalization capabilities remain a significant research puzzle.

A central debate in generative modeling revolves around the balance between "memorization" and "generalization." Previous studies (e.g., Carlini et al. 2023, Somepalli et al. 2023b) have demonstrated that large diffusion models can memorize specific training samples, raising concerns about data privacy and intellectual property. Conversely, other works (e.g., Kadkhodaie et al. 2024) suggest that models generalize in sufficiently large data regimes, pointing towards a "sharp phase transition" between memorization and generalization (Yoon et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2024).

The paper addresses one of the proposed explanations for this puzzling generalization: the role of *inductive biases* inherent in deep learning architectures and, more specifically, the *stochastic nature* of the conditional flow matching (CFM) loss. Some theories, notably from Vastola (2025), have suggested that generalization in diffusion models might arise from this inherent "noisy nature" of the training loss, coined "generalization through variance." This paper directly challenges this hypothesis for flow matching models, seeking to determine if target stochasticity is indeed a key driver of generalization in high-dimensional settings. By leveraging the closed-form expression of the optimal velocity field, the authors aim to provide a more definitive answer to this foundational question, contributing to a deeper theoretical understanding of why these powerful models generalize.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to rigorously investigate whether the noisy or stochastic nature of the training targets in flow matching models is a critical factor for their generalization ability. The authors explicitly state their core research question: "Does training on noisy/stochastic targets improve flow matching generalization? If not, what are the main sources of generalization?"

This objective is motivated by a recognized paradox in flow matching theory: if a model perfectly minimizes the conditional flow matching loss on empirical data, its learned velocity field would converge to a closed-form optimal velocity field (ˆu⋆) that, in turn, would only generate exact copies of the training data. This implies *memorization*, not generalization. Yet, practical flow matching models do generalize effectively. This discrepancy prompts the authors to explore why and how generalization arises in these models despite this theoretical tendency towards memorization.

The paper outlines four specific contributions to achieve this objective:
1. **Challenge the "noisy loss" hypothesis:** Empirically demonstrate that in high-dimensional, realistic data regimes, the stochastic and closed-form versions of the flow matching loss yield nearly equivalent targets, implying that the target is not inherently noisy for most of the generative process.
2. **Identify generalization source:** Propose that generalization emerges precisely when the limited-capacity neural network fails to accurately approximate the optimal closed-form velocity field (ˆu⋆).
3. **Pinpoint critical time intervals:** Identify specific time intervals within the generative process (early and late stages) where the neural network's approximation of ˆu⋆ is particularly poor, and demonstrate that early-time approximation failures are crucial for generalization.
4. **Validate empirically:** Show that directly regressing against the optimal closed-form velocity field (a less stochastic target) does not hinder generalization; in fact, it can sometimes improve performance on standard image datasets.

These objectives collectively aim to redefine the understanding of generalization in flow matching, shifting focus from the stochasticity of the loss to the interplay between model capacity, optimization, and the structure of the underlying optimal velocity fields.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multifaceted empirical and theoretical approach, leveraging the recently discovered closed-form expression of the optimal velocity field in conditional flow matching (CFM).

#### 3.1. Recalls on Conditional Flow Matching and the Closed-Form Formula

The paper begins by recalling the fundamentals of CFM. It describes the goal of finding a velocity field `u(x, t)` that transports a source distribution `p0` (typically `N(0, Id)`) to a target data distribution `pdata` by solving an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). In CFM, this is achieved by defining a conditional probability path `p(·|z, t)` (e.g., `N(tx1, (1-t)²Id)` conditioned on a data sample `x1`). The key insight is Proposition 1, which states that when the true data distribution `pdata` is replaced by an *empirical* distribution `ˆpdata` (a sum of Dirac deltas at training points `x⁽ⁱ⁾`), the optimal velocity field `ˆu⋆(x, t)` has a closed-form solution (Equation 6). This `ˆu⋆` is a weighted average of `n` conditional velocity fields, where the weights are softmax-normalized terms dependent on the distance to each training sample. Crucially, the authors highlight that theoretically, if a neural network `uθ` perfectly minimizes the CFM loss to match `ˆu⋆`, it would only generate training samples, implying memorization.

#### 3.2. Investigating Key Sources of Generalization (Section 3)

- **Target Stochasticity Analysis (Section 3.1, Figure 1):** To challenge the "generalization through variance" hypothesis, the authors measure the cosine similarity between the full `ˆu⋆` and a single conditional target `ucond` (which is what vanilla CFM regresses against) at various time steps `t`. They compare this behavior on a low-dimensional toy dataset (2-moons) and high-dimensional real image datasets (CIFAR-10, Imagenette). High cosine similarity indicates that `ˆu⋆` is effectively non-stochastic, collapsing to a single `ucond`.
- **Failure to Learn `ˆu⋆` (Section 3.2, Figure 2):** They train standard CFM models on varying subsets of CIFAR-10 (from 10 to 10⁴ samples). For each model, they quantify the approximation error `||uθ(xt, t) - ˆu⋆(xt, t)||²` across time `t`. Generalization is assessed using FID-10k (with DINOv2 embeddings) on a test set and a "memorization" metric: the average LPIPS distance from generated samples to their nearest neighbors in the training set. This helps correlate the network's failure to learn `ˆu⋆` with its generalization ability.
- **Temporal Origin of Generalization (Section 3.3, Figure 3):** To determine *when* generalization arises during the generative process, they devise a "hybrid model." This model follows the perfectly memorizing `ˆu⋆` for an initial interval `t ∈ [0, τ]` and then switches to the learned `uθ` for `t ∈ [τ, 1]`. By varying the threshold `τ`, they observe how the "creativity" (measured by LPIPS distance of generated samples to the training data) changes, pinpointing the critical time interval for generalization.

#### 3.3. Learning with the Closed-Form Formula: Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) (Section 4)

- **Addressing Computational Cost:** Directly computing `ˆu⋆` involves summing over all `n` training samples, which is computationally prohibitive (O(n × |B| × d)).
- **Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) Objective (Equation 7):** The authors propose `LEFM`, a Monte Carlo approximation of `ˆu⋆`, denoted `ˆu⋆M` (Equation 8). This estimator uses a minibatch of `M` samples, importantly ensuring that one of these samples (`b⁽¹⁾`) is the `x1` that generated the current `xt`.
- **Theoretical Guarantees (Proposition 2):** They prove that this specific formulation of `ˆu⋆M` is an *unbiased* estimator of `ˆu⋆` and has *lower variance* than the standard CFM target (`ucond`). This makes it a theoretically sound and empirically more stable target for regression.
- **Experiments with EFM (Section 4.2, Figure 4):** They train models using vanilla CFM, Optimal Transport CFM (OTCFM), and their proposed EFM (with `M ∈ {128, 256, 1000}`) on CIFAR-10 and CelebA 64x64. Performance is evaluated using FID on both training and test sets, using both Inception-V3 and DINOv2 embeddings. This directly tests whether reducing target stochasticity (by using `ˆu⋆M`) impacts generalization.

The methodological rigor, combining theoretical insights from the closed-form `ˆu⋆` with well-designed empirical experiments, allows the authors to dissect the complex phenomenon of generalization in flow matching models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several key findings that significantly contribute to the understanding of generalization in flow matching models:

1. **Target Stochasticity is Negligible in High Dimensions (Figure 1):** The experiments demonstrate that for high-dimensional real datasets (CIFAR-10, Imagenette), the optimal velocity field `ˆu⋆` rapidly aligns with a single conditional velocity field `ucond` at very early time steps (`t < 0.2`). This implies that the "stochastic target" of vanilla CFM is effectively non-stochastic for most of the generative process in practical settings. This observation directly challenges the "generalization through variance" hypothesis, suggesting that in high dimensions, the noisy nature of the loss is not a significant factor. In contrast, for low-dimensional toy data, this alignment occurs much later.

2. **Generalization Correlates with Network's Approximation Failure (Figure 2):**
 - For small dataset sizes, the neural network `uθ` can closely approximate `ˆu⋆`. In this regime, models exhibit high FID scores on test data and low nearest-neighbor distances to training data, indicating memorization rather than generalization.
 - As the dataset size increases, the complexity of `ˆu⋆` grows, and the fixed-capacity `uθ` struggles to approximate it accurately. This approximation failure is observed at two critical time intervals: around `t ≈ 0.15` and near `t = 1`.
 - Crucially, better generalization (lower test FID and increased nearest-neighbor distance to training data) directly correlates with the network's inability to perfectly approximate `ˆu⋆`. This implies that generalization does not come from exact learning of the optimal path.

3. **Generalization Primarily Arises at Early Time Steps (Figure 3):** The hybrid model experiments reveal that generalization capacity is predominantly determined during the initial phases of the generative process. If the model follows the memorizing `ˆu⋆` for even a short initial period (e.g., `τ ≥ 0.2` for CIFAR-10, `τ ≥ 0.3` for CelebA), the final generated samples are almost identical to training samples. This suggests that the early-time failure of `uθ` to approximate `ˆu⋆` is critical for breaking memorization and fostering generalization, whereas approximation failures at later time steps (`t → 1`) are less impactful for the final sample diversity.

4. **Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) Improves Performance (Figure 4, Tables 1 & 2):** The introduction of Empirical Flow Matching (EFM), which directly regresses against a *more deterministic* (lower variance) estimate of `ˆu⋆` using a Monte Carlo approximation, consistently yields performance improvements. Increasing the number of samples `M` used in the empirical mean (thereby making the target even *less stochastic*) leads to modest but steady improvements in FID scores on both training and test sets for CIFAR-10, CelebA, and FMNIST. This finding is a direct empirical refutation of the hypothesis that target stochasticity is necessary for generalization; in fact, reducing it enhances performance.

In summary, the results strongly suggest that the widespread belief in target stochasticity as a driver of generalization in flow matching is misplaced, especially for high-dimensional data. Instead, generalization appears to stem from the practical limitations of neural networks in perfectly modeling the theoretically memorizing optimal velocity field, particularly in the early stages of data generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the theoretical understanding and practical development of deep generative models, particularly flow matching.

**Theoretical Significance:**
- **Rebuttal of "Generalization Through Variance":** The most impactful theoretical finding is the strong empirical refutation of the "generalization through variance" hypothesis in the context of high-dimensional flow matching. By demonstrating that the stochastic target loss effectively becomes deterministic very early in the generative process for real data, the paper redirects attention away from the "noise" in the loss as a primary driver of generalization.
- **New Perspective on Generalization:** The work shifts the explanatory focus for generalization from inherent noise to the *finite capacity and inductive biases* of the neural network. Generalization is shown to arise precisely when the network *fails* to perfectly approximate the theoretically memorizing optimal velocity field, especially during the initial stages of the flow. This offers a more nuanced understanding of how practical neural networks avoid overfitting to the empirical data distribution.
- **Temporal Dynamics of Generalization:** Identifying that early time steps are crucial for generalization provides valuable insight into the generative process. This temporal partitioning of generalization suggests that future research could focus on designing architectural biases or training strategies specifically tailored to these critical early-time dynamics.

**Practical Impact:**
- **Improved Training Efficiency and Performance:** The proposed Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) method, which explicitly targets a more deterministic, lower-variance version of the optimal velocity field, demonstrably improves performance (lower FID) without sacrificing generalization. This implies that reducing the variance of the target can be beneficial for training, potentially leading to more stable and faster convergence. EFM offers a concrete algorithmic improvement for practitioners.
- **Guiding Model Design:** By highlighting the importance of early-time dynamics and the network's approximation capacity, the research provides cues for designing more effective flow matching architectures and training objectives. Understanding where and why networks fail to approximate the optimal velocity field can inform strategies to induce desired generalization properties.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge that their work is primarily empirical and focuses on learned models, not precisely characterizing the *learned* velocity field itself or its behavior outside optimal trajectories. They suggest future work could:
- Leverage existing research on the inductive biases of specific architectures to better understand how they interact with the optimal velocity field.
- Investigate the interplay between architectural inductive biases and optimization procedures, a known challenging but active area of research.
- Uncover new properties of the true velocity field for deeper theoretical insights.

**Broader Impact:**
As with all advancements in generative AI, the paper's findings have dual-use implications. While improving training efficiency and understanding of generative models is a positive societal impact, the ability to generate increasingly realistic synthetic content raises concerns regarding misinformation (e.g., deepfakes), data privacy, and potential misuse. By advancing the fundamental understanding of these models, the work contributes to the broader scientific effort to both enhance and responsibly manage generative AI technologies.
