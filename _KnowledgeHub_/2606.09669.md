# SpatialWorld: Benchmarking Interactive Spatial Reasoning of Multimodal Agents in Real-World Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> SpatialWorld establishes a unified, simulator-agnostic benchmark to evaluate the interactive spatial understanding of multimodal agents in real-world tasks, operating under vision-only partial observability and a text-based action interface. Evaluation of 15 state-of-the-art MLLMs revealed limited reliability, with the top model achieving only 17.4% average task success rate, underscoring critical vulnerabilities in dynamic physical environments.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks for MLLM spatial reasoning are either passive, failing to capture dynamic, interactive real-world challenges under partial observability.
- Current embodied AI benchmarks are often simulator-specific, providing privileged information or low-level interfaces that hinder assessment of general interactive spatial reasoning.
- A unified evaluation framework was needed to assess MLLMs' high-level spatial understanding using only egocentric vision and language-based actions across diverse 3D environments.

## Method

- Developed SPATIALWORLD, a benchmark operating under vision-only ==partial observability== with only egocentric RGB input, mimicking human perception.
- Implemented a unified, high-level, text-based action interface, allowing MLLMs to express decisions directly in language across diverse environments.
- Integrated eight heterogeneous simulation backends (e.g., AI2-THOR, CARLA, Block3D) into a simulator-agnostic protocol for consistent evaluation of interactive spatial reasoning.

## Results

- State-of-the-art MLLMs achieved an average Task Success Rate (TSR) of only **17.4%** (GPT-5) across the benchmark, and **14.4%** for physical tasks, showing limited reliability.
- Models with higher TSR did not consistently exhibit higher Step Efficiency (SE), suggesting successful agents often achieve goals through extensive exploration rather than efficient planning.
- Performance leadership varied by domain: GPT-5 excelled in household/travel, Qwen-3.5-**397B**-A17B in physical entertainment, and Gemini-3.1-Pro in digital games.
- Hybrid navigation-interaction tasks proved most challenging with a mean TSR of **4.2%**, significantly lower than pure interaction tasks (**50.2%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current MLLMs demonstrate limited interactive spatial reasoning, achieving low task success rates and often relying on inefficient trial-and-error strategies.
> - The performance of MLLMs is highly domain-specific, with no single model universally superior across household, travel, digital games, or other environments.
> - Critical vulnerabilities exist in handling multi-step reasoning, active exploration, long-horizon planning, and robust action execution, especially in hybrid navigation-interaction tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Spatial reasoning is a foundational capability for multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to effectively interact with and understand the physical world. Existing methods for evaluating spatial reasoning in MLLMs primarily fall into two categories: passive evaluation and simulator-specific embodied benchmarks.

Passive evaluation paradigms, such as static Visual Question Answering (VQA) on images or understanding pre-recorded videos, assess models' basic comprehension of spatial relations, object layouts, and scene structures. However, these methods do not capture the interactive and dynamic nature of real-world spatial understanding. Physical environments are partially observable, requiring agents to actively navigate, gather visual evidence, update their spatial beliefs, and plan actions sequentially.

Embodied benchmarks address the need for interactivity by providing testbeds for navigation, manipulation, and task execution. Nevertheless, many of these benchmarks are designed with simulator-specific embodiments, sensor assumptions (e.g., providing depth maps or global maps in addition to visual input), action interfaces, or execution pipelines. This specificity makes it challenging to determine whether an agent's success reflects general interactive spatial reasoning abilities or merely its adaptation to the particular characteristics of a single simulator or its associated action space. Consequently, there is a recognized need for an evaluation framework that can rigorously assess the general interactive spatial reasoning capabilities of off-the-shelf MLLMs without requiring task- or simulator-specific training. This necessitates an evaluation regime with several key properties: agents operating under vision-only partial observability, utilizing a text-based action interface native to MLLMs for high-level decisions, and adhering to a simulator-agnostic protocol across heterogeneous 3D environments. SPATIALWORLD aims to fill this identified gap by introducing such a unified and rigorous benchmark.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish SPATIALWORLD, a unified benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the interactive spatial understanding of multimodal agents within complex real-world tasks. The motivation for developing SPATIALWORLD stems from several limitations observed in previous spatial reasoning benchmarks for MLLMs:

1. **Transitioning from Passive to Active Evaluation:** Current benchmarks predominantly rely on passive evaluation methods, such as static VQA or understanding pre-recorded videos. While these tasks assess fundamental spatial comprehension, they do not adequately capture the dynamic and interactive requirements of physical environments, where agents must actively explore to gather information and update their understanding. SPATIALWORLD aims to move beyond this passive paradigm to assess dynamic exploration and interactive task completion.
2. **Addressing Simulator-Specific Limitations:** Many existing embodied benchmarks are tailored to specific simulators, featuring unique embodiments, sensor inputs, action interfaces, or execution pipelines. This specificity makes it difficult to ascertain if an agent's performance reflects its general interactive spatial reasoning capabilities or its optimization for a particular simulator's characteristics. A key motivation is to decouple this dependency, providing a simulator-agnostic protocol that allows for fair and generalizable evaluation of MLLMs.
3. **Ensuring Realistic Perceptual Constraints:** The authors aim for agents to operate under vision-only partial observability, meaning they should perceive the environment solely through egocentric visual observations, akin to human perception, without privileged information such as depth maps, global object coordinates, or semantic metadata. This constraint ensures a genuine evaluation of how MLLMs integrate visual perception with spatial reasoning.
4. **Leveraging MLLM-Native Interfaces:** To enable direct evaluation of off-the-shelf MLLMs and facilitate interpretable reasoning, the benchmark is designed with a unified, text-based action interface. This allows MLLMs to express high-level navigation, viewpoint adjustments, object interactions, and task control decisions using natural language, consistent with their inherent capabilities for explicit chain-of-thought reasoning.
5. **Facilitating Comprehensive Diagnostics:** By integrating eight heterogeneous simulation backends under a shared, simulator-agnostic protocol, SPATIALWORLD intends to provide a diverse range of challenges. This design enables the diagnosis of failures across complementary forms of 3D reasoning rather than reducing an agent's performance to a single score tied to a specific simulator.
6. **Guaranteeing Evaluation Reliability:** To ensure reliable and reproducible evaluation, each task in SPATIALWORLD is paired with a human-validated initial state, a reference trajectory, and a task-specific terminal-state verifier. These components allow for objective measurement of task success and efficiency, ensuring that success is achieved through efficient and interpretable action sequences.

In summary, the core motivation is to provide a rigorous, unified, and comprehensive testbed that pushes MLLMs beyond static scene recognition to develop robust active exploration and decision-making capabilities in interactive 3D environments, mirroring the complexities of real-world tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPATIALWORLD is designed as a unified benchmark to evaluate interactive spatial understanding through a modular architecture and a comprehensive data construction pipeline.

**Task Formulation:**
Each task is formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), defined by `⟨S, O, A, T, Ω, R⟩`. At each step `t`, the agent receives a natural-language goal `g` and a raw egocentric RGB observation `o_t`. Crucially, no privileged state signals (e.g., depth or global maps) are provided. An MLLM-based policy `π_θ` predicts the next high-level action `a_t` based on the trajectory history `H_t = (o_1, a_1..., o_t)` and goal `g`. The simulator then executes `a_t`, transitions the hidden environment state `s_{t+1}`, and renders the next visual observation `o_{t+1}`. This closed-loop interaction continues until the agent executes `EndTask` or exhausts a predefined step budget.

**Benchmark Protocol Design Principles:**
1. **Pure Egocentric Vision:** Agents receive only first-person, vision-only observations, ensuring that performance genuinely reflects the coupling between visual perception and spatial reasoning.
2. **Cross-Platform Unification:** Simulator-specific complexities are abstracted into a unified observation-action interface, allowing direct evaluation of general MLLMs across distinct domains while preserving native physical challenges.
3. **Factored Complexity:** The benchmark includes both photorealistic embodied setups and abstract 3D games. This factorization aims to isolate specific bottlenecks in a model's spatial cognition by decoupling visual semantics from pure geometric reasoning.
4. **Execution-Based Verification:** Task success is objectively verified based on terminal environment states, rather than strict adherence to predefined action trajectories. This approach accommodates diverse reasoning paths and open-ended exploration.

**SpatialWorld Architecture:**
The benchmark employs a modular architecture with five standardized components:
- **Environment and Verification Interfaces:** Manage task initialization and deterministic success checking.
- **Agent Module:** The central MLLM-based reasoning engine.
- **Observation Interface:** Provides standardized egocentric RGB screenshots at the simulator's native resolution. No auxiliary modalities (e.g., depth, semantic segmentation, global occupancy maps) are provided.
- **Action Interface:** Exposes a unified high-level, text-based action space, abstracting heterogeneous simulator backends. This space is categorized into:
 - **Navigation:** `Move` (forward, backward, left, right, up, down, with various granularities or numeric distances).
 - **Viewpoint & Posture:** `Rotate`, `Tilt` (with direction and angle), `ChangePosture` (crouch, stand).
 - **Interaction:** `Pick/Place`, `ChangeState` (open, close, on, off, clean, dirty, sliced, cooked, filled, empty, used_up), `Manipulate` (push, pull, throw, touch, look_at, drink).
 - **Task-Control & Coordination:** `EndTask` (DONE, FAIL), `Communicate` (free-form message, for multi-agent tasks).
 The Action Interface translates these unified primitives into simulator-specific execution calls.

**Environment Suite:**
SPATIALWORLD integrates eight diverse 3D backends, grouped into three families:
1. **Indoor Simulation:** AI2-THOR, ProcTHOR, VirtualHome. These environments provide explicit physical affordances for fine-grained object grounding, temporally ordered routines, and multi-agent coordination (via multi-agent variants).
2. **Outdoor Navigation:** CARLA, EmbodiedCity. These extend to macroscopic scales, evaluating long-range route planning and progress estimation in dynamic urban and aerial topologies.
3. **Digital Game Environments:** Block3D, Maze, Maze3D, Rubik’s Cube, Snake3D. These lightweight environments isolate abstract spatial logic and topological reasoning by stripping away photorealistic semantics, focusing on geometric counting, maze planning, state tracking, and spatial transformation.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Task Success Rate (TSR):** The primary metric, calculated as the fraction of tasks where the terminal goal is fully satisfied as determined by a custom verifier `V_i`.
- **Step Efficiency (SE):** For successful tasks, SE measures the ratio of human-annotated reference steps (`n^*_i`) to the agent's actual step count (`n_i`). This distinguishes efficient agents from those relying on extensive trial-and-error.

**Benchmark Construction:**
The benchmark comprises 760 human-annotated tasks, built through a rigorous three-stage pipeline:
1. **Task Design:** Annotators define natural-language instructions and configure the initial environment state.
2. **Human Execution:** Trained annotators solve each task in the simulator, recording the ground-truth terminal state and a reference action sequence.
3. **Verification:** Separate expert reviewers cross-check task feasibility, instruction clarity, and the correctness of evaluation scripts. All verifier logic and success conditions are validated through inter-annotator cross-checking.

**Task Taxonomy:**
Tasks are categorized along two dimensions:
- **Scenario Categories:** Household routines, work/study, entertainment, travel, social collaboration, and digital spatial games.
- **Complexity Levels:**
 - **Navigation:** Requires exploration to reach a target location or object without state manipulation.
 - **Interaction:** Involves object-level state changes (e.g., picking, opening) without extensive exploration.
 - **Hybrid:** Combines long-horizon navigation with multi-step manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 15 advanced MLLMs on SPATIALWORLD yielded several findings regarding the current state of interactive spatial reasoning capabilities.

1. **Significant Performance Gap:** Current MLLM agents demonstrate limited capabilities in solving 3D physical tasks. The strongest model, GPT-5, achieved an average Task Success Rate (TSR) of 17.4% across the full benchmark, with a 14.4% Physical Overall TSR. The leading open-source model, Qwen-3.5-397B-A17B, reached 14.1% average TSR and 12.2% Physical Overall TSR. These results indicate a substantial gap between current MLLM performance and robust human-level spatial intelligence, with successes often restricted to short-horizon operations. Performance in digital domains was moderately better but still reflected low overall success rates.

2. **Mismatch Between Success and Efficiency:** Analysis of Step Efficiency (SE) revealed that models with comparable TSRs often exhibited varying efficiencies. For instance, Kimi-K2.5 and GPT-5.4 had similar Physical Overall TSRs (9.2% vs. 6.6%), but GPT-5.4 achieved a higher SE (0.745 vs. 0.584). This suggests that some models achieve success through more extensive trial-and-error or redundant actions, indicating a lack of efficient planning.

3. **Domain-Specific Performance Variation:** No single model consistently dominated across all task categories. GPT-5 demonstrated superior performance in daily household, travel, and social collaboration tasks. Qwen-3.5-397B-A17B performed comparably to GPT-5 in Work & Study tasks and led in physical entertainment. Gemini-3.1-Pro achieved the highest scores in digital games. This variation underscores that different MLLMs possess distinct strengths across diverse spatial reasoning challenges, validating SPATIALWORLD's multi-faceted design for exposing varied bottlenecks.

**Detailed Analysis across Sub-categories:**

- **Indoor vs. Outdoor Physical Environments:** GPT-5 (14.1%) and Qwen-3.5-397B-A17B (13.7%) led in indoor tasks, suggesting proficiency in fine-grained object grounding and low-level control. In contrast, Gemini-3-Flash (9.0%) and GPT-5 (8.3%) performed best in outdoor navigation, indicating strengths in long-horizon spatial reasoning and macro-level route planning.
- **Complexity Modes:** Tasks combining navigation and interaction (hybrid tasks) proved to be the most challenging, with a mean TSR of 4.2%. Interaction-only tasks had a higher mean TSR of 50.2%. Performance varied across these modes, with Gemini-series models often leading navigation tasks (8.6%) and GPT-5 dominating interaction (69.4%) and hybrid modes (12.1%).
- **Multi-Agent Social Environments:** GPT-5 achieved the highest pooled social TSR (34.8%), primarily driven by performance in Multi-AI2THOR environments (object-centric cooperative routines). Multi-ProcTHOR, involving procedural multi-agent layouts, remained significantly more challenging, with top models reaching only 5.9%. This indicates difficulties in shared progress tracking and role assignment in less structured multi-agent scenarios.
- **Game-Level Breakdown:** Models showed strong reactive control in navigation and Snake tasks. However, they consistently struggled with games requiring explicit geometric reasoning and multi-step state transformations (e.g., Rubik's Cube, Block3D), highlighting spatial manipulation as a fundamental bottleneck.
- **Perceptual Factors (Ablations):** Changes in image resolution had a minimal and non-monotonic effect on TSR, suggesting that resolution does not materially impair interactive spatial understanding. Higher fields of view (FOV) generally improved performance, with gains plateauing beyond a certain point.
- **Inference-time Hyperparameters (Ablations):** Temperature sensitivity was marginal, with most models performing optimally at `τ = 1.0`. The optimal history window size was found to be `w = 30`, with diminishing returns beyond this. No universal preference was observed for continuous versus discrete action parameterization, indicating model-dependent optimal choices.
- **GPT-5 vs. GPT-5.4 Case Study:** GPT-5 exhibited higher shared-task TSR, particularly in VirtualHome, AI2THOR, and CARLA. Analysis revealed that GPT-5.4 often failed due to premature termination, explicitly stopping before task completion. GPT-5, conversely, tended to persist longer, sometimes reaching the step limit, indicating a slower but more persistent search strategy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SPATIALWORLD represents an advancement in the evaluation of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) by shifting the focus from passive observation to interactive spatial reasoning within complex, dynamic 3D environments. The benchmark's significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Paradigm Shift in MLLM Evaluation:** By introducing a unified, simulator-agnostic protocol that requires vision-only partial observability and a text-based action interface, SPATIALWORLD facilitates a move toward evaluating MLLMs as general-purpose spatial agents. This enables a more realistic assessment of their capacity for active exploration and decision-making in environments that mimic real-world interactions.

2. **Rigorous Diagnostic Capabilities:** The benchmark's design, which integrates diverse environments and features detailed task taxonomies and performance metrics (TSR and SE), allows for a granular diagnosis of MLLM capabilities. It exposes distinct bottlenecks in active exploration, long-horizon planning, execution efficiency, geometric reasoning, and multi-agent coordination. This detailed insight is crucial for identifying specific areas for improvement in future MLLM architectures and training methodologies.

3. **Foundation for Future Research:** The challenging nature of SPATIALWORLD, as evidenced by the low success rates of leading MLLMs, positions it as a foundational testbed. It is expected to stimulate research aimed at developing more robust spatial reasoning abilities, improved planning algorithms, and more efficient interaction strategies for MLLMs. The benchmark can guide the development of models that are better equipped to handle the complexities and uncertainties of physical spaces.

4. **Promoting Generalizability and Reproducibility:** The unified interface across heterogeneous simulators, coupled with human-validated initial states, reference trajectories, and terminal-state verifiers, ensures greater generalizability and reproducibility of evaluation results. This standardization helps mitigate the issue of simulator-specific biases, fostering a more transparent and comparable research landscape.

5. **Potential for Real-World Applications:** Improvements in interactive spatial reasoning driven by benchmarks like SPATIALWORLD could have a significant impact on various real-world applications. These include more capable embodied AI for household robots, advanced autonomous driving systems, intelligent navigation for delivery services, and sophisticated virtual assistants capable of complex environmental interactions.

6. **Ethical Considerations:** As MLLMs gain more sophisticated spatial reasoning and interactive capabilities, the potential for autonomous systems to perform unintended physical-world manipulations or engage in surveillance increases. The development of robust evaluation tools like SPATIALWORLD, which systematically characterize agent failure modes, also contributes to building more reliable and trustworthy systems, while implicitly highlighting the need for parallel development of safety guidelines and ethical frameworks.

In conclusion, SPATIALWORLD provides a critical step forward in assessing and advancing the interactive spatial intelligence of MLLMs, pushing the research community towards developing models capable of seamless and intelligent interaction within the physical world.
