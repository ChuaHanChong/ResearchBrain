# Architecture Is All You Need: Diversity-Enabled Sweet Spots for Robust Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Caltech MCE introduces a minimal Layered Control Architecture (LCA) for humanoid robots, demonstrating that separating control responsibilities by timescale is crucial for robust locomotion over varied terrain. The approach, leveraging a two-stage training curriculum, achieved approximately three times lower contacts per step on stair tasks and a 10 percentage point higher success rate on uneven terrain in simulation compared to monolithic designs, with strong validation on a physical Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Achieving robust humanoid locomotion across mixed and unstructured terrain is challenging, requiring the integration of rapid stabilization with slower, higher-level perception and decision-making.
- Traditional 'end-to-end' or monolithic control policies often struggle with robustness in high-dimensional, perception-conditioned problems due to the entanglement of different processing timescales and information requirements.
- A quantitative understanding of whether architectural design, rather than just increased network complexity or model size, is the primary enabler of robustness in such systems was lacking.

## Method

- Developed a minimal ==Layered Control Architecture (LCA)== composed of a fast, proprioceptive low-level stabilizer and a slower, exteroceptive navigation policy that conditions the lower layer based on local heightmap information.
- Implemented a two-stage training curriculum: first, a 'blind' stabilizer learns proprioceptive control, followed by fine-tuning with perception-critical inputs for terrain adaptation.
- Utilized compact network components (small CNN/MLP for perception encoder, MLP/LSTM for actor) and a simple perception pipeline to isolate the benefits of architectural separation from network complexity.

## Results

- In simulation, two-stage policies demonstrated approximately three times lower contacts per step on out-of-distribution stair tasks and outperformed one-stage policies by about 10 percentage points in success rate on uneven terrain.
- Hardware experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot showed two-stage policies robustly handled both navigation-dominant (stair ascent/descent) and control-dominant (hinged/soft ledge) tasks, whereas one-stage monolithic policies frequently failed.
- Ablation studies confirmed that removing the layered architectural structure led to large performance degradations, while specific choices of network components (e.g., CNN versus MLP for perception encoder) had comparatively minor impacts on overall robust performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robustness in humanoid locomotion fundamentally arises from a layered, multi-rate architecture, akin to guidance-navigation-control systems, where fast stabilization and slower, long-horizon decision-making operate distinctly.
> - The concept of 'Diversity-Enabled Sweet Spots' (DeSS) is quantitatively validated, showing that the synergistic combination of specialized, diverse control layers yields superior robustness compared to any single monolithic component.
> - A minimalist design of the control architecture, focusing on the separation of timescales and information processing, is sufficient for achieving robust perception-conditioned locomotion without relying on overly complex models or learned world representations.
