# Architecture Is All You Need: Diversity-Enabled Sweet Spots for Robust Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Caltech MCE introduces a minimal Layered Control Architecture (LCA) for humanoid robots, demonstrating that separating control responsibilities by timescale is crucial for robust locomotion over varied terrain. The approach, leveraging a two-stage training curriculum, achieved approximately three times lower contacts per step on stair tasks and a 10 percentage point higher success rate on uneven terrain in simulation compared to monolithic designs, with strong validation on a physical Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Achieving robust humanoid locomotion across mixed and unstructured terrain is challenging, requiring the integration of rapid stabilization with slower, higher-level perception and decision-making.
- Traditional 'end-to-end' or monolithic control policies often struggle with robustness in high-dimensional, perception-conditioned problems due to the entanglement of different processing timescales and information requirements.
- A quantitative understanding of whether architectural design, rather than just increased network complexity or model size, is the primary enabler of robustness in such systems was lacking.

## Method

- Developed a minimal ==Layered Control Architecture (LCA)== composed of a fast, proprioceptive low-level stabilizer and a slower, exteroceptive navigation policy that conditions the lower layer based on local heightmap information.
- Implemented a two-stage training curriculum: first, a 'blind' stabilizer learns proprioceptive control, followed by fine-tuning with perception-critical inputs for terrain adaptation.
- Utilized compact network components (small CNN/MLP for perception encoder, MLP/LSTM for actor) and a simple perception pipeline to isolate the benefits of architectural separation from network complexity.

## Results

- In simulation, two-stage policies demonstrated approximately three times lower contacts per step on out-of-distribution stair tasks and outperformed one-stage policies by about 10 percentage points in success rate on uneven terrain.
- Hardware experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot showed two-stage policies robustly handled both navigation-dominant (stair ascent/descent) and control-dominant (hinged/soft ledge) tasks, whereas one-stage monolithic policies frequently failed.
- Ablation studies confirmed that removing the layered architectural structure led to large performance degradations, while specific choices of network components (e.g., CNN versus MLP for perception encoder) had comparatively minor impacts on overall robust performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robustness in humanoid locomotion fundamentally arises from a layered, multi-rate architecture, akin to guidance-navigation-control systems, where fast stabilization and slower, long-horizon decision-making operate distinctly.
> - The concept of 'Diversity-Enabled Sweet Spots' (DeSS) is quantitatively validated, showing that the synergistic combination of specialized, diverse control layers yields superior robustness compared to any single monolithic component.
> - A minimalist design of the control architecture, focusing on the separation of timescales and information processing, is sufficient for achieving robust perception-conditioned locomotion without relying on overly complex models or learned world representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robust humanoid locomotion over mixed and unstructured terrain remains a grand challenge in robotics, despite decades of research. The core difficulty lies in reconciling the need for rapid, low-level stabilization to handle dynamic contact events and disturbances with slower, higher-level perceptual decision-making for long-horizon navigation.

Historically, guidance–navigation–control (GNC) systems, particularly in aerospace, have demonstrated the efficacy of layered, multi-rate designs: a slow guidance layer, an intermediate trajectory generation layer, and a fast feedback control layer. This "slow and flexible" above "fast and rigid" paradigm, with well-defined interfaces, has been a cornerstone of robust control engineering. The paper explicitly draws this analogy, arguing that the same principles are critical for humanoid locomotion.

In the context of modern robotics, particularly with the advent of deep reinforcement learning (RL), there has been a trend towards "end-to-end" or "monolithic" control policies. These policies attempt to learn direct mappings from raw sensor inputs (e.g., visual depth maps, proprioception) to control actions (e.g., joint torques or positions) without explicit architectural separation. While powerful in some domains, the paper contends that these monolithic designs often struggle with robustness in high-dimensional, perception-conditioned control problems due to the entanglement of different timescales and information processing needs.

Other related works include:
- **Two-stage training pipelines:** Several studies use two-stage curricula, either prioritizing contact selection before stabilization or, more commonly, learning a robust proprioceptive stabilizer first, then conditioning it on exteroceptive cues. This paper falls into the latter category, emphasizing a "blind-then-vision" approach, but crucially highlights its implementation within a *minimal* layered architecture.
- **Perception encoders:** It is standard practice to encode visual depth or heightmaps into a latent representation before fusing them with proprioception. This preserves rate separation and prevents slow, noisy exteroception from destabilizing fast feedback. The present work adopts this pattern but keeps the encoder intentionally compact and local.
- **Model-based stepping and hybrid stacks:** Classical approaches combine footstep planners (often mixed-integer optimization) with RL for low-level tracking. These inherently layered systems provide a precedent for the efficacy of architectural decomposition.
- **Teacher-student and distillation methods:** These techniques use privileged information to train a capable "teacher" and then distill its knowledge into a simpler "student" policy [28–33]. The paper acknowledges these as complementary but notes that their architectural equivalence to explicit layering remains an open question.

The key gap this work addresses is demonstrating that *architectural separation of timescales*, rather than network scale or complexity, is the *primary driver of robustness* for perception-conditioned humanoid locomotion. It challenges the prevailing notion that increasingly sophisticated models, learned world representations, or intricate reward shaping are necessary for task success, arguing instead that a *well-posed layered architecture* is sufficient and more robust. This work aims to quantitatively validate the "diversity-enabled sweet spots" (DeSS) hypothesis in the context of robotic control, asserting that the combined stack can outperform any single monolithic component tuned in isolation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper's core motivation stems from the fundamental insight derived from decades of GNC and biological sensorimotor systems: robustness emerges when fast, low-level stabilization mechanisms are paired with slower, longer-horizon navigation and decision-making. The authors crystallize this into two central claims:

1. **Necessity of Layered, Multi-Rate Design:** Robust locomotion inherently requires a layered, multi-rate architecture. This involves a reflexive controller that stabilizes the robot using proprioceptive feedback at a high rate, complemented by a navigation layer that updates more slowly using exteroceptive cues (like terrain perception) to set short-horizon trajectories or conditions for the lower layer.
2. **Minimalist Approach to Robustness:** A minimal instantiation of this Layered Control Architecture (LCA) can achieve complex, robust locomotion tasks without relying on "heavy machinery"—meaning, without complex environment estimators, mixed-integer footstep search, elaborate world models, or overly complex network architectures.

The overarching objective is to empirically validate that the *architectural separation of timescales and information processing*, rather than the scale or complexity of the neural networks or the richness of learned representations, is the critical enabler for robust perception-conditioned locomotion. The authors propose that the "performance sweet spot" arises from different parts of the control architecture handling information at different rates and with different information budgets, allowing each layer to specialize and contribute to overall robustness. This is directly linked to the concept of "diversity-enabled sweet spots" (DeSS), where the combination of diverse, specialized components yields superior performance.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide strong evidence that a thoughtfully designed control *architecture* provides a more fundamental pathway to robust robotic performance than simply increasing model size or computational intensity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on instantiating and testing a **minimal Layered Control Architecture (LCA)** for humanoid locomotion, coupled with a specific two-stage training curriculum.

**A. Layered Control Architecture (LCA):**
The proposed LCA consists of two primary layers:
- **Fast Low-Level Stabilizer:** This layer operates at a high rate, primarily using proprioceptive information (joint positions, velocities, base angular velocity, gravity direction, last action). Its objective is fast, reactive stabilization, implemented with largely standard locomotion reinforcement learning (RL) rewards focused on joint-space tracking and maintaining balance.
- **Slow Navigation Policy:** This layer processes exteroceptive information (a compact local heightmap) at a moderate rate. It conditions the low-level stabilizer, providing longer-horizon terrain geometry information that allows the robot to adapt its gait or foot placement strategies to the perceived environment. The interface between these layers is intentionally narrow, with references (e.g., planned conditioning) descending to the stabilizer and tracking errors or status ascending.

**B. Optimization Analysis:**
The paper provides a theoretical justification for the two-stage training from an optimization perspective. It argues that for highly nonconcave reward landscapes typical of RL, solving the problem in sequence (first optimizing fast parameters, then both) helps avoid poor local maxima by providing a good initial condition. The first stage, optimizing only the fast-rate controller (proprioceptive stabilizer) without perception, simplifies the problem, making it more likely to find a satisfactory local optimum for stabilization. The second stage then fine-tunes both fast and slow parameters, leveraging this robust starting point.

**C. Observations and Normalization:**
- **Actor Observation ($o_{\pi_k}$):** Concatenation of a K-length history of robot state (joint positions/velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity direction), current planar velocity command, last applied action, and a noisy, sparse 11x11 heightmap ($H_{\pi}$) covering 1.0m x 1.0m around the robot. The heightmap provides local temporal information due to its coverage relative to step period.
- **Critic Observation ($o_{V_k}$):** Similar to the actor's, but includes world-frame body velocities and a larger, more accurate 1.5m x 1.5m heightmap ($H_V$) with zero noise. This privileged information aids the critic in estimating the advantage function more accurately.
- **Normalization:** Both heightmaps are normalized by subtracting the grid mean and clipping to [-1, 1] to remove steady-state offsets and simplify MLP biases.

**D. Network Architecture:**
To demonstrate architectural benefits independently of network complexity, the authors explore multiple choices for the components:
- **Perception Encoder:** Either a small Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) or a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), mapping the heightmap ($H$) to a latent embedding ($z_H$).
- **Primary Actor Network:** Either an LSTM (for sequential data processing) or an MLP, taking a concatenation of proprioception and the latent perception embedding to output joint position setpoints.
- **Critic Network:** An MLP with hidden layers {512, 256, 128}, receiving privileged inputs.

**E. Rewards:**
A comprehensive set of standard locomotion rewards is used consistently across all policies to isolate the effects of architecture. Key components include:
- Phase-contact consistency: Encourages stable foot contact in appropriate gait phases.
- Foot-strike cost: Penalizes lateral ground-reaction forces (scuffs).
- Feet sliding cost: Suppresses slip during stance.
- Feet orientation cost: Encourages flat foot placement.
- Feet clearance cost: Penalizes deviation from target swing height.
These are combined with standard locomotion rewards (e.g., linear/angular velocity tracking, torque/acceleration minimization, joint deviation, height regulation).

**F. Two-Stage Training Curriculum:**
1. **Stage 1 (Blind Stabilization):** The actor's heightmap input ($H$) is set to zero (H ≡ 0). The robot learns to stabilize and traverse challenging terrains (stairs, uneven ground, flat) purely from proprioception. This trains the fast, low-level stabilizer.
2. **Stage 2 (Perception-Critical):** The heightmap input ($H$) is re-enabled for the actor. The previously trained "blind" policy is fine-tuned, allowing it to condition its actions on local terrain perception, thereby learning longer-horizon, terrain-adaptive behaviors.

**G. Perception Filtering (Deployment):**
For hardware deployment, a simple and robust perception pipeline is used:
1. **Downsampling:** Raw depth pointclouds from two Realsense D435 cameras are aggregated into an 11x11 grid (1.0m x 1.0m) by taking the *minimum* valid point height in each cell. This helps with stair occlusions.
2. **Outlier Rejection:** Grid heights are filtered by clamping values far from the mean ($\pm \gamma \sigma_z$), removing noise from robot's own legs or small anomalies.
3. **Zero-mean and Quantize:** The grid is zero-centered (subtracting mean height), clipped to [-1, 1], and quantized to 5cm steps, matching simulation perturbations.

**H. Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulation:** Trained in IsaacSim on an RTX 4090 with 4096 parallel environments using the asymmetric actor-critic algorithm. Policies include a blind baseline, one-stage, and two-stage variants with MLP, LSTM, and CNN components. Evaluated on medium and hard difficulty stair and uneven terrain tasks using success rate, contacts per step, and tracking error.
- **Hardware:** Deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Perception (Jetson Orin NX) and RL controller (Framework Laptop) run on-board or off-board. Two Intel Realsense D435 cameras provide depth. Evaluated on four tasks: stair ascent, stair descent (navigation-dominant), hinged ledge, and soft ledge (control-dominant) using success rate and net lateral drift (precision).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted in both simulation and on hardware, consistently demonstrate the superiority of the layered control architecture, particularly when coupled with the two-stage training curriculum, over monolithic, one-stage alternatives.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
- **Training Rewards:** All policies achieved largely identical cumulative training rewards, indicating that in-distribution performance alone might not reveal architectural advantages.
- **Medium Difficulty (In-Distribution):** All policies performed similarly well, with near-parity across metrics. This suggests that for simpler, in-distribution tasks, even monolithic policies can learn adequate performance.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Effects:** The true benefits of the LCA emerged in harder, OOD scenarios:
 - **Stairs (OOD):** Two-stage policies exhibited approximately **3 times lower contacts per step** compared to their one-stage counterparts. This indicates more stable and precise foot placement. The two-stage policies' performance was closer to the blind baseline in this metric, suggesting they could effectively fall back on a robust proprioceptive stabilizer when perception was noisy or less critical for stabilization.
 - **Uneven Terrain (OOD):** Differences were even more pronounced. Two-stage policies consistently **outperformed one-stage policies by approximately 10 percentage points in success rate**, with corresponding reductions in contacts per step. This highlights the architectural advantage in unstructured environments that require both fast stabilization and longer-horizon planning, capabilities explicitly separated and co-trained in the two-stage pipeline but entangled in monolithic models.
- **Architectural Ablations:** The simulation results showed that while specific network choices (MLP, LSTM, CNN for encoder/actor) produced only small performance differences, *removing the layered structure (i.e., using one-stage training) caused large drops in success and tracking metrics*. This strongly supports the central claim that architecture, not merely network complexity, is key.

**B. Hardware Experiments:**
The hardware experiments on the Unitree G1 humanoid provided crucial real-world validation:
- **One-Stage MLP Underperformance:** The one-stage MLP policy consistently underperformed, often failing outright (e.g., 0/5 success on hinged and soft ledges). This suggests that in real-world scenarios with inherent noise and complexity, monolithic policies become overly reliant on noisy heightfields for low-level control, leading to rapid degradation of stabilization.
- **Blind Policy Strengths and Weaknesses:** The blind policy transferred reasonably well to hardware and was particularly strong on control-dominant tasks like the hinged and soft ledges, where stabilization under transient impacts is paramount. However, it failed more frequently on navigation-dominant tasks (stair ascent/descent) due to its lack of foresight, resulting in missed steps or edge strikes. This confirms its robust stabilization capabilities but highlights its limitation in structured navigation.
- **Two-Stage Policies' Robustness:** The two-stage MLP and two-stage CNN+MLP policies performed similarly and robustly on *both* navigation-dominant (stair) and control-dominant (ledge) tasks. They successfully navigated stairs requiring precise foot placement and also maintained stability over compliant, dynamic ledges. This is the strongest empirical validation of the paper's central hypothesis.
- **Network Choice Secondary:** The fact that both two-stage MLP and CNN+MLP policies performed robustly and similarly implies that once the layered, multi-rate architecture is in place, the specific choice of perception encoder (CNN vs. MLP) or actor backbone (MLP vs. LSTM) becomes of *lesser importance* for achieving robust baseline performance. While further tuning might yield marginal gains, it's not the primary enabler of robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the field of humanoid locomotion and robotic control more broadly:

1. **Empirical Validation of Layered Control Architectures:** The paper provides compelling empirical evidence, both in simulation and on real hardware, for the necessity and effectiveness of layered, multi-rate control designs for robust humanoid locomotion in unstructured environments. It demonstrates that conceptual principles from classic GNC are highly relevant and effective when applied to modern RL-based robotic systems.
2. **Challenging the "More is Better" Paradigm:** In an era where increasing model complexity and network scale often dominate research, this work offers a refreshing counter-narrative. It explicitly shows that a *minimal instantiation* of a well-posed architecture can achieve robust performance on complex tasks, implicitly arguing that computational resources and design effort might be better spent on architectural design rather than brute-force model scaling.
3. **Demonstrating "Diversity-Enabled Sweet Spots" (DeSS):** The results quantitatively support the "diversity-enabled sweet spots" concept. By separating control concerns into distinct layers that operate at different timescales and process information with different budgets, the combined system achieves a level of robustness and adaptability that individual monolithic components cannot. Each layer specializes where it is most effective, and their well-defined interaction yields synergistic benefits.
4. **Practical Implications for Robot Design:** The findings offer a clear blueprint for designing more robust and deployable control systems for humanoid robots. Focusing on architectural separation and multi-rate processing, rather than solely on end-to-end learning or large models, can lead to more efficient training, better sim-to-real transfer, and inherently more stable behaviors in the face of real-world uncertainties (noisy sensors, unmodeled dynamics, varied terrain). This could accelerate the deployment of humanoids in various demanding applications.
5. **Guiding Future Research:** The paper opens several avenues for future work. It encourages deeper investigation into optimal interface design between layers, explores how to systematically derive or learn such layered architectures, and invites reconsideration of existing complex systems through an architectural lens. It also poses an interesting question about the architectural equivalence of teacher-student distillation methods, suggesting that they might implicitly learn multi-rate decompositions.

In conclusion, "Architecture Is All You Need" posits that a fundamental understanding and application of control architecture principles, specifically layered and multi-rate designs, are paramount for achieving robust humanoid locomotion. It shifts the focus from optimizing individual components or scaling network size to optimizing the *system's structure*, offering a more principled and potentially more sustainable path towards truly agile and robust autonomous robots.
