# GraphOmni: A Comprehensive and Extensible Benchmark Framework for Large Language Models on Graph-theoretic Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> A new benchmark framework, GRAPHOMNI, evaluates Large Language Models' (LLMs) graph-theoretic reasoning by systematically varying graph types, serialization formats, and prompting schemes. The evaluation demonstrates high variability in LLM performance across configurations and significant room for improvement on complex tasks, alongside an efficient reinforcement learning-based method to find optimal settings.

## Problem

- Large Language Models' (LLMs) structured reasoning capabilities on graph-based data remain underexplored despite their proficiency in NLP tasks.
- Existing evaluations of LLMs on graph tasks are fragmented, focusing on isolated factors and lacking a comprehensive understanding of how different design choices interact.
- There is an absence of a unified, extensible evaluation framework capable of consistently benchmarking LLMs across diverse graph types, serialization formats, and prompt schemes.

## Method

- The research introduces GRAPHOMNI, a comprehensive and extensible benchmark framework that systematically varies ==graph types==, serialization formats, and prompt schemes across six canonical graph-theoretic tasks.
- A diverse suite of both open-source and closed-source LLMs is evaluated using GRAPHOMNI, with an extended analysis on larger graphs, real-world datasets, and NP-hard tasks.
- A reinforcement learning-based adaptive mechanism (RL-Opt) is developed to efficiently identify optimal combinations of serialization formats, prompt schemes, and LLMs for specific tasks.

## Results

- LLM performance exhibits substantial variability and considerable room for improvement, particularly on harder tasks requiring global graph information (e.g., BFS order, Diameter, Triangle counting), with leading closed-source models declining significantly on these instances.
- Error analysis reveals common issues, including misinterpretation of textual graph serialization formats and fundamental conceptual misunderstandings of graph-theoretic definitions.
- The RL-based method (RL-Opt) effectively identifies optimal prompt and serialization combinations, achieving a **0.9** average success rate while exploring only **25%** of the total possible configurations, demonstrating improved computational efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLM performance on graph tasks is highly sensitive to the interplay of graph type, serialization format, and prompting scheme, with no single strategy proving universally optimal.
> - Algorithm-based prompts, which embed explicit domain knowledge, consistently improve accuracy for tasks requiring detailed step-by-step reasoning.
> - Model scaling primarily improves robustness on easier tasks, while dedicated reasoning-focused model designs yield larger gains on more challenging tasks demanding complex multi-step exploration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advanced capabilities in various Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, including text generation and problem-solving. However, their proficiency in structured reasoning, particularly concerning graph-based data, remains less explored. Graphs are fundamental data structures that encapsulate complex relationships critical to numerous real-world applications such as social network analysis, recommendation systems, and drug discovery. Traditionally, such tasks have been addressed by specialized Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).

This research addresses a recognized gap: the lack of a comprehensive understanding of whether LLMs can effectively interpret and manipulate graph-theoretic concepts when presented in natural language. Previous research efforts in this domain have largely focused on individual aspects, such as specific prompting strategies, textual graph serialization methods, or particular graph families. This segmented approach has limited the ability to discern how these factors interact and collectively influence LLM performance. This paper aims to integrate these dimensions into a unified evaluation framework, thereby offering a more holistic and robust assessment of LLM capabilities in graph reasoning compared to existing, more narrowly focused benchmarks. The work seeks to provide a foundation for future advancements in model design and evaluation for structured data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce GRAPHOMNI, a comprehensive and extensible benchmark framework explicitly designed to evaluate the reasoning capabilities of LLMs on graph-theoretic tasks that are articulated in natural language. The motivation for developing GRAPHOMNI stems from several observations within the current research landscape:

Firstly, despite the demonstrated prowess of LLMs in diverse NLP tasks, their application and performance in structured reasoning, particularly involving graph-based data, remain relatively underexplored. Graphs, with their ability to represent complex relationships, are integral to various real-world applications, yet current LLMs are primarily trained on unstructured or semi-structured text. This creates a fundamental question regarding their ability to effectively understand and manipulate graph-theoretic concepts when graphs are described linguistically.

Secondly, existing efforts to evaluate LLMs on graph-related tasks have typically adopted a piecemeal approach. Prior studies often examine individual components in isolation, such as specific prompting strategies, different textual serialization formats for graphs, or evaluations restricted to particular graph families. This fragmented view hinders a comprehensive understanding of how these various design choices interact to influence an LLM's performance. Critically, it prevents researchers from definitively attributing performance gains to either the underlying model architecture, the representation format of the graph, or the specific instructional methods used. A robust understanding requires a multi-dimensional evaluation that systematically varies and analyzes the interplay of these factors.

Thirdly, there is a recognized absence of a unified, comprehensive, and extensible evaluation system that can consistently benchmark LLMs across a broad spectrum of graph types, serialization formats, and prompting schemes. Such a framework is necessary to provide fine-grained insights into LLM behavior, uncover critical interactions among these dimensions, and establish a standardized methodology for advancing the field.

Therefore, the key objectives for GRAPHOMNI are to:
- Develop a novel benchmark that is the most comprehensive to date for evaluating graph-theoretic reasoning in LLMs, encompassing a wide range of synthetic graph types, diverse serialization formats, and varied prompt schemes.
- Design a flexible and extensible evaluation framework that allows for the seamless integration of new graph generators, serialization methods, and prompt strategies, ensuring its adaptability to future research developments.
- Generate insightful empirical observations regarding the performance of state-of-the-art LLMs on these tasks, highlighting areas for improvement and revealing the impact of different configurations.
- Inspire and develop practical methods, such as a reinforcement learning-based adaptive mechanism, that can dynamically select optimal evaluation factors to enhance accuracy efficiently.

Through these objectives, the research aims to deepen the understanding of LLM performance on structured graph reasoning and lay a robust foundation for advancing model design and evaluation in this challenging domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces GRAPHOMNI, a unified benchmark framework comprising four core components: Benchmark Tasks, Graph Types, Prompt Schemes, and Serialization Formats. These components are systematically varied to create a multi-dimensional evaluation pipeline.

**Benchmark Tasks:**
The benchmark includes six canonical graph-theoretic tasks designed to test both local and global reasoning capabilities:
1. **Connectivity**: Determines if a path exists between two nodes.
2. **Cycle detection**: Verifies the presence of any cycle.
3. **Diameter calculation**: Computes the maximum shortest distance between any two nodes.
4. **BFS order**: Generates the Breadth-First Search traversal sequence from a starting node.
5. **Triangle counting**: Enumerates all 3-cycles in the graph.
6. **Shortest path**: Identifies the shortest path between two designated nodes.
Tasks are categorized into three difficulty levels based on the number of nodes: Easy (5–10 nodes), Medium (10–20 nodes), and Hard (20–30 nodes). For extended analysis, the benchmark also includes two NP-hard tasks: Hamiltonian cycle detection and Max-Cut.

**Graph Types (Generators):**
Seven synthetic graph generators are used to produce diverse graph structures, reflecting different real-world network characteristics:
1. **Erdős–Rényi M-Edges (ERM)**: Random graphs with a fixed number of edges.
2. **Erdős–Rényi Probability (ERP)**: Random graphs with edges determined by a probability.
3. **Bipartite Erdős–Rényi M-Edges (BERM)**: Bipartite graphs with a fixed number of edges.
4. **Bipartite Erdős–Rényi Probability (BERP)**: Bipartite graphs with edges determined by a probability.
5. **Barabási–Albert Graphs (BAG)**: Scale-free networks generated via preferential attachment.
6. **Barabási–Albert Forests (BAF)**: Acyclic Barabási–Albert graphs.
7. **Scale-Free (SF) Graphs**: Networks with power-law degree distributions, potentially disconnected.
The selection aims to cover a wide range of structural motifs while ensuring control over graph properties and minimizing contamination from pre-training data. An extended study also tests performance on larger graphs (30–50 nodes) and real-world datasets (IMDB-MULTI and ogbg-molhiv).

**Prompt Schemes:**
Nine distinct prompting methods are systematically evaluated, varying in explicit guidance:
1. **0-Shot**: Plain question without additional cues.
2. **0-CoT (Chain-of-Thought)**: Encourages step-by-step reasoning without examples.
3. **0-Instruct**: Uses directive language tailored for instruction-based models, no examples.
4. **0-Algorithm**: Explicitly delineates an algorithm without examples.
5. **k-Shot**: Provides five exemplars from simpler graph instances.
6. **CoT**: Encourages intermediate reasoning steps, includes few-shot examples.
7. **Instruct**: Uses directive language, includes few-shot examples.
8. **Algorithm**: Explicitly delineates an algorithm, includes few-shot examples.
9. **LTM (Least-to-Most)**: Breaks down problems into sub-problems.

**Serialization Formats:**
Seven textual graph representations are utilized:
1. **Adjacency List (AL)**
2. **Adjacency Matrix (AM)**
3. **Adjacency Set (AS)**
4. **Edge List (EL)**
5. **Edge Set (ES)**
6. **Graph Modeling Language (GMoL)**: Structured, tag-based format.
7. **Graph Markup Language (GMaL)**: XML-based format.

**Dataset Statistics and Models:**
The dataset comprises 241,726 unique queries. A diverse suite of LLMs was evaluated:
- **Open-source**: Llama-3 (8B), Llama-3.1 (8B), Mistral (7B), Phi-4 (14B), Qwen-2.5 (7B, 72B), Qwen-3 (8B).
- **Closed-source**: Claude-3.5, Gemini-2.0, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o4-mini.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
Performance is measured by binary accuracy (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect). Rule-based functions are used for qualitative/sequential tasks, and numerical value extraction for quantitative tasks. Random baselines are included for each task.

**Reinforcement Learning-Based Prompt Search (RL-Opt):**
An RL-based approach using Deep Q-Networks (DQN) was developed to dynamically select optimal combinations of serialization formats, prompt schemes, and LLMs for specific tasks and difficulties. This framework aims to reduce the computational cost of finding optimal configurations by exploring a limited subset of all possible combinations. The performance is assessed using "Cost" (ratio of explored combinations) and "Rate" (accuracy of RL's best-found combination relative to the benchmark's highest). An RL-Scale experiment also investigated the scalability of this method by incorporating additional prompt design factors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation using GRAPHOMNI generated several findings concerning LLM performance on graph-theoretic tasks:

**High Variability and the Need for Comprehensive Evaluation (Result ❶):**
LLM performance exhibited substantial variability across different combinations of graph types, serialization formats, and prompting schemes. No single serialization or prompting strategy consistently outperformed others across all tasks and difficulty levels. Optimal results often required specific, adaptive selections of serialization-prompt combinations tailored to the task's characteristics. For instance, in the Diameter calculation task, GPT-4o showed accuracy differences of up to 40% when only the input representation was varied, indicating a significant sensitivity to input formatting.

**Room for Improvement in Model Performance (Result ❷):**
While models demonstrated capabilities in graph reasoning, overall performance indicated considerable room for improvement. Closed-source models like Claude-3.5 and o4-mini generally led, yet even their performance significantly declined on harder tasks, particularly those requiring global graph information such as BFS order, Diameter calculation, and Triangle counting. For example, o4-mini’s accuracy dropped to 32.45% on BFS order (Hard), 34.61% on Diameter calculation (Hard), and 17.53% on Triangle counting (Hard). Open-source models, with Qwen-3 (8B) as a top performer in its category, consistently lagged behind the leading closed-source models.

**Common Errors Indicating Fundamental Gaps (Result ❸):**
Error analysis identified recurrent patterns:
- **Misinterpretation of serialization formats**: Models sometimes failed to correctly interpret textual graph representations, leading to an incorrect understanding of graph structure. Examples included misinterpreting edge existence in Edge List or Adjacency List formats, leading to incorrect connectivity or triangle counts.
- **Incorrect reasoning about graph-theoretic concepts**: LLMs frequently exhibited conceptual misunderstandings. For instance, in Triangle counting, some models used inaccurate heuristics like "triangles ≈ n/3." In Diameter calculation, models sometimes confused the diameter with the length of the longest path, rather than the longest *shortest* path.

**Fine-Grained Empirical Findings:**
- **Domain-specific knowledge improves performance (Finding ❶)**: Algorithm-based prompts, which explicitly detail graph-theoretic algorithms, consistently improved accuracy in tasks requiring step-by-step reasoning, such as BFS order and Diameter calculation. This highlights the benefit of incorporating explicit domain knowledge into prompts.
- **Scaling vs. Reasoning effects (Finding ❷)**: A comparison between Qwen-2.5 (7B), Qwen-2.5 (72B), and Qwen-3 (8B) suggested distinct benefits. Scaling up within the same family (7B to 72B) primarily improved robustness on easier tasks, lifting the "floor" of performance. In contrast, a reasoning-focused model (Qwen-3 8B, comparable in size) achieved larger gains on the hardest tasks that demand multi-step exploration and combinatorial checks, indicating it lifted the "ceiling" of reasoning ability.
- **Divergent impacts of prompt schemes (Finding ❸)**: Open-source models generally benefited from multi-shot exemplars, whereas closed-source models showed more complex sensitivities, with simpler prompts like 0-CoT sometimes performing better than k-shot approaches. Complex multi-step prompts could occasionally hinder closed-source model performance on challenging tasks.

**Extended Studies:**
- **Scaling to Larger Graphs (30–50 nodes)**: Evaluation on larger graphs showed a uniform degradation in performance, particularly for sequential or combinatorial tasks. However, the relative rankings of models and the performance gap between open- and closed-source systems remained consistent, confirming the robustness of the benchmark's conclusions.
- **Representative Check on Real-World Graphs**: Testing on IMDB-MULTI and ogbg-molhiv datasets corroborated the synthetic results. Real-world graphs sometimes simplified tasks (e.g., high connectivity due to graph properties), suggesting that synthetic graphs provide a more balanced and controlled evaluation environment.
- **Exploration on NP-Hard Tasks**: For Hamiltonian cycle detection and Max-Cut, performance patterns mirrored those of canonical tasks, with closed-source models outperforming open-source ones but still achieving imperfect scores. LLMs did not show a clear distinction in difficulty between polynomial-time and NP-hard tasks, as typically observed in human problem-solving.
- **Efficiency–accuracy trade-off**: An analysis of output token usage revealed that accuracy often correlates with longer responses. Closed-source models like GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 achieved high accuracy with relatively compact outputs (<300 tokens). In contrast, o4-mini achieved similar high accuracy but relied on significantly longer Chain-of-Thought generations (over 1.6K tokens). Open-source models often required substantially longer outputs for comparable performance.

**RL-Based Prompt Search (RL-Opt):**
The RL-based method demonstrated effectiveness in identifying optimal prompt and serialization combinations. It achieved an average success rate of 0.9 (relative to the maximum possible accuracy) while exploring only 25% of the total possible combinations, significantly reducing the computational cost of evaluation. The RL-Scale experiment further indicated that the computational cost growth of RL is substantially lower than that of exhaustive grid search as the number of factors in the serialization process increases.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of GRAPHOMNI represents a significant contribution to the field of large language models and graph reasoning. Its comprehensive, multi-dimensional design addresses critical limitations of prior, more isolated evaluation efforts, providing a more holistic understanding of LLM capabilities on structured data.

The framework's extensibility is a notable strength, allowing researchers to easily integrate new graph generators, serialization methods, and prompt strategies. This modularity ensures the benchmark's long-term relevance and adaptability as LLM research and graph theory evolve. By systematically dissecting the influence of graph types, serialization formats, and prompt schemes, GRAPHOMNI delivers fine-grained empirical insights. These observations reveal that no single strategy is universally optimal, and performance is highly sensitive to the interplay of these factors, emphasizing the need for context-aware evaluation and model design.

The findings highlight that even state-of-the-art LLMs possess considerable room for improvement, particularly on complex graph-theoretic tasks that demand global information and combinatorial reasoning. The identification of common error patterns, such as misinterpretation of graph formats and fundamental conceptual misunderstandings, provides actionable targets for future model development. The distinction between the effects of model scaling (improving robustness on easier instances) and reasoning-centric design (enhancing performance on complex tasks) offers guidance for architectural choices. Furthermore, the observed differential sensitivity of open-source versus closed-source models to prompt schemes and serialization formats can inform tailored optimization strategies.

From a practical perspective, the development of an RL-based adaptive mechanism is a tangible outcome of the benchmark's insights. This method allows for efficient identification of optimal prompt and serialization configurations, significantly reducing the computational cost associated with extensive LLM evaluation. This has direct implications for researchers and practitioners seeking to optimize LLM performance on graph tasks without incurring prohibitive costs.

In summary, GRAPHOMNI establishes a robust foundation for advancing research towards truly general-purpose graph reasoning models. By providing a detailed, reproducible, and adaptable evaluation platform, it facilitates a deeper understanding of current LLM limitations and offers clear directions for future innovations in structured AI reasoning.
