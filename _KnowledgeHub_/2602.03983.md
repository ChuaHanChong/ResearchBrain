# Efficient Long-Horizon Vision-Language-Action Models via Static-Dynamic Disentanglement

> [!summary] Summary
> DySta disentangles VLA visual tokens into multi-level static tokens (cached once, reused across frames) and dynamic tokens (recomputed each step), with a learned gate deciding when to refresh static caches. This compresses multi-frame context and reuses KV-cache, cutting FLOPs while improving temporal-memory task success and inference speed.

## Problem

- Most VLAs process only the current frame, failing on tasks needing memory of past states like whether a button was already pressed.
- Prior multi-frame methods use compressed or indirect history access (pooling, patch-mixing, single-frame decoder fusion), losing information or model capacity.
- Existing acceleration methods reuse cached patches based on pixel-space visual similarity, which does not hold since transformer attention makes identical patches non-static in latent space.

## Method

- ==Static-dynamic disentanglement== splits each frame's visual tokens into ==multi-level static tokens== (persistent, cached once) and ==dynamic tokens== (recomputed every step), shrinking effective context length.
- A learnable ==recache gate==, trained via ==Gumbel-softmax==, predicts per-level whether cached static tokens should be refreshed or reused, replacing non-learnable heuristics.
- Training combines task loss with an ==InfoNCE contrastive objective== enforcing temporal persistence of static tokens and a ==gate regularization loss== biasing reuse over short intervals.

## Results

- On SimplerEnv with CogACT, DySta improves success rate by **3.9%** over the best baseline while cutting FLOPs to **43.4%** and achieving **2.26x** speedup versus **1.38x** for VLA-Cache.
- On the new LIBERO-Memory benchmark, DySta reaches **69.8%** On-Stove, **83.0%** Position Reset, and **0.26s** Doneness error, beating ContextVLA's **50.8%/22.3%/0.37s**.
- Real-world Cobot Magic trials show DySta improving average success rate by **10.6%** over the base model with **2.21x** latency speedup, and **23.3%** absolute gain on memory-dependent tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pixel-space visual similarity is an unreliable proxy for latent-space temporal invariance in transformer VLA backbones, undermining prior cache-reuse heuristics.
> - Explicitly separating temporally persistent from time-varying visual information lets a model extend context without proportionally growing sequence length or compute.
> - Benchmarks claiming to test temporal reasoning may be solvable from a single frame, so evaluating memory-dependent capability requires tasks provably requiring history.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a paradigm for generalist robotic control, building upon the architectures of vision-language models (VLMs). These models demonstrate capabilities in predicting actions based on visual observations and language instructions, achieving performance and generalization across various tasks, often trained on large-scale datasets such as Open-X-Embodiment.

However, the deployment of existing VLA models faces two primary challenges. First, their reliance on transformer-based architectures results in a constrained context window. This limitation typically forces VLA models to operate in a memoryless manner, processing only the current observation. Consequently, they struggle with tasks requiring temporal dependency or memory tracking, such as remembering past actions or object states. A direct approach of concatenating multiple past observations quickly leads to prohibitively long input sequences for vision backbones, which generate hundreds of tokens per image, exacerbating the quadratic complexity of transformer attention. Previous attempts to address multi-frame integration have involved indirect or compressed representations (e.g., pooling historical frames) or limited the exposure of multiple frames to only the decoder head. These methods either risk information loss or bypass the language model's ability for joint reasoning across frames.

Second, VLA models incur high per-step inference latency due to their large parameter counts and quadratic attention complexity. This bottleneck hinders real-time robotic control, a critical requirement for many real-world applications. While generic machine learning acceleration techniques like quantization, token pruning, or layer pruning have been applied, they often do not leverage the intrinsic temporal characteristics of VLA tasks. More recent works have attempted to exploit temporal redundancy by reusing computations across consecutive frames, for example, through Key-Value (KV) cache reuse or action reuse. However, these methods typically depend on heuristic, non-learnable criteria and often assume that visual similarity in pixel space translates to temporal consistency in latent representations, an assumption that the authors illustrate does not consistently hold in transformer-based vision and language backbones.

The presented work, DySta, addresses these specific challenges by proposing a novel framework that leverages the observation that much of a robotic scene remains static or changes slowly over time. It aims to develop a learnable, intrinsic mechanism to handle temporal redundancy, thereby improving both multi-frame integration and inference efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research outlines three key objectives motivated by the identified limitations of current VLA models:

#### 2.1. Enhance Multi-Frame Integration for Temporally Dependent Tasks

- **Motivation:** Contemporary VLA models are often memoryless, processing only a single, current observation. This design fundamentally limits their ability to perform tasks that necessitate remembering past states, tracking object trajectories, or understanding sequences of events. For instance, a robot instructed to press a button might repeatedly press it if it cannot recall its previous action. Incorporating historical frames directly into the model's input sequence leads to an exponential increase in context length due to the high number of visual tokens per frame and the quadratic complexity of transformer attention mechanisms. This makes multi-frame processing computationally infeasible for all but very short temporal windows. Existing solutions, which either compress historical information or expose it only to a lightweight decoder, risk significant information loss or prevent the core language model from jointly reasoning over comprehensive temporal contexts. The objective is to enable VLA models to effectively leverage extended temporal contexts without incurring prohibitive computational costs or sacrificing critical information.

#### 2.2. Improve Inference Efficiency for Real-time Robotic Control

- **Motivation:** The large parameter counts and computational complexity of VLA models translate into high latency for each inference step. In practical robotic applications, rapid response times are crucial for safety-critical operations and efficient task completion. Furthermore, advanced training methodologies, such as reinforcement learning, often require extensive model rollouts, making inference speed a significant bottleneck during the training phase itself. While general model compression techniques exist, they do not capitalize on the specific temporal redundancies inherent in robotic environments. Prior attempts to exploit temporal redundancy in VLA models by reusing computations across frames have relied on heuristic, non-learnable criteria and an implicit, often incorrect, assumption that pixel-level visual similarity guarantees stable latent representations. The objective is to design a VLA-specific acceleration method that reduces inference latency significantly while maintaining or improving task performance, by intelligently reusing computations across timesteps.

#### 2.3. Develop a More Effective Benchmark for Multi-Frame Integration

- **Motivation:** Current VLA simulation benchmarks, such as SimplerEnv and LIBERO, are frequently composed of tasks that can be optimally solved using only the current observation, or a very short history, rendering them inadequate for assessing a model's true multi-frame integration or temporal reasoning capabilities. For example, a task like "put X in the basket" often does not inherently require remembering past trajectories or complex temporal dependencies. This inadequacy makes it difficult to ascertain whether reported improvements in temporal modeling stem from genuine advancements or from other confounding factors. The objective is to introduce a benchmark specifically designed to challenge and evaluate a VLA model's ability to retain and utilize information from past observations over extended periods, thereby providing a clearer measure of temporal dependency modeling.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, DySta, addresses the aforementioned challenges through a static-dynamic disentanglement mechanism combined with a learned recaching strategy and a new evaluation benchmark.

#### 3.1. Model Architecture: DySta Framework

- **Static-Dynamic Disentanglement:** Standard VLA vision backbones encode images into a set of visual tokens. DySta disentangles these tokens into two main categories: dynamic tokens and multi-level static tokens.
 - **Dynamic Tokens (Z^d_t):** These tokens are recomputed at every timestep and are designed to capture rapidly changing, timestep-specific visual information, such as the agent's gripper or objects currently being manipulated.
 - **Multi-Level Static Tokens (Z^s_l_t):** These tokens represent visual information with different degrees of temporal persistence. For instance, Level 1 (L1) static tokens might capture long-horizon static elements like the background or global scene layout, while Level 2 (L2) static tokens could capture semi-static objects (e.g., a drawer that remains stationary for most of a task but might eventually move). The ratio of static to dynamic tokens is a configurable hyperparameter. This disentanglement allows the model to differentiate between ephemeral and persistent visual cues.

- **Temporal Dependency Modeling:** By disentangling tokens, DySta reformulates the input to the VLA's Language Model (LLM) backbone. Instead of concatenating full image tokens from multiple timesteps (which would be prohibitively long), DySta includes static tokens only once for a given temporal period. The input sequence then consists of these single copies of static tokens concatenated with the dynamic tokens from multiple preceding timesteps. This approach, represented as `π_LLM(Z^s_1, Z^s_2, ..., Z^d_{t-T}, ..., Z^d_t)`, significantly reduces the effective context length required for multi-frame integration, making it computationally tractable.

- **Learned Recache Gate (g_l):** A crucial component of DySta is a learned recache gate for each static level `l`. This gate determines whether previously cached static tokens should be reused or recomputed.
 - The gate `g_l(Z_{t-Δ}, Z_t)` predicts a probability (between 0 and 1) of recomputing static tokens, given the current observation `Z_t` and a cached reference from `Δ` timesteps earlier.
 - During training, the Gumbel-softmax trick is used to enable differentiable binary decisions.
 - At inference, static tokens are refreshed if `g_l` exceeds a predefined threshold `δ_l`. A hierarchical refreshing mechanism is implemented: if a higher-level cache (e.g., L1) is refreshed, all lower-level caches (e.g., L2) are also forcibly refreshed.
 - The recache gate is designed to be lightweight, utilizing an MLP-based architecture, to minimize additional inference latency.

#### 3.2. Training Objective

Beyond the standard task loss (`L_task`) of the underlying VLA base model, DySta incorporates two auxiliary objectives:

- **Learning Static Tokens (`L^l_InfoNCE`):** To ensure that static tokens are temporally persistent and encode task-relevant information suitable for reuse, a contrastive learning objective (InfoNCE loss) is applied. Observations from different timesteps within the same trajectory are treated as positive pairs, encouraging their static tokens to be similar. Conversely, observations from different trajectories form negative pairs, pushing their static tokens apart. This encourages static tokens to maintain stability over time within a consistent scene.

- **Training the Recache Gate (`L_gate`):** To prevent the recache gate from trivially recomputing static tokens at every timestep (which would negate efficiency benefits), a regularization term is introduced: `L_gate = -p_Δ log g - (1-p_Δ) log(1-g)`. Here, `p_Δ = 1 - e^(-λΔ)` is a predefined prior probability that biases the gate towards reusing cached tokens when observations are temporally close (i.e., `Δ` is small). This term guides the gate to adaptively refresh static tokens only when necessary, balancing computational efficiency with model performance.

- **Full Training Objective:** The overall loss function is a weighted sum: `L = L_Task + Σ_l α_l L^l_InfoNCE + βL_gate`, where `α_l` and `β` are weighting coefficients.

#### 3.3. Computational and Acceleration Analysis

- **Context Length:** DySta reduces the effective context length from `NT` (where `N` is total tokens per observation, `T` is number of observations) to `NT - rN(T-1)`, where `r` is the fraction of cached static tokens. This significantly alleviates the context window bottleneck.
- **FLOPs Reduction:** The theoretical FLOPs of the LLM backbone are reduced by a factor of `1-r` because only the dynamic tokens and language instructions are fully reprocessed in the multi-head attention and feedforward layers. The recache gate introduces a negligible overhead (approximately 1.27%).

#### 3.4. LIBERO-Memory Benchmark

To provide a rigorous evaluation of multi-frame integration, the authors introduce the LIBERO-Memory benchmark:

- **Design:** The benchmark features a tabletop robotic environment with three objects (two distinct cans and a stove). Each episode comprises three subtasks designed to explicitly test different aspects of episodic memory:
 1. **Grasp and Heat:** Grasp one specified can and place it on the stove for heating.
 2. **Return and Wait:** After a specified duration, remove the can from the stove and return it to its original position.
 3. **Heat Second Can:** Grasp the *remaining* can and place it on the stove.
- **Memory Requirements:**
 - **"Where" (Position Reset):** The robot must remember the initial spatial layout to return the first can to its precise original location.
 - **"When" (Doneness):** The model must track the elapsed time since the first can was placed on the stove to know when to remove it, requiring temporal reasoning.
 - **"What" (On-Stove):** The robot must remember which can has already been heated to correctly identify and manipulate the *other* can in the final step.
- **Implementation:** The benchmark uses Robosuite as the simulation framework, with programmatic generation of layouts and oracle demonstrations. Evaluation metrics include success rate for position reset and placing the second can, and absolute time difference for "doneness."

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DySta focused on both inference acceleration and multi-frame integration, utilizing existing benchmarks (SimplerEnv, LIBERO) and the newly proposed LIBERO-Memory, alongside real-world experiments.

#### 4.1. Inference Acceleration

- **SimplerEnv Benchmark (Base Model: CogACT):** DySta achieved an average success rate of 81.0%, outperforming the base CogACT (80.8%) and the best baseline (VLA-Cache at 79.3%) by 4.9% and 3.9% respectively. Concurrently, DySta achieved a 2.26× inference speedup, reducing FLOPs to 43.4% of the original. Baselines like FlashVLA, TTF-VLA, and VLA-Cache showed speedups between 1.29× and 1.38×, sometimes with reduced success rates.
- **LIBERO Benchmark (Base Model: OpenVLA-OFT):** DySta achieved an average success rate of 96.0%, which was comparable to the base OpenVLA-OFT (95.4%) and higher than all other baselines. This was accomplished with a 1.70× speedup in inference latency, reducing FLOPs to 63.4%.
- **Real-World Benchmark (Cobot Magic Robot, Base Model: OpenVLA-OFT):** Across 7 general manipulation tasks, DySta improved the average success rate by 10.6% over the base model, achieving a 2.21× speedup in inference latency. FLOPs were reduced to 30.5%.

These results indicate that DySta consistently enhances or maintains task performance while providing substantial reductions in inference latency across simulation and real-world environments.

#### 4.2. Multi-Frame Integration for Temporal-Dependency Modeling

- **LIBERO-Memory Benchmark:** DySta significantly outperformed all baselines.
 - **"On Stove" (what):** DySta achieved 69.8% success, notably higher than ContextVLA (50.8%) and OpenVLA-OFT† (39.0%), a naive baseline with maximum frames.
 - **"Position Reset" (where):** DySta achieved 83.0% success, surpassing OpenVLA-OFT† (78.0%) and ContextVLA (22.3%).
 - **"Doneness" (when):** DySta demonstrated the lowest absolute error of 0.26 seconds, compared to ContextVLA (0.37s) and OpenVLA-OFT† (0.44s).
 - Overall, DySta showed an average improvement of 24.5% across metrics. Methods relying on single-image inputs or indirect temporal cues (e.g., TraceVLA, MemoryVLA) generally performed poorly, underscoring the necessity of direct multi-frame reasoning.
- **Real-World Memory-Dependent Tasks (Cobot Magic Robot):** On tasks requiring temporal reasoning (Heat Vegetables, Pour Twice, Press Buttons), DySta achieved an average success rate of 58.3%, a 23.3% absolute improvement over the best baseline (ContextVLA at 35.0%).

These findings suggest that DySta's explicit disentanglement and multi-frame processing capabilities enable robust temporal reasoning and memory utilization in complex tasks.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies validated the contribution of key DySta components:
- **Removal of Contrastive Learning:** Excluding the `L_InfoNCE` objective for static tokens led to a noticeable degradation in performance, indicating its role in enforcing temporal consistency.
- **Removal of L2 Static Cache:** Using only a single-level static cache (removing L2) also reduced performance, supporting the efficacy of the multi-level static token design.
- **Fixed-Interval Recache:** Replacing the learned recache gate with a fixed-interval refresh strategy (matching DySta's average refresh rate) resulted in a further performance drop, demonstrating the importance of the adaptive, learned recaching mechanism.

#### 4.4. Visualizations (Attention Maps)

Analysis of attention maps revealed that:
- **Dynamic Tokens:** Consistently focused on movable objects (e.g., gripper, apple), aligning with their role in capturing real-time changes.
- **L1 Static Tokens:** Primarily attended to background and ambient regions, exhibiting high temporal invariance, functioning as "sink" tokens for coarse, global scene context.
- **L2 Static Tokens:** Showed intermediate behavior, focusing on semi-static objects (e.g., drawer), which move less frequently, indicating capture of object-level static information with moderate temporal persistence.
- **Latent Representation Drift (Appendix G):** An analysis of cosine similarity in latent space confirmed that pixel-level static regions can still undergo substantial drift in their latent representations across timesteps, validating the need for a learned recache mechanism that operates in the representation space rather than relying on pixel-level heuristics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DySta framework introduces a method for enhancing Vision-Language-Action models by addressing the critical issues of limited context windows and inefficient inference. By effectively disentangling visual inputs into static and dynamic components and employing a learned, adaptive recache gate, DySta enables VLA models to integrate longer temporal contexts without a proportional increase in computational burden.

The demonstrated improvements in performance on temporally dependent tasks, both in simulation and real-world settings, indicate that DySta can lead to more capable and generalized robotic policies. The ability to effectively leverage memory over extended horizons is crucial for complex, multi-step tasks that are common in practical robotic applications.

Furthermore, the significant inference speedups achieved by DySta contribute to the practicality and deployability of VLA models. Reduced latency allows for real-time control, which is essential for responsive and safe human-robot interaction and operation in dynamic environments. It also facilitates more efficient training paradigms, such as reinforcement learning, which often require extensive model rollouts.

The introduction of the LIBERO-Memory benchmark is a notable contribution, providing a standardized and challenging environment specifically designed to assess temporal reasoning and multi-frame integration. This benchmark fills a gap in existing evaluation protocols and will be valuable for future research aiming to improve VLA capabilities in memory-dependent tasks.

Overall, this work suggests that explicitly modeling temporal persistence in visual inputs and dynamically managing cached representations is a promising direction for developing scalable, efficient, and robust VLA systems for generalist robotic control. The approach has the potential to broaden the range of tasks that VLA models can reliably address in real-world scenarios. The authors note a limitation that their approach is based on pre-trained VLAs, suggesting that future work could explore pretraining VLAs with the DySta architecture from scratch for further performance optimization.
