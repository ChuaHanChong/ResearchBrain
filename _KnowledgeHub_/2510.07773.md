# Trajectory Conditioned Cross-embodiment Skill Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> TrajSkill transfers manipulation skills from human demonstration videos to robots by extracting sparse optical flow trajectories as an embodiment-agnostic motion cue, then jointly generating robot-consistent videos and decoding them into executable actions. Achieves zero-shot cross-embodiment imitation without reinforcement learning or paired human-robot datasets, cutting FVD 39.6% and lifting success rate up to 16.7% over baselines.

## Problem

- Reward-shaping and meta-learning imitation methods for human-to-robot transfer depend on costly human interventions or brittle one-shot alignment strategies that limit scalability.
- Video-conditioned policy methods like Vid2Robot and Human2Robot require large paired human-robot datasets, creating a bottleneck since such pairs are expensive to collect.
- Keypoint-retargeting and domain-alignment techniques remain sensitive to embodiment discrepancies, since morphological mismatches between human and robot bodies degrade transfer fidelity.

## Method

- ==Embodiment-Invariant Flow Sampling== computes dense ==RAFT== optical flow from human video, then probabilistically samples and propagates sparse keypoint trajectories weighted by flow magnitude, Gaussian-smoothed to remove appearance and morphology cues.
- ==Trajectory Conditioned Video Generation== uses a ==Diffusion Transformer== backbone fusing initial frame, text instruction, and sparse trajectory signals, trained in two stages: dense flow supervision then sparse trajectory alignment.
- ==Video Policy to Robot Execution== aggregates generated video frames into a reference embedding fused with current observation and state, decoded by a policy network into an executable robot action sequence.

## Results

- On MetaWorld and Franka video generation, TrajSkill cuts FVD by **39.6%** and KVD by **36.6%** versus CogVideo on MetaWorld, and **27.6%**/**31.6%** on Franka, at 480x720 resolution.
- On MetaWorld 50-task execution, TrajSkill reaches **44.7%** overall success rate versus **38.3%** SmolVLA, **31.6%** TinyVLA, and **10.5%** Diffusion Policy, with **38.0%** on hard tasks versus rivals' sharp drop-off.
- Real-robot Franka Panda pick-and-place on bananas at varying distances hits **90.9%** pick and **81.8%** place success, versus **81.8%**/**72.7%** for Diffusion Policy and **0%**/**0%** for Octo.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sparse, magnitude-weighted optical flow sampling can serve as a compact embodiment-agnostic motion abstraction, discarding appearance while retaining task-relevant dynamics for transfer.
> - Two-stage training from dense to sparse flow supervision lets a generator first learn precise dynamics then adapt to the sparser conditioning signal actually available at inference.
> - Video generation as an intermediate policy representation can enable zero-shot cross-embodiment imitation without reinforcement learning or paired datasets, though gains shrink on harder, longer-horizon tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied intelligence faces the challenge of enabling robots to learn complex manipulation skills. A promising direction involves learning from human demonstration videos, which offer a natural way to capture manipulation dynamics. However, a significant obstacle in this area is the "embodiment gap" – the substantial differences in morphology, kinematic constraints, and physical embodiment between humans and robots. This gap makes direct skill transfer difficult.

Prior research has attempted to address this gap through various methods. Some approaches utilize reinforcement learning (RL) with hand-crafted reward functions or meta-learning techniques for one-shot imitation. Other strategies focus on domain alignment between human and robot embodiments. However, these methods often necessitate costly human intervention, the availability of large, paired datasets (human actions explicitly mapped to robot actions), or reliance on brittle alignment strategies, which limits their scalability and generalizability in real-world applications.

Recent advancements in video generation models, particularly diffusion models, have opened new avenues for robot policy learning. These models can synthesize long-horizon motion sequences, offering potential for informing planning in robotics. Concurrently, a body of work directly aims to learn robot policies from human videos. Examples include "Learning by Watching," which translates human motions to robot actions via keypoint-based representations, but requires accurate embodiment mappings. "Vid2Robot" proposes an end-to-end video-conditioned policy, yet its dependence on large paired datasets and explicit embodiment alignment remains a limitation. More recent efforts like "Human2Robot" and "Motion Tracks" leverage diffusion models and keypoint retargeting, respectively, but still exhibit sensitivity to embodiment discrepancies and often require paired data.

The current work, TrajSkill, directly addresses the fundamental embodiment gap by seeking a motion representation that is compact, embodiment-agnostic, and capable of retaining essential task dynamics. It attempts to overcome the limitations of existing methods by proposing a novel, unified motion representation that allows for zero-shot imitation without the need for reinforcement learning or paired human-robot datasets. This positions TrajSkill as a contribution towards developing more scalable and robust methods for cross-embodiment skill transfer in robotics, specifically by enhancing the capability of generative models to produce robot-executable trajectories from human visual inputs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, named TrajSkill, for "Trajectory Conditioned Cross-embodiment Skill Transfer," enabling robots to acquire manipulation skills directly from human demonstration videos. This objective is motivated by several challenges and limitations inherent in current robotic learning paradigms:

1. **Bridging the Embodiment Gap:** The fundamental motivation stems from the significant morphological and kinematic differences between human bodies and robot manipulators. Human demonstration videos naturally capture task dynamics, but directly transferring these skills to robots is impractical due to this embodiment disparity. Existing methods often rely on explicit mappings, paired datasets, or reward engineering, which are resource-intensive and lack generalization.
2. **Achieving Scalability and Generalization:** Current approaches often depend on costly human interventions, large paired datasets, or brittle domain alignment strategies. These dependencies limit their scalability for deploying robots in diverse, unstructured real-world scenarios. The authors aim to overcome these limitations by proposing a method that requires neither paired datasets nor hand-crafted rewards, thereby enhancing scalability and generalization.
3. **Developing an Embodiment-Agnostic Motion Representation:** A core motivation is the need to discover a motion representation that is compact, invariant to morphological differences, and capable of preserving the essential dynamics and intent of a task. The authors hypothesize that sparse optical flow trajectories can serve this purpose by effectively filtering out appearance and morphological variations while retaining the key motion intent, thus achieving embodiment invariance.
4. **Enabling Zero-Shot Imitation:** The framework seeks to allow robots to learn and reproduce skills from human demonstrations without prior robot-specific training data or extensive fine-tuning. This "zero-shot" capability would significantly reduce the effort and data required for robot skill acquisition.
5. **Jointly Synthesizing and Executing Robot Actions:** The research aims to not only generate realistic robot manipulation videos that reflect human intent but also to translate these generated videos into executable robot actions. This two-stage process (video generation followed by action decoding) is crucial for practical skill transfer.

In summary, the key objectives are:
- To propose TrajSkill, a framework that jointly enables controllable video generation and executable robot policy learning directly from human demonstration videos.
- To introduce sparse optical flow trajectories as an embodiment-agnostic representation to bridge the morphological gap between human and robot embodiments, providing effective motion cues for skill transfer.
- To validate the effectiveness of TrajSkill across diverse manipulation benchmarks, demonstrating improvements in video generation quality, cross-embodiment success rates, and real-robot skill execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TrajSkill framework is designed to facilitate trajectory-conditioned cross-embodiment skill transfer by employing sparse optical flow as an embodiment-agnostic motion representation. The methodology is structured into three main components: Embodiment-Invariant Flow Sampling, Trajectory Conditioned Robot Execution, and Cross-embodiment Skill Transfer.

**A. Embodiment-Invariant Flow Sampling**
This component is responsible for extracting a compact and embodiment-agnostic representation of motion from human demonstration videos.
1. **Dense Optical Flow Computation:** Given a human demonstration video, the method first computes dense optical flow between consecutive frames. This is achieved using the RAFT (Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms) model, which provides a detailed pixel-wise displacement field ($F_t$).
2. **Sparse Keypoint Trajectory Sampling:** To obtain a compact representation, the dense flow is sampled into sparse keypoint trajectories.
 - A grid of candidate positions is defined across the image.
 - Each candidate is assigned a probability proportional to the magnitude of its initial flow vector, ensuring that regions with significant motion are more likely to be selected.
 - A uniform number of samples ($N$) is drawn from a maximum budget ($N_{max}$).
 - $N$ distinct candidate keypoints are then selected without replacement based on their probabilities, forming the initial keypoint set ($K_0$).
3. **Keypoint Propagation:** The selected keypoints are propagated through time by integrating the local flow vectors. At each timestep, a keypoint's new position is calculated by adding the flow vector at its current position to its current coordinates. This process generates a set of sparse trajectories ($T$), each representing the path of a keypoint over time.
4. **Flow Smoothing:** To reduce noise and enhance spatial consistency, a Gaussian blur smoothing is applied to the sparse flow field. This smoothed flow is then used to construct the final trajectory representation. This step is crucial for discarding embodiment-specific appearance details and focusing solely on the essential motion intent, thereby eliminating the embodiment gap.

**B. Trajectory Conditioned Robot Execution**
This component translates the embodiment-invariant trajectories and task instructions into executable robot actions.

1. **Trajectory Conditioned Video Generation:**
 - **Architecture:** A latent Diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone is employed for synthesizing robot manipulation sequences. Unlike traditional UNet-based models, DiT utilizes global attention, which is designed to capture long-range temporal dependencies, leading to improved temporal coherence in generated long-horizon robot motions.
 - **Inputs:** The video generator is conditioned on three types of inputs: an initial frame ($I_0$), a textual task instruction ($C_{text}$), and the sparse trajectory signals ($C_{traj}$) extracted from human demonstrations.
 - **Two-Stage Training Strategy:**
 - **Stage 1: Dense Flow Supervision:** The model is initially trained using dense optical flow. This stage allows the generator to learn precise robot dynamics and object interactions by providing detailed motion cues.
 - **Stage 2: Sparse Trajectory Alignment:** The training then transitions to aligning the generated video with sparse flow trajectories. This stage ensures that the model can effectively leverage sparse trajectory prompts for morphology-invariant motion control, aligning the supervision with the eventual inference conditions.
 - The output of this stage is a temporally consistent robot manipulation video ($V_r$).

2. **Video Policy to Robot Execution:**
 - The generated robot video ($V_r$) must be translated into discrete robot actions.
 - **Policy Conditioning:** The policy network is conditioned on the robot's real-time visual observation ($O$), its low-level state information ($S$), and the generated video ($V_r$).
 - **Input Integration:** The generated video frames are temporally aggregated into a compact reference image. This reference image is then projected into the model's latent space and fused with the current state embedding.
 - **Action Decoding:** The fused representation is subsequently decoded by the policy network into a sequence of executable robot actions ($A_r$).

**C. Cross-embodiment Skill Transfer**
This final component describes how the above modules collectively enable one-shot cross-embodiment imitation. The embodiment-invariant flow sampling provides the universal motion cues, while the trajectory-conditioned robot execution generates and executes robot-specific actions guided by these cues. This integrated approach allows a robot to reproduce skills demonstrated by a human without the need for paired datasets or reinforcement learning optimization, effectively bridging the embodiment gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted to evaluate TrajSkill focused on trajectory controllability in generated videos, cross-embodiment transfer, and robot execution for manipulation tasks, utilizing simulation (MetaWorld, XSkill) and real-world (Franka Multi-Tasks, kitchen environment) datasets.

**A. Trajectory Conditioned Video Generation**
1. **Visual Quality:** Quantitative evaluation on MetaWorld and Franka datasets demonstrated that TrajSkill produced videos with higher visual quality and temporal consistency compared to baseline methods.
 - On MetaWorld, TrajSkill reduced the Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) by 39.6% and Kernel Video Distance (KVD) by 36.6% relative to CogVideo, a state-of-the-art video generator.
 - On the Franka dataset, TrajSkill achieved FVD reductions of 27.6% and KVD reductions of 31.6% compared to CogVideo.
 - The method also generated videos that were longer and clearer, attributes deemed important for manipulation planning.
2. **Trajectory Controllability:** Experiments confirmed that the generated robot motions closely aligned with input trajectories, including both simple linear paths and complex curved patterns. This finding indicates that the 2D sparse trajectories effectively convey precise spatiotemporal guidance while abstracting away embodiment-specific morphology. This capability is central to faithfully reinterpreting human demonstrations into robot-consistent motion videos.

**B. Cross-embodiment Skill Transfer**
1. **Simulation Transfer (Sphere → Robot):** Using demonstrations from a sphere-agent in the XSkill dataset, TrajSkill successfully extracted sparse flow trajectories and conditioned robot motion generation, with the generated videos accurately following the demonstrated motions. This validated the approach's ability to transfer skills from an abstract simulated agent to a robot.
2. **Real-World Transfer (Human → Robot):** The framework demonstrated successful transfer from human hand demonstrations to real robot arm execution. Sparse trajectories extracted from human videos effectively guided the generation of robot motions for complex multi-step tasks in simulated environments. These results indicate TrajSkill's capacity to bridge embodiment gaps in both simulated and real-world scenarios, generalizing motion knowledge across distinct agents.

**C. Robot Execution**
1. **Simulation Rollouts (MetaWorld):** TrajSkill was evaluated on the MetaWorld 50 benchmark, encompassing 49 robot tasks across varying difficulty levels.
 - TrajSkill achieved the highest overall success rate (44.7%), outperforming Diffusion Policy (10.5%), TinyVLA (31.6%), and SmolVLA (38.3%).
 - Its performance was robust across task difficulties, achieving 81.8% success rate in easy tasks and 38.0% in hard tasks, while maintaining 30.0% in very hard tasks. This performance profile suggests that trajectory-conditioned video generation not only enables execution but also scales effectively to more challenging scenarios.
2. **Real-Robot Experiments (Franka Panda in Kitchen Environment):** Deployment on a Franka Panda robot for a "Put the Banana in the Basket" task further validated the approach.
 - Human hand videos provided sparse trajectories to guide robot motion generation for picking and placing a banana at varying distances (20cm, 30cm, 40cm).
 - TrajSkill achieved a 90.9% success rate for pick actions and 81.8% for place actions, outperforming Diffusion Policy (Pick: 81.8%, Place: 72.7%) and OCTO (Pick: 0.0%, Place: 0.0%).
 - These real-world results confirm TrajSkill's ability to generalize from simulation to real-world robot execution, demonstrating robust performance in challenging manipulation scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of TrajSkill represents a notable development in the field of robotic learning from demonstration, particularly concerning cross-embodiment skill transfer. The core contribution lies in demonstrating that sparse optical flow trajectories can serve as an effective embodiment-agnostic representation for motion intent. This approach addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: bridging the inherent morphological and kinematic differences between human demonstrators and robotic manipulators.

The significance of TrajSkill stems from several key aspects:
- **Reduced Reliance on Paired Datasets and Reinforcement Learning:** By enabling zero-shot imitation directly from human demonstration videos, TrajSkill reduces the dependence on costly and time-consuming paired human-robot datasets or complex reinforcement learning setups that often require hand-crafted reward functions. This lowers the barrier to entry for teaching robots new skills.
- **Enhanced Scalability and Generalization:** The embodiment-agnostic nature of sparse optical flow allows for more scalable skill acquisition. Robots can potentially learn from a wider range of human demonstrations without requiring explicit re-targeting or recalibration for each new robot morphology or task variation. This promises improved generalization across different robotic platforms and task contexts.
- **Improved Video Generation Quality and Controllability:** The framework's ability to generate high-quality, temporally consistent robot manipulation videos, accurately conditioned on sparse trajectories and textual instructions, suggests an advancement in controllable video synthesis for robotic applications. Such controlled generation is crucial for accurate planning and execution.
- **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The validation through real-robot experiments, where TrajSkill successfully executes complex kitchen manipulation tasks, indicates its potential for practical deployment in real-world scenarios. This moves beyond theoretical feasibility to demonstrated operational capability.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. It suggests a promising direction for creating more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems that can acquire skills from readily available human visual data. This could accelerate the deployment of robots in service industries, manufacturing, and household environments where human demonstrations are abundant but traditional robot programming is prohibitive. Future work, as suggested by the authors, including extending to more complex long-horizon tasks, incorporating language grounding for more detailed task specifications, and applying the framework to a wider variety of robot morphologies in open-world environments, could further broaden its impact and utility.
