# Learning Agile Robotic Locomotion Skills by Imitating Animals

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google Research and UC Berkeley developed a framework for quadruped robots to learn diverse and agile locomotion skills by imitating animal motion data. The method enables a Laikago robot to perform gaits like trotting at 1.08m/s and backward trotting at 1.20m/s, achieving faster speeds than its manufacturer's controller, through a sample-efficient latent space adaptation for real-world deployment.

## Problem

- Manually designing controllers for agile, diverse, and natural robotic locomotion is complex, time-consuming, and difficult to generalize.
- Reinforcement learning methods for robotic control struggle with the arduous task of designing effective reward functions to elicit natural behaviors.
- Policies trained purely in simulation often fail on physical robots due to the sim-to-real gap, requiring inefficient real-world data collection for adaptation.

## Method

- ==Motion retargeting== maps animal motion capture data onto the robot's morphology using ==Inverse Kinematics (IK)==, generating feasible reference trajectories for the robot.
- ==Motion imitation== trains a control policy in simulation using reinforcement learning (==PPO==) to track these retargeted motions, incorporating ==domain randomization== to enhance robustness to varying dynamics.
- A ==latent space domain adaptation== method, conditioned by an ==information bottleneck==, enables efficient transfer to the real robot by searching for an optimal latent encoding using ==Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR)== over approximately 50 real-world episodes.

## Results

- The Laikago robot learned diverse and agile locomotion skills, including pacing, trotting, and agile turns, achieving faster speeds (e.g., **1.08m/s** for trotting, **1.20m/s** for backwards trotting) than the manufacturer's manual controller.
- Adaptive policies, using latent space adaptation with a small number of real-world trials (around **50 episodes**), consistently outperformed non-adaptive and domain-randomized-only policies on the physical robot, demonstrating superior balance and consistency.
- Ablation studies confirmed the information bottleneck's role in improving policy performance by providing a balance between initial robustness and efficient adaptability, leading to better results both before and after real-world fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Imitating diverse animal motions allows robots to achieve higher agility and a broader repertoire of locomotion skills compared to traditional engineering or pure RL methods.
> - A latent space adaptation framework, combined with an information bottleneck, provides an effective and sample-efficient approach for transferring policies from simulation to physical robots.
> - The information bottleneck helps manage a fundamental trade-off, enabling policies to be both robust enough for initial deployment and adaptable for efficient fine-tuning in the real world.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic locomotion has long sought to replicate the agility and versatility observed in animals. Historically, this has involved **manually-designed controllers**, which, while capable of complex behaviors, are notoriously time-consuming, difficult to develop, and require deep expertise for each specific skill and robot morphology. These methods often rely on simplified models or pre-planned trajectories, limiting their adaptability to novel situations or dynamic environments.

More recently, **reinforcement learning (RL)** has emerged as a promising alternative, offering the potential to automate controller development. RL-based methods have shown impressive results in simulation, producing sophisticated behaviors that are difficult to engineer manually. However, RL faces two major hurdles for real-world robotic deployment:
1. **Reward Function Design:** Crafting effective reward functions that elicit natural and desired behaviors can be as challenging and laborious as manual controller design itself, often leading to unnatural or unsafe policies.
2. **Sim-to-Real Gap:** Policies trained purely in simulation often fail catastrophically when transferred to the real world due to discrepancies between simulated and physical dynamics (e.g., inaccuracies in friction, motor models, latencies).

This paper addresses these critical limitations by integrating three key research directions:

- **Motion Imitation:** Instead of manually designing reward functions, this work leverages **motion imitation**, where a robot learns by observing and replicating reference motion data (e.g., motion capture from real animals). This approach inherently provides natural, agile behaviors and simplifies the reward design problem into a pose-tracking objective. While motion imitation has been applied in simulation for highly acrobatic skills, its application to dynamic legged robots in the real world has been limited.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To overcome the sim-to-real gap, the paper employs advanced **domain transfer techniques**. Traditional methods include building more accurate simulators, adapting simulators with real-world data, or domain randomization. This work builds on domain randomization by incorporating a **sample-efficient domain adaptation technique** that fine-tunes policies on the real robot using a learned latent dynamics representation. This is crucial for bridging the gap between high-fidelity simulated training and the complexities of real-world physics.
- **Latent Space Adaptation:** The specific domain adaptation technique utilizes a *latent space approach*, which involves learning an encoding of environmental dynamics during simulation training. This allows the robot to adapt its behavior by searching for the optimal latent code in the real world, rather than retraining the entire policy. This builds on prior work in meta-learning and domain adaptation that seeks robustness and efficient adaptation in new environments. The novelty here is the integration of an **information bottleneck** with this latent space adaptation, providing a principled way to trade off between policy robustness and adaptability.

In essence, this research pushes the boundaries of robotic locomotion by combining the strengths of data-driven imitation learning for natural agility with sophisticated sim-to-real transfer methods, making complex animal-like behaviors accessible to physical robots with significantly reduced manual effort.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **enable legged robots to learn diverse and agile locomotion skills by imitating real-world animals**.

The motivations behind this objective are multifaceted:

- **Overcoming Manual Control Limitations:** Manually designing controllers for agile and diverse animal-like locomotion is exceedingly complex, time-consuming, and requires specialized expertise. Such controllers often struggle to achieve the fluidity and grace seen in biological systems and may not generalize well to different skills or environments. The authors aim to automate this laborious process.
- **Addressing Reinforcement Learning Challenges:** While RL offers an appealing alternative for automated controller development, it presents its own set of challenges. Firstly, designing effective reward functions for complex, agile behaviors is difficult and can lead to unnatural solutions. Secondly, the high sample complexity of RL algorithms necessitates simulation-based training, but transferring these policies to real robots is hindered by the sim-to-real gap. The paper seeks to bypass the reward design issue by using imitation and to mitigate the sim-to-real problem through efficient adaptation.
- **Leveraging Animal Agility:** Animals demonstrate remarkable agility and a broad repertoire of skills. The core idea is that if robots could directly imitate these natural motions, they could achieve superior agility and versatility compared to purely engineered or task-specific RL-trained controllers. This "imitate nature" approach seeks to tap into an existing wealth of robust and dynamic behaviors.
- **Developing a Generalizable Framework:** The authors aim for a single, unified learning-based approach capable of synthesizing controllers for a *diverse repertoire* of behaviors, rather than requiring skill-specific engineering for each. This promotes scalability and versatility.
- **Achieving Robust Real-World Deployment:** The ultimate goal is to deploy these learned skills on physical robots. This necessitates robust policies that can tolerate real-world dynamics and uncertainties. The paper specifically aims for sample-efficient adaptation techniques to make real-world fine-tuning practical.

In summary, the motivation is to simplify and automate the development of agile, natural, and diverse locomotion skills for legged robots by drawing inspiration directly from animal movements, while simultaneously ensuring these learned policies can reliably operate in the physical world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for learning agile robotic locomotion skills by imitating animals is structured into three distinct, sequential stages: Motion Retargeting, Motion Imitation, and Domain Adaptation.

#### 3.1. Motion Retargeting

The initial step addresses the morphological differences between the animal (source subject) from which motion data is recorded (e.g., a dog using motion capture) and the robot (target subject).
- **Process:** Motion data, typically in the form of 3D keypoint trajectories, is mapped from the animal's body to the robot's morphology.
- **Technique:** Inverse Kinematics (IK) is used. Keypoints are specified on both the animal (source) and the robot (target), including feet and hips.
- **Objective Function:** At each timestep, IK computes a sequence of robot poses ($q_{0:T}$) that minimizes the squared difference between the source keypoint positions ($\hat{x}_i(t)$) and the corresponding target keypoint positions on the robot ($x_i(q_t)$). A regularization term is added to keep the robot's poses similar to a default pose ($\bar{q}$), controlled by a diagonal weight matrix $W$, ensuring more natural and feasible joint configurations for the robot.

#### 3.2. Motion Imitation

This stage involves training a control policy to reproduce the retargeted motion using reinforcement learning (RL) in a simulated environment.
- **RL Formulation:** Motion imitation is framed as an RL problem where the policy $\pi$ learns to maximize expected return.
- **Policy Input:** The policy receives the current state ($s_t$) of the robot and an additional *goal* input ($g_t$), which specifies target poses from the reference motion at four future timesteps (spanning approximately 1 second). The state $s_t$ includes the robot's poses and actions from previous timesteps (e.g., $q_{t-2:t}$, $a_{t-3:t-1}$), using IMU readings for root orientation and local joint rotations, but excluding root position to avoid real-world estimation issues.
- **Action Output:** The policy outputs target rotations for PD (Proportional-Derivative) controllers at each joint, which are then smoothed by a low-pass filter before application.
- **Reward Function:** A composite reward function ($r_t$) is designed to encourage close tracking of the reference motion. It includes five weighted components:
 - **Pose Reward ($r_p_t$):** Exponentially penalizes differences in joint rotations between the robot and the reference motion.
 - **Velocity Reward ($r_v_t$):** Exponentially penalizes differences in joint angular velocities.
 - **End-Effector Reward ($r_e_t$):** Exponentially penalizes differences in the relative 3D positions of the robot's end-effectors (feet) compared to the reference.
 - **Root Pose Reward ($r_{rp}_t$):** Exponentially penalizes differences in the robot's root (torso) global position and orientation.
 - **Root Velocity Reward ($r_{rv}_t$):** Exponentially penalizes differences in the robot's root linear and angular velocities.
- **Training Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training, leveraging about 200 million samples in simulation.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** During training, various dynamics parameters (e.g., mass, inertia, motor strength, friction, latency) of the simulated robot and environment are randomized within specified ranges (Table I). This makes the learned policy more robust to variations and uncertainties, helping bridge the sim-to-real gap.

#### 3.3. Domain Adaptation

This critical stage transfers the policy learned in simulation to the real world, addressing the residual sim-to-real gap that DR alone might not cover.
- **Adaptive Policy Architecture:** Instead of a fixed policy, the learned policy $\pi(a|s, g, z)$ is conditioned on a *latent embedding* $z$. An encoder $E(z|\mu)$ maps randomized dynamics parameters $\mu$ to this latent embedding $z$, which is then fed into the policy network.
- **Information Bottleneck (IB):** A key innovation is the inclusion of an information bottleneck during simulation training. This imposes a constraint on the mutual information $I(M, Z)$ between the dynamics parameters $M$ and their latent encoding $Z$. This constraint, approximated using the KL divergence between the encoder distribution $E(\cdot|\mu)$ and a prior distribution $\rho(z)$, is incorporated as a regularization term (with coefficient $\beta$) in the RL objective. The IB encourages the policy to be robust to uncertainty in dynamics by limiting the information it relies on from the exact dynamics parameters, preventing overfitting to specific simulated dynamics. This creates a trade-off: higher $\beta$ leads to more robust but less adaptable policies, while lower $\beta$ results in less robust but more adaptable policies.
- **Real-World Transfer (Search for $z^*$):** Once trained in simulation, the policy is adapted to the real robot by searching for an optimal latent encoding $z^*$ that maximizes the policy's return on the physical system.
- **Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR):** This search is performed using Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR), a sample-efficient off-policy RL algorithm.
 - **Process:** Starting with an initial search distribution over $z$, the system samples a latent encoding $z_k$, executes an episode on the real robot, and records the return $R_k$.
 - **Update:** AWR then updates the search distribution (modeled as a Gaussian $\omega_k(z)$) by assigning higher likelihoods to samples $z_i$ that yielded higher "advantages" (returns relative to an average baseline). This process is iterated for a small number of real-world trials (approximately 50 episodes), converging to an optimal $z^*$ for the real robot.

This three-stage methodology provides a comprehensive pipeline, moving from raw animal motion data to agile, real-world robotic skills, leveraging the strengths of imitation learning, domain randomization, and an innovative latent space adaptation technique with an information bottleneck.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed framework on the Laikago robot, an 18-DoF quadruped.

#### 4.1. Learned Skills and Agility

- **Diverse Repertoire:** The system successfully learned a wide variety of dynamic locomotion skills by imitating motion capture data from real dogs and artist-animated motions. These include:
 - Different locomotion gaits: Pacing (legs on same side move in unison) and Trotting (diagonal pairs of legs move together).
 - Backwards gaits: Learned by simply playing mocap clips backward.
 - Agile turns and spins: Demonstrated highly dynamic maneuvers like a 90-degree "Hop-Turn" mid-air.
- **Enhanced Agility:** The learned gaits achieved significantly faster speeds than the manufacturer's manually-designed controller. For instance, the "Dog Trot" policy reached 1.08m/s (compared to 0.84m/s for the manufacturer's fastest gait), and "Backwards Trotting" reached an even higher 1.20m/s. This highlights the potential of imitation learning to unlock more dynamic behaviors.
- **Robustness to Imperfections:** Even with hand-animated motions (which are not physically correct), the policies were able to closely imitate most motions on the real robot, demonstrating robustness.
- **Challenging Motions:** While successful for many skills, some motions, like "Running Man" (backward travel with forward-walking style), proved challenging, resulting in shuffling rather than lifting feet. This indicates limitations in the framework's ability to perfectly reproduce all input motions.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Domain Adaptation

The core claim of the paper—that domain adaptation improves real-world performance—was strongly supported by comparisons to baseline policies:
- **Adaptive vs. Non-Adaptive/Robust Policies:**
 - **Non-Adaptive (No Rand):** Policies trained without domain randomization consistently failed to transfer to the real world for most skills, showcasing the severe sim-to-real gap.
 - **Robust (Domain Randomization only):** Policies trained with domain randomization were better, succeeding for simpler skills (e.g., In-Place Steps, Side-Steps). However, for more dynamic and complex skills (e.g., "Dog Pace," "Dog Spin"), these policies were prone to falling and less consistent.
 - **Adaptive (Ours - with AWR in latent space):** The adaptive policies, after a small number of real-world trials (approx. 50 episodes), significantly outperformed both baselines on most skills. They exhibited superior balance and consistency, often maintaining balance for the maximum episode length (Figure 7), highlighting the crucial role of efficient real-world fine-tuning.
- **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Dynamics:** In simulated environments with dynamics parameters sampled from a *wider range* than those seen during training (out-of-distribution), the adaptive policies consistently achieved higher returns across more diverse dynamics than non-adaptive policies (Figure 8). This suggests that the adaptation process not only bridges the sim-to-real gap but also improves generalization to unforeseen dynamic variations.
- **Sample Efficiency:** The learning curves (Figure 9) demonstrated that policies could adapt to new simulated environments (and by extension, the real world) in a relatively small number of episodes, validating the sample efficiency of their AWR-based latent space adaptation.

#### 4.3. Impact of Information Bottleneck

- **Robustness vs. Adaptability Trade-off:** The ablation study on the $\beta$ coefficient (Lagrange multiplier for the information penalty) in the information bottleneck revealed a clear trade-off (Figure 10):
 - **Higher $\beta$:** Policies are forced to rely less on precise dynamics information, resulting in higher performance *before adaptation* (more robust initial policy). However, they also showed *smaller improvements after adaptation* (less adaptable).
 - **Smaller $\beta$:** Policies rely more on dynamics information, leading to lower performance *before adaptation* (less robust initially). But they exhibited *larger performance improvements after adaptation* (more adaptable).
- **Overall Improvement:** Crucially, policies trained *with* the information bottleneck (all $\beta$ values) generally achieved better performance both before and after adaptation compared to the "No IB" policies (unconstrained latent representation). This confirms the information bottleneck's role in guiding the learning process towards more effective and balanced robustness-adaptability profiles. The authors found $\beta = 10^{-4}$ provided a good balance.

In essence, the findings validate that the proposed imitation learning framework can teach a quadruped robot agile, diverse, and fast locomotion skills from animal motions. The key enabler for real-world deployment is the effective and sample-efficient latent space domain adaptation method, further enhanced by the information bottleneck for a robust-adaptive trade-off.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in robotic locomotion and intelligent control, with wide-ranging potential impacts:

- **Democratization of Robotic Skill Development:** By automating the process of generating complex controllers from high-level motion data (e.g., animal videos, mocap), the framework drastically reduces the manual effort and specialized expertise traditionally required. This could make it easier for researchers and engineers without deep control theory backgrounds to develop sophisticated robotic behaviors, democratizing access to agile robot control.
- **Unlocking New Levels of Agility and Versatility:** The ability to directly imitate animal motions allows robots to achieve agility and fluidity that often surpasses what can be achieved with hand-engineered or even purely RL-trained controllers. This could lead to robots that are more capable of navigating challenging, unstructured environments, performing dynamic maneuvers, and interacting safely and naturally with their surroundings. The demonstrated speed improvements over manufacturer controllers are a tangible example.
- **Robust and Practical Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed latent space adaptation with an information bottleneck offers a highly sample-efficient method for bridging the sim-to-real gap. This is critical for practical robot learning, as it minimizes the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection and retraining. This methodology could become a standard for deploying complex, learned policies on physical hardware.
- **Foundation for More Complex Behaviors:** The framework provides a solid foundation for learning an even broader repertoire of skills. As mentioned by the authors, integrating learning from less structured data like video clips could massively expand the available "training data" for robots, potentially leading to more sophisticated and nuanced behaviors beyond basic locomotion.
- **Advancement in Reinforcement Learning and Domain Adaptation:** The integration of the information bottleneck with latent space adaptation is a notable algorithmic contribution. It offers a principled way to manage the trade-off between robustness and adaptability, which is a fundamental challenge in transferring learned skills to new, unseen environments. This theoretical advancement has implications beyond robotic locomotion for other real-world RL applications.
- **Impact on Human-Robot Interaction and Entertainment:** Robots capable of natural, agile, and expressive movements could significantly enhance human-robot interaction experiences, making robots more intuitive, engaging, and less "clunky." This could find applications in areas like companionship, entertainment (e.g., robotic pets, performers), and even assistive technologies where natural motion is key.

**Potential Limitations and Future Work (as noted by authors):**
- **More Dynamic Behaviors:** The current system still faces challenges with extremely dynamic behaviors like large jumps and runs, indicating a need for further algorithmic and hardware improvements to fully replicate extreme animal agility.
- **Controller Stability:** Learned controllers, while agile, may not yet be as inherently stable as the best manually-designed controllers, suggesting areas for improving robustness for mission-critical applications.
- **Learning from Diverse Data Sources:** Expanding the input data beyond motion capture to more accessible sources like video clips is a promising future direction, which could greatly scale the diversity of learnable behaviors.

Overall, this paper represents a significant leap towards enabling robots to learn and perform agile, natural, and diverse behaviors directly from observed animal motions, greatly simplifying the development process and enhancing their real-world capabilities.
