# Constraint-Aware Intent Estimation for Dynamic Human-Robot Object Co-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the GRASP Laboratory, University of Pennsylvania, developed a framework for dynamic human-robot object co-manipulation that estimates 6 Degrees of Freedom human intent and adapts robot assistance without relying on force-torque sensors. The approach achieved task completion times 83% of standard admittance control and reduced human linear impulse by 24% in experiments involving co-carrying a 4.5kg load.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to intuitively interpret full 6 Degrees of Freedom (position and orientation) human intent in co-manipulation tasks, often relying on predefined goals or translational movements.
- Many existing physical human-robot collaboration methods require force-torque (F/T) sensors, which can provide ambiguous measurements with extended objects and increase hardware complexity.
- Ensuring safe and fluent collaboration, especially with powerful robots, requires dynamic adaptation of robot assistance and consideration of both human and robot physical constraints (e.g., reachability, manipulability).

## Method

- Human intent (goal and motion for 6DoF) is modeled using first-order Dynamical Systems (DS) for both Cartesian position and rotational orientation.
- Online, real-time intent estimation is performed by two independent particle filters (for position and orientation) that use only motion tracking data, not force-torque sensor input.
- A confidence-based variable impedance control scheme adaptively adjusts robot assistance, providing active help when intent is clear and becoming passive otherwise, with particle trimming to ensure human manipulability and robot kinematic constraints are met.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved task completion times **83%** faster than standard admittance control and **97%** faster than a task adaptation baseline (using robot-side F/T sensors) in a 4.5kg object co-manipulation task.
- Human effort was reduced, with the method requiring **24%** less linear impulse and **23%** less angular impulse compared to admittance control.
- The framework demonstrated online adaptation to individual human preferences and successfully operated without force-torque sensors, mitigating issues of ambiguous force measurements experienced by F/T-dependent baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Comprehensive 6DoF human intent, encompassing both goal and desired motion, can be robustly estimated from past motion data alone, effectively bypassing the limitations of force-torque sensors in dynamic co-manipulation scenarios.
> - Dynamical Systems (DS) provide a suitable model for human intent, allowing for combined goal and motion estimation while offering theoretical guarantees for global asymptotic stability.
> - Directly incorporating human manipulability and robot kinematic constraints (reachability, joint limits) into the intent estimation and control process is crucial for generating feasible, comfortable, and safe co-manipulation motions for both agents.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of human-robot interaction (HRI), particularly physical human-robot collaboration (pHRC), is increasingly focused on enabling robots to work intuitively and safely alongside humans. Traditional industrial robots often operate in segregated environments due to safety concerns and high physical capabilities. Conversely, robots designed for human-centric environments are typically lightweight, limiting their ability to perform physically demanding tasks. A key challenge in bridging this gap is developing robots that can provide fluent and safe physical assistance, especially in tasks like co-manipulation of heavy or bulky objects where neither agent can perform the task alone. This often requires the robot to interpret human intent without *a priori* knowledge of the task.

Prior research in pHRC has explored various methods for human intent estimation and motion generation. These include:
- **Learning-based approaches:** Utilizing Gaussian Processes (GP), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Weighted Random Forests (WRF), or other machine learning techniques to predict intent trajectories or classify goals based on past motion data or force inputs. These often require extensive data collection and calibration, which can limit generalizability across different tasks or human subjects.
- **Model-based approaches with online adaptation:** Employing bio-inspired control, variable impedance controllers, or Dynamical Systems (DS) to represent desired motions. Many of these methods rely on force-torque (F/T) sensors and often focus on translational motion, requiring predefined discrete goals.
- **Role adaptation and confidence:** Strategies for the robot to switch between active/passive assistance or leader/follower roles, often based on confidence measures from intent estimators.

Several limitations exist in the current landscape. Many approaches primarily focus on predicting translational motion and goals, with few addressing full 6 Degrees of Freedom (DoF) co-manipulation (3D position and 3D orientation). The "rotation-translation problem" with extended objects often leads to ambiguous F/T sensor measurements, making reliance on such sensors potentially problematic. Furthermore, the need for extensive calibration or data collection hinders rapid deployment and generalization. The proposed work addresses these gaps by presenting a framework that estimates 6DoF intent without F/T sensing, combines goal and motion estimation, and integrates constraint satisfaction for both human and robot capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables robots to physically collaborate and interact with humans in dynamic object co-manipulation tasks by interpreting human intentions in real-time and dynamically adjusting robot actions while considering both robot and human physical constraints. The motivation stems from the desire to enable robots with significant physical capabilities (e.g., those found in factories) to work intuitively and safely with humans, assisting in tasks that are challenging for humans alone.

The authors identify three main challenges that drive the objectives of this work:

1. **Intent Estimation (Goal and Motion):**
 - **Motivation:** Humans can co-manipulate objects solely through physical interaction, even without verbal cues. For fluid and effective collaboration, robots need a similar capability to discern human intent, encompassing both the desired task goal (long-term outcome) and the immediate desired motion (velocities, directions). Current methods often separate these or rely on predefined discrete goals. Moreover, most existing works focus only on translational motion, neglecting the complexities of 6DoF co-manipulation, where rotational intent can be difficult to infer, especially without F/T sensors that can provide ambiguous measurements under extended object manipulation.
 - **Objective:** To predict human intent, including both goal (position and orientation) and motion (linear and angular velocity), in 6DoF co-manipulation tasks using a low-dimensional, parameterized Dynamic Systems (DS) model. This estimation must occur online, in real-time, and crucially, without requiring external force-torque (F/T) sensing.

2. **Role Adaptation (Active/Passive Assistance):**
 - **Motivation:** In co-manipulation with powerful robots, knowing when to provide active assistance versus when to remain passive is critical for safety and user experience. Over-assistance from a high-payload robot can cause injury or discomfort. Existing approaches often use discrete gain adjustments, which may not be smooth or intuitive.
 - **Objective:** To propose a novel confidence-based variable impedance control scheme that fluently adapts the robot's impedance. The robot should offer active assistance only when the intent estimation is confident, and become passive otherwise, ensuring safe and effortless operation.

3. **Constraint Satisfaction (Robot and Human Feasibility):**
 - **Motivation:** Physical coupling between a robot and a human via a co-manipulated object means that the team's motion is constrained by their respective capabilities (e.g., reachability, joint limits, manipulability). Ignoring these can lead to unsafe or uncomfortable interactions. While robot constraints are relatively straightforward, human capability requires careful modeling, such as considering manipulability based on posture.
 - **Objective:** To ensure that kinematic and feasibility constraints for both the robot and the human are considered during intent estimation and task execution. This involves incorporating robot reachability and joint limits, as well as human manipulability, to generate comfortable and feasible co-manipulation motions.

By addressing these challenges collectively, the research aims to enable fluid, safe, and effective physical human-robot collaboration in dynamic 6DoF co-manipulation tasks without relying on F/T sensors or *a priori* task knowledge.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates online intent estimation with adaptive control, structured around a Dynamic Systems (DS) model for human intent, particle filters for estimation, and a variable impedance controller for robot assistance.

#### 3.1. Modeling Human Intent as a Dynamical System

Human intent for object co-manipulation is modeled as a goal-oriented first-order autonomous Dynamical System (DS) in both Cartesian (position) and Rotational (orientation) spaces. This model is chosen for its ability to combine motion and goal estimation, approximate accuracy, simple parameterization, and amenability to compliant control.
- **Cartesian DS:** $\dot{p} = A_p(p - p^*)$, where $p$ is the object's position, $p^*$ is the desired position attractor, and $A_p \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is a diagonal dynamics matrix that is negative definite ($A_p \prec 0$). This ensures global asymptotic stability (GAS) towards $p^*$.
- **Rotational DS:** $\omega = A_o k_q(q, q^*) \log(q \otimes \bar{q}^*)$, where $\omega$ is the angular velocity, $q$ is the object's orientation quaternion, $q^*$ is the desired orientation attractor, and $A_o \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is a diagonal dynamics matrix ($A_o \prec 0$). $k_q(q, q^*)$ is a scalar term ensuring the log map is well-defined, and $\log(q \otimes \bar{q}^*)$ computes the quaternion error. This rotational DS is also proven to be globally asymptotically stable towards $q^*$.

The core idea is to estimate the unknown parameters of these DS models, namely the attractors ($p^*, q^*$) and the dynamics matrices ($A_p, A_o$), online and in real-time.

#### 3.2. Capability-Aware Intent Estimation using Particle Filters

Two independent particle filters are employed: one for the Cartesian DS (estimating $\zeta_p = [A_p, p^*]$) and one for the Rotational DS (estimating $\zeta_o = [A_o, q^*]$).
- **DS Parameter Estimation:** The particle filters use a zero dynamics model for intent parameters (i.e., parameters are assumed constant unless influenced by noise). Observations are derived from the object's measured linear velocity ($\dot{p}$) and acceleration ($\ddot{p}$), and angular velocity ($\omega$) and acceleration ($\alpha$). Crucially, **force-torque sensor data is not used for observation**, making the approach robust to ambiguous force measurements and applicable in a wider range of scenarios. Weights for each particle are computed based on how well its predicted velocities and accelerations match the observed ones, using exponential functions with tunable weights. The estimated intent states ($\hat{\zeta}_p, \hat{\zeta}_o$) are obtained by a weighted average of the particles' states. The estimated DS parameters are guaranteed to be globally asymptotically stable.
- **Confidence of Parameter Estimation:** To determine when the robot should provide active assistance, a confidence measure ($c_p, c_o$) is calculated for each DS. Instead of relying on probability mass, which might be overly confident even with external forces, the confidence is based on integrated velocity tracking errors ($e_p, e_o$). If tracking errors are consistently high (e.g., due to human input), confidence decreases, allowing the human to lead. The confidence values are integrated over a time window and clipped to $$.
- **Human Manipulability Guided Process Noise:** To incorporate human capabilities, the process noise terms ($n_p, n_o$) for the particle filters are dynamically adjusted. When confidence is low, noise increases, allowing the filters to explore a wider state space for intent. Additionally, for the Cartesian DS, the noise covariance is reshaped locally by the human's manipulability ellipsoid, extracted from their posture. This is based on the observation that human directional motion preferences align with manipulability ellipsoids, guiding the estimated goal distribution.
- **Constraint-aware Particle Trimming (Motion and Goal Feasibility):** To ensure estimated goals and motions are feasible for the robot and human, a particle trimming scheme is applied. For each particle's proposed motion ($\hat{\dot{p}}, \hat{\omega}$) and goal state ($\hat{p}^*, \hat{q}^*$), an inverse kinematics (IK) solver (Curobo) checks if these are kinematically feasible for the robot. If not, the particle's weight is set to zero, effectively removing infeasible intents from the estimation. This ensures the estimated intent is within the robot's operational workspace and joint limits.

#### 3.3. Confidence-based Variable Impedance Control

A variable impedance control law is proposed to track the estimated desired velocities from the DS model.
- **Control Law:** The robot's control input wrench ($u_{DS}$) is defined as a negative velocity feedback controller: $u_{DS} = -\begin{bmatrix} D_p(c_p(t)) & 0 \\ 0 & D_o(c_o(t)) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \dot{p} - \hat{\dot{p}} \\ \omega - \hat{\omega} \end{bmatrix}$. The damping matrices $D_p, D_o$ are proportional to the confidence values $c_p, c_o$, and a set of constant damping gains $\Lambda_p, \Lambda_o$. This means the robot's damping (and thus its resistance to human input) increases linearly with confidence. When confidence is zero, the robot is fully passive (only gravity compensation).
- **Apparent Damping and Stiffness:** The control law's effect on interaction is analyzed by deriving the apparent damping ($D_h$) and stiffness ($K_h$) experienced by the human. Damping depends on both the confidence and its rate of change, while stiffness depends on confidence and the estimated DS matrices. This formulation allows the robot to adapt its "feel" dynamically.
- **Energy Analysis and Passivity:** The closed-loop dynamics of the robot-object system are analyzed using a storage function that includes kinetic and potential energy. The system's stability and passivity are evaluated under different confidence scenarios.
 - When confidence is constant (0 or 1) or decreasing, the system is passive or stable (energy-dissipating).
 - When confidence is increasing, the robot can temporarily inject energy into the system, potentially losing passivity for a short duration. This "stiffening" behavior is described as desirable for a proactive robot. The rate of energy injection is bounded and tunable via the confidence ascent rate parameter ($d_p$).

#### 3.4. Capability-Aware Control: Robot Motion Feasibility

To ensure the robot respects its physical limits during active assistance and passive compensation, a joint-limits-aware torque controller is used.
- **Joint Limit Avoidance:** Repulsive potential fields are generated as torques ($\tau_{lim}$) that activate when joints approach their limits, preventing violations.
- **Nullspace Control:** For redundant manipulators (like the 7-DoF Kuka-iiwa), a secondary task torque ($\tau_N$) is projected into the nullspace of the Jacobian. This allows the robot to maintain a familiar or nominal joint configuration while executing the primary task, enhancing intuitive interaction for the human.
- The total torque command to the robot is a sum of the joint limit torques, nullspace torques, and the primary task torques derived from the desired Cartesian input wrench ($u_d^r$).

This comprehensive approach combines robust online intent estimation, adaptive impedance control, and constraint satisfaction for both agents, all without relying on a force-torque sensor at the robot end-effector.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed framework was validated on a real-world human-robot co-manipulation task using a Kuka iiwa-14 robot. The task involved co-carrying a 4.5kg load towards a 3D goal pose (longitudinal position, lateral position, and roll angle) from a central starting point. The robot operated without prior knowledge of the goal pose.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Robot:** Kuka iiwa-14, a 7-DoF torque-controlled manipulator.
- **Object:** A 4.5kg load.
- **Tracking:** OptiTrack motion capture system for human pose and object kinematics.
- **Goal Presentation:** Goal pose displayed on a TV for the human partner.
- **Task Dimensions:** The task focused on 3 DoF (x, y positions, and roll orientation), with other DoF (z position, pitch, yaw) controlled to predefined values.
- **Subjects:** Three subjects participated, each completing 20 trials for each method (20 distinct goal poses).
- **Metrics:** Task Completion Time (time to reach within 0.13m/35deg of goal with combined velocity < 0.1), Human Effort (linear and angular impulse, average force, and average torque, measured by an F/T sensor on the human handle).

#### 4.2. Baselines

The proposed method was compared against three baselines:
1. **Standard Admittance Controller (F/T on robot side):** A common approach where external force translates into desired motion, tuned for intuitive feel with the load.
2. **Task Adaptation (F/T on robot side):** A method that adapts between a few known goals (home pose, ground truth goal) and uses an admittance component, deciding influence based on a power filter. This baseline represents an upper bound as it has knowledge of the ground truth goal.
3. **Task Adaptation (F/T on human side):** An unrealistic but informative baseline to assess the impact of F/T sensor placement and accuracy, by simulating a perfect F/T measurement.

#### 4.3. Quantitative Results

Table I summarizes the mean performance metrics across all subjects and 20 trials per method.
- **Task Completion Time:** The proposed method achieved mean task completion times of 5.6s, 5.2s, and 6.0s for subjects 1, 2, and 3, respectively. When compared to baselines over all trials (Fig. 5), the proposed method required **83%** of the time compared to Admittance, **97%** compared to Task Adaptation (F/T robot side), and **92%** compared to Task Adaptation (F/T human side). This indicates generally faster task execution than admittance control and comparable or slightly faster execution than task adaptation.
- **Human Linear Impulse:** The proposed method required mean linear impulses of 38.9 Ns, 44.3 Ns, and 65.7 Ns. Compared to baselines (Fig. 6, left), it required **76%** of the impulse of Admittance, **99%** of Task Adaptation (F/T robot side), and **138%** of Task Adaptation (F/T human side). This shows lower linear impulse for the proposed method compared to Admittance and Task Adaptation with F/T on the robot side. The higher impulse compared to the F/T on the human side for Task Adaptation suggests that precise and direct force sensing on the human side can lead to more efficient (lower impulse) control.
- **Human Angular Impulse:** Mean angular impulses were 7.7 Nms, 8.3 Nms, and 10.7 Nms. Compared to baselines (Fig. 6, right), it required **77%** of Admittance, **104%** of Task Adaptation (F/T robot side), and **127%** of Task Adaptation (F/T human side). Similar to linear impulse, the proposed method generally performed better than Admittance and Task Adaptation with robot-side F/T, but less efficiently than the ideal human-side F/T setup for Task Adaptation.
- **Average Force and Torque:** The proposed method exhibited average forces of 6.5 N, 6.5 N, and 7.4 N, and average torques of 0.40 Nm, 0.57 Nm, and 0.66 Nm. Compared to baselines (Fig. 7), it required **80%** of the average force of Admittance, **88%** of Task Adaptation (F/T robot side), and **157%** of Task Adaptation (F/T human side). For average torque, it required **71%** of Admittance, **96%** of Task Adaptation (F/T robot side), and **126%** of Task Adaptation (F/T human side). These metrics generally align with the impulse results, indicating lower average effort for the proposed method compared to baselines where F/T is on the robot side.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Results and Observations

- **Maneuverability and User Experience:** Participants subjectively reported reduced physical strain and a smoother collaboration experience with the proposed method. It allowed for higher velocities with lower wrench input compared to admittance, making guidance easier.
- **Dynamic Adaptation:** The proposed method's online estimation of the dynamics matrix allowed it to adapt to individual subject preferences, addressing a limitation of Task Adaptation where fixed dynamics could lead to slower or less responsive behavior.
- **F/T Sensor Limitations:** A recurring issue with baselines using F/T sensors on the robot side was the measurement of "non-existent forces" under heavy loads, even after careful system identification. This degraded the performance of admittance-based methods, often requiring constant human force input to keep the object still. While Task Adaptation with a power filter mitigated this, the proposed method bypasses this issue entirely by not using F/T sensors for intent estimation.

In summary, the proposed constraint-aware intent estimation framework generally outperformed admittance control in terms of efficiency (task completion time, human effort) and provided comparable or improved performance over task adaptation methods, particularly when considering the challenges associated with F/T sensor placement and heavy loads. Its ability to adapt dynamics online and operate without F/T sensors was identified as a key advantage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The presented framework represents an advancement in physical human-robot collaboration, particularly for dynamic, 6DoF object co-manipulation. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Comprehensive 6DoF Intent Estimation:** The work addresses the gap in 6DoF co-manipulation by combining both goal and motion intent estimation for position and orientation, utilizing a quaternion-based Dynamical Systems model. This allows for a more complete understanding of human intent compared to methods limited to translational motion or discrete goals.
- **Elimination of Force-Torque Sensors for Intent:** A core contribution is the framework's ability to estimate human intent solely from past motion data and tracking errors, without relying on external force-torque (F/T) sensors. This mitigates issues such as ambiguous measurements during extended object co-manipulation, reduces hardware complexity and cost, and enables more natural interaction across the robot's body or the object, rather than just at a sensorized end-effector. This makes the approach more robust and broadly applicable to various robot platforms and interaction scenarios.
- **Adaptive and Safe Collaboration:** The confidence-based variable impedance control scheme allows the robot to adapt its assistance level fluently, transitioning between passive and active roles based on the certainty of its intent estimation. This enhances safety, particularly with powerful robots, by preventing over-assistance when intent is unclear, while still enabling proactive leading behavior when confident.
- **Constraint-Awareness for Human and Robot:** By integrating human manipulability measures and robot kinematic constraints (reachability, joint limits) directly into the particle filter estimation and control, the framework ensures that generated motions and goals are feasible and comfortable for both the human and the robot. This contributes to safer and more ergonomic collaboration.
- **Improved Performance and User Experience:** Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method can reduce human effort and task completion time compared to traditional admittance control. Subjective feedback from participants indicates a smoother and less physically demanding collaborative experience.
- **Theoretical Foundation:** The framework provides theoretical guarantees for closed-loop stability and passivity under specific conditions, which is crucial for safety-critical pHRC applications. The analysis of apparent damping and stiffness provides insight into the robot's dynamic behavior during interaction.

The potential impact of this research is significant for various applications requiring close physical human-robot teamwork, such as manufacturing, logistics, healthcare (e.g., patient transfer, rehabilitation), and assistive robotics for daily living. By enabling robots to robustly interpret complex human intentions without specialized sensors, the framework can facilitate more intuitive, efficient, and safer co-manipulation of heavy or unwieldy objects, ultimately extending the capabilities of both humans and robots in shared workspaces.

Future work directions include dynamically adjusting confidence ascent rates online to further optimize the balance between disturbance rejection and maneuverability, and extending the framework to mobile manipulators for collaborative transport over longer distances.
