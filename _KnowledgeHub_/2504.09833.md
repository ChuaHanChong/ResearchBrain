# PPF: Pre-training and Preservative Fine-tuning of Humanoid Locomotion via Model-Assumption-based Regularization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Georgia Institute of Technology and Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology present the PPF framework for humanoid locomotion, integrating pre-training with a novel Model-Assumption-based Regularization (MAR) during reinforcement learning fine-tuning. This approach mitigates catastrophic forgetting, enabling a Digit humanoid robot to achieve robust locomotion at speeds up to 1.5 m/s on diverse and challenging terrains.

## Problem

- Humanoid locomotion is inherently complex due to high-dimensional, hybrid dynamics, requiring robust and adaptive control for diverse environments.
- Existing two-stage learning methods (imitation followed by RL fine-tuning) often suffer from "catastrophic forgetting," where fine-tuning erases stable behaviors learned from a model-based controller.
- Model-based controllers provide stability but have limited adaptability to complex scenarios, while pure reinforcement learning policies demand extensive engineering and often struggle with sim-to-real transfer.

## Method

- A pre-training stage uses ==Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)== to imitate an Angular Momentum Linear Inverted Pendulum (ALIP) model-based controller, establishing fundamental stable gait behaviors in a neural network policy.
- Reinforcement learning (PPO) fine-tuning enhances the pre-trained policy's capabilities for higher speeds and diverse terrains, incorporating curriculum learning and domain randomization.
- Model-Assumption-based Regularization (MAR) is introduced during fine-tuning, dynamically weighting the regularization term based on how much the robot's state (e.g., vertical CoM velocity) violates the underlying model assumptions of the model-based controller.

## Results

- PPF achieved a maximum forward walking speed of **1.5** m/s on flat terrain and maintained a **100%** success rate on slippery surfaces, outperforming model-based control (**0.5** m/s) and other baselines.
- The framework demonstrated superior robustness, successfully traversing all levels of sloped and uneven terrains in simulation, and exhibited zero-shot deployment capabilities on diverse outdoor terrains (sloped, uneven, sandy) on a physical Digit robot.
- MAR effectively prevented catastrophic forgetting by dynamically adjusting the regularization weight, leading to lower tracking errors and improved performance in scenarios where model assumptions were violated compared to constant regularization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Catastrophic forgetting in humanoid two-stage learning can be mitigated by adaptively regulating the policy's deviation from model-based control actions based on model assumption validity.
> - Dynamically reducing regularization when model assumptions are violated allows the policy to learn novel, more effective behaviors beyond the model-based controller's limitations.
> - Integrating model-based knowledge as a context-dependent guide, rather than a strict constraint, enables efficient learning of both stable gaits and adaptive responses to complex environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid locomotion is a complex challenge due to its high-dimensional, hybrid dynamics and the necessity for robots to adapt to diverse and often unpredictable environments. The broader research landscape in this field has seen developments across several methodologies:

**Model-Based Control:** Traditional approaches often employ simplified models, such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) or Angular Momentum Linear Inverted Pendulum (ALIP), to compute foot placement and ensure stability. These are frequently integrated with optimization-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) to enhance constraint handling. While effective in structured environments, these methods can exhibit limited adaptability in complex settings. Using more accurate full-order models often incurs high computational costs or sacrifices real-time accuracy. Furthermore, the hybrid nature of bipedal locomotion complicates optimization formulations, often restricting them to fixed contact sequences.

**Learning-Based Approaches (Reinforcement Learning - RL):** More recently, deep reinforcement learning has been applied to bipedal locomotion, building on its success in quadrupedal systems. These methods train control policies by optimizing data-driven objective functions. While demonstrating enhanced robustness and agility, they typically require extensive and intricate reward engineering, long training times, and can suffer from a lack of interpretability, making interactive tuning difficult. A significant challenge for learning-based methods is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation may not perform well on physical hardware without further adaptation. Domain randomization is a common technique to mitigate this, but it can be sample-inefficient and challenging to tune.

**Hybrid (Model-Based and Learning-Based) Approaches:** To leverage the strengths of both paradigms, researchers have explored combining model-based and learning-based methods. These hybrid strategies have shown promise in areas like contact planning for humanoids. However, some implementations require the model-based controller to run continuously in the background during execution, which can be computationally expensive at runtime.

**Two-Stage Learning (Imitation then RL Fine-tuning):** A particular hybrid strategy gaining traction, especially in quadrupedal locomotion, involves a two-stage process. First, a model-based controller's behavior is imitated by a neural network policy (behavior cloning/pre-training). Subsequently, this pre-trained policy is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to enhance performance and robustness. This approach aims to efficiently learn robust policies and acquire periodic, symmetric gaits without extensive reward shaping.

**Identified Gap and Contribution:** This paper identifies a critical limitation within the two-stage learning process when applied to humanoids: *catastrophic forgetting*. During the RL fine-tuning phase, the policy can significantly deviate from the stable, pre-trained motions inherited from the model-based controller, leading to performance degradation, particularly given the inherent instability of humanoid dynamics. The current work, PPF, directly addresses this gap by introducing a novel framework that enhances the two-stage learning process. It aims to train a robust humanoid locomotion policy that not only achieves higher speed and robustness but also preserves the symmetric and periodic gaits of the model-based controller by actively mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This is achieved through an adaptive regularization mechanism that leverages model assumptions during fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel learning framework, termed PPF (Pre-training and Preservative Fine-tuning), for training a humanoid locomotion policy. This framework is designed to effectively imitate a model-based controller's behavior while extending its capabilities to handle more complex locomotion tasks, such as higher velocities and challenging terrains.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in humanoid locomotion control:

1. **Complexity and High-Dimensionality:** Humanoid robots possess complex, high-dimensional dynamics and hybrid contact interactions, making robust locomotion control difficult.
2. **Limitations of Model-Based Controllers (MBCs):** While MBCs provide stability and interpretable gaits, they often rely on simplified models and well-defined environments, limiting their adaptability to diverse and unpredictable real-world scenarios, particularly at higher speeds or on irregular terrains. Adjusting their parameters for extended capabilities often requires significant manual effort.
3. **Limitations of Learning-Based Approaches:** Pure reinforcement learning (RL) policies, while capable of achieving agility, typically require extensive reward engineering and prolonged training. Crucially, they can struggle with the sim-to-real gap and may develop unnatural or unstable behaviors that do not transfer well to hardware.
4. **Catastrophic Forgetting in Two-Stage Learning:** The existing two-stage learning paradigm (imitation followed by RL fine-tuning), which has shown promise in quadrupedal locomotion, faces a significant issue in humanoids: catastrophic forgetting. During RL fine-tuning, the policy tends to "forget" the stable, symmetric, and periodic motion style learned from the MBC during pre-training. This deviation can lead to unstable dynamics, degraded performance, and poor transferability to hardware, as the learned adaptations may overfit to the training environment. For humanoids, whose dynamics are inherently less stable than quadrupeds, preserving the fundamental motion style is particularly critical.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the PPF framework are:
- To enable the training of a robust humanoid locomotion policy that can achieve speeds up to 1.5 m/s and navigate diverse challenging terrains (slippery, sloped, uneven, sandy).
- To ensure the policy effectively imitates the beneficial symmetric and periodic gaits of a model-based controller during an initial pre-training phase.
- To introduce a mechanism, Model-Assumption-based Regularization (MAR), during the subsequent RL fine-tuning phase to prevent catastrophic forgetting. MAR is designed to dynamically align the policy with the MBC's actions specifically when the robot's state adheres to the MBC's underlying model assumptions.
- To allow the policy to learn beyond the MBC's limitations when its model assumptions are violated, thereby extending capabilities to scenarios the MBC alone cannot handle.
- To validate the effectiveness of the PPF framework through comprehensive simulation tests and real-world hardware experiments on a full-size humanoid robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PPF framework integrates three core components: pre-training, reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning, and Model-Assumption-based Regularization (MAR).

**A. Background Model-Based Controller (MBC)**
The framework commences with a pre-existing model-based controller for humanoid locomotion, specifically the controller developed by Shamsah et al.. This MBC consists of two main parts:
- **Foot Placement Controller:** Utilizes the Angular Momentum Linear Inverted Pendulum (ALIP) model. This reduced-order model simplifies bipedal dynamics, assuming a constant Center of Mass (CoM) height (z = z̄) and zero vertical velocity (ż = 0) and acceleration (z̈ = 0). It calculates desired foot placement based on one-step-ahead prediction using current angular momentum and desired CoM velocity.
- **Passivity-Based Whole-Body Inverse Dynamics Controller:** Given the desired foot placement, this controller generates smooth joint trajectories by solving full-body inverse kinematics. It then computes desired joint torques by solving linearized inverse dynamics and applies a passivity-based feedback control for stabilization.

**B. Imitation of the Model-Based Controller (Pre-training)**
In the initial stage, the expert MBC's knowledge is transferred to a learnable neural network policy using behavior cloning with Dataset Aggregation (DAgger). The DAgger loss minimizes the squared Euclidean norm between the actions generated by the expert MBC and those predicted by the neural network policy. This process yields a pre-trained policy (μ_θ) that emulates the MBC's fundamental behaviors, serving as a robust starting point for further learning.

**C. Fine-tuning of the Pre-trained Policy with Reinforcement Learning (RL)**
Following pre-training, the policy's performance is refined using reinforcement learning, specifically the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. This stage aims to enhance the policy's capabilities beyond simple imitation, such as achieving higher speeds and greater robustness in diverse environments.
- **Curriculum Learning:** To facilitate learning and generalize across different scenarios, a velocity curriculum is applied for forward movement, and a terrain curriculum (including uphill, downhill, flat, and uneven terrains) is used.
- **Domain Randomization:** To bridge the sim-to-real gap, the training process incorporates randomization of dynamic parameters and adds noise to observations.
- **Reward Function:** A simple reward structure is designed to promote desired behaviors, primarily tracking target linear and angular velocities. Additionally, penalties are applied for excessive joint torques and base motion to encourage efficient and stable movements (Table I).
- **Lipschitz Continuity Penalty:** To mitigate motor vibrations, a Lipschitz continuity penalty is introduced, similar to Chen et al.. This penalty is applied to the norm of the gradient of the network's mean output (∇_s μ_θ(s)), ensuring smoother control actions.

**D. Model-Assumption-based Regularization (MAR)**
To counteract catastrophic forgetting during RL fine-tuning and improve upon direct regularization approaches, the PPF framework integrates MAR.
- **Motivation for MAR:** A straightforward regularization approach (FullReg), which uses a constant weight to enforce similarity between policy actions and MBC actions, can limit the policy's ability to surpass the MBC's performance. The MBC's actions might be suboptimal or even unstable when its underlying model assumptions are violated.
- **Dynamic Regularization:** MAR addresses this by adaptively adjusting the weight of the regularization term based on how much the current robot state violates the MBC's model assumptions.
- **Assumption Violation Criterion:** For the ALIP-based MBC, the critical model assumptions include constant CoM height and zero vertical velocity. Therefore, the vertical velocity of the robot's base (ż(s)) is chosen as the primary criterion to determine model assumption violation.
- **Weighting Function:** A continuous weighting function, w(s) = w_0 * e^(-ż(s)^2 / δ), is applied. Here, w_0 is a coefficient and δ is a smoothing parameter. This function dynamically reduces the regularization weight as the vertical velocity (and thus the model assumption violation) increases. This allows the RL fine-tuning to prioritize novel behaviors that address the violation (e.g., adapting to uneven terrain or high-speed dynamics) rather than strictly adhering to potentially suboptimal MBC actions. Conversely, when the robot's state aligns well with the model assumptions (low vertical velocity), a higher regularization weight encourages the policy to maintain the MBC's stable behaviors.
- **Combined Loss Function:** The final PPF loss function (L_PPF) combines the PPO loss with the adaptively weighted regularization term: L_PPF = L_PPO + E[(w(s) ||a_E - μ_θ(s)||^2)^2].

**E. Experimental Setup**
- **Robot Platform:** Experiments were conducted on the Agility Robotics Digit, a full-size humanoid robot.
- **Simulators:** MuJoCo was used for training and initial testing, while AR-Sim (a company-provided, high-fidelity simulator) was used for sim-to-sim transfer evaluations, closely mirroring the physical robot's dynamics.
- **Computational Resources:** Training was performed on a GeForce RTX 4090 GPU and an Intel i9-14900K CPU. The DAgger stage required approximately 5.5 hours, and the fine-tuning stage approximately 31.5 hours.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the PPF framework involved comprehensive simulation tests and hardware experiments on the Digit humanoid robot, comparing it against several baselines: MBC (model-based control), IFM (Imitation and Fine-tuning Model Predictive Control, without MAR), FullReg (full regularization with constant weight), and PureRL (pure reinforcement learning).

**A. Training Environment (MuJoCo) Experiments**
- **Robustness on Sloped and Uneven Terrains:** PPF and PureRL exhibited the best performance, successfully traversing all levels of both sloped and uneven terrains within the allotted time.
- **IFM Limitations:** IFM frequently failed on uneven terrain due to unexpected foot contacts, leading to falls caused by lateral and angular disturbances, indicating instability under challenging conditions.
- **FullReg Performance:** FullReg demonstrated robustness similar to PPF in terms of completing terrains, but its tracking performance degraded under model-assumption violations, causing it to time out before reaching the goal.
- **PureRL Motion Quality:** While PureRL completed the terrains, its policy was observed to be overfitted to the simulation environment, resulting in degraded motion quality, which raised concerns about its transferability.

**B. Sim-to-Sim Transfer (AR-Sim) Experiments**
- **Uphill Robustness:** PPF, IFM, and FullReg demonstrated superior robustness, achieving a 100% success rate across 10°, 12°, and 14° uphill platforms. In contrast, MBC consistently failed (0% success), and PureRL's success rate significantly decreased with steeper slopes (85% on 10°, 60% on 12°, 0% on 14°). This highlighted the benefit of MBC imitation for transferability.
- **Tracking Accuracy:** Among the methods with 100% success, PPF achieved the lowest tracking errors and showed the smallest increase in error as the slope angle increased, indicating better adaptive control. FullReg exhibited higher tracking errors compared to PPF, suggesting that constant regularization limited its ability to adapt when model assumptions were violated.
- **Random Velocity Tracking:** PPF outperformed all baselines in tracking random linear and angular velocity commands on flat terrain, achieving the highest mean undiscounted return for tracking rewards. IFM performed worse than other regularization-based methods due to stability issues (tripping) when confronted with sudden command changes. PureRL exhibited the worst performance in AR-Sim, even underperforming MBC, further emphasizing the importance of distilling MBC motion for domain shifts.

**C. Hardware Experiments (Digit Robot)**
- **Maximum Forward Velocity:** PPF achieved a maximum forward walking speed of 1.5 m/s on flat terrain. This significantly surpassed FullReg (1.1 m/s) and MBC (0.5 m/s). IFM was deemed unsafe and halted at 1.0 m/s due to stability issues, and PureRL's velocity could not be measured reliably due to poor tracking.
- **Step-and-Slip Platform:** On a whiteboard covered with poppy seeds or olive oil (inducing slips), PPF maintained a 100% success rate over five trials at 0.3 m/s. MBC and FullReg failed all trials due to slipping. IFM had a 40% success rate but demonstrated unsafe behavior. PureRL failed completely, with its foot becoming stuck on the surface.
- **Outdoor Terrains:** Both PPF and FullReg successfully traversed diverse outdoor terrains, including sloped, uneven, and deformable sandy surfaces, demonstrating zero-shot deployment capabilities for terrains not explicitly included in training. MBC showed limited robustness outdoors. IFM and PureRL were not deployed outdoors due to safety concerns from indoor tests.

**D. Effect of MAR**
- **Adaptive Weight Distribution during Training:** MAR dynamically adjusted the regularization weight based on vertical velocity, indicating model assumption violation. On flat terrain, where violations were infrequent, a large proportion of MBC actions were deemed reliable, leading to higher regularization weights. Conversely, on uphill terrain, increased violations resulted in lower regularization weights, allowing the policy to learn deviations from suboptimal MBC actions.
- **Performance under Violation during Testing:** During the 14-degree uphill test, the policy trained with MAR (PPF) maintained consistent performance even when model-assumption violations were pronounced. In contrast, FullReg showed a notable increase in linear velocity tracking error as violations became more frequent.
- **Generalization:** MAR's benefits were observed not only on challenging terrains but also in simpler scenarios like high-speed locomotion on flat terrain and slippery surfaces, suggesting that its ability to ignore irrelevant or suboptimal expert samples contributed to more generalized and robust performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PPF framework presents a significant advancement in humanoid locomotion control by effectively addressing the challenge of catastrophic forgetting within a two-stage imitation and reinforcement learning paradigm. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Humanoid Locomotion Capabilities:** PPF enables humanoid robots to achieve performance metrics (e.g., 1.5 m/s forward speed) and robustness across diverse and challenging terrains (slippery, sloped, uneven, sandy) that exceed those of prior methods, including pure model-based or standard hybrid approaches. This directly contributes to realizing the potential of humanoids to operate in complex, human-centric environments.
2. **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting:** The core innovation of Model-Assumption-based Regularization (MAR) provides a solution to the critical problem of catastrophic forgetting during RL fine-tuning. By dynamically adjusting regularization strength based on the validity of model assumptions, PPF preserves the beneficial characteristics (e.g., stable, periodic gaits) of model-based control while allowing the policy to adapt and learn new, more effective behaviors in situations where model assumptions are violated.
3. **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The framework’s ability to maintain a robust, learned motion style and adapt to real-world complexities in a zero-shot manner (without further hardware fine-tuning) significantly enhances the practicality of learning-based control for physical humanoid robots. This reduces the often-prohibitive cost and time associated with real-world data collection and iterative hardware testing.
4. **Principled Hybrid Learning:** PPF offers a more principled way to combine the strengths of model-based control (stability, interpretability) with reinforcement learning (adaptability, robustness). It moves beyond simply running an MBC in the backend or using constant regularization, providing a mechanism for intelligent knowledge distillation and extension. This adaptive regularization is more direct and intuitive than extensive, often trial-and-error, reward engineering.
5. **Foundation for Future Research and Applications:**
 - **Broader Model Assumptions:** The concept of MAR can be extended to incorporate a wider range of model assumptions, such as estimated foot slipperiness or contact force models, which could further improve policy robustness and adaptability in more dynamic scenarios.
 - **Sophisticated MBC Integration:** Applying MAR with more advanced and complex model-based controllers could reveal whether stronger prior knowledge during pre-training leads to even greater training efficiency and final performance.
 - **Dynamic Behaviors:** The framework could be adapted for more agile and high-impact motions like running or jumping by refining the model assumptions and regularization techniques to handle faster dynamics and increased instability. This would broaden the applicability of learning-based control for versatile robotic behaviors.

In essence, PPF represents a step towards developing robust, high-performance, and safe locomotion controllers for humanoid robots, bridging the gap between theoretical stability guarantees of model-based methods and the adaptive capabilities of data-driven learning.
