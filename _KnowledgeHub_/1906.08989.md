# Data-Efficient Learning for Sim-to-Real Robotic Grasping using Deep Point Cloud Prediction Networks

> [!summary] Summary
> A two-step framework for table-top instance grasping that needs zero real-world grasping data. A self-supervised point cloud prediction network turns a single RGBD view (plus mask) of a detected object into a full 3D point cloud, trained with multi-view reprojection consistency; a PointNet critic trained only in simulation scores sampled grasps, reaching 61% real-world success, 10% above a 2.5D depth baseline.

## Problem

- End-to-end grasp learning needs massive labeled real-robot interaction data, collected with parallel robot fleets, making deployment to new tasks or settings costly and slow.
- Domain randomization and pixel- or feature-level adaptation still need large amounts of unlabeled real imagery, and adapted models often fail to transfer to a new task.
- Raw depth sensing gives only partial 2.5D geometry and injects noise on dark, transparent, or misaligned surfaces, so sim-trained depth policies degrade badly on real hardware.

## Method

- ==Point cloud prediction network== takes 5 channels (RGBD plus instance mask) from a **Mask R-CNN** detection, with dynamic cropping and cropped-camera-intrinsics input, and regresses a real-scale 3D point set.
- Self-supervised training loss combines a Huber bounding-box term with a projected point-cloud reprojection term against multi-view 2D masks, so no ground-truth 3D annotation is needed for real data.
- Grasping critic adapted from **PointNet** scores a 4-DoF grasp (3D position plus yaw) on points transformed into the gripper frame; cross-entropy-method sampling picks the best grasp at runtime.

## Results

- Real-world instance grasping succeeds **61%** of the time with zero real grasping data, versus **51%** for the 2.5D raw-depth critic, and far above a prior sim-only image-adaptation result of **23.53%**.
- In simulation the 2.5D baseline is slightly better (**68%** vs **64%**), showing the reported real-world gain comes from resisting domain shift rather than better clean-input geometry.
- Shape prediction IOU rises with the number of supervision views: **0.188** to **0.803** on Kitchenware and **0.186** to **0.625** on real data, saturating between 4 views and full views.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inserting an explicit geometric intermediate representation between perception and control can beat direct pixel- or depth-level domain adaptation for sim-to-real transfer.
> - A representation that loses simulation accuracy can still win in deployment: measure transfer gap, not just in-domain score, when picking an interface.
> - Multi-view reprojection is a cheap real-world supervision signal, needing only a few hundred short RGBD snapshot episodes instead of costly robot interaction labels.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly using deep learning, is significantly challenged by the substantial amount of real-world data required for training robust policies. Traditional end-to-end training frameworks necessitate large-scale datasets with interaction labels (e.g., grasp success or failure), which are costly and time-consuming to collect, often requiring multiple robots operating in parallel. This high data cost limits the deployment of such systems to new settings or tasks.

To mitigate this data scarcity, researchers have explored methods leveraging simulated data. However, a significant "sim-to-real gap" exists, where policies trained solely in simulation often fail to transfer effectively to physical robots. Existing approaches to bridge this gap include domain randomization, which generates diverse configurations in simulation, and pixel-level or feature-level domain adaptation techniques. While these methods reduce the need for *labeled* real-world interaction data, they still frequently require considerable amounts of *unlabeled* real-world data for adaptation, and their transferability to novel tasks can be limited.

This work positions itself as an alternative to purely end-to-end training and existing domain adaptation techniques by focusing on learning visual structures as an *intermediate representation* for sim-to-real transfer. It builds upon recent advancements in deep neural networks for predicting 3D structures (e.g., 3D voxel grids, point clouds, triangle meshes) from single-view observations. The paper argues that 3D structure is inherently more useful for shape-based object manipulation tasks like grasping, as it provides geometric information invariant to surface textures or environmental conditions.

Specifically, the authors propose using 3D point clouds as the intermediate representation, contrasting it with other 3D representations like voxels or triangle meshes which can be heavier or suffer from aliasing. While previous work has explored 3D representation learning for robot-object interaction and point set generation, this paper distinguishes itself by focusing on self-supervised reconstruction of *full* 3D point clouds of objects from RGBD inputs and demonstrating its efficacy for robust sim-to-real transfer in grasping, particularly with zero real-world grasping data. It also addresses practical challenges in applying existing 3D prediction models to robotics, such as noise in real-world depth sensors and the need to handle multiple objects in a scene. The approach aims to provide a more data-efficient and robust solution to the sim-to-real transfer problem in robotic grasping.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for table-top instance grasping of a wide variety of objects using *no real-world grasping data*. This addresses the significant challenge of data collection cost and time associated with training deep network policies for robot manipulation in real-world environments.

The motivation stems from several identified limitations in current robotic learning paradigms:
- **High Cost of Real-World Data Collection:** Training deep learning models for robot manipulation typically demands extensive real-world interaction data, including various object arrangements, geometries, textures, materials, and environmental illumination conditions. Collecting such data is costly and time-consuming.
- **Limitations of Simulation-Only Training:** While simulation offers an efficient way to generate data, the "sim-to-real gap" prevents direct transfer of policies to the real world. Existing domain randomization and adaptation methods often still require substantial real-world data for generalization or adaptation, which remains a bottleneck.
- **Inadequacy of 2D Representations:** Policies trained on 2D sensory inputs (RGB or RGBD images) are highly susceptible to variations in appearance, lighting, and sensor noise, contributing to the sim-to-real gap.
- **Challenges with Full 3D Annotations:** Directly obtaining full 3D ground-truth object geometry for supervised learning in real-world scenarios is notoriously difficult, costly, and sensitive to sensor noise and unmodeled non-linear characteristics.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose a two-step learning procedure motivated by the pursuit of a compact, domain-invariant, and semantically interpretable intermediate representation:
1. **Learn a domain-invariant 3D shape representation:** The motivation here is to extract the fundamental geometry of objects, which is less sensitive to appearance variations and environmental noise than raw 2D image data. Point clouds are chosen for this representation because they are lightweight, flexible, describe the full 3D shape (making them inherently invariant to surface textures or environmental conditions), and can directly be used for object localization, simplifying policy training. Self-supervision using multi-view projections is employed to circumvent the need for explicit 3D ground truth in real-world data.
2. **Learn a critic grasping policy in simulation only:** By training the grasping policy solely on the domain-invariant 3D shape representation obtained in the first step, the objective is to leverage the data efficiency of simulation without incurring the real-world data collection costs for grasping. The domain-invariant nature of the representation is hypothesized to facilitate robust transfer from simulation to the real world without further adaptation.

Ultimately, the goal is to achieve data-efficient and robust sim-to-real transfer for robotic grasping, significantly reducing the dependency on real-world interaction data for policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method consists of a two-step learning procedure designed to achieve data-efficient sim-to-real robotic grasping.

### 4. Step 1: Learning Domain-Invariant Representation (Deep Point Cloud Prediction Networks)

This step focuses on learning a domain-invariant 3D point cloud representation of objects from RGBD observations.

- **Input Data:** The system takes as input a set of RGBD observations $\{O_1, O_2, \ldots, O_N\}$, where each observation $O_n \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times 4}$ (RGBD channels) can originate from simulation or the real world. Multiple snapshots of a scene are collected by moving a mobile manipulator around the workspace, allowing an object to be present in several views.
- **Object Detection and Segmentation:** For real-world data, Mask R-CNN is used to detect object bounding boxes $B_{m,n}$ and associated binary masks for each object $m$ in frame $n$. In simulation, bounding boxes are directly obtained. These masks are used to extract depth values associated with specific objects.
- **Self-Supervised Labeling:** To avoid the cost of obtaining full 3D point cloud ground truth in the real world, the framework employs multi-view projections as a self-supervision signal.
 - A deep network predicts a 3D point cloud $\hat{P}$ for a target object.
 - This predicted 3D point cloud $\hat{P}$ is then re-projected back into 2D image space using the camera intrinsic matrix $E$ to obtain a predicted 2D projection $\hat{M}_{m,n}$ and its corresponding bounding box $\hat{B}_{m,n}$.
 - The loss function $L_\theta$ is defined as a combination of Huber loss for bounding boxes ($L_B$) and a projected point-cloud prediction loss ($L_M$) (adapted from), comparing the predicted 2D projections ($\hat{M}_{m,n}, \hat{B}_{m,n}$) against the ground-truth 2D masks and bounding boxes derived from the input RGBD data. This mechanism ensures cross-view consistency, where different input views of the same object should lead to consistent 3D shape reconstructions when accounting for camera transformations.
- **Network Architecture (Point Cloud Prediction Network):**
 - The architecture consists of several encoder-decoder modules and a fully-connected layer, similar to, but with adaptations for real-world robotics:
 1. **Input Channels:** The network accepts 5 input channels: RGBD + Mask (RGBDM). The mask provides crucial information for handling scenes with multiple objects.
 2. **Dynamic Image Cropping:** A dynamic cropping step is applied to the RGBDM channels to focus the network on the target object instance.
 3. **Adapted Camera Intrinsics:** An additional input, representing the camera intrinsic characteristics adapted due to cropping, is fed into the network after the encoder.

### 5. Step 2: Learning Point Cloud-based Grasping Policy

This step utilizes the learned domain-invariant 3D point cloud representation to train a grasping critic network.

- **Critic Network:** A critic network $G(P, s, T_C^R)$ is trained to predict the probability of success for a sample table-top grasp $s \in \mathbb{R}^4$. The grasp sample $s = (p, \psi)$ comprises a 3D gripper position $p$ (with respect to the robot base) and a gripper yaw rotation $\psi$. The input to the critic also includes the predicted point cloud $P$ for the target object and the transformation from the robot base to the camera frame ($T_C^R$).
- **Preprocessing:** Before being fed to the critic network, the predicted point cloud $P$ is transformed into the proposed grasp frame using $P_s = T_s^R T_C^R P$, where $T_s^R$ is calculated from the sample grasp pose $s$. The order of points in the transformed point cloud is then shuffled to enhance robustness to point ordering variations.
- **Network Architecture (Grasping Critic Network):**
 - The network architecture is derived from PointNet.
 - It consists of four fully-connected layers, each followed by a ReLU activation function and BatchNorm.
 - The final layer is a linear layer reducing the output size to 1, followed by a sigmoid activation function to output the grasp success probability.
- **Generating Grasping Data for Training:** Grasping data is collected *exclusively in simulation*.
 - A heuristic grasping policy is used:
 1. The center of volume of the object ($p_{obj}$) is computed from the predicted point cloud.
 2. The translation component of the grasp pose ($p_{grasp}$) is set to $p_{obj}$ plus some random noise.
 3. A yaw angle is randomly sampled from a uniform distribution.
 - Grasp success is evaluated in simulation by moving the robot arm to a pre-grasp pose, then to the target grasp pose $s$, closing the gripper, and lifting the object. Success is determined by checking if the object moves above the table, which is easily verifiable using ground-truth object pose information available in simulation. This data is collected in parallel simulations and used to train the off-policy grasping network.
- **Runtime Grasp Inference:**
 - The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) is employed for derivative-free optimization to find the most suitable grasp pose.
 - CEM iteratively samples a batch of $N_{CEM}$ grasp candidates, evaluates them using the learned critic network, selects $N_{elite}$ best candidates, fits a Gaussian distribution to these elite samples, and then samples a new batch. This process is repeated until a grasp candidate with a success probability above a threshold $\alpha$ is found or a maximum number of iterations is reached.
 - Constraints, such as workspace limits and kinematic feasibility, can be incorporated into the sampling strategy.

### 6. Datasets

- **Shape Prediction Datasets:** Comprised of 187 real-world object instances (8 categories), 1,345 ShapeNet objects (6 categories), and 69 Kitchenware objects for training. Test sets included 20 Kitchenware objects, 43 real-world objects, and 1,000 procedurally generated objects.
- **Simulated Grasping Datasets:** Used Kitchenware (69 objects for training) and Procedural (1,000 objects for training) datasets. Grasping evaluation was performed on 20 held-out Kitchenware objects and 43 real-world objects.
- **Scene Construction:** Both real and simulated environments involved a table, randomized table height, and 4-5 objects placed with arbitrary locations/orientations. A mobile manipulator took 5 RGBD snapshots from different angles. For simulation, 4 virtual viewpoints were added for 360-degree capture.

### 7. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate both the shape prediction performance and the real-world robotic grasping success.

### 8. Evaluation on Shape Prediction

- **Qualitative Results:** The shape prediction model demonstrated the ability to generate meaningful 3D point clouds from single RGBD observations (Figure 6). The generated point clouds were shown to align with the objects when projected from different viewpoints, indicating that the model learned geometric representations beyond simple 2D mask mapping. The model also exhibited generalization capabilities to unseen object categories, such as those from the Procedural dataset.
- **Quantitative Results (IOU):** The performance was measured using the average Intersection over Union (IOU) of 2D projections with ground-truth masks on unseen objects from the Kitchenware and Real test sets.
 - The IOU significantly improved with an increasing number of viewpoints used during training for supervision. For example, on the Kitchenware test set, the IOU increased from 0.188 with 1 view to 0.803 with 4 views. Similarly, for the Real test set, it rose from 0.186 (1 view) to 0.625 (4 views). This highlights the benefit of multi-view self-supervision for learning robust 3D representations.

### 9. Evaluation on Instance Grasping

- **Real-World Grasping Success:** The CEM policy guided by the learned critic model, using the reconstructed 3D point clouds, achieved a **61% instance grasping success rate in real-world execution** on unseen objects. Crucially, this was accomplished using **zero real-world grasping data** for training the policy.
- **Simulated Grasping Success:** In simulation, the same policy achieved a 64% instance grasping success rate.
- **Comparison to Baseline (2.5D Shape Representation):**
 - A baseline critic model, trained solely on raw 2.5D sensor depth as input, was also evaluated. In simulation, this baseline achieved a slightly higher success rate of 68%.
 - However, when deployed in the real world, the baseline policy's success rate dropped significantly to **51%**.
 - This considerable drop (17 percentage points) in the baseline's real-world performance is attributed to the "domain shift," including unmodeled noise from real-world depth sensors and noise introduced by the Mask R-CNN object detection module.
- **Performance Advantage of Proposed Method:** The authors' method, utilizing the domain-invariant 3D point cloud representation, achieved a **10% higher success rate in the real world (61% vs. 51%)** compared to the baseline using raw 2.5D depth. This improvement is presented as evidence of the advantages of the compact and geometry-aware domain-invariant representation in facilitating robust sim-to-real transfer.

### 10. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: the high cost and time associated with collecting real-world interaction data for training deep learning policies.

**Significance:**
- **Data Efficiency:** The most significant contribution is the demonstration of successful real-world robotic grasping with *zero real-world grasping data*. This represents a substantial advancement in data efficiency for robotic learning, as the grasping policy is trained entirely in simulation based on a learned intermediate representation.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed self-supervised framework for learning domain-invariant 3D point cloud representations enables more robust transfer from simulation to the real world. By extracting a geometry-aware representation, the policy becomes less sensitive to visual domain shifts, sensor noise, and environmental variations present in real-world RGBD data, as evidenced by the 10% performance gain over the 2.5D baseline in real-world settings.
- **Self-Supervision for 3D Perception:** The use of multi-view self-supervision for reconstructing full 3D point clouds from single RGBD images in real-world settings is a notable technical contribution, bypassing the need for expensive explicit 3D annotations.
- **Intermediate Representation Utility:** The work validates the utility of learned intermediate 3D representations as a robust interface between perception and action, simplifying policy learning and enhancing generalization.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Lowering Barriers to Entry:** This approach could significantly lower the barrier to entry for developing and deploying robot manipulation systems. Researchers and practitioners could develop and test grasping policies primarily in simulation, requiring only minimal real-world data for object shape learning, rather than extensive interaction data.
- **Accelerated Research and Development:** By reducing the data collection bottleneck, the method has the potential to accelerate research and development in robot manipulation, allowing for faster iteration cycles and exploration of more complex tasks and environments.
- **Broader Applicability of 3D Representations:** The learned 3D point cloud representation is described as not specific to grasping. This suggests its potential applicability to a wider range of other robot-object interaction tasks beyond grasping, such as placement, assembly, or tool use, enhancing the robot's overall understanding of objects and their geometric properties.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The paper outlines future research directions, including exploring more diverse object categories, end-effector configurations, and a larger number of manipulation tasks. These avenues suggest that the proposed framework could serve as a foundational component for developing more versatile and intelligent robotic systems capable of operating in unstructured real-world environments.
