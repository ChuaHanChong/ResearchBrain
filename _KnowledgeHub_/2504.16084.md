# TTRL: Test-Time Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> TTRL is a framework enabling Large Language Models to self-improve through Reinforcement Learning on unlabeled test data, using self-generated rewards derived from majority voting. This approach substantially boosts performance on complex reasoning tasks, with one model achieving a 211% improvement on AIME 2024, by fostering a self-reinforcing learning loop.

## Problem

- Applying Reinforcement Learning to Large Language Models for reasoning tasks is bottlenecked by the high cost and scarcity of human-annotated ground-truth labels for reward estimation.
- Existing RL methods for LLMs heavily rely on supervised reward signals, making continuous self-improvement difficult on newly encountered or unlabeled test data.
- The inability to obtain explicit reward signals at inference time limits the ability of LLMs to adapt and enhance generalization to new distributions without human intervention.

## Method

- Introduces ==Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)==, a framework that enables LLMs to update their parameters and enhance generalization at test time using RL.
- Generates reward signals without explicit ground-truth labels by using repeated sampling of model outputs and deriving a =='consensus output' via majority voting==.
- Calculates ==binary rule-based rewards== for individual sampled outputs based on their match with the majority-voted pseudo-label, which are then used to optimize the model's policy via an RL algorithm like GRPO.

## Results

- TTRL consistently improved performance across various LLMs and reasoning benchmarks; for instance, Qwen-2.5-Math-7B achieved an approximate **211%** improvement in pass@1 performance on AIME 2024 (from **12.9%** to **40.2%**).
- The method proved effective even on highly capable Large Reasoning Models, yielding approximately **10-point** improvements on models like Skywork-OR1-Math-7B and Qwen3-8B.
- TTRL demonstrated a sustainable self-evolution, consistently surpassing the initial majority accuracy (maj@n) of the model, and closely approached the performance of models trained with ground-truth labels (RL with leakage).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Repeated sampling combined with majority voting can reliably generate pseudo-rewards for test-time reinforcement learning, even without ground-truth labels.
> - Large Language Models can self-evolve by dynamically improving their own self-supervision signals, leading to a self-reinforcing learning loop that surpasses initial accuracy.
> - Test-time adaptation through Reinforcement Learning on unlabeled data yields generalizable improvements in reasoning capabilities, rather than just overfitting to specific test sets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen rapid advancements, with models demonstrating remarkable performance on complex tasks, especially when enhanced through Reinforcement Learning (RL) on human-annotated data. However, a significant bottleneck remains: the escalating cost and impracticality of obtaining high-quality, human-labeled data, particularly for novel and challenging reasoning problems. This paper directly addresses this critical limitation by proposing a method for LLMs to self-improve without explicit ground-truth labels.

The work builds upon two crucial paradigms in AI:
1. **Reinforcement Learning for Language Models:** Traditional RL for LLMs, exemplified by Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022) and outcome-based RL for reasoning (e.g., GRPO by Shao et al., 2024, or DeepSeek-R1 by Guo et al., 2025), has been instrumental in aligning models with human preferences and enhancing complex reasoning capabilities (Wei et al., 2022). However, these methods invariably rely on supervised reward signals, whether from human preferences or verifiable ground-truth outputs (e.g., correct math solutions). TTRL challenges this dependency by enabling RL in an unsupervised setting.
2. **Test-Time Scaling (TTS) and Test-Time Training (TTT):** TTS aims to boost model performance by allocating more computational resources at inference time. It broadly encompasses Test-Time Inference (TTI) and Test-Time Training (TTT). TTI involves techniques like majority voting or best-of-N sampling (Wang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020) to refine outputs during inference. TTT, on the other hand, involves adapting model parameters at test time by exploiting the intrinsic structure or distributional properties of incoming unlabeled test data (Sun et al., 2019; Akyürek et al., 2024). While TTT has gained traction, its integration with RL, especially for complex reasoning tasks, has been largely underexplored.

TTRL uniquely bridges these two paradigms. It marries Test-Time Training with Reinforcement Learning, enabling a model to learn and adapt on the fly using *self-generated* reward signals derived from its own outputs. This distinguishes it from prior self-training or self-rewarding approaches (e.g., Yuan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024) which often focus on open-domain instruction following or preference-based optimization rather than online reinforcement learning for rule-based reasoning. The concept of models autonomously improving through "experience," as advocated by Silver & Sutton (2025), serves as a philosophical underpinning for TTRL, pushing the frontier towards continual, self-evolving AI systems that are less reliant on static, human-curated datasets. By addressing the critical challenge of reward acquisition in unlabeled environments, TTRL carves out a new and promising direction for LLM development, particularly for highly challenging and continuously evolving reasoning benchmarks where ground-truth labels are scarce or non-existent.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core challenge identified by the authors is the difficulty of applying Reinforcement Learning to reasoning tasks in LLMs when explicit ground-truth labels for reward estimation are unavailable during inference. Existing state-of-the-art Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) achieve impressive performance by leveraging RL on expensive, human-annotated data. However, the continuous emergence of complex and unlabeled questions, such as those found in new benchmarks like ARC-AGI-2, highlights the inherent limitations of approaches heavily reliant on human supervision. Scaling up with more labeled data and computational resources is often insufficient or impractical.

Motivated by the vision of AI systems that can autonomously improve through "experience" – moving into an "era of experience" as posited by Silver & Sutton (2025) – the paper sets out to explore how LLMs can self-evolve via RL on unlabeled data. This "self-evolution" can manifest as adaptation to test-time data or training on external unlabeled data, with this work focusing on the former.

Therefore, the **key objectives** of this research are:
1. To develop a novel framework, Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL), that enables LLMs to update their parameters and enhance generalization *at test time* using RL.
2. To overcome the critical challenge of *obtaining reward signals* for RL in the absence of ground-truth labels.
3. To demonstrate that common Test-Time Scaling (TTS) practices, specifically majority voting, can yield surprisingly effective pseudo-rewards suitable for driving RL training.
4. To validate TTRL's effectiveness across various tasks, models, and scales, showing consistent performance improvements.
5. To investigate TTRL's ability to "self-evolve" beyond the limitations of its initial self-generated supervision signals and potentially approach the performance of models trained with ground-truth labels.

The overarching **motivation** is to significantly reduce the reliance on costly human annotations for RL-based LLM training, thereby enabling more scalable, continual learning and pushing the boundaries of AI capabilities, particularly for complex reasoning problems where new, unlabeled challenges constantly arise. TTRL aims to empower LLMs to generate their own experiences, estimate rewards, and iteratively improve their performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TTRL introduces a novel approach to training pre-trained LLMs during inference on unlabeled test data using Reinforcement Learning. The central innovation lies in its method of generating reward signals without access to ground-truth labels, achieved primarily through majority voting.

The methodology can be broken down into the following steps:

1. **Problem Formulation:** The task is defined as training a pre-trained model (parameterized by $\theta$) at test time using RL on unlabeled data. Given an input prompt $x$, the model generates an output $y$ sampled from its policy $\pi_\theta(y | x)$.

2. **Label Estimation via Repeated Sampling and Majority Voting:**
 - For a given test question $x$, the LLM is prompted to generate multiple candidate outputs, denoted as $\{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_N\}$, through repeated sampling (also referred to as rollouts).
 - An answer extractor processes these raw outputs to obtain their corresponding predicted answers, $P = \{\hat{y}_i\}_{i=1}^N$.
 - A "consensus output" or "estimated label," denoted as $y^*$, is then derived from these candidate answers. The primary method for this aggregation is **majority voting**, where $y^*$ is the most frequently occurring prediction within the set $P$. This mechanism provides a pseudo-label for the unlabeled test instance.

3. **Rule-Based Reward Calculation:**
 - Once the estimated label $y^*$ is determined, rule-based rewards are computed for each individual sampled output $\hat{y}_i$ from the initial set.
 - The reward function is binary:
 $R(\hat{y}_i, y^*) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } \hat{y}_i = y^* \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
 - This means an output receives a positive reward (1) if its extracted answer matches the majority-voted answer, and a negative reward (0) if it does not.

4. **Policy Optimization via Reinforcement Learning:**
 - With the pseudo-rewards $r(y, y^*)$ generated, the model's parameters $\theta$ are updated to maximize the expected reward. The RL objective is to maximize $E_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(y, y^*)]$.
 - The parameters are updated via gradient ascent: $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta \nabla_\theta E_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(y, y^*)]$, where $\eta$ is the learning rate.
 - The authors specifically use **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)** (Shao et al., 2024) as the RL algorithm for implementation, noting its compatibility with other algorithms like PPO and PRIME.

5. **Online Learning and Self-Evolution:** This process is performed iteratively. As the model's policy improves through RL training, its ability to generate more accurate initial responses increases. This, in turn, leads to more reliable majority-voted pseudo-labels, creating a positive feedback loop where the quality of the self-supervision signal improves over time.

**Experimental Setup Details:**
- **Models:** Tested across various LLM families (Qwen, LLaMA, Mistral, DeepSeek), sizes (1.5B to 32B), and types (base, instruct, LRMs) to demonstrate generalizability.
- **Benchmarks:** Evaluated on challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2024, AMC, MATH-500) and a general knowledge QA benchmark (GPQA-Diamond).
- **Rollout Parameters:** For voting-based label estimation, 64 responses were sampled (with temperature 0.6 or 1.0 for LRMs). For training, 32 responses were downsampled per prompt.
- **Compute:** Experiments were conducted on 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.

This systematic approach allows TTRL to adapt LLMs during inference, continuously improving their performance on potentially distribution-shifted inputs without requiring any human-provided ground-truth labels for the test data itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate compelling evidence for the effectiveness and potential of Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL). The main findings are:

1. **Consistent and Substantial Performance Improvements:** TTRL consistently boosts performance across a diverse range of models (Qwen, LLaMA, Mistral, DeepSeek families, Skywork) and challenging reasoning tasks (AIME 2024, AMC, MATH-500, GPQA).
 - **Dramatic Gains on Math Tasks:** Notably, TTRL applied to Qwen-2.5-Math-7B achieved an approximate **211% improvement** in pass@1 performance on AIME 2024 (from 12.9% to 40.2%). Across AIME 2024, AMC, and MATH-500, it delivered an average gain of 76.5% for this model.
 - Even smaller models showed significant uplift; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B saw a 123.2% increase on MATH-500 (+40.3 points).
 - **Effectiveness on Vanilla and Instruct Models:** TTRL substantially improved both vanilla base models and instruct-tuned models, indicating its broad applicability regardless of initial training. Qwen2.5-7B (vanilla) improved by 194.9% on AIME 2024, and Qwen2.5-32B by 203.8% on the same.

2. **Strong Performance on Large Reasoning Models (LRMs):** TTRL proved effective even on powerful LRMs that have undergone extensive and expensive post-training. For instance, it yielded approximately 10-point improvements on Skywork-OR1-Math-7B and Qwen3-8B (thinking mode) in their respective evaluations. This highlights TTRL's ability to further refine highly capable models.

3. **Natural Scaling Behavior:** The performance of TTRL consistently improves with larger model sizes (e.g., 1.5B to 7B, 7B to 32B). This suggests a positive feedback loop: larger, more capable models produce more accurate majority-voted pseudo-rewards, which in turn leads to more effective self-improvement.

4. **Generalization Beyond Target Task:** Models trained with TTRL on one benchmark (e.g., AIME 2024) showed substantial performance improvements when evaluated on other, out-of-distribution benchmarks (e.g., AMC, MATH-500). This indicates that TTRL does not lead to overfitting but rather acquires generalizable reasoning capabilities through self-improvement.

5. **Compatibility with Diverse RL Algorithms:** TTRL's core mechanism is compatible with different RL algorithms, as demonstrated by similar performance trajectories using GRPO, PPO, and PRIME on MATH-500. This flexibility suggests TTRL's potential for widespread adoption across various RL frameworks.

6. **Sustainable Self-Evolution (Surpassing Initial Maj@n):** A remarkable finding is that TTRL not only approaches but consistently **surpasses the majority accuracy (maj@n) of the initial model**, which intuitively acts as its self-supervision signal. This "bootstrapping" effect means TTRL can dynamically generate higher-quality supervision signals as the model improves, leading to a self-reinforcing learning loop. Both average performance (pass@1/avg@16) and majority performance (maj@16) metrics showed consistent upward trends during training.

7. **Approaching Ground-Truth Performance:** Perhaps the most significant finding is that TTRL's performance gains closely approach that of "RL (leakage)," which represents the theoretical upper bound of performing RL directly on test data with access to ground-truth labels. This indicates that TTRL can achieve a level of self-improvement comparable to supervised learning, even in an information-leaked scenario, showcasing its substantial efficiency and performance gains without actual ground-truth labels.

8. **Robustness via "Lucky Hit" Mechanism:** The analysis reveals that TTRL remains effective even when the estimated pseudo-labels from majority voting are imperfect. This is attributed to the "Lucky Hit" phenomenon, where the rule-based verifier can still assign correct negative rewards to incorrect predictions, even if the estimated majority label is wrong, simply because it differs from the incorrect prediction. This mechanism contributes to a consistently high "reward accuracy" (e.g., 92% on AIME 2024 despite only 37% "label accuracy"), providing reliable training signals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) represents a significant advancement in the capabilities of Large Language Models, particularly for complex reasoning tasks. Its potential impact spans several critical areas:

1. **Reduced Reliance on Labeled Data:** The most immediate and profound impact of TTRL is its ability to enable effective RL training on *unlabeled* test data. This substantially reduces the dependency on expensive, time-consuming, and often scarce human annotations, which currently pose a major bottleneck for scaling LLMs to increasingly complex and niche domains. This addresses a fundamental limitation of current RL approaches for LLMs, making continuous improvement more feasible and economical.

2. **Enabling True Self-Evolution and Lifelong Learning:** TTRL moves LLMs closer to the vision of "self-evolving" AI systems, capable of learning from their own "experience." By dynamically generating and refining its own supervision signals (rewards derived from majority voting), TTRL establishes a powerful positive feedback loop. The finding that TTRL can consistently surpass the majority accuracy of the initial model – its own "training signal" – is a groundbreaking demonstration of this self-improving capability, potentially unlocking unbounded lifelong learning for LLMs on large-scale, unsupervised data streams.

3. **Adaptive and Generalizable LLMs:** The method allows LLMs to adapt to the specific distribution and characteristics of incoming test data, leading to enhanced generalization. The observed improvements in out-of-distribution performance suggest that the self-improvement gained via TTRL is not mere overfitting but rather the acquisition of more robust and generalizable reasoning abilities. This is crucial for real-world applications where models constantly encounter novel and diverse problems.

4. **High Performance Ceiling with Efficiency:** TTRL's ability to closely approach the performance of models trained directly on ground-truth labeled test data (the "RL (leakage)" upper bound) is a testament to its efficiency and efficacy. This suggests that the quality of self-generated rewards, especially combined with the "Lucky Hit" phenomenon, is sufficiently high to drive substantial performance gains comparable to highly supervised settings, but without the cost.

5. **Broad Applicability and Compatibility:** The extensive experiments demonstrating TTRL's effectiveness across various LLM architectures, sizes, and its compatibility with different RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO, PRIME) underscore its versatility. This indicates that TTRL is a generalizable framework that can be integrated into existing LLM development pipelines.

6. **Foundational Step for Future AI:** TTRL serves as a preliminary but crucial step towards RL with self-labeled rewards, paving the way for learning from continuous streams of experience. This direction holds immense promise for developing more autonomous and intelligent AI agents capable of tackling increasingly complex agentic tasks and contributing to scientific discovery without constant human intervention.

**Limitations and Future Works:**
The authors acknowledge that TTRL is an initial exploration. Key limitations include the need for a deeper analysis of the impact of prior knowledge (as performance degrades on harder problems for weaker models) and the sensitivity to hyperparameter configurations inherent in RL. Future work aims to:
- Develop a formal theoretical analysis of TTRL's convergence properties.
- Extend TTRL to real-time online learning scenarios with streaming data (Test-Time Adaptation).
- Scale up TTRL for massive datasets and models in truly self-supervised regimes.
- Apply TTRL to more complex, open-ended agentic tasks and multi-step scientific reasoning, leveraging its ability to learn from self-generated experiences.

In conclusion, TTRL represents a significant leap forward in unsupervised LLM training, offering a pathway to continually improve and adapt powerful reasoning models in the absence of explicit human supervision. Its implications for the scalability, autonomy, and generalizability of future AI systems are substantial.
