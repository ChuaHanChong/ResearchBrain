# MVG-WAM: Multiple View Geometry-Aware World-Action Modeling for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> MVG-WAM is a video-based world-action model that treats synchronized cameras as geometrically related projections of one scene, not tiles on a canvas. It combines an epipolar-constrained cross-view global state, view-indexed VGGT-Omega geometric conditioning routed to each camera region, and training-only multi-horizon metric-depth supervision. Without embodied pretraining, it reaches 99.1% on LIBERO, 92.07% on RoboTwin 2.0 and 91.3% on a real robot.

## Problem

- World-action models built on video-generation backbones tile multiple camera views on one canvas or concatenate their tokens, so calibrated cross-view geometric relations stay implicit.
- Because those relations are implicit, the model must learn cross-view correspondence alongside visual dynamics and actions, which weakens geometric coherence and spatial reasoning for manipulation.
- Prior geometry-aware VLAs and WAMs mainly choose which geometric cues to encode or predict, not how synchronized cameras should be geometrically related inside the representation.

## Method

- **Epipolar-constrained global state**: dense ==DINOv3== features attend only to candidates sampled along calibrated ==epipolar== lines, with a null candidate and confidence gate, then a query bank pools them into a compact residual.
- View-indexed geometric conditioning: a frozen ==VGGT-Omega== encoder produces per-view register states, and camera-aware routing cross-attends each video token to its own camera's state plus the global state through ==zero-initialized gates==.
- Future metric-depth supervision: a convolutional decoder reads intermediate denoising features and predicts log-depth at several future horizons, using ==Huber==, gradient and boundary losses weighted by noise level. It is dropped at inference.

## Results

- Without embodied pretraining it reaches **99.1%** average on LIBERO and **92.07%** on RoboTwin 2.0, beating FastWAM-Joint by 0.6 and **1.49 points** and sitting 0.17 below pretrained LingBot-VA.
- On RoboTwin Clean2Random it reaches **29.2%** versus **1.3%** for FastWAM-Joint. On LIBERO-Plus the overall score rises from **68.7%** to **70.4%**, but it drops under language and lighting shifts.
- Ablations show each component helps: removing depth supervision costs **1.09 points** on all tasks and 2.84 on Hard-13. Real robot success is **91.3%**, beating pi0.5 at **89.3%**, and RGB prediction PSNR improves by **3.74 dB**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating multiple cameras as projections of one shared world, with known calibration, is a cheap inductive bias that video-backbone policies otherwise have to learn from data.
> - Training-only auxiliary heads like metric depth can ground latent representations at no deployment cost. The geometric benefit comes through the features, not from decoded outputs.
> - Explicit geometry helps most under unseen randomization and camera shifts, not uniformly. Gains on saturated in-distribution benchmarks are small and can hide regressions on language or lighting shifts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

World–Action Models (WAMs) learn visual change alongside robot actions, so their predictions can help a policy reason about what interaction will do to the scene. Robots commonly observe the workspace with a scene camera and wrist cameras. The scene camera supplies workspace context, and the wrist cameras reveal details near the gripper and contact point. If the system treats those synchronized views as separate tiles or token streams, the model has to infer their geometric relationships while also learning to predict video and actions. MVG-WAM gives each camera region its own matching geometric context while also sharing information across views. Without additional embodied pretraining, it reports 99.1% average success on LIBERO and 92.07% on RoboTwin 2.0. On a physical robot, it reports 137 successful trials out of 150 (91.3%) across three tasks.

Cobot Magic radish-to-plate demonstration: picking up the radish and placing it on the plate. This is a representative successful execution recorded from an external viewpoint, not the policy's camera feeds.

### 2. From camera tiles to shared geometry

MVG-WAM treats the views as related projections of one physical scene. It works in three steps.

**Shared cross-view state.** The model uses camera calibration to restrict cross-view feature retrieval to locations that could correspond geometrically. Those locations lie on an epipolar line, which is the image-space path on which a matching point must lie when viewed from another calibrated camera. Attention selects among sampled candidates along that path. A confidence gate and a no-match option limit unsupported updates. These dense DINOv3 features are pooled into a compact shared cross-view state.

**Per-view context.** A frozen VGGT-Ω geometry encoder jointly processes synchronized views and produces view-indexed register tokens. Each video token receives the per-view context for the camera region containing its latent-grid center, along with the shared global state. Action tokens can then attend to the geometry-enhanced video representation.

**Depth supervision.** During training, metric depth at multiple future horizons and across views encourages physical-scale information in intermediate video features. This is auxiliary supervision. The depth decoder is disabled at inference.

(a) Dense DINOv3 features pass through epipolar-constrained cross-view aggregation into a compact global geometric state. (b) Frozen VGGT-Ω produces per-view geometric tokens routed to matching camera regions. (c) A video–action diffusion transformer receives geometric conditioning in the video stream, and action tokens access it through mixed attention. Its outputs are future video and an action sequence. (d) Intermediate video states are decoded to metric depth during training only. The decoder is not used during action rollout.

### 3. Does explicit geometry help control?

The benchmarks report success percentages averaged across the four LIBERO suites and across the RoboTwin Clean and Random domains. Compared with FastWAM-Joint's 98.5% and 90.58%, MVG-WAM's gains are 0.6 and 1.49 percentage points. MVG-WAM is competitive rather than best in every comparison. On RoboTwin, the embodied-pretrained LingBot-VA reports 92.24%.

The controlled RoboTwin ablation removes one component at a time. It uses 50 clean and 200 randomized demonstrations per task with shared seeds, a smaller training set than the standard benchmark's 500 randomized demonstrations. Two removals test the geometry directly:

- Removing the epipolar constraint keeps the dense encoder and retrieval budget but removes the geometric restriction.
- Removing view routing gives every canvas region access to the full per-view token bank.

Removing metric-depth supervision lowers success from 92.05% to 90.96% on all 50 tasks. On Hard-13, which an independent FastWAM-Joint validation pass selected as tasks below 90% success, it lowers success from 77.65% to 74.81%. These results support the authors' interpretation that the components contribute under this protocol. They do not establish that any one component is the sole cause of the complete model's advantage.

RoboTwin 2.0 ablation success rates (%) on all 50 tasks and Hard-13. Full MVG-WAM scores 92.05 and 77.65, and FastWAM-Joint scores 89.44 and 71.96. Each removal has lower success than the full-model row in both columns.

### 4. On a physical robot

On Cobot Magic, the authors evaluated each task over 50 trials, and interventions and safety stops counted as failures. MVG-WAM's success rates were 96% for radish-to-plate, 90% for cup stacking, and 88% for pot opening followed by pepper placement. This is evidence on those three tasks, not a claim of broad real-world generalization.

### 5. The world behind the views

MVG-WAM passes shared scene evidence and camera-specific context into the representation used for action. Its depth decoder is used only in training. Within the ablation protocol, removing any of the geometric components lowered success, though no single component is shown to be the sole cause.
