# Mitigating Covariate Shift in Imitation Learning via Offline Data Without Great Coverage

> [!summary] Summary
> MILO (Model-based Imitation Learning from Offline data) tackles offline imitation learning by fitting a dynamics-model ensemble on a suboptimal behavior dataset, deriving a disagreement-based pessimism penalty, and solving a penalized min-max IPM objective against a few expert state-action pairs entirely inside the learned model. It provably combats covariate shift under only partial coverage, and imitates on MuJoCo tasks where behavior cloning fails.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning trains only on expert data, so it makes arbitrary mistakes off the expert distribution and compounds errors quadratically in the horizon.
- Interactive methods like DAgger need a queryable expert and still fail when the expert is not recoverable; other guarantees demand costly or unsafe online interaction.
- Offline and off-policy alternatives such as ValueDICE, DICE variants and Cascaded Supervised IRL assume global coverage of the entire state-action space, an assumption rarely satisfied.

## Method

- Model learning: an ==ensemble of neural dynamics models== is fit on the offline transition triples, with maximum pairwise ensemble disagreement serving as the calibrated uncertainty measure sigma.
- ==Pessimism penalty== b(s,a) scaling as H times sigma is added to the imagined cost, pushing the policy away from state-actions the behavior data fails to cover.
- Pessimistic model-based min-max imitation: an ==MMD==-with-RBF discriminator (closed-form maximizer) against expert samples, optimized purely inside the learned model by ==natural policy gradient== plus a behavior-cloning regularizer.

## Results

- Across five MuJoCo tasks with behavior policies scoring **25-66%** of expert return, **MILO** outperforms behavior cloning and ValueDICE on all five and nears expert level on three.
- On Humanoid-v2, using **100** expert state-action pairs plus a **1M**-sample offline set from a **1505**-return behavior policy, MILO imitates the **3248**-return expert while both BC variants collapse.
- Coverage ablation gives Hopper normalized scores **0.95**, **0.66**, **0.42** for roughly 50%, 25%, and random behavior data; removing pessimism destabilizes training and lowers final return.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Partial coverage is enough: offline data need only cover the expert's own state-actions, a far weaker condition than global concentrability over the whole space.
> - Pessimism carries over from offline RL to imitation with no reward signal, since model-uncertainty penalties can stand in for the missing cost function.
> - Cheap suboptimal interaction data is a substitute for expert data volume, and shifts error scaling from quadratic to linear in the horizon.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenge of covariate shift in Imitation Learning (IL), a fundamental issue that can lead to compounding errors and poor generalization in learned policies. Traditional IL methods, such as Behavior Cloning (BC), are known to suffer when the agent encounters states not present in the expert demonstration dataset.

Prior research has approached covariate shift mitigation through several avenues:
- **Interactive IL:** Methods like DAgger, LOLS, DART, and AggreVaTe(D) rely on an "interactive expert" that can be queried during training to provide labels for novel states. While effective, this approach requires continuous expert availability, which is often costly or impractical in real-world scenarios. Furthermore, these methods cannot guarantee avoidance of covariate shift if the expert policy is not recoverable.
- **Known Dynamics or Online Interaction:** Another line of work leverages either a known environment transition dynamics model or actively interacts with the real environment (e.g., ValueDICE, Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL)). These methods can offer strong theoretical guarantees, but real-world interactions are frequently expensive, time-consuming, or unsafe. ValueDICE, for instance, theoretically requires global coverage of the state-action space, often achieved through online interaction to build a comprehensive replay buffer.
- **Full Expert Coverage Assumption:** Some approaches assume that the expert's policy already covers the entire state-action space, an assumption that is often unrealistic for complex or high-dimensional environments.

The current work distinguishes itself by proposing a pure **offline learning setting**. In this context, the learner has access to neither an interactive expert nor the environment for additional online interactions. Instead, it utilizes two pre-collected datasets: a small dataset of expert state-action pairs and a larger batch of offline state-action-next state transitions gathered from a potentially sub-optimal behavior policy. A key departure from much existing work, including ValueDICE and Cascaded Supervised IRL (CSI), is that this method does not require global coverage of the state-action space from the offline dataset. Instead, it operates under a "partial coverage" assumption, meaning the offline data only needs to sufficiently cover the *expert's* state-action traces, rather than the entire state-action space. This concept is formalized using a "relative condition number" for continuous environments.

The paper also relates to the field of Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL), where algorithms often employ pessimism or regularization to address similar data distribution shifts and lack of coverage. The authors highlight that their theoretical analysis for non-parametric models, particularly concerning distribution-dependent bounds under partial coverage, represents a novel contribution even within the broader offline RL literature, which has largely focused on finite-dimensional linear models or discrete MDPs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an algorithmic framework capable of mitigating the covariate shift problem in Imitation Learning (IL) within a purely offline setting. This involves enabling an agent to learn to imitate an expert without any online environment interactions or additional queries to the expert demonstrator during the learning process.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several practical and theoretical challenges in current IL paradigms:
- **Compounding Errors in Traditional IL:** Simple methods like Behavior Cloning (BC) are prone to compounding errors. Small deviations from the expert's trajectory can lead the agent into unobserved states, where BC provides arbitrary or incorrect actions, further exacerbating the deviation. This limits the generalization capabilities of such policies.
- **Cost and Infeasibility of Online Interaction:** Many real-world applications of IL, such as robotics, autonomous driving, or healthcare, involve environments where online trial-and-error exploration or continuous expert supervision is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, or unsafe. A truly offline learning paradigm is highly desirable in these contexts.
- **Expert Burden:** Relying on an interactive expert places a significant burden on the expert, requiring their presence and input throughout the training phase. Reducing this dependency makes IL more scalable and accessible.
- **Underutilization of Available Data:** There is often a wealth of pre-recorded, static offline data from various behavior policies (e.g., previous system attempts, human-generated logs). This data, while potentially sub-optimal, contains valuable information about environment dynamics. Traditional offline IL methods often struggle to effectively leverage such sub-optimal data.
- **Limitations of Current Coverage Assumptions:** Existing robust IL and offline RL methods often assume "global coverage," meaning the offline dataset must cover all possible state-action pairs, or at least be dense enough everywhere. This is a strong assumption that is often not met in practice. The motivation is to achieve robust imitation with a more realistic "partial coverage" assumption, where the offline data only needs to cover the regions visited by the expert policy.
- **Theoretical Gap in Non-parametric Models:** A specific motivation is to extend the theoretical understanding of pessimistic model-based learning to more complex function approximation settings, particularly non-parametric models like Gaussian Processes (GPs), and to derive distribution-dependent finite-sample error bounds under realistic coverage conditions.

By addressing these points, the authors aim to make imitation learning more practical and theoretically robust for scenarios where expert interactions are limited and online exploration is not feasible.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, named Model-based Imitation Learning from Offline data (MILO), is a three-step algorithmic framework designed to leverage both a small expert dataset and a large, potentially sub-optimal, offline dataset to mitigate covariate shift.

The core idea of MILO is to imitate the expert by optimizing an Integral Probability Metric (IPM) distance between the agent's state-action distribution and the expert's state-action distribution, incorporating a pessimistic penalty term. The process involves:

1. **Model Learning and Uncertainty Estimation:**
 - **Dynamics Model (`P_hat`):** A dynamics model `P_hat: S × A → S'` is learned from the offline dataset `D_o = {(s_i, a_i, s'_i)}`. This model predicts the next state `s'` given a state-action pair `(s, a)`.
 - **Uncertainty Measure (`σ`):** Alongside `P_hat`, an uncertainty measure `σ(s, a)` is derived, quantifying the model's confidence in its predictions for any given state-action pair. The framework assumes a "calibrated model" where the L1-distance between the learned and true dynamics `||P_hat(•|s, a) - P(•|s, a)||_1` is bounded by `min(σ(s, a), 2)`.
 - **Instantiation Examples:**
 - **Discrete MDPs:** `P_hat` is the empirical transition probability, and `σ(s, a)` scales inversely with the square root of the number of times `(s, a)` is observed (`N(s,a)`).
 - **Kernelized Nonlinear Regulators (KNRs):** For continuous state-action spaces, if the true transition is `s' = W*φ(s,a) + ε` (where `φ` is a feature mapping), `P_hat` is learned via Ridge regression. `σ(s,a)` is related to `φ(s,a)^T Σ_no^-1 φ(s,a)`, where `Σ_no` is the data covariance matrix.
 - **Gaussian Processes (GPs):` If `s' = g*(s,a) + ε`, `P_hat` is represented by GP posteriors, and `σ(s,a)` is proportional to the kernel function `k_no((s,a), (s,a))` evaluated at `(s,a)`.
        - **Practical Implementation:** For continuous control tasks, an ensemble of neural network dynamics models is trained. `σ(s,a)` is then approximated by the maximum disagreement (L2-norm difference) among the ensemble's predictions for `s'`.

2.  **Pessimistic Penalty Design (`b(s, a)`):**
    - A penalty function `b(s, a) = H * min(σ(s, a), 2)` is constructed, where `H` is the planning horizon.
    - This penalty is designed to be high for state-action pairs where `P_hat` is uncertain (i.e., `σ(s, a)` is large, indicating poor coverage by `D_o`). Conversely, it is low in regions well-covered by `D_o`.
    - The purpose of this penalty is to discourage the agent's policy from venturing into state-action regions where the learned dynamics model is unreliable, thereby mitigating covariate shift.

3.  **Offline Min-Max Model-Based Policy Optimization:**
    - The core optimization objective is: `min_π∈Π max_f∈F [ E_(s,a)~d_π_P_hat [f(s, a) + b(s, a)] - E_(s,a)~D_e [f(s, a)] ]`.
    - `π` is the policy to be learned, `F` is a class of cost functions, `d_π_P_hat` is the state-action distribution of policy `π` under the learned dynamics `P_hat`, and `D_e` is the expert dataset. The realizability assumption `c ∈ F` (true cost function is in `F`) is made.
    - This objective encourages the agent's policy `π` to generate state-action sequences that are similar to the expert's, while simultaneously penalizing trajectories that enter regions of high model uncertainty.
    - **Practical Solution:**
        - The cost function class `F` is instantiated using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, allowing for a closed-form solution for the maximizing discriminator `f`.
        - A behavior cloning (BC) regularization term, `λ_BC * E_(s,a)~D_e [`(a, s, π)]`, is added to the objective. This term ensures the policy remains close to the expert data, further stabilizing learning.
 - The optimization is performed iteratively: first, the discriminator `f_t` (and thus the effective cost function) is updated given the current policy `π_t`. Then, the policy `π_t+1` is updated using Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) (specifically TRPO in experiments) within the learned model `P_hat`, using the combined cost `(1 - λ_penalty) * f_t(s,a) + λ_penalty * b(s,a)`. `λ_penalty` is a hyperparameter to balance the influence of the learned cost function and the pessimism term.

**Theoretical Framework for Partial Coverage:**
The analysis of MILO introduces and utilizes the concept of "partial coverage." Unlike traditional methods requiring global coverage, MILO's theory is robust as long as the offline dataset's distribution `ρ` covers the expert's state-action distribution `d_πe_P`. This is quantified via:
- **π_e-concentrability coefficient (`C_πe`):** For discrete MDPs, `max_(s,a) d_πe_P(s,a) / ρ(s,a)`.
- **Relative condition number:** For continuous MDPs (KNRs and GPs), defined as `sup_x (x^T Σ_πe x / x^T Σ_ρ x)`, where `Σ_πe` and `Σ_ρ` are feature covariance matrices under expert and offline data distributions, respectively. This metric can be finite even when density ratios are infinite.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical guarantees and empirical validations of the MILO framework.

**Theoretical Findings:**

- **General Error Bound (Theorem 3):** MILO's performance, measured as the difference between its learned policy's cost and the expert's cost (`V_hat_π_IL_P,c - V_πe_P,c`), is bounded by two main error terms: `Err_o` (offline data-related) and `Err_e` (expert data-related).
 - `Err_o` scales with `H^2 * E_(s,a)~d_πe_P [min(σ(s, a), 1)]`. Critically, this term is shown to diminish to zero as the number of offline samples (`n_o`) increases, provided the partial coverage condition is met.
 - `Err_e` scales with `H * sqrt(log(|F|)/n_e)`, where `n_e` is the number of expert samples. This indicates a linear dependence on the planning horizon `H`, which is an improvement over the `H^2` dependence typically observed in traditional Behavior Cloning methods, suggesting effective mitigation of compounding errors.
- **Partial Coverage Analysis:** The theoretical results formally demonstrate that MILO operates effectively under a partial coverage assumption.
 - **Discrete MDPs (Theorem 5):** `Err_o` scales as `O(H^2 * sqrt(C_πe * |S|^2*|A| / n_o))`, where `C_πe` is the `π_e`-concentrability coefficient. This confirms that the error depends on how well the offline data covers the expert's specific state-action space, rather than requiring global coverage.
 - **KNRs (Theorem 6):** `Err_o` scales as `O(H^2 * (rank(Σ_ρ)^2 / n_o) * sqrt(d_S * C_πe * log(n_o)))`. This bound incorporates the rank of the offline data's feature covariance matrix (`rank(Σ_ρ)`) and the relative condition number `C_πe`, indicating a distribution-dependent and potentially tighter bound than worst-case analyses.
 - **Gaussian Processes (GPs) (Theorem 10):** `Err_o` scales as `O(H^2 * (d*^2 / n_o) * sqrt(d_S * C_πe * log^3(n_o) * log(n_o)))`, where `d*` is the effective dimension. This is highlighted as a novel contribution, providing finite-sample error bounds for pessimistic model-based learning with non-parametric function approximation under partial coverage.
- **Implications for Offline RL:** The theoretical framework and analysis techniques developed for MILO are noted to extend to offline Reinforcement Learning, providing novel non-parametric analyses under partial coverage that improve upon prior offline RL results which often assumed global coverage or were restricted to linear models.

**Empirical Findings:**

MILO was evaluated on five continuous control tasks in the MuJoCo simulator: Hopper-v2, Walker2d-v2, HalfCheetah-v2, Ant-v2, and Humanoid-v2. The evaluations focused on imitation performance, robustness to low-quality offline data, and the impact of the pessimism term.

- **Superior Performance with Sparse Expert Data (Figure 2):** MILO consistently outperformed baseline methods, including Behavior Cloning (BC) on expert data, BC on combined offline and expert data, and ValueDICE. With only 100 expert state-action pairs, MILO achieved near-expert level performance on Hopper, HalfCheetah, and Ant, and significantly better results than baselines on all five tasks. BC, when trained solely on such a small expert dataset, frequently failed.
- **Pessimism's Role (Figure 3, Left 2):** Ablation studies demonstrated that the inclusion of the pessimistic penalty term (`b(s, a)`) was beneficial, generally leading to more stable training and improved final performance for MILO across environments like Hopper and Walker2d.
- **Impact of Offline Data Coverage (Table 2):** Experiments investigating varying qualities of offline datasets showed that MILO's performance decreased as the behavior policy collecting the offline data became less proficient (e.g., from ≈50% expert performance to random). However, MILO still maintained reasonable performance on several environments even with significantly lower coverage datasets, aligning with the theoretical assertion of operating under partial coverage.
- **Efficiency with More Expert Data (Figure 3, Right 2 & Figure 4 in Appendix):** When provided with a single full expert trajectory (rather than just 100 sampled state-action pairs), MILO successfully achieved expert-level performance on environments like Walker2d and Humanoid, confirming its ability to leverage expert data efficiently when more contiguous demonstrations are available.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MILO framework presents a notable advancement in Imitation Learning, offering a method to address the long-standing covariate shift problem in a practical, offline setting.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Enhanced Practicality of Imitation Learning:** By operating purely offline and requiring only partial coverage from a potentially sub-optimal behavior policy, MILO substantially reduces the need for costly and often impractical online environment interactions or continuous expert supervision. This makes IL more feasible for real-world applications in domains such as robotics, autonomous systems, and healthcare, where safety, cost, or expert availability are critical constraints.
- **Efficient Data Utilization:** The ability to leverage large, pre-collected, and potentially low-quality offline datasets, in conjunction with a very small number of expert demonstrations, represents an efficient use of available data resources. This is particularly impactful for scenarios where acquiring high-quality expert data is challenging or expensive.
- **Stronger Theoretical Foundations:** The introduction and formalization of "partial coverage" through `π_e`-concentrability coefficients and relative condition numbers provide more realistic and less restrictive theoretical assumptions for offline learning compared to traditional global coverage requirements. The derivation of finite-sample error bounds for non-parametric models (e.g., Gaussian Processes) under these conditions is a significant theoretical contribution, extending the state-of-the-art in both IL and offline RL theory.
- **Mitigation of Compounding Errors:** The theoretical demonstration of improved horizon dependence (linear vs. quadratic) in error bounds, coupled with empirical results, indicates that MILO effectively mitigates the compounding errors characteristic of basic IL methods like Behavior Cloning.
- **Generalizability of Pessimistic Learning:** The success of applying pessimistic principles, commonly used in offline RL to avoid unknown state-action regions, to the IL context (where reward signals are absent) suggests the broader utility of such approaches for robust policy learning from static datasets.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework opens avenues for future research, such as scaling MILO to more complex perception inputs like image-based control, further exploring the interplay between model uncertainty and policy learning in offline settings, and applying these theoretical insights to a wider range of sequential decision-making problems.

**Ethical Considerations:**
The authors explicitly acknowledge the potential negative societal and ethical implications of IL systems. They highlight that an IL algorithm's performance and inherent biases are directly inherited from the expert demonstrations. This underscores the critical need for developers and users of MILO (and similar IL technologies) to exercise due diligence in ensuring that expert data is free from negative biases, harmful intentions, or unsafe behaviors when deploying these systems in real-world applications.
