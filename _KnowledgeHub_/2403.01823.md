# RT-H: Action Hierarchies Using Language

> [!summary] Summary
> RT-H structures robot control by predicting fine-grained "language motions" as an intermediate step, enabling better data sharing across diverse tasks. This approach improves robot performance and generalization while allowing for more sample-efficient human intervention using natural language.

## Problem

- Existing language-conditioned robot policies struggle with data efficiency and generalization across semantically diverse tasks.
- Traditional robot correction methods via teleoperation are often costly, non-intuitive, and sample-inefficient.
- Scaling robot learning to a wide array of real-world tasks requires better mechanisms for sharing learned behaviors.

## Method

- A single ==Vision-Language Model== (PaLI-X 55B) is used with a two-phase query mechanism for hierarchical action prediction.
- Automated extraction of language motions from robot proprioception data generates supervision without manual annotation.
- Enables human intervention by allowing users to provide language motions directly, with targeted learning that updates only the high-level policy.

## Results

- RT-H achieves a **15%** higher average success rate compared to RT-2 on diverse multi-task robot manipulation, with **20%** lower end-to-end action prediction MSE offline.
- Training with 30 episodes of language motion corrections improves RT-H's average success rate from **40%** to **63%**, outperforming teleoperated correction methods.
- The system demonstrates improved generalization to new scenes and achieves a **65%** success rate on tasks with novel objects, compared to **55%** for the baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Introducing an intermediate language-based action hierarchy can improve data sharing and generalization across diverse robot tasks.
> - Language motions are learned to be contextual and flexible, adapting their precise execution based on the visual scene and task, rather than being rigid primitives.
> - Correcting robot behavior at the level of language motions is more sample-efficient and intuitive for humans than correcting low-level actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

RT-H positions itself at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields in robotics and artificial intelligence: language-conditioned robot learning, hierarchical control, and interactive imitation learning.

**Language-Conditioned Policies:** The paper acknowledges the growing success of language as a powerful interface for robot control. Prior works, including Google DeepMind’s own RT-1 and RT-2, have demonstrated how policies can map high-level language task descriptions and visual observations directly to low-level robot actions. These methods leverage the compositional structure of natural language to generalize across semantically similar tasks (e.g., "pick coke can" vs. "pick an apple"). However, RT-H identifies a critical limitation: as tasks become semantically *diverse* (e.g., "pick coke can" vs. "pour a cup"), data sharing becomes challenging, necessitating substantially more demonstration data. RT-H addresses this by introducing an intermediate layer of "language motions" to bridge the semantic gap, effectively enabling better data sharing across disparate tasks by revealing shared low-level behaviors.

**Hierarchical Action Representation:** The concept of breaking down complex robot tasks into simpler, manageable sub-tasks or skills is a long-standing paradigm in robot learning. Many approaches have explored learning general "skill" representations as parameterized primitives or embeddings. RT-H innovates in this space by explicitly using *language* to define these hierarchical skills (language motions). Unlike methods that rely on abstract, non-interpretable skill embeddings or pre-defined, hard-coded motion primitives (which often lack contextuality), RT-H's language motions are learned and dynamically interpreted based on the visual scene and high-level task. This allows for both interpretability and flexibility, as the same language motion (e.g., "move arm forward") can manifest differently depending on the context. This approach differentiates it from LLM-based planning frameworks (like SayCan), which focus on decomposing long-horizon goals into medium-horizon tasks, whereas RT-H focuses on the decomposition from medium-horizon tasks to fine-grained motions.

**Interactive Imitation Learning and Correction:** A significant challenge in robot learning is efficiently correcting robot behavior when it deviates from the intended task. Traditional interactive imitation learning methods (e.g., DAgger, IWR) often rely on costly and non-intuitive human interventions through teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching. RT-H presents a compelling alternative: language-based correction. By allowing humans to intervene using natural language motions, it democratizes the correction process, making it more accessible to non-experts. Furthermore, the paper highlights that learning from these higher-level language corrections is significantly more sample-efficient than learning from low-level teleoperated actions, offering a practical path to improve policies with less human effort. This aligns with a growing trend towards more intuitive and natural human-robot interaction.

**Vision-Language Model (VLM) Backbones:** RT-H firmly situates itself within the cutting-edge of VLM-driven robotics, leveraging the PaLI-X 55B architecture, similar to RT-2. This reliance on large-scale models pre-trained on internet-scale vision and language data is a defining characteristic of modern embodied AI. RT-H extends the utility of these powerful VLMs by demonstrating how they can be effectively structured to learn and execute hierarchical actions, not just flat policies. This shows a promising direction for future VLM applications in robotics, where the rich world knowledge embedded in these models can be distilled and applied to more structured and nuanced robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind RT-H stems from the observation that while natural language is an incredibly powerful tool for humans to decompose complex concepts, communicate, and generalize, its full potential in robot learning, particularly for diverse tasks, remains untapped. Current language-conditioned policies often struggle to scale efficiently when tasks become semantically very different, as direct mapping from diverse high-level language tasks to low-level actions requires immense amounts of data to learn shared structure.

The central insight of RT-H is to introduce an *intermediate prediction layer* that captures fine-grained, low-level motions using language. These "language motions" (e.g., "move arm forward," "close gripper," "rotate arm right") serve as a bridge between high-level task descriptions and precise robot actions. This insight is driven by several key objectives:

1. **Enhance Data Sharing Across Diverse Multi-task Datasets:** The primary objective is to enable robots to better leverage multi-task datasets, even when tasks are semantically very different. By forcing the policy to predict language motions as an intermediate step, RT-H helps the model identify and learn the shared structure of low-level behaviors that are common across seemingly disparate tasks (e.g., both "picking a coke can" and "pouring a cup" might involve initial "move arm forward" motions). This improves data efficiency and allows the model to generalize better.

2. **Facilitate Contextual and Flexible Action Generation:** Language motions are not intended as rigid, pre-defined primitives. Instead, an objective is for them to be *learned* in the context of the current task and visual scene. For instance, "move arm forward" should adapt its precise execution (speed, exact direction, subtle rotations) based on the object's position and the overall task goal. This flexibility is crucial for successful real-world robot manipulation.

3. **Enable Intuitive Human Intervention and Correction:** A major objective is to provide a more natural and scalable way for humans to interact with and correct robot behavior. By exposing the intermediate language motion layer, RT-H allows humans to provide intuitive, high-level feedback ("move arm left" instead of "press button 7, then 3, then 2 for joint angles"). This aims to make robot intervention accessible to non-experts and streamline the debugging process.

4. **Promote Efficient Learning from Human Feedback:** Beyond just allowing corrections, a key objective is to enable the robot to *learn* effectively from these language-based interventions. The hypothesis is that correcting at the level of language motions, rather than raw actions, provides a more structured and sample-efficient signal for policy improvement, as the robot is taught *what* to do at a higher semantic level, rather than *how* to move at every joint.

In summary, RT-H seeks to build a more robust, flexible, and human-friendly robot learning system by embedding a language-based action hierarchy within a powerful Vision-Language Model framework, thereby overcoming limitations of flat policies and traditional intervention methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RT-H (Robot Transformer with Action Hierarchies) is an end-to-end framework designed to learn and utilize action hierarchies through language motions. Its methodology can be broken down into model architecture, training details, language motion extraction, and inference/correction mechanisms.

**Model Architecture and Training (RT-H Model Overview):**
RT-H is instantiated using a single Vision-Language Model (VLM) based on the PaLI-X 55B architecture, similar to Google DeepMind's previous RT-2 model. This VLM benefits from co-training on internet-scale vision and language data, providing a broad prior knowledge base. The architecture employs a ViT (Vision Transformer) encoder to process images into tokens, and an Encoder-Decoder transformer to convert streams of image and natural language tokens into action tokens. Each action dimension (e.g., end-effector delta positions, rotations, gripper status, termination flag) is discretized into 256 bins and encoded as integer values.

RT-H employs a two-phase query mechanism to implement its action hierarchy:
1. **Language Motion Query (π_h):** This query models the high-level policy, mapping visual observations (`o`) and the high-level task description (`g`) to a fine-grained language motion (`z`). The prompt used is "Q: What skill should the robot do to [task]? A: [language motion]". This stage aims to teach the model to predict appropriate behaviors in a coarser, more compressed action space.
2. **Action Query (π_l):** This query models the low-level policy, mapping the observation (`o`), the task (`g`), and crucially, the *inferred language motion* (`z`) to low-level robot actions (`a`). The prompt is "Q: What action should the robot do to [task], with current skill: [motion]? A: [action]". This stage trains the model to contextually decode the language motion into precise actions, considering the current scene and task.

Both queries are trained within the same single VLM, with equal sampling rates (25% each for language motion and action queries from robot data, along with 50% for the original pre-training mixture). This ensures that both levels of the hierarchy benefit from the VLM's extensive prior knowledge. The ViT encoder is kept frozen during robot data co-training.

**Automated Language Motion Extraction:**
A key challenge for learning language motions is obtaining labels without extensive manual annotation. RT-H addresses this with an automated procedure that extracts language motions directly from robot proprioception information:
- It analyzes changes in the robot's end-effector pose (e.g., 3D position, 3D orientation, gripper status, base movement).
- Each dimension of change is mapped to a spatial dimension (e.g., z-axis for up/down).
- A list of dominant spatial movements is determined (e.g., "move arm up and right", "close gripper", "rotate arm counterclockwise").
- A "small action" threshold filters out negligible movements.
- The remaining dominant actions are composed into descriptive language motions (e.g., "move arm forward and close gripper").
This procedure generates a rich library of over 2500 unique language motions, offering a scalable solution to annotation. The authors acknowledge the trade-off in abstraction level and argue their choice strikes a good balance for accuracy and labeling cost.

**Inference and Correction:**
At test time, RT-H performs sequential inference: first predicting the language motion with `π_h`, then using this prediction as input to `π_l` to generate actions. To mitigate the increased inference time due to two sequential queries, RT-H uses an **asynchronous querying** approach. It predicts the language motion one step into the future, and then uses the previously inferred language motion for the current action prediction. This allows for batching queries and achieves nearly identical querying lag as a flat model like RT-2.

For **human correction**, RT-H allows users to intervene by providing new language motions via keyboard or voice. When a human intervenes, the human-specified language motion bypasses `π_h` and is directly fed into `π_l` to generate contextual actions.
**Learning from Correction Data:** This is a crucial aspect. When successful corrections are made, the system records the (observation, task, human-provided language motion) tuples. Critically, to learn from these corrections, *only the language motion query (`π_h`) is updated* using the new human-labeled language motions. The action query (`π_l`) is generally not re-trained with intervention data, as it already knows how to map language motions to actions, reducing the complexity and increasing sample efficiency of learning from feedback. This intervention data is co-trained with the original dataset, but significantly upweighted (50x) to ensure the model learns from these targeted corrections.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

RT-H’s experimental evaluation addresses four key questions, demonstrating its effectiveness across various metrics. The experiments utilize a large multi-task dataset, Diverse+Kitchen (D+K), comprising 100K demonstrations across 24+ semantic task categories, including the Kitchen dataset (70K demos). Evaluation focuses on eight challenging tasks requiring complex, precise motions.

**Q1: Performance on Diverse Multi-task Datasets:**
- **On-Robot Performance:** RT-H significantly outperforms RT-2, achieving a 15% higher average success rate across the 8 evaluation tasks. While RT-2 achieves nonzero performance on only 4/8 tasks, RT-H performs nonzero on 6/8, and RT-H-Joint (a variant using a single autoregressive query) performs nonzero on all tasks, indicating better capability in handling task diversity.
- **Ablations:**
 - RT-H-Joint performs comparably to RT-H, showing robustness to query mechanism.
 - RT-H-Cluster (using K-means generated action clusters instead of language motions) performs slightly worse than RT-H on average, though it surprisingly performs better on the hardest, most precise tasks, suggesting its clusters might offer finer-grained context. However, the lack of language makes prediction harder for broader tasks.
 - RT-H-OneHot (replacing language motions with integer class labels) performs significantly worse than RT-H, underscoring the vital role of natural language's inherent structure in enabling effective hierarchical learning.
- **Offline Performance:** RT-H achieves approximately 20% lower Mean Squared Error (MSE) for end-to-end action prediction compared to RT-2. When using ground truth language motions as input to the action query (RT-H (GT)), the MSE is 40% lower than end-to-end RT-H, confirming that accurate language motions are highly informative for predicting precise actions.

**Q2: Contextuality & Flexibility of Language Motions:**
- **Contextuality:** Qualitative examples demonstrate that language motions in RT-H are highly contextual. For instance, "move arm forward" results in different precise actions (e.g., subtle changes in speed, direction, or gripper tilt) depending on the specific object, its pose, and the overall task, confirming that these are not fixed primitives but adapt to the scene. Quantitative analysis of action dimension means and standard deviations for basic language motions also supports this, showing non-trivial variance in non-dominant axes.
- **Flexibility:** RT-H demonstrates flexibility in responding to out-of-distribution language motions for in-distribution tasks. It correctly performs tasks even when given novel, but valid, sequences of language motions (e.g., two different ways to "pull napkin"). It also shows robustness to more general language motions not specifically seen with a given task (e.g., "move arm away from jar" for "close pistachio jar"). This flexibility is crucial for effective human intervention.

**Q3: Training on Online Corrections:**
- **Correctability:** RT-H + Human Intervention (human-in-the-loop language motion corrections) achieves near-perfect success rates, serving as an upper bound and indicating that language motion prediction is often the primary bottleneck, and that RT-H's action query can leverage these human corrections effectively.
- **Learning from Corrections:** RT-H-Intervene (trained on just 30 episodes of language motion corrections per task) significantly outperforms RT-2-IWR (trained on 30 episodes of teleoperated corrections). RT-2-IWR's performance degrades, while RT-H-Intervene improves RT-H's average success rate from 40% to 63%. This strongly suggests that learning from language motion corrections is substantially more sample-efficient and robust than learning from teleoperated actions, likely because language corrections are higher-level and more consistent with the base policy.
- **RT-H-InterveneAction** (training both language motion and action queries on intervention data) also improves upon RT-H but shows signs of policy degeneration, indicating that fine-tuning actions based on potentially sub-optimal model-generated actions during intervention can be detrimental.
- **Failure Modes:** Identified issues include systemic language motion prediction errors leading to oscillatory behavior, and potential frustration for operators if language motion corrections don't result in desired actions (especially for out-of-distribution tasks).

**Q4: Generalization:**
- **New Scenes:** RT-H and RT-H-Joint demonstrate greater robustness to scene variations (new backgrounds, lighting, flooring) compared to RT-2, especially for challenging tasks like "place upright" and "open/close drawer".
- **New Objects:** RT-H achieves a 65% success rate on "pick" and "move" tasks with novel objects (unseen during training), outperforming RT-2’s 55%, indicating better generalization to object variations.
- **New Tasks (Limited Corrections):** Qualitatively, RT-H shows the ability to generalize initial "picking" phases of completely unseen tasks without correction, showcasing learned shared structures. When zero-shot generalization fails, only a few well-timed language motion corrections are needed to successfully complete the novel tasks, highlighting the efficiency of language-based intervention for expanding task capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RT-H represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly in bridging the gap between high-level human instructions and low-level robot control through an intuitive, interpretable, and learnable action hierarchy. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Enhanced Robot Learning from Diverse Data:** By effectively structuring the policy learning problem with language motions, RT-H enables better data sharing across semantically diverse multi-task datasets. This means robots can learn more efficiently from large, varied demonstration datasets, requiring less overall data to achieve robust performance across a wider range of tasks. This is crucial for scaling robot capabilities in complex real-world environments.

2. **More Intuitive and Scalable Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability for humans to intervene and correct robot behavior using natural language motions is a game-changer. It lowers the barrier for interaction, making robot control and debugging accessible to non-experts. This intuitive interface can democratize robot deployment and accelerate the iterative refinement of robot skills in various applications, from manufacturing to home assistance.

3. **Improved Efficiency in Learning from Human Feedback:** The finding that learning from language motion corrections is substantially more sample-efficient than from teleoperated actions is highly significant. This suggests a pathway to rapidly improve robot policies with minimal human effort. Instead of needing to re-demonstrate entire tasks or provide precise joint commands, humans can provide high-level, semantic guidance, leading to quicker adaptation and higher success rates.

4. **Leveraging the Power of Large Vision-Language Models:** RT-H successfully integrates the immense prior knowledge embedded in internet-scale VLMs (like PaLI-X) into a structured robotic control framework. This demonstrates that VLMs can not only interpret high-level language but also facilitate hierarchical reasoning for physical actions, pushing the boundaries of what these foundation models can achieve in embodied AI.

5. **Foundation for Future Robot Autonomy:** RT-H provides a strong foundation for future research in several directions. It opens avenues for exploring deeper, multi-level action hierarchies (e.g., from long-horizon instructions to tasks, then to language motions, and finally actions). It also suggests the potential for using language motions as a compressed, interpretable action space for reinforcement learning, which could lead to more sample-efficient exploration and more controllable learning. Furthermore, its ability to generalize across scenes and objects lays groundwork for more robust and versatile robot deployments.

6. **Towards More Interpretable and Debuggable Robot Systems:** The explicit language motion layer makes robot decision-making more transparent. When a robot fails, humans can often pinpoint the exact language motion that was incorrect, making debugging and intervention more targeted and effective. This interpretability is vital for building trustworthy and reliable AI systems.

In essence, RT-H moves beyond merely allowing robots to *understand* language to enabling them to *reason and act* through language, paving the way for more capable, adaptable, and user-friendly robotic systems.
