# Uncertainty-Aware Decision Making in Multimodal Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey organizes uncertainty-aware multimodal large language models around a source-signal-calibration-action chain: uncertainty sources in perception, grounding, language priors, reasoning, answerability and shift produce observable signals, which calibration and conformal risk control turn into decision-ready estimates that select an action - answer, hedge, abstain, clarify, request better input, retrieve, self-check, or escalate. It synthesizes 67 focused evidence rows into five synthesis tables and an action-aware evaluation agenda.

## Problem

- Text-only uncertainty and abstention surveys cover calibration and selective prediction, but say little about visual evidence quality, grounding, cross-modal conflict, or multimodal answerability.
- MLLM hallucination and safety surveys organize around failure symptoms and attack-defense settings rather than uncertainty signals, calibration targets, and the response policy that should follow.
- Existing methods collapse perception, grounding, reasoning and answerability into one confidence number, and report scores without testing whether the score changes what the system does.

## Method

- The **source-signal-calibration-action** framework is the organizing contribution: five source classes spanning sensory, grounding, language-prior-and-reasoning, answerability, and distributional uncertainty, each paired with likely signals and actions.
- A ==signal taxonomy== indexed by model access: ==model-internal logits== and visual tokens, sampling and ==semantic disagreement==, grounding and perturbation checks, ==verbalized confidence== and IDK scores, verifier or judge scores, ==conformal scores==.
- An ==action-policy taxonomy== plus an evaluation guide that pairs each calibration target with an action - answer, hedge, abstain, clarify, request better input, retrieve, self-check, route - and names its failure mode.

## Results

- The evidence base is **67** focused evidence rows from MLLM, LVLM, VLM-bridge and domain studies, kept separate from background survey citations and one non-counted bridge row for traceability.
- Synthesis is condensed into **five** tables and **six** figures: a positioning table against **four** adjacent survey families, plus **five** sources, **seven** signal families, **seven** actions, and **five** evaluation questions.
- Across **77** references, image-text settings dominate while audio, video, multi-image dialogue, embodied and omni-modal work stays sparse, so no quantitative meta-analysis is attempted across differing access, thresholds and targets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A confidence number is not uncertainty awareness; the test is whether the estimate changes behavior under insufficient, conflicting, shifted, or high-risk evidence.
> - Pair every calibration target with its action: answer-accuracy calibration does not certify grounding, answerability, or perceptual adequacy, so one generic score misroutes decisions.
> - Distinguish clarification from better-input requests: ambiguous questions need disambiguation, degraded sensors need new evidence; conformal guarantees also hold only under the calibration distribution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly being deployed in diverse applications, including visual question answering, chart and document understanding, video reasoning, medical image interpretation, and embodied agents. This expansion introduces new challenges for reliability, as MLLM failures can stem from various sources beyond linguistic errors, such as poor input quality, perceptual errors, weak grounding, cross-modal conflicts, unstable reasoning, distribution shifts, or tasks that are unanswerable from the provided evidence.

Existing research on uncertainty quantification in large language models (LLMs) primarily focuses on text-only contexts, addressing issues like confidence calibration, semantic uncertainty, and selective prediction. While foundational, these text-centric approaches are insufficient for MLLMs because they do not account for multimodal specificities, such as the quality of visual evidence, the faithfulness of grounding, potential conflicts between modalities, or the need for a wider range of actions beyond merely answering or abstaining.

Adjacent survey families cover broader MLLM architectures and capabilities, MLLM hallucination, or MLLM safety. However, these surveys typically do not center on the comprehensive "source-to-action chain" for multimodal uncertainty. This survey distinguishes itself by specifically integrating multimodal uncertainty estimation with a decision-making policy, offering a structured framework to understand how uncertainty should influence MLLM behavior. It positions itself by addressing the gap where uncertainty is not merely detected but actively used to guide system actions under varying multimodal evidence conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this survey is to organize the literature on uncertainty-aware MLLMs around a decision-centered framework. The authors argue that uncertainty in MLLMs should not be evaluated solely as a confidence score but, rather, by its ability to improve the system's behavior when faced with insufficient, conflicting, shifted, or high-risk multimodal evidence.

The paper formalizes the problem by defining an uncertainty-aware MLLM as a system that, given multimodal evidence (M), a prompt (q), and optional context (c), produces not only an answer (y) but also estimates an uncertainty signal (s), converts this signal into a calibrated confidence or risk estimate (r), and selects an appropriate action (a) via a policy (π). This formulation, (a, y, r) = π(q, M, c, s(q, M, c)), emphasizes the active role of uncertainty in shaping system responses.

The motivation for this decision-centered approach stems from the diverse nature of MLLM failures. A fluent answer may conceal fundamental issues such as a blurred image, a misrecognized object, a lack of visual grounding, or an unanswerable question. Recognizing these distinct uncertainty sources is crucial because different sources necessitate different system responses. For example, a blurry image might prompt a request for clearer input, while missing evidence could lead to abstention, and cross-modal conflict might require self-checking or external retrieval.

The survey makes four specific contributions:
1. It introduces a source–signal–calibration–action taxonomy for classifying and understanding uncertainty-aware MLLMs.
2. It synthesizes uncertainty sources that are unique to multimodal systems, including sensory ambiguity, perceptual error, grounding failure, cross-modal conflict, language-prior dominance, reasoning instability, answerability uncertainty, and distributional shift.
3. It connects uncertainty estimation directly to a range of response actions, such as abstention, clarification, retrieval, self-checking, and escalation.
4. It advocates for reorganizing evaluation paradigms to focus on MLLM behavior under uncertainty rather than solely on the quality of confidence estimates.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey's methodology is structured around its proposed "source–signal–calibration–action" framework (Figure 1). This framework provides a roadmap for analyzing the entire pipeline of uncertainty management in MLLMs.

**Sources of Uncertainty (Section 3):** The survey begins by identifying the origins of unreliability within the MLLM pipeline. It categorizes uncertainty into distinct types, arguing that these distinctions are critical for determining appropriate actions:
- **Sensory and perceptual uncertainty:** Arises from degraded or ambiguous inputs (e.g., blur, occlusion, noise) or errors in object recognition and detail perception.
- **Grounding and cross-modal uncertainty:** Relates to whether the answer is adequately supported by multimodal evidence and whether different modalities (e.g., text, image) are in agreement or conflict.
- **Language-prior and reasoning uncertainty:** Concerns instances where MLLMs rely excessively on strong language priors to fill visual gaps or exhibit unstable reasoning paths.
- **Answerability uncertainty:** Occurs when the question cannot be answered from the provided multimodal input due to missing or insufficient evidence.
- **Distributional, adversarial, and deployment uncertainty:** Encompasses issues like domain shift, adversarial inputs, or high-risk deployment conditions that can compromise calibration or reliability.
The survey highlights the interactions and propagation among these sources, emphasizing that they are not independent but can cascade through the system (Figure 3).

**Observable Signals and Estimation Methods (Section 4):** This section details how latent uncertainty sources can be made observable through various signals. These signals are categorized based on the level of model access they require and their primary diagnostic targets:
- **Model-internal signals:** Utilize information directly from the model, such as token probabilities, logit margins, entropy, sequence likelihood, hidden representations, or visual token features. These are often direct but require white-box access.
- **Sampling and semantic-disagreement signals:** Involve observing model stability under repeated generations, paraphrased prompts, or perturbed inputs. Semantic disagreement among responses serves as an uncertainty indicator, often usable with gray-box or black-box access.
- **Evidence-grounding and perturbation signals:** Assess whether the generated answer is supported by the supplied input evidence, through methods like region-grounding scores, attribution scores, or input perturbation sensitivity. These connect directly to the multimodal nature of MLLMs.
- **Verbalized confidence, IDK scores, verifiers, and judges:** Involve prompting the model to explicitly state its confidence, express "I don't know," or using separate verifier or judge models to evaluate responses. These are often suitable for black-box MLLMs but require careful calibration.
The survey presents a table (Table 3) summarizing the trade-offs of these signal families, including typical access requirements, primary diagnoses, action value, and main caveats.

**Calibration and Risk Control (Section 5):** The survey then addresses how raw uncertainty signals are processed to become decision-ready. Key aspects include:
- **Calibration targets:** Defining what "confidence" refers to (e.g., final answer correctness, grounding faithfulness, answerability). The survey emphasizes that targets should be clearly defined and often paired with specific actions.
- **Calibration methods:** Discussing techniques like post-hoc adjustments (e.g., temperature scaling), prompt-based elicitation, verbalized confidence alignment, and domain-specific calibration to ensure confidence estimates align with empirical correctness.
- **Source-aware and decoupled calibration:** Emphasizing the need to separate and calibrate different sources of uncertainty (e.g., visual vs. reasoning confidence) to enable tailored actions.
- **Conformal prediction and risk-control policies:** Introducing methods that construct prediction sets or abstention thresholds with empirical coverage guarantees, enabling risk-controlled decision-making.

**From Uncertainty to Action (Section 6):** This section connects calibrated uncertainty to concrete system actions, moving beyond a simple answer/refuse dichotomy. The action space includes:
- **Answering, hedging, and selective answering:** Deciding when to provide a direct answer, qualify it with a hedge, or selectively answer only when risk is below a threshold.
- **IDK, refusal, and answerability:** Formulating "I do not know" responses when evidence is insufficient or the question is unanswerable.
- **Clarification and better-input requests:** Engaging in dialogue to resolve ambiguous queries or requesting higher-quality sensory inputs.
- **Retrieval, self-checking, and verification:** Using uncertainty to trigger external knowledge retrieval, internal consistency checks, or external verification.
- **Routing and escalation:** Directing cases to stronger models, specialized modules, or human experts when uncertainty is high or the risk is unacceptable.
A taxonomy (Table 4) summarizes these action policies, aligning them with appropriate evidence conditions and potential failure modes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's comprehensive analysis reveals several key findings concerning uncertainty-aware decision-making in MLLMs:

- **Multifaceted Nature of MLLM Uncertainty:** Unlike text-only models, MLLMs face a wider array of uncertainty sources originating from diverse modalities and processing stages. These include ambiguities in sensory input (e.g., blurred images), errors in perception (e.g., misrecognizing objects), failures in grounding (e.g., answer not supported by visual evidence), conflicts between modalities (e.g., text contradicts image), reliance on language priors, instability in reasoning, unanswerability of questions given the input, and performance degradation due to distribution shift. The survey highlights that these sources are interconnected and can propagate throughout the MLLM pipeline.
- **Decision-Centered Utility of Uncertainty:** The core finding is that the value of uncertainty lies in its capacity to drive appropriate system actions. A numerical confidence score alone is insufficient; the system's ability to adapt its behavior—whether by answering, hedging, abstaining, seeking clarification, requesting better input, retrieving external information, self-checking, routing to specialized tools, or escalating to human experts—is the true measure of uncertainty awareness. This emphasizes a shift from merely measuring uncertainty to actively operationalizing it.
- **Diversity of Uncertainty Signals and Access Constraints:** A range of observable signals can reveal underlying uncertainty. These signals vary in their model access requirements:
 - **White-box signals** (e.g., token logits, hidden states, visual token features) offer detailed diagnostic information but require full model access.
 - **Gray-box signals** (e.g., sampling for semantic disagreement, perturbation analysis) can infer uncertainty from output variations or behavior under slight input changes.
 - **Black-box signals** (e.g., verbalized confidence, external verifier/judge models) rely on prompts, responses, or external evaluations, making them applicable to closed-source MLLMs. The survey notes that the choice of signal depends on the specific uncertainty source being diagnosed and the practical constraints of model access.
- **Context-Dependent Calibration:** Raw uncertainty signals require calibration to ensure they accurately reflect empirical correctness or risk. Calibration targets must be precisely defined, whether for overall answer accuracy, grounding faithfulness, answerability, or specific reasoning steps. The survey emphasizes the importance of source-aware calibration (e.g., decoupling visual confidence from reasoning confidence) and domain-specific calibration, as MLLMs calibrated in one domain may become miscalibrated under shifts in modality, data distribution, or application context (e.g., medical vs. general VQA).
- **Action-Aware Evaluation Protocols:** Traditional evaluation metrics focusing solely on answer accuracy or generic confidence are inadequate for uncertainty-aware MLLMs. Effective evaluation protocols must connect benchmark tasks to specific evidence conditions, assess the chosen uncertainty signal, evaluate calibration or risk control, and, crucially, measure the quality and appropriateness of the *action* taken. This involves benchmarks designed for unanswerability, hallucination detection, and explicit risk control, employing metrics beyond standard accuracy, such as abstention precision, empirical coverage for prediction sets, and measures of helpfulness for clarification or retrieval actions.
- **Deployment Implications for Trustworthy AI:** The survey underscores that deployment settings significantly influence which uncertainty sources are most critical and which actions are acceptable. High-risk domains (e.g., medical, embodied robotics) demand conservative policies, inspectable grounding, and robust escalation mechanisms. Modality-specific uncertainty patterns (e.g., temporal absence in video, numerical extraction errors in charts) require tailored uncertainty-aware actions. Furthermore, effective human-facing uncertainty communication is essential for building trust, requiring MLLMs to clearly explain *why* they are uncertain and suggest actionable next steps to users.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey represents a significant contribution to the field of Multimodal Large Language Models by providing a comprehensive, decision-centered framework for understanding and addressing uncertainty. Its impact can be observed in several areas:

- **Advancing Trustworthy MLLMs:** By systematically delineating uncertainty sources, signals, calibration, and actions, the survey offers a robust conceptual foundation for building more reliable and trustworthy MLLMs. It moves beyond superficial notions of "confidence" to actionable insights, which is crucial for the safe deployment of MLLMs in sensitive and high-stakes applications.
- **Guiding Research Directions:** The paper identifies critical open problems and a detailed research agenda (Figure 6). This roadmap will likely shape future research efforts by calling for:
 - **Source-aware decomposition:** Developing methods to accurately pinpoint the origin of uncertainty (e.g., perceptual, grounding, reasoning) to enable more precise interventions.
 - **Action-aware benchmarks:** Creating new evaluation frameworks that assess the utility of diverse actions (abstention, clarification, retrieval, escalation) rather than just answer correctness.
 - **Robust calibration under real-world constraints:** Focusing on uncertainty estimation for black-box MLLMs and ensuring calibration holds true under distribution shifts.
 - **Broader modality and interaction coverage:** Extending uncertainty-aware principles to complex modalities like audio, video, documents, and interactive, agentic systems.
 - **Improved reproducibility and human-centered communication:** Establishing reporting standards and investigating how uncertainty information can be effectively communicated to human users to facilitate better decision-making.
- **Shifting Paradigms from Measurement to Action:** The central argument—that uncertainty is valuable only when it changes system behavior—encourages a paradigm shift in MLLM development. Instead of merely quantifying how "confident" a model is, researchers and developers will be prompted to design MLLMs that are inherently "uncertainty-aware" in their operational policies, leading to more adaptive and responsible AI systems.
- **Practical Implications for MLLM Design and Deployment:** The detailed taxonomy of actions provides a practical toolkit for MLLM engineers and product developers to incorporate uncertainty into their systems. It highlights that a nuanced approach, involving tiered policies (e.g., direct answer for low risk, escalation for high risk), can enhance the overall utility and safety of MLLMs in various real-world scenarios.
