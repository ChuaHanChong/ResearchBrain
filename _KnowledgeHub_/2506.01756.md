# Learning with pyCub: A Simulation and Exercise Framework for Humanoid Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> A Python-based simulation and exercise framework, pyCub, enables accessible learning of humanoid robotics fundamentals for the iCub robot by abstracting complex C++ and YARP middleware dependencies. The platform allows students to directly engage with core robotics concepts through hands-on exercises and demonstrates real-time simulation performance on standard hardware.

## Problem

- Existing robotics education tools, particularly for humanoid robots like iCub, often present high barriers to entry due to complex C++ programming and middleware requirements.
- Students using previous iCub simulation environments reported difficulties with system setup and troubleshooting, diverting focus from core robotics principles.
- There was a recognized need for more accessible, hands-on simulation and exercise frameworks to complement theoretical humanoid robotics textbooks.

## Method

- Developed pyCub, a Python-centric simulation framework for the iCub humanoid robot, utilizing the PyBullet physics engine and a custom Open3D-based GUI.
- Implemented a detailed iCub model including simulated eye cameras and over 4000 tactile sensors for artificial skin feedback, offering customizable features via YAML configurations.
- Created a suite of scalable educational exercises, ranging from basic robot control (joint and Cartesian) to advanced tasks like gaze control, reactive tactile-based interaction, and vision-guided grasping.

## Results

- The pyCub simulation achieved real-time factors sufficient for educational use (e.g., approximately **0.95**-**1.46** with GUI and skin on an average laptop, and up to **10.82** with a simplified model without skin on a workstation).
- Course evaluations demonstrated a reduction in reported system complexity and an improved focus on core robotics concepts among students compared to previous C++/YARP-based approaches.
- Students exhibited higher engagement and creativity in solving exercises, with the framework and its associated exercises made publicly available as an open-source resource.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Abstracting complex middleware and language dependencies (C++, YARP) into a Python-only environment significantly lowers the technical barrier for students in humanoid robotics education.
> - A well-designed simulation with customizable features, including sensory feedback like artificial skin and cameras, can provide a rich and engaging platform for learning advanced robotics concepts.
> - Structured educational exercises, coupled with an intuitive simulation framework, effectively redirect student attention from software infrastructure to the fundamental principles of robot control, perception, and interaction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of pyCub addresses a recognized challenge in robotics education: the scarcity of hands-on exercises and accessible simulation tools that complement theoretical textbooks. While various robotics toolboxes exist, such as Peter Corke's Python Robotics Toolbox for dynamics and kinematics, and frameworks built around the Robot Operating System (ROS) like those for compliant actuators or general robot learning platforms, many still present significant barriers to entry for students.

Specifically, in the domain of humanoid robotics, the iCub platform is an open-source research robot with existing simulation environments, including "iCub SIM" and an iCub Gazebo simulator. These existing simulators, however, predominantly require C++ programming and rely on the Yet Another Robot Platform (YARP) middleware for communication. While Python bindings for YARP exist, their syntax can be complex for novice users. This complexity can shift the educational focus from core robotics principles to troubleshooting middleware and compilation issues, as observed in previous course implementations.

The presented work aims to bridge this gap by offering a more accessible, Python-centric simulation and exercise framework for the iCub robot. By eliminating the necessity for C++ and YARP, pyCub positions itself as an alternative educational tool designed to simplify the initial learning curve, allowing students to engage directly with humanoid robotics concepts without extensive prior experience in specific programming languages or middleware systems. This aligns with a broader trend in robotics education towards developing more user-friendly, high-level programming interfaces and integrated learning environments, as seen in projects like Unibotics or frameworks combining Conda and Jupyter Notebooks for ROS.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop an accessible and user-friendly simulation and exercise framework, named pyCub, specifically for teaching the fundamentals of humanoid robotics. This framework was designed to facilitate hands-on learning for students, particularly within a university course setting.

The motivation for developing pyCub originated from direct feedback and observations during previous runs of a humanoid robotics course. In these courses, students utilized the iCub Gazebo simulator, which is controlled via C++ code and the YARP middleware. Despite efforts to simplify the setup using Docker containers, student feedback consistently highlighted the complexity of the existing system, citing difficulties with YARP and C++ as significant impediments to their learning process. This complexity often diverted student attention from the core robotic concepts to the intricacies of software infrastructure and programming language specifics.

Against this background, the specific goals and design choices for pyCub were formulated:
- **Accessibility**: To create a simulator that is easy to install and operate, reducing the technical overhead for students with varying levels of programming experience. Python was chosen as the primary development and control language due to its reputation for user-friendliness.
- **Focus on iCub**: To simulate the iCub humanoid robot, an open-source research platform known for its human-like dimensions (resembling a four-year-old child), 53 active Degrees of Freedom (DoF), and distinctive whole-body artificial skin featuring over 4000 tactile sensors. This choice ensures relevance to advanced humanoid robotics research while providing a rich environment for learning.
- **Middleware Abstraction**: To operate without external middleware like YARP, thus streamlining the control pipeline and simplifying the robot's interaction.
- **Comprehensive Simulation Features**: To include a physics-based simulation of the complete iCub robot, incorporating all articulations, two cameras in the eyes, and a functional simulation of the artificial skin.
- **Structured Educational Exercises**: To provide a set of exercises with adjustable difficulty levels, ranging from basic robot control (velocity, joint, and Cartesian space) to more complex, typical humanoid tasks such as gazing, grasping, and reactive control based on tactile feedback. These exercises were intended to be modular and scalable to cater to different learning stages.
- **Public Availability**: To make the entire framework, including code, documentation, and exercises, open-source and publicly available to support broader educational and research communities.

The overarching motivation was to enable students to concentrate on the fundamental principles of robotics, such as kinematics, dynamics, control, perception, and human-robot interaction, by providing a simplified and intuitive platform for practical application.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of pyCub involved the creation of a physics-based simulation environment for the iCub humanoid robot, accompanied by a suite of educational exercises. The framework was designed with Python as the sole programming language to enhance accessibility.

#### 3.1. Simulation Environment (pyCub)

- **Physics Engine**: The core physics simulation is handled by the PyBullet engine. This engine was selected for its open-source nature, cross-platform compatibility, and relatively low computational demands compared to other contemporary simulators.
- **Robot Model**: A detailed simulation of the iCub robot was implemented, encompassing all its limbs and articulations. Key features included two simulated cameras in the robot's eyes, capable of providing RGB and depth images, and a simulation of the iCub's artificial whole-body skin, which consists of over 4000 individual tactile sensors. For performance considerations, a simplified model with less detailed meshes and without fingers was also developed.
- **Graphical User Interface (GUI)**: A custom GUI was developed using the Open3D library. This decision was made to overcome the limitations of PyBullet's built-in GUI, specifically its lack of customization, performance issues, and inability to support custom simulation stepping. The custom GUI allows for native window display or a web-based version, offers output visualization from the robot's eyes, and supports additional visualizations such as gaze vectors.
- **Time Management**: The simulation operates with manual stepping, requiring users to explicitly call `update_simulation` for each step. This approach grants users control over the simulation pace, enabling detailed observation of movements or allowing time for computations between steps. An option to synchronize simulation steps with elapsed real-time duration is also provided for slowing down movements.
- **Customization**: The environment is highly customizable via `.yaml` configuration files. Users can toggle features like the GUI, artificial skin, eye camera output, logging, and self-collisions. Initial joint positions, the end-effector for Cartesian control, and the simulation step size are also configurable. The framework supports importing additional objects into the simulation using Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) files or `.obj` triangular-mesh files, allowing specification of position, color, gravity influence, and movability.
- **Installation**: To ensure ease of deployment, pyCub can be installed from PyPI. Additionally, prebuilt Docker images and an online version accessible via the GitPod platform are provided. The framework was tested across multiple Linux distributions, Windows 10/11, and macOS.

#### 3.2. Control Modes

The robot offers three distinct control modes:
- **Joint Velocity Control**: Provides direct, low-level control by setting the angular velocity (in rad/s) for each controlled joint. This mode is suitable for reactive control.
- **Joint Position Control**: Sets target angular positions (in rad) for each joint. The robot moves towards these set-points, optionally stopping upon collision detection, including self-collisions.
- **Cartesian Position Control**: Allows an selected end-effector to be commanded to a desired 6D pose (3D position and 3D orientation). This mode internally computes Inverse Kinematics (IK) using PyBullet's Damped Least Squares method with null-space support and then uses joint position control. It does not include an internal path planner, which is intended as a learning point for students to understand trajectory generation.

#### 3.3. Artificial Skin Simulation

The simulation of the iCub's artificial skin is implemented using a raycasting method. For each of the over 4000 taxels, a short ray is cast. If a ray intersects with another object (robot part or environment object), the sensor output is computed proportionally to the ray's length, simulating contact intensity and the spread of activation observed in real skin due to deformation. To optimize computational load, raycasting is performed only for skin parts that are in proximity to other objects, determined by bounding box overlaps. Activated taxels are visualized as green points in the GUI, and their values are stored in a dictionary.

#### 3.4. Educational Exercises

A structured set of exercises was developed, each comprising a configuration file, an assignment script, and a testing script. Videos demonstrating each exercise are provided. The exercises are designed to be scalable in difficulty.
- **Push the Ball!**: An introductory exercise focusing on basic simulator control, where the goal is to move a ball as far as possible from a table.
- **Smooth Movements**: Aims to teach students how to generate smooth, continuous Cartesian trajectories (e.g., straight lines, circles) by understanding path sampling and preemption to avoid jerky motions.
- **Gaze**: Focuses on implementing gaze control, where the robot uses its neck and eye joints to fixate on a moving target in the XY-plane, utilizing given gaze vectors.
- **Reactive Control and Touch Processing**: Explores reactive behaviors to avoid obstacles using tactile feedback. Students process artificial skin data to locate contact points and apply Resolved-Rate Motion Control (RRMC) to move limbs away, experimenting with different Jacobian inverse methods.
- **Grasping**: Involves vision-based grasping of a green ball. Students detect the ball in camera images, deproject its 2D position to 3D, and execute a stable grasp, then lift the ball.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The deployment and evaluation of the pyCub framework yielded findings related to its performance characteristics and its educational effectiveness.

#### 4.1. Simulation Performance

Performance tests were conducted on two hardware configurations (an average laptop CPU and a workstation CPU) and with two robot models (a full model with detailed meshes and fingers, and a simplified model). Various configurations (with/without GUI, with/without skin) were assessed based on the real-time factor (ratio of simulation time to real time).
- **Full Model Performance**: On a laptop, the full model with GUI and skin enabled operated at approximately 0.95 times real-time. Without the skin, the real-time factor increased to 1.37. On a workstation, the full model achieved real-time factors of 1.29 with skin and 2.25 without skin.
- **Simplified Model Performance**: The simplified model demonstrated higher real-time factors across all configurations. On a laptop, it achieved 1.46 with GUI and skin, and 6.73 without skin. On a workstation, these figures rose to 2.58 with skin and 10.82 without skin.
- **Overall Assessment**: The observed performance, even on an average laptop, was deemed sufficient for educational applications, allowing for real-time or slower-than-real-time visualization of robot movements and computations. The single-core nature of PyBullet's computations meant that workstation improvements primarily reflected single-core efficiency.

#### 4.2. Educational Efficacy

The pyCub framework was integrated into two iterations of a humanoid robotics course. Comparative observations were made against previous course runs that utilized the iCub Gazebo simulator with C++ and YARP middleware.
- **Reduced Complexity**: A notable finding was the reduction in system complexity reported by students. Previous course evaluations indicated that students, even those with C++ experience, found the YARP middleware and C++ programming environment to be "troublesome to learn," leading to complaints about compilation issues and the overall system setup.
- **Shifted Learning Focus**: The transition to the Python-based pyCub framework allowed students to reorient their focus. Feedback suggested that students were able to concentrate more directly on the robotic aspects of the exercises, such as kinematics, control algorithms, and perception, rather than on resolving middleware or language-specific infrastructure challenges.
- **Enhanced Engagement**: The introductory "Push the Ball!" exercise, designed for basic simulator familiarity, proved particularly engaging. Students explored diverse and creative solutions beyond simple arm-based hitting, including grasping and throwing, or even kicking the ball, indicating a higher level of interactive engagement with the simplified platform.

#### 4.3. Framework Availability

The complete pyCub framework, including the simulation code, comprehensive documentation, pre-built Docker images, and illustrative videos of the exercises, has been made publicly available.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The pyCub framework presents a significant contribution to robotics education, particularly in the domain of humanoid robotics, through its emphasis on accessibility and focused learning.

- **Democratization of Humanoid Robotics Education**: By providing a Python-based simulation environment for the iCub robot that eschews the complexity of C++ and YARP middleware, pyCub substantially lowers the entry barrier for students and educators. This allows individuals with less extensive programming backgrounds or those new to robotics to engage with complex humanoid robot platforms and concepts. This accessibility can broaden the participation in humanoid robotics studies and research.
- **Improved Pedagogical Efficiency**: The framework's design directly addresses a documented problem in robotics education: the time and effort students expend on technical setup and debugging of complex middleware. By abstracting these elements, pyCub enables students to allocate more attention to core robotic principles such as kinematics, control, perception, and human-robot interaction. This shift has the potential to enhance learning outcomes and foster deeper conceptual understanding.
- **Comprehensive Practical Learning**: The integrated set of exercises, spanning from basic control to advanced tasks like gaze control, reactive tactile-based interaction, and vision-guided grasping, offers a structured and comprehensive pathway for practical learning. The adjustability of exercise difficulty further supports diverse learning needs, from introductory courses to more specialized modules.
- **Foundation for Research and Development**: While primarily educational, pyCub's open-source nature and Python interface could also serve as a rapid prototyping environment for researchers. New algorithms for humanoid control, perception, or interaction could be initially developed and tested in pyCub before being adapted for more advanced simulators or real robot platforms.
- **Community Contribution**: The public availability of the code, documentation, and Docker images facilitates community adoption, modification, and expansion of the framework. This open-source approach can foster collaborative development of new exercises, features, and educational resources, thereby amplifying its impact beyond a single institution.
- **Addressing a Gap in Existing Tools**: The framework directly addresses a specific limitation identified in existing iCub training programs, offering a practical and effective alternative that enhances the student learning experience.

Future development plans, such as integrating a more sophisticated Cartesian planner or a ROS interface, indicate the framework's potential for continued evolution and integration into broader robotics ecosystems, further extending its utility and impact.
