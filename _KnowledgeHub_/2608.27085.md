# Pass the Bucket: Efficient, Robust, Local Load Balancing for Teams of Heterogeneous Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> A decentralized load-balancing scheme for heterogeneous robot teams sharing a one-dimensional interval. Robots reverse on collisions with neighbors or walls, self-organizing into territories proportional to their speeds. Because undamped collision dynamics are provably conservative and never settle, each robot stores its last collision position as a token and slows by a factor after crossing it, which damps oscillation and drives convergence to the balanced partition in simulation.

## Problem

- Classical bucket-brigade theory assumes worker ordering by velocity and infinite backward speed, assumptions that fail for robot teams with identical bidirectional travel speeds.
- Existing multi-robot load balancing relies on centralized planners, localization, or message passing, all unavailable in confined aisles and corridors lacking communication infrastructure.
- Prior robotic bucket-brigade deployments show feasibility but never analyze heterogeneous velocities, damping, or whether collision dynamics actually converge rather than oscillate forever.

## Method

- ==Event-based collision-abscissa formalism==: an affine three-banded map on adjacent-pair meeting points, decomposed into per-event operators whose product gives the one-sweep transfer matrix.
- ==Token damping==: each robot stores one collision position; crossing it cuts its speed by factor alpha until the next reversal, dropping the token at the next collision.
- Two token placements compared, a single one-directional boundary token (==Token-1==) versus two-directional tokens between every adjacent pair (==Token-all==), plus a sweep over alpha.

## Results

- A symmetric positive-definite tridiagonal invariant proves the undamped map is an isometry with all eigenvalues on the unit circle, so trajectories never converge unless started at equilibrium.
- Token-all converges faster than Token-1 across **1000 trials** with **3 to 7** robots; optimal slowdown factor falls from **0.38** to **0.29** as robot count grows from **3** to **9**.
- Reconvergence holds under robot addition or removal, velocity scaling by **0.5** or **2**, and position jitter; jitter cost grows at most linearly up to **25 percent** displacement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A conserved quadratic invariant is the diagnostic for why a coordination scheme oscillates forever; breaking the isometry, not tuning gains, is what restores convergence.
> - One scalar of memory plus a local speed cut can replace communication, localization, and central planning for proportional territory allocation among unequal agents.
> - Damping strength has an interior optimum that shifts with team size, so a fixed conservative slowdown is suboptimal and overly aggressive damping degrades convergence superlinearly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses fundamental challenges in multi-robot coordination, specifically decentralized, self-organized task sharing for heterogeneous robot swarms. Multi-robot systems offer potential benefits in efficiency through parallelism and robustness through redundancy. However, they frequently encounter obstacles such as communication overhead, scalability limitations of centralized control architectures, and a lack of resilience to various perturbations, particularly when dealing with teams of heterogeneous autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) with limited sensing capabilities.

The paper situates its work within the context of collision-driven bucket brigades on a one-dimensional interval. The concept of bucket brigades originates from Operations Research, where studies by Bartholdi and Eisenstein demonstrated that deterministic assembly lines could self-balance under specific conditions, such as workers being ordered by increasing velocity and possessing infinite backward speed. Subsequent research extended these concepts to throughput maximization, stochastic task times, and different topologies. The current work departs from these classical approaches by focusing on collision-driven dynamics for self-organizing territory partitioning, rather than relying on predefined ordering or centralized scheduling. It specifically addresses the complexity introduced by finite and distinct forward/backward velocities, a scenario that previous work found challenging for convergence.

The problem shares conceptual similarities with "fence patrolling" research, where agents with varying velocities minimize point latency on a line segment, often resulting in periodic schedules that partition the line proportionally to velocities—mirroring the equilibrium state pursued in this study.

Existing robotic deployments of bucket brigades in areas like foraging, assembly, and transport have demonstrated practical feasibility. However, the authors note that these prior works often lack systematic analysis concerning heterogeneous teams, the mechanisms for damping system oscillations, and guaranteed convergence properties. This paper aims to fill this gap by providing a systematic analysis of these aspects, particularly under conditions of minimal individual robot capabilities (local collision sensing, direction reversal, minimal memory, no shared clock, no explicit communication).

The work distinguishes itself from alternative load-balancing and coordination approaches that rely on non-zero communication or centralized control. While communication can simplify coordination by allowing direct exchange of information or remote position sharing, it introduces requirements for infrastructure, accurate self-knowledge, and onboard computation that are not always feasible. This research instead emphasizes a communication-free design, where coordination emerges from local physical interactions and a minimal local damping mechanism. By doing so, it contributes a novel approach to achieving robust load balancing in resource-constrained and infrastructure-limited environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a decentralized, self-organized task-sharing mechanism for a swarm of heterogeneous robots operating in a confined, one-dimensional spatial environment. The overarching goal is to optimize the throughput of the entire system without relying on central control or explicit communication. This is to be achieved by inducing an interval partition where each robot covers a share of the space proportional to its intrinsic velocity.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in multi-robot systems, especially for heterogeneous teams:
1. **Scalability and Robustness:** Centralized control systems often face scalability limitations with large fleets and can become single points of failure, making them susceptible to perturbations. Decentralized, self-organized approaches offer robustness through redundancy and distributed decision-making.
2. **Resource Constraints:** Many real-world robotic applications, such as those in confined warehouse aisles, agricultural rows, or infrastructure corridors, operate with resource-constrained platforms. These robots may have minimal sensing capabilities (only able to detect collisions with neighbors or walls), limited memory, no shared clock, and no communication channels beyond physical contact. The research is motivated by the need for coordination mechanisms that function effectively under such severe limitations.
3. **Heterogeneity:** Real-world robot teams are often heterogeneous, meaning individual robots possess different capabilities, most notably varying speeds. The objective is to leverage this heterogeneity to achieve an optimal system throughput by proportionally distributing the workload based on each robot's speed.
4. **Addressing Chaotic Behavior:** The authors identify that, in an undamped collision-driven system, the dynamics are intricate and can lead to persistent oscillations and chaotic behavior, preventing asymptotic convergence to a stable, optimal state. Even with just two identical robots, indefinitely oscillating patterns can emerge. This motivated the need for a mechanism to stabilize the system.
5. **Practical Application:** The research aims to provide a practical mechanism that can serve as a basis for more complex scenarios. This involves demonstrating not only convergence to a desired equilibrium but also robustness against common real-world disturbances such as robot failures (addition or removal), changes in individual robot velocities, and localization noise.

In summary, the key objectives are to analytically understand the dynamics of collision-driven bucket brigades, to design a simple local mechanism (a "token") that eliminates chaotic oscillations and ensures convergence to a velocity-proportional spatial load distribution, to empirically evaluate its convergence speed and robustness to various perturbations, and to propose a viable, low-overhead solution for multi-robot coordination in constrained environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research combines mathematical analysis with extensive event-driven simulations to develop and validate a decentralized load-balancing mechanism for heterogeneous robot teams.

**System Model:**
The system consists of *n* robots operating on a one-dimensional line segment. Each robot *i* has a constant velocity magnitude *v<sub>i</sub> > 0*, a position *p<sub>i</sub>(t)*, and a direction *d<sub>i</sub>(t)*. Robots move at constant velocity between discrete events. Their direction changes only upon interaction:
- **Head-on Neighbor Encounters:** Both robots reverse direction.
- **Same-Direction Catch-ups:** Only the faster robot reverses.
- **Wall Contacts:** Reversal occurs at boundaries 0 or 1.
The system is designed such that the left-to-right order of robots is preserved, preventing overtakes. The goal equilibrium is a partition where adjacent robots meet at fixed points *x*^*<sub>i</sub>*, such that the subinterval lengths are proportional to their respective velocities, defined as *x*^*<sub>i</sub>* = (sum of *v<sub>j</sub>* for *j=1...i*) / (sum of all *v<sub>j</sub>* for *j=1...n*).

**Mathematical Analysis (Undamped System):**
The initial phase of the research focuses on the behavior of the undamped system.
- **Event-based Tracking:** The analysis tracks the discrete collision positions *x<sub>i</sub>(k)* for each adjacent pair *(i, i+1)*, considering boundary markers *x<sub>0</sub> = 0* and *x<sub>n</sub> = 1*.
- **Rotational Regime Assumption:** The analysis concentrates on a "rotational regime" where only alternating head-on encounters occur, and catch-up collisions are absent, as this is the state observed near the desired equilibrium.
- **Affine Map Formulation:** The update from one set of collision positions *x* to the next *x'* after one alternating sweep of head-on encounters is modeled as a three-banded affine system: *Ax' = Bx + c*.
- **Centered Dynamics:** By defining *u = x - x*^* (deviations from equilibrium), the dynamics are linearized to *u' = Mu*, where *M = A⁻¹B*. The equilibrium *x*^* is the unique fixed point, meaning *u=0* is the fixed point for the centered dynamics.
- **G-Isometry Proof:** A central theoretical contribution is the proof that the matrix *M* is a G-isometry. This involves constructing a symmetric positive definite tridiagonal matrix *G* such that *MᵀGM = G*. This property implies that *uᵀGu* is an invariant quadratic form, meaning each trajectory lies on an invariant ellipsoid. This directly proves that the undamped system is conservative and, therefore, cannot converge to the equilibrium state *u=0* unless it starts there.

**Damping Mechanism (Token Mechanics):**
To overcome the non-convergence of the undamped system, a local damping mechanism, termed a "token," is introduced.
- **Mechanism:** A token is a stored collision abscissa between adjacent robots. When a robot crosses this stored position, its velocity is temporarily reduced by a factor *α* ∈ (0, 1) until its next reversal. Upon a subsequent collision, the token is effectively "dropped" and re-established at the new collision point.
- **Token Variants:**
 - **Single, One-directional Token ("Token-1"):** A single token is placed between the first pair of robots (robots 1 and 2), affecting only robot 1's velocity. Mathematical analysis (determinant contraction) showed that *|det M<sub>α</sub>| < 1*, providing a step towards convergence. A full spectral contraction proof for *M<sub>α</sub>* remains a conjecture.
 - **Ubiquitous Tokens ("Token-all"):** Tokens are placed between every adjacent pair of robots. Each robot stores its last collision abscissa with both neighbors and adjusts its velocity accordingly.

**Simulation Experiments:**
To empirically evaluate the behavior of the damped system and address analytically unresolved aspects, a Python-based event-driven simulator was developed, utilizing high-precision arithmetic.
- **Experiment Design:** Monte Carlo simulations were conducted with *N = 1000* trials, involving up to *n = 9* robots. Initial positions were random in, directions were random, and velocities were sampled uniformly from [0.5, 10].
- **Comparison Groups:** Simulations compared systems with no tokens, Token-1, and Token-all (with a slowdown factor *α = 0.5* unless varied).
- **Metrics:** Key metrics included convergence time (defined as the time until the previous 10 collision abscissas for every pair were within *ε = 5 × 10⁻⁴* of their respective equilibrium abscissa), the ratio of catch-up events, and reconvergence times after perturbations.
- **Research Questions Explored:**
 - **RQ1 (Rotational Regime):** Investigated if systems consistently converge to the catch-up-free rotational regime by tracking the ratio of catch-up events.
 - **RQ2 (Token Placement Impact):** Compared convergence speeds of Token-1 vs. Token-all for varying *n* using empirical complementary cumulative distribution functions (CCDFs).
 - **RQ3 (Velocity Heterogeneity):** Examined the impact of velocity range on convergence by sweeping the upper bound of uniformly sampled velocities.
 - **RQ4 (Optimal Damping Factor):** Assessed the influence of *α* on mean convergence time for different *n* (0 < *α* < 1 in 0.01 increments) to identify empirically optimal *α* values.
 - **RQ5 (Robustness to Perturbations):** Applied various perturbations (robot addition/removal, position jitter of individual or all robots, velocity jitter) from an equilibrium-like state and measured reconvergence times. Statistical tests (Mann–Whitney U with Bonferroni correction) were used for comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the dynamics, convergence, and robustness of the bucket brigade system:

**Undamped System Dynamics:**
- **Non-Convergence:** Mathematical analysis definitively proved that the undamped system is conservative. It functions as a G-isometry in centered coordinates, meaning trajectories lie on invariant ellipsoids. Consequently, the system cannot converge to its velocity-proportional equilibrium state unless it is initialized precisely at that equilibrium. Instead, it exhibits persistent oscillations.

**Convergence to Rotational Regime (RQ1):**
- Simulations demonstrated that, regardless of the token mechanism used, the ratio of same-direction catch-up events to total collisions rapidly decreases and eventually reaches zero. This suggests that the system reliably enters and remains in the desired rotational regime, where only head-on collisions occur. Tokens accelerate this transition.

**Impact of Token Placement on Convergence (RQ2):**
- Both Token-1 and Token-all mechanisms successfully induce convergence of collision abscissas towards the equilibrium.
- **Faster Convergence with Ubiquitous Tokens:** Systems employing "Token-all" (tokens between all adjacent robot pairs) exhibited significantly faster convergence times compared to those using "Token-1" (a single one-directional token between the first pair).
- **Scaling with Number of Robots:** Convergence time generally increased with the number of robots (*n*), particularly with Token-1, where the increase was more pronounced.

**Influence of Velocity Heterogeneity (RQ3):**
- Increasing the maximum value of the uniformly sampled velocity range, while keeping the minimum constant, resulted in faster convergence times. This suggests that higher average velocities and potentially increased collision rates may contribute to quicker system stabilization.

**Optimal Damping Factor (RQ4):**
- Simulations identified an empirically optimal slowdown factor *α* for the "Token-all" configuration that minimizes the mean convergence time.
- **Dependence on Number of Robots:** The optimal *α* value was observed to decrease as the number of robots (*n*) increased (e.g., *α* ≈ 0.38 for n=3, decreasing to *α* ≈ 0.29 for n=9).
- **Sensitivity to α:** Deviations from the optimal *α* in either direction led to a super-linear increase in average convergence time, indicating a sensitivity to this parameter.

**Robustness to Perturbations (RQ5):**
The system demonstrated robustness across various types of perturbations, reliably reconverging to a (potentially new) equilibrium state.
- **Robot Addition/Removal:** The system successfully reconfigured and reconverged to the new velocity-proportional equilibrium after a robot was either added or removed. Reconvergence after robot removal was consistently faster than after robot addition. Token-all maintained its advantage, inducing faster reconvergence than Token-1.
- **Position Jitter:** When robots' initial positions were jittered (up to 25% of the interval length), the mean reconvergence time increased, but this growth was at most linear. No catastrophic degradation or system failure was observed even with substantial position displacements, indicating graceful performance degradation.
- **Velocity Perturbation:** Changes in a single robot's velocity (by factors of 0.5 or 2) also triggered reconvergence. The system recovered significantly faster than from a random initial start. Increasing a robot's velocity (x2) led to faster reconvergence than decreasing it (x0.5), consistent with the general trend observed for velocity heterogeneity.

In summary, the token-based damping mechanism effectively addresses the inherent non-convergence of undamped collision-driven systems. It enables robust, self-organized load balancing in heterogeneous robot teams, demonstrating adaptability to changes in team composition, individual capabilities, and environmental noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-robot systems by offering a fundamentally decentralized, communication-free, and infrastructure-independent solution for load balancing among heterogeneous robots operating in confined linear spaces. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized across theoretical, practical, and application domains.

**Theoretical Significance:**
The paper provides a rigorous mathematical analysis demonstrating that undamped collision-driven bucket brigades are conservative systems, incapable of converging to a stable equilibrium unless initiated precisely in that state. This theoretical finding clarifies a fundamental limitation of such dynamics. The introduction of the "token" mechanism, a simple local damping strategy, provides a novel approach to overcome this inherent non-convergence. While a full spectral contraction proof for the damped system remains a conjecture, the empirical evidence strongly supports its efficacy, bridging a gap between theoretical understanding and practical implementation for self-organizing systems.

**Practical Impact and Minimal Requirements:**
The proposed token mechanism requires minimal capabilities from individual robots: only velocity measurement, acceleration control, collision detection, and a single-value memory (to store the last collision position). This low overhead distinguishes it from many existing multi-robot coordination strategies that demand complex communication protocols, centralized computation, or advanced sensing infrastructure (e.g., global positioning, remote object tracking). This makes the approach highly suitable for resource-constrained platforms and environments where such infrastructure is unreliable, unavailable, or too costly to deploy.

**Robustness and Resilience:**
The extensive simulation experiments highlight the system's remarkable robustness against a variety of operational disturbances, including robot addition or removal, changes in individual robot velocities (simulating battery degradation or payload variations), and localization inaccuracies (position jitter). The consistent reconvergence to the (potentially new) optimal equilibrium demonstrates an inherent resilience that is crucial for reliable long-term operation in dynamic real-world settings. This robustness translates directly into enhanced reliability and reduced operational downtime.

**Potential Application Domains:**
The communication-free and decentralized nature of this load-balancing approach has significant potential impact across several application areas:
- **Warehouse & Intralogistics:** In scenarios with large fleets of AMRs navigating narrow aisles, centralized control faces scalability limitations. This mechanism offers a decentralized alternative for task allocation and coordination, allowing heterogeneous robot teams to dynamically adjust to changing demands or robot failures without needing a central orchestrator. This can improve throughput and adaptability in dynamic warehouse environments.
- **Agricultural Automation:** Agricultural robots often operate in challenging environments with limited communication infrastructure and in row-constrained spaces. The bucket brigade approach enables heterogeneous multi-robot teams (e.g., varying capacities or speeds for different tasks) to coordinate effectively for tasks like planting, harvesting, or inspection without relying on external communication, while also being resilient to environmental perturbations.
- **Infrastructure Inspection:** For linear structures such as pipelines, tunnels, or power lines, multi-robot inspection is often limited by the absence of communication infrastructure in confined spaces. This communication-free coordination mechanism enables teams of robots to efficiently cover long linear segments, adapting to robot failures or changing inspection requirements.

**Foundation for Future Research:**
The work lays a foundation for future extensions, such as applying the concept to more complex graph topologies or investigating slightly more sophisticated local coordination mechanisms (e.g., different token types or "traffic light" rules). By establishing a robust baseline for 1D heterogeneous load balancing, it opens avenues for developing more advanced, yet still locally governed, multi-robot behaviors.

In conclusion, this research provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated mechanism for robust, efficient, and locally managed load balancing in heterogeneous robot teams. Its minimal requirements and demonstrated resilience position it as a valuable complementary approach for multi-robot coordination in diverse, infrastructure-limited environments.
