# A Case Study on the Acceptance of a Humanoid Robotic Head Employed in Three Public Spaces

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stuttgart Media University assessed public acceptance of an interactive android robotic head, Kim, deployed in a tourist information center, building authority, and city library in Germany. The study found predominantly positive user perceptions of usefulness and ease of use, high utilization of multilingual features, but identified slow response times as a key impediment to natural dialogue.

## Problem

- Lack of comparative research on android robot acceptance across multiple distinct public settings using validated questionnaire frameworks.
- Limited empirical data on how highly anthropomorphic robots are perceived and accepted by the general public in varied, real-world service contexts.
- Need to understand factors influencing human acceptance of human-like robots beyond controlled laboratory settings.

## Method

- Deployed an interactive android robotic head (**Kim**) with a sophisticated software architecture for NLP, multimodal responses, and emotional expressions in three public locations: a tourist information center, a building authority office, and a city library.
- Utilized location-specific OpenAI assistants with ==Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)== to provide context-sensitive textual responses and an emotion simulation backend (==WASABI==) for emotional expressions.
- Evaluated user acceptance, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use across locations using a German translation of the ==TAM2== questionnaire, complemented by feedback on emotionality, interaction time, languages, and open-ended suggestions.

## Results

- Approximately one-third of participants (**18** out of **49**) had prior knowledge of the robot, with half of those specifically visiting the location to interact with it.
- Median user acceptance metrics (ITU, PU, PEOU) were generally positive (above **4** on a 7-point scale) across all locations, but no statistically significant differences were found between the three deployment sites (**p > 0.05**).
- The robot's multilingualism facilitated interactions in **17** diverse languages; however, **20%** of users reported that slow response times impeded dialogue flow.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Public acceptance metrics (Intention to Use, Perceived Usefulness, Perceived Ease of Use) for the android robot showed no statistically significant differences across the three distinct public settings, suggesting a relatively consistent baseline perception.
> - Multilingual capabilities were highly utilized and appreciated by diverse visitors, interacting in 17 languages beyond German and English, underscoring their importance for inclusive human-robot interaction.
> - Slow response times were identified by users as a critical technical barrier impeding natural human-robot dialogue and overall user experience, despite generally positive perceptions of the robot's emotional displays.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of human-like robots into everyday societal functions is a growing area of research within Human-Robot Interaction (HRI). This field investigates the factors influencing human acceptance of robots, including their appearance, perceived usefulness, task performance capabilities, and human expectations and attitudes. Cultural norms are also recognized as significant determinants of robot acceptance. Previous studies have explored various applications of humanoid and android robots in public and service-oriented environments, such as salespeople in retail, receptionists in various institutions, HR experts, interviewers, restaurant staff, and information kiosks in museums.

Android robots, a subclass of humanoid robots designed for close human resemblance, present unique challenges and opportunities for acceptance due to their human-like appearance, which can evoke complex psychological responses. While various studies have deployed humanoid robots in public spaces, existing literature demonstrates a comparative gap. Specifically, there is a lack of research that conducts a direct comparative analysis of an *android* robot's acceptance across *multiple distinct public settings* using a validated and widely recognized questionnaire framework such as the Technology Acceptance Model Version 2 (TAM2). This paper aims to address this identified research gap by providing a real-world, comparative assessment of an android robotic head's acceptance in diverse public environments, thus contributing empirical data to the understanding of human interaction with highly anthropomorphic robots in varied contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research stems from the increasing potential for human-like robots to become integrated into daily life and the critical role of human acceptance in enabling this integration. Prior research indicates that a robot's acceptance is heavily influenced by the operational setting and its ability to perform relevant tasks effectively. To further understand these dynamics, particularly for highly human-like robots, direct investigation in uncontrolled public environments is necessary.

The key objectives of this study were:

- **To detail the technical implementation of the android robotic head:** This included describing its hardware components and the sophisticated software architecture enabling natural language processing, multimodal verbal responses, and the integration of emotional expressions via an emotion simulation backend. The aim was to transparently present the robot's capabilities that facilitate human interaction.
- **To deploy the interactive robotic head in diverse public locations:** The researchers aimed to introduce their android robot, Kim, to three distinct public spaces—a tourist information center, a building authority office, and a city library—to observe and document interactions with unsolicited visitors in their natural environments. This objective sought to gather real-world data under varying contextual conditions.
- **To evaluate user acceptance, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use across locations:** Utilizing a German translation of the Technology Acceptance Model Version 2 (TAM2) questionnaire, the study aimed to quantitatively measure key acceptance metrics such as intention to use (ITU), perceived usefulness (PU), and perceived ease of use (PEOU) from visitors who interacted with the robot at each site. A specific goal was to compare these metrics across the different locations to identify potential context-dependent variations in acceptance.
- **To assess user perceptions of the robot's emotional expressions and multilingual capabilities:** The research intended to gather user feedback on the robot's ability to display emotional reactions and the appropriateness of these responses. Furthermore, it aimed to document the extent to which the robot's multilingual capabilities were utilized and perceived by the public, alongside other qualitative feedback.
- **To identify factors influencing the human-robot dialogue flow and user experience:** By collecting data on interaction times and open-ended suggestions, the study sought to identify technical or interaction design aspects that either enhanced or impeded the conversational experience, such as response time or general impressions of the robot.

In essence, the study was motivated by the need for empirical data on how highly anthropomorphic robots are accepted by the general public in varied, real-world service contexts, and to identify specific design and deployment considerations that impact this acceptance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research involved the deployment of a human-like android robot head, named Kim, in three public spaces in Stuttgart, Germany, over a period of three weeks, with continuous operation for several days at each location. The study combined detailed technical descriptions of the robot with an observational and survey-based methodology to assess public acceptance.

#### 3.1. Hardware and Software Components

**1. Hardware:**
The robot head, Kim, was manufactured by a Japanese company known for producing other prominent androids like Andrea and Erica. It is equipped with 14 pneumatic actuators that control facial movements, enabling expressive animations. These movements are controlled by sending 14 integer values (0-255) via RS-485 at 25 Hz using a custom-developed software framework. Unlike some other androids, Kim lacks integrated eye cameras; instead, a webcam placed behind the head detects and targets the closest person for eye gaze. Audio input is captured by an external microphone, and output is provided by a loudspeaker, facilitating conversational interaction. The robot's operations are managed by an Nvidia Jetson Orin, powered by compressed air and electricity for its actuators. Kim typically wore a black T-shirt and sometimes a baseball cap to conceal its rear components.

**2. Software Components:**
The software framework is an extended version of previous work, integrating four open-source Machine Learning (ML) models:
- **Speech-to-Text (STT):** `whisper-large-v3-turbo` model is employed for transcribing user audio input, supporting multiple languages.
- **Text-to-Speech (TTS):** XTTS is used for speech synthesis, supporting 17 languages. A specific design-congruent voice was cloned and consistently used across all languages.
- **Lip-sync Animations:** FaceXHubert replaced an older model to accelerate the generation of lip-sync animations synchronized with the speech output.
- **People Tracking:** PoseNet continuously analyzes the webcam video stream to identify human faces and direct the robot's eye gaze towards the closest detected person when speaking or awaiting input.

Interaction initiation required users to manually activate the microphone, which triggered a "listening" animation. This design decision was made to prevent false activations in noisy public environments. After input, the robot displayed a "thinking" animation. To enhance human-likeness, the robot blinks randomly and yawns during periods of inactivity.

Context-sensitive textual responses were generated by three location-specific OpenAI assistants, built upon the `gpt-4.1` model. These assistants were configured with system prompts containing information about the robot and its current location. Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) was employed to provide additional context, such as location-specific databases.

Emotion simulation was managed by WASABI, running as a concurrent process. The OpenAI assistant was prompted to calculate the valence (ranging from -100 to +100) of the user's last input sentence, which was then fed to WASABI. WASABI, in turn, returned the likelihood of emotions such as "happy," "sad," "angry," "fearful," "disgusted," "surprised," or "neutral." The internal dynamics of WASABI allowed the robot's emotional state to gradually return to "neutral" in the absence of new inputs. These emotional states were expressed through validated static facial expressions, with thinking and speech animations taking priority over emotional expressions for relevant actuators.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup

The robot was deployed at three distinct public locations, with location-specific information pre-loaded into the ChatGPT-based backend for RAG:

- **Tourist Information (Stuttgart):** May 26-30, 2025, 10 AM - 6 PM daily. The robot was placed on the front desk, visible to visitors. A roll-up banner, table display, and poster provided information and interaction instructions. The robot wore a Stuttgart-branded cap and an employee T-shirt. The database included information on opening hours, tours, transport, tickets, services, and city tips.
- **Building Authority (Stuttgart):** June 3-6, 2025, with restricted hours (Tuesday/Wednesday 2-4 PM, Thursday 9 AM-6 PM, Friday 9 AM-12 PM). Located in a less busy area, it attracted more employees than general visitors. Similar informational banners and displays were used. The robot was placed on a table at user eye-level. The database contained relevant laws.
- **City Library (Stuttgart):** June 10-14, 2025, 9 AM - 6 PM daily. Situated on the ground floor in a large, open space, ensuring visibility. Informational materials were also present. The database included information on opening hours, building details, and book category locations.

#### 3.3. Study Procedure and Measurement

After interacting with the robot, visitors were invited to complete a questionnaire available in both English and German, preceded by informed consent. The total number of completed questionnaires was 49 (Tourist Information: 19; Building Authority: 7; City Library: 23).

The questionnaire was based on a German translation of TAM2 and covered:
- **General Information:** Location, date, time, age.
- **Prior Knowledge:** Whether participants knew about the robot beforehand and if it influenced their visit (yes/no questions).
- **TAM2 Items:** Measured Intention to Use (ITU), Perceived Usefulness (PU), and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) using a 7-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 7=strongly agree). Each construct was measured with two to four items, and mean values were calculated per participant.
- **Emotion-related Questions:** Assessed perceived emotionality (displaying reactions, appropriate responses, mood swings) using a 7-point Likert scale.
- **Additional Questions:** Self-reported interaction time, languages used, and open-ended suggestions.
- **Overall Usefulness:** A distinct 0-10 Likert scale (0='not at all', 10='very much') for "How useful do you think would it be to use this robot here today?".

**Measurement and Statistical Analysis:**
Descriptive statistics (N, mean, median, standard deviation) were computed for all variables by location. Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Due to significant deviations from normal distribution in several groups, non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied to compare ITU, PU, PEOU, and "Usefulness today" across the three locations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of the collected data revealed several observations regarding the public's interaction with and acceptance of the android robotic head, Kim, across the three public spaces.

#### 4.1. Previous Knowledge of the Robot

Out of 49 total participants, 18 (approximately one-third) reported prior knowledge of the robot's availability at the respective location. Among these 18 individuals, 9 (half) indicated that their decision to visit the location was specifically influenced by the opportunity to interact with the robot. This suggests a notable level of public interest in engaging with human-like robots in public settings.

#### 4.2. Differences by Location (TAM2 and Perceived Usefulness)

Descriptive statistics were calculated for Intention to Use (ITU), Perceived Usefulness (PU), Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU), and "Usefulness today" for each location. Median values for ITU, PU, and PEOU across all locations were generally above 4 on the 7-point Likert scale, suggesting a predominantly positive perception on these measures. Similarly, the "Usefulness today" measure, on a 0-10 scale, showed median values of 7 for Tourist Information and City Library, and 5 for Building Authority.

However, formal statistical analysis using the Kruskal-Wallis test showed no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05 for all variables) between the three locations for ITU (p=0.900), PU (p=0.501), PEOU (p=0.190), or "Usefulness today" (p=0.364). This indicates that, statistically, the acceptance metrics did not vary significantly across the tourist information, building authority, and city library environments, despite some descriptive trends (e.g., lower medians for ITU and "Usefulness today" at the Building Authority).

#### 4.3. Perceived Emotionality, Interaction Time, Languages Used, and Suggestions

- **Perceived Emotionality:** Participants generally perceived the robot as "rather emotional," with median values greater than four (on a 7-point scale) for "I have the impression that the robot displays emotional reactions" (Tourist Info: 4.0, Building Authority: 6.0, City Library: 5.0) and "I find the robot’s emotional responses appropriate to the situation" (Tourist Info: 5.0, Building Authority: 6.0, City Library: 5.0). Conversely, participants generally did not perceive the robot as suffering from "mood swings," with median values less than four (Tourist Info: 2.5, Building Authority: 2.0, City Library: 2.0). No significant differences in emotional perception were found across the locations.
- **Interaction Time:** Self-reported interaction times ranged from "not at all" to thirty minutes, with an average of seven minutes and a median of five minutes.
- **Languages Used:** The robot's multilingual capabilities were widely utilized. Participants interacted with the robot in a diverse array of 17 languages beyond German and English, including French, Spanish, Italian, Turkish, Arabic, Chinese, Croatian, Korean, Latvian, Persian, Polish, Portuguese, Russian, Swabian (a local German dialect), and Vietnamese. German was the most frequently used language (40 instances), followed by English (10 instances).
- **User Suggestions and Comments:**
 - A notable proportion (10 out of 49, or approximately 20%) of users commented on the robot's **slow response time**, indicating it impeded the dialog flow.
 - Five users found the robot head to appear "uncanny."
 - Conversely, seven users described the robot as "funny," "nice," or "innovative."

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study makes several contributions to the field of Human-Robot Interaction, particularly concerning the deployment and acceptance of highly anthropomorphic robots in public spaces.

Firstly, it represents one of the initial attempts to conduct a comparative analysis of an *android* robot across *multiple, distinct real-world public settings* using a validated acceptance questionnaire (TAM2). This approach provides empirical data on public perception in uncontrolled environments, which is crucial for understanding the practical implications of robot deployment beyond laboratory settings. The findings contribute to the growing body of literature on real-world HRI, specifically with very human-like robotic heads.

Secondly, despite the lack of statistical significance across locations for acceptance metrics, the descriptive trends observed, particularly the lower intention to use and perceived usefulness at the building authority compared to the tourist information and city library, suggest a context-dependency for android robot acceptance. This aligns with broader survey findings indicating higher acceptability of robots in cultural and public leisure environments than in administrative services. This implies that robot designers and deployers should consider the functional and social fit of a robot within a given environment, suggesting that service-oriented contexts with explicit information-seeking needs (e.g., tourist information, libraries) may be more suitable for interactive robotic heads than administrative or governmental settings.

Thirdly, the research highlights the practical value of advanced robot capabilities. The robot's multilingualism was demonstrably appreciated and utilized by a diverse range of visitors, interacting in 17 different languages. This underscores the importance of such features in enhancing inclusivity and user experience in multicultural public spaces. However, the study also identifies a critical area for improvement: response time. The frequent mention of slow response times by users indicates a significant barrier to natural and fluent human-robot dialogue, impeding the overall interaction flow and potentially affecting long-term acceptance. Addressing this technical limitation could substantially enhance user experience.

Fourthly, the study provides insights into user perceptions of robot emotionality. The robot was generally perceived as displaying appropriate emotional reactions and not exhibiting mood swings, suggesting that the emotion simulation backend (WASABI) and its facial expression integration were largely effective in conveying intended emotional cues.

Finally, the reported public interest—with approximately one-third of participants having prior knowledge of the robot and half of those specifically visiting to interact with it—indicates a general curiosity and willingness among the public to engage with very human-like robots. This suggests a foundational receptiveness that, if cultivated through improved interaction dynamics and context-appropriate deployment, could facilitate the broader integration of such technology into society.

The limitations of the study, such as the relatively small sample size, particularly at the building authority, and the uncontrolled nature of public interactions, suggest pathways for future research. These include conducting studies with larger participant pools, employing more controlled environments to isolate variables, exploring long-term deployments to assess sustained acceptance, and comparing acceptance with less anthropomorphic social robots. Nevertheless, this work underscores the potential of advanced robotic heads in service applications and provides valuable real-world data to inform future research and development in HRI.
