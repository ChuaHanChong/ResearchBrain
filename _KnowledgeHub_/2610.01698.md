# ActiveWAM: Evidence-Aware Active Vision for World-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> ActiveWAM is a unified world-action model that generates both bimanual arm actions and pan/tilt head actions, including deliberate stay and reacquisition moves, for active-vision manipulation. Training-time inversion through a frozen Wan video prior, constrained to preserve task-bearing cues and temporal changes, creates paired histories; deployment uses raw observations only. It beats Fast-WAM by 20.0 points on RoboTwin-AV compound shift and 26.7 points on real kitchen tasks.

## Problem

- Prior active-vision policies decouple gaze from manipulation, via look-at inverse kinematics or separate modules, so they cannot reason jointly about how camera and arm actions change observations.
- Test-time viewpoint search methods such as See2Act add compute overhead from candidate ranking and lose the closed-loop reactivity that manipulation needs.
- Diffusion-inversion augmentation like Stem-OB transforms single frames generically and never specifies which historical evidence must survive view changes, so finite history windows lose task-critical cues when the camera moves.

## Method

- ==Training-Time Inversion (TTI)==: an eight-step partial inverse through a ==frozen Wan2.2 video prior==, corrected by task-weighted cross-attention ==feature-preservation== and ==mutual-nearest-neighbor dynamics losses==, producing transformed histories that keep task cues.
- Unified head-arm generation: a shared ==DiT== with a ==view-aware history adapter== encoding camera identity, pose, exposure time and validity jointly ==flow-matches== future-video latents and a 16-D action including pan/tilt and zero-motion stay.
- Paired learning: raw and inverted histories share weights, noise and identical original targets plus an ==action-velocity consistency loss==; also ==RoboTwin-AV==, a 50-task RoboTwin 2.0 extension with executable pan/tilt control.

## Results

- On TAVIS, ActiveWAM beats the strongest baselines by up to **17.0** points, for example **47.0%** vs **30.0%** spatial-OOD and **24.7%** vs **11.7%** pose-OOD on Head/GR1T2.
- On RoboTwin-AV compound shift it reaches **53.3%** versus **33.3%** for Fast-WAM; the factorial gives **23.7%** with neither component, **30.3%** inversion-only, **41.7%** head-control-only.
- Learned head control reaches **65.0%** pose success with **1.7** rad travel versus **57.3%** and **4.1** rad for object tracking; real kitchen total-task success is **50.0%** versus **23.3%** for Fast-WAM.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Good observation includes keeping a view, not only searching for a better one; learned stay behavior cut head travel while raising co-visibility and success.
> - Evidence retention and active acquisition are complementary: history inversion helps most when the camera can move, so augmentation and sensing should be designed together, not tuned separately.
> - Video prediction works as a training signal only: action-only inference matches joint decoding, so world-action models can drop test-time future-video generation without losing performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A bimanual robot in a kitchen depends on what its head camera shows. Turning the camera toward the next relation it needs can also push an already useful cue out of the robot's short visual history. ActiveWAM trains one policy to keep task-relevant visual history and to move the camera head and both arms together. The approach is tested in simulation and on physical kitchen tasks.

### 2. The view is part of the action

Different stages of a task need different evidence. During the approach, the robot needs to see how its hands relate to the object. During placement, it needs to see the destination. A camera turn can bring in new evidence, but it can also push older context out of view. Sometimes holding a good view is the right behavior. The authors call this an evidence-aware **retain–acquire** problem: the robot keeps a useful view when it is enough, and acquires or reacquires evidence when it is not.

ActiveWAM is a world–action model, meaning a model trained both to predict visual consequences and to generate executable actions. One shared generator produces both arm actions and pan/tilt camera actions. Its inputs include:

- recent history tagged by camera identity, measured pose, exposure time, and validity;
- the current RGB image, which remains available for fine-grained contact cues.

The history summary is recomputed from the available recent window at each decision. The robot executes a short prefix of the generated actions, then refreshes its context from newly measured RGB.

Staying still is a valid camera action. Future-video prediction is a training co-task, not a required test-time output. Deployment does not perform inversion or candidate ranking.

ActiveWAM overview. In the training-only path, raw history is transformed into a task-guided history, and raw and transformed conditions share the original action and future targets. View-aware history and current RGB condition a shared DiT that jointly trains noisy future-video and action tokens. Execution outputs arm plus two-degree-of-freedom pan/tilt actions, executes a prefix, then receives new RGB.

### 3. What training preserves, and what the controls test

During training, a frozen Wan video prior transforms only history the robot has already observed. Two constraints guide the transformation:

- Instruction-linked attention weights prioritize task-bearing source features.
- Matched features across frames constrain visible temporal changes.

The recorded future and action continuation stay fixed, so the transform does not add physical evidence. Training on paired raw and transformed histories is meant to transfer this signal to a policy that receives raw input.

The evaluation uses RoboTwin-AV, which extends all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 task definitions with synchronized RGB and executable pan/tilt control. The metric is success rate (SR), the reported percentage of episodes that meet native task-success predicates. The compound-shift evaluation has three seeds and 100 test episodes per task, seed, and condition, and the compared methods share sensor/action interfaces.

Under compound appearance-plus-head-pose shifts, ActiveWAM reports **53.3±1.5%** benchmark SR, compared with **33.3±1.2%** for Fast-WAM, a 20.0-point difference.

A trajectory-consistent factorial comparison under the same compound shifts separates the two components:

- **Neither component:** 23.7% success
- **Inversion alone:** 30.3%
- **Active head control alone:** 41.7%
- **Both together:** 53.3%

From these means, the authors calculate an approximately 5-point interaction. They interpret it as a greater inversion benefit when head control is available. This supports complementarity in this setup. It does not establish that these are the only causes of the gain.

Ablations test the specific inversion constraints against simpler alternatives:

- The full model reaches 53.3% compound SR.
- Removing task preservation gives 41.0%.
- Removing dynamic preservation gives 44.7%.
- Same-Wan framewise inversion gives 43.5%.

### 4. Physical trials

The physical evaluation covers three composed kitchen task types, with 20 trials per task per method. Full-task success means completing both subtasks in the same trial. ActiveWAM completes 30 of 60 trials (50.0%), compared with 14 of 60 (23.3%) for Fast-WAM.

Stage switching is prescribed, and the physical tasks compose separately trained policies.

### 5. Closing

ActiveWAM treats view acquisition and evidence retention as coupled decisions inside one world–action model, and the factorial comparison is the clearest test of that idea. The main boundary is that the system is not an unrestricted long-horizon end-to-end system: the head has fixed focal length, history is short, and stage switching is prescribed.
