# Learning to Navigate Efficiently with Only 0.58M Trainable Parameters

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Nanyang Technological University developed a decomposed visual navigation system that integrates classical analytical methods with three compact neural modules, achieving performance on par with larger, end-to-end models on InternRobotics benchmarks with only 0.58M trainable parameters. The system recorded an average success rate of 61.5% and a success weighted by path length of 57.3%, alongside reduced collision rates and enhanced robustness to sensor noise.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art visual navigation policies typically demand immense computational resources, involving hundreds of millions of parameters and extensive training data.
- Resource-constrained robotic systems require navigation solutions with low parameter counts, small memory footprints, and fast inference speeds for practical on-device deployment.
- End-to-end learned navigation policies often lack interpretability, making it challenging to diagnose and correct failure modes in real-world scenarios.

## Method

- A ==decomposed navigation architecture== that strategically combines classical analytical computations (e.g., for ==occupancy mapping== and coordinate transforms) with three compact, learned neural modules.
- Three specialized neural modules: an **Egress Predictor** for local pixel-goal targeting, a **Navigation Predictor** for estimating a ==goal-conditioned posterior probability field==, and a **B-spline Generator** for denoising trajectory residuals.
- Training was optimized for efficiency, utilizing a ==frozen pre-trained image encoder== and training all learnable modules cumulatively in under one hour on a single GPU, achieving a 50 Hz inference speed.

## Results

- The system achieved an average **Success Rate of 61.5%** and **SPL of 57.3%** on InternRobotics benchmarks using only **0.58M trainable parameters**, approaching the performance of models with **233x more parameters**.
- Demonstrated a lower collision rate (**0.111 per 10m**) than all evaluated baselines, with failures tending towards stalling rather than aggressive impacts, and exhibited robustness to RGB and certain depth corruptions.
- The architecture generalized to no-goal exploration by retraining only a **123k-parameter** egress head, outperforming a larger model's native exploration capabilities and achieving better explored area metrics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-performance visual navigation can be achieved with significantly reduced model complexity by leveraging a structured decomposition that integrates analytical solutions for components with closed-form structures.
> - Using explicit, analytically derived interfaces between learned modules effectively preserves information, enabling generalizability to new tasks like no-goal exploration with minimal retraining.
> - Training the trajectory generator against multimodal posterior samples from the navigation predictor, rather than ground-truth paths, is crucial for preserving density space diversity and achieving higher performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visual navigation for autonomous robots has seen significant advancements, largely driven by the development of large-scale machine learning models. Contemporary approaches often involve end-to-end policies, such as reinforcement learning policies, trained on billions of steps or imitation learning policies trained on extensive cross-embodiment datasets. More recent developments include diffusion-based foundation policies for trajectory generation, also leveraging large-scale simulated data. These methods, while demonstrating improved capabilities in tasks like point-goal navigation, object-goal navigation, and vision-language navigation, typically require substantial computational resources in terms of parameter counts, dataset sizes, and inference costs. This scaling has enabled models to implicitly learn complex abilities, including geometric understanding, spatial mapping, and embodiment awareness.

This work positions itself as an exploration into an alternative paradigm, questioning the necessity of such extensive scale for a single task family like point-goal navigation. It diverges from the predominant trend of implicitly learning all operational components through large-scale data. Instead, it proposes a hybrid approach that integrates classical methods with known closed-form solutions (e.g., projective geometry, occupancy mapping, coordinate transforms) with small, specialized neural network modules. This allows the learned components to focus on operations where explicit analytical solutions are less straightforward, thereby reducing the overall learning load and the required parameter count. The research relates to decomposed planners and subgoal-based navigation, where complex tasks are broken down into simpler sub-tasks. However, it distinguishes itself by emphasizing interfaces that primarily rely on analytical transformations rather than being coupled through additional learning objectives.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether state-of-the-art performance in specific navigation tasks, such as point-goal navigation, can be achieved with significantly reduced model complexity and training data, compared to the current trend of large-scale, end-to-end learning policies. The authors specifically ask: "for a single task family such as point-goal navigation, how much of this scale is actually required, and specifically what structure facilitates this objective?"

The motivation for this inquiry stems from the practical limitations faced by resource-constrained robotic systems. Many robots, especially those requiring on-device inference, cannot practically deploy or operate large, computationally expensive policies at the necessary speed. Such systems would benefit from the high task-specific navigation performance demonstrated by larger models but need solutions that are more efficient in terms of parameter count, memory footprint, and inference latency. By exploring a structured, hybrid approach that combines analytical methods with compact neural modules, the authors aim to develop navigation capabilities that are both performant and feasible for deployment on hardware with limited computational resources. This includes considerations for faster inference speeds and reduced energy consumption. Additionally, the decomposition aims to provide a more transparent and interpretable system, where failure modes might be more easily identifiable and correctable due to the explicit separation of concerns.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a decomposed navigation model that combines classical, analytically computed operations with three small, learned neural modules. This hybrid architecture aims to minimize the learning load on the neural components by handling operations with known closed-form structures analytically.

**Overall Architecture:**
The system is composed of three distinct learnable modules that interact through closed-form geometric transformations:
1. **Egress Predictor ($f_{eg}$):** Learns a local pixel-goal target in the current view, conditioned on the episode-level goal.
2. **Navigation Predictor ($f_{np}$):** Estimates a goal-conditioned posterior probability field in the Bird's-Eye-View (BEV) frame, indicating feasible trajectory regions.
3. **Generator ($f_{gen}$):** Predicts the shape of the trajectory, parameterized as a B-spline, with its endpoint determined by the egress predictor.

At each control cycle, the system receives an RGB image ($I_t$) and depth map ($D_t$). An occupancy map ($O_t$) in the BEV-frame is analytically calculated from the unprojected depth. A costmap ($C_t$) is derived from this occupancy map, incorporating an exponential repulsion from obstacles and a bearing ramp penalizing out-of-view regions.

**A. Egress Predictor ($f_{eg}$): Endpoint Selection**
The egress predictor takes the RGB image and the episode goal as input. It utilizes a fully frozen DepthAnythingv2 ViT-S as the image encoder, ensuring no additional navigation-specific properties are learned by this large pre-trained component. The egress predictor itself is a small neural network that processes features from the image encoder and the goal to output a grid of logits ($z_t$). It is trained using a cross-entropy loss to predict the point ($c_t$) on the rasterized image-frame where the path to the episode goal exits the current BEV window or passes behind objects, subject to a maximum distance truncation. During inference, the maximum logit defines the image-frame goal, which is then converted to a 3D position on the ground plane via ray-casting.

**B. Navigation Predictor ($f_{np}$)**
This module estimates a conditional distribution ($P_t$) representing the posterior probability of where trajectories should travel, given the depth-derived BEV occupancy map ($O_t$) and the local egress goal ($g_t$). It is a lightweight operator that emits logits in the BEV coordinate frame, with each discrete coordinate encoding a scalar value and direction. The head produces $K$ recurrent posteriors, each supervised against a target route ($\tau_k$) that anneals from a straight-line prior to the ground-truth path. The loss function includes a cross-entropy term for the route and a cosine similarity term for the path tangent (heading), masked to the support of the target route. An additional collision penalty on the final posterior is included in the overall loss for the navigation predictor. The final posterior ($P_K$) is used at test-time, and the occupancy map is updated to increase costs in frontier regions upon collision detection.

**C. Generator ($f_{gen}$): Trajectory Shaping**
The generator uses B-splines for trajectory parameterization due to their continuity properties. Unlike existing methods that learn the entire trajectory, this system first derives an endpoint from the egress predictor. The B-spline is defined residually around a straight line to this egress point. A standard Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) is employed to denoise only these residuals. The generator predicts the offset ($\hat{\delta}_0$) conditioned on the noisy residuals, current diffusion step, and contextual information including the navigation predictor's posterior ($P_t$), egress goal ($g_t$), and current velocity ($v_t$). The generator is trained to denoise over 10 steps, minimizing the mean squared error between the predicted and true residuals, augmented by a collision cost based on the costmap $C_t$. The learning targets for $\delta_0$ are drawn from the navigation predictor's posterior predictions rather than ground-truth trajectories, enabling the generator to sample from a multimodal posterior distribution. During generation, a set of candidate trajectories are denoised from Gaussian noise.

**D. Critic**
To select the optimal trajectory from the diverse paths generated by the diffusion model, a post-hoc deterministic critic is employed. The critic minimizes a cost function ($J(\tau)$) over the candidate set of trajectories ($T_N$), which is essentially the costmap without the bearing ramp, penalizing trajectories that pass close to obstacles.

**Training and Evaluation Details:**
The system was trained on approximately 44k random single-frame states from 55 Matterport3D scenes, with all learnable modules cumulatively training in under one hour on a single RTX 4090 GPU. Evaluation was conducted on the InternRobotics benchmarks (6060 episodes across 60 environments), using Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) metrics. Inference speed is reported at 50 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the performance, efficiency, robustness, and generalizability of the proposed decomposed navigation model.

**Performance on Point-Goal Navigation:**
The system, with only 0.58M trainable parameters (out of 22.7M total parameters, including a frozen image encoder), achieved navigation performance comparable to state-of-the-art models. On the InternRobotics benchmarks, it approached the performance of NavDP (135.7M parameters), trailing by 2-5% in Success Rate (SR) on home and commercial scenes, while using 233x fewer trainable parameters. Specifically, the system recorded an average SR of 61.5% and SPL of 57.3% across all environments, outperforming ViPlanner (71.0M trainable parameters) and iPlanner (53.3M trainable parameters) on average SR.

**Collision Safety:**
The proposed method demonstrated a lower collision rate compared to all other evaluated methods. On the full 6060 episodes, its collision rate per 10m was 0.111, significantly lower than NavDP (0.216), ViPlanner (0.218), and iPlanner (0.173). Further analysis on a subset of InternScenes-Home showed the lowest average collision rate (0.050 per 10m) and the lowest rate of hard collisions (>25N impact force, 0.010 per 10m). The failure distribution indicated that collisions were distributed relatively uniformly regardless of episode success, similar to ViPlanner, suggesting a graceful failure mode where the robot tends to stall rather than impact obstacles aggressively.

**Sensor Robustness:**
The system exhibited robustness to certain simulated sensor corruptions. It was highly robust to RGB corruption, attributed to the pre-trained image encoder. It showed generally greater robustness to depth corruption compared to NavDP. For example, under 30% depth dropout, the system maintained 91% of its clean SR, whereas NavDP dropped to 61%. However, the system's performance severely degraded under significant Gaussian depth noise (σ = 0.05z²), reducing its SR to 19% of the clean input. This failure was attributed to the analytic BEV calculation. The authors demonstrated that this specific failure mode was transparent and rectifiable with a simple 3x3 median filter applied to the input depth, recovering baseline performance.

**Generalization to Other Tasks (No-Goal Exploration):**
The decomposed architecture demonstrated generalizability to no-goal exploration tasks. By retraining only the 123k-parameter egress head (zeroing goal conditioning), and reusing the navigation predictor and generator without modification, the system outperformed NavDP's native no-goal exploration. It achieved a mean explored area of 18.3 m² (vs. NavDP's 18.2 m²) and a median explored area of 18.8 m² (vs. NavDP's 18.5 m²), over a longer exploration time (25.7 s vs. 17.2 s), on a randomly chosen scene. This suggests that the subgoal interface effectively preserves information necessary for undirected navigation.

**Efficiency:**
The system's end-to-end latency was approximately 20 ms (50 Hz) on a single RTX 4090 GPU, with the image encoder consuming the majority of this time. This inference speed supports deployment on systems where real-time performance is critical.

**Ablation Studies:**
Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of the proposed modules:
- Removing the navigation predictor resulted in a significant drop in SR (from 58.4% to 3.0%) and SPL (from 52.0% to 3.0%).
- Removing the egress predictor also led to a substantial reduction in SR (to 20.8%) and SPL (to 20.4%).
These results indicate that both the local subgoal prediction and the goal-conditioned navigability field are crucial components of the system.
Further ablations on design choices showed:
- Sampling from the egress predictor instead of using soft arg max marginally reduced performance (SR 54.5% vs 58.4%).
- Training the generator against ground-truth trajectories instead of posterior samples led to poor performance (SR 34.7% vs 58.4%), suggesting the importance of preserving multimodality in density space for trajectory generation.
- Joint end-to-end training of all modules significantly degraded SR (35.6% vs 58.4%), supporting the benefits of the proposed decomposition and separate training.
- Classical A* planning on the derived costmap performed poorly (SR 21.8%), demonstrating that the neural modules contribute non-trivial planning capabilities beyond simple pathfinding.
- Velocity conditioning in the DDPM and collision detection were found to increase SR slightly but were not individually highly significant.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work demonstrates that high-performance visual navigation can be achieved with a substantially reduced parameter count and training data footprint, challenging the prevalent paradigm of large-scale end-to-end learning. The proposed decomposed architecture, integrating analytical geometric computations with compact neural modules, provides an alternative route to developing sophisticated navigation capabilities.

**Reduced Resource Requirements:** The most significant implication is the ability to achieve near state-of-the-art performance with only 0.58M trainable parameters and training data of 44k frames in under one GPU-hour. This contrasts sharply with models requiring hundreds of millions of parameters and billions of frames, making the proposed system particularly relevant for **resource-constrained robots** and **on-device inference applications** where computational power, memory, and energy consumption are critical factors. This could expand the accessibility of advanced navigation capabilities to a broader range of robotic platforms.

**Enhanced Safety and Robustness:** The observed low collision rate, particularly the lowest rate of hard collisions, indicates improved safety characteristics of the navigation policy. The graceful failure mode, where the robot tends to stall rather than aggressively impact obstacles, is beneficial for real-world deployment. Furthermore, the explicit separation of sensor inputs (RGB for egress, depth for BEV) contributes to a **transparent failure mode** under sensor corruption. The ability to analytically pinpoint and correct failures (e.g., applying a median filter for specific depth noise) suggests a more maintainable and debuggable system compared to opaque end-to-end models.

**Generalizability and Modularity:** The demonstrated transferability to no-goal exploration tasks by retraining only a small part of the system (123k-parameter egress head) highlights the modularity and generalizability of the interfaces. This suggests that components learned for one task family could be adapted for others with minimal retraining, potentially reducing development time and computational costs for new tasks. The structured approach also offers improved interpretability, as the function of each module is explicitly defined.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The authors acknowledge limitations, primarily the restriction to single-floor navigation due to the use of depth-derived BEV maps. Addressing multi-floor navigation would require adapting to 3D representations like voxel maps. Evaluations were conducted exclusively in simulation, and future work will involve testing in real-world conditions to account for factors such as localization drift, control-loop latency, and real depth sensor quality. The application of this framework to image-goal navigation, another potential task, remains to be explored.

Overall, this research offers a substantial contribution by demonstrating that judicious architectural decomposition and the strategic integration of classical methods can enable efficient and robust navigation comparable to large-scale learning models, opening avenues for practical deployment in a wider array of robotic systems.
