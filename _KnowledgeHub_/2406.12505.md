# Demonstrating Agile Flight from Pixels without State Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> This work by the Robotics and Perception Group (RPG) at the University of Zurich demonstrates agile quadrotor flight purely from a first-person-view video stream, directly mapping raw pixel observations to low-level control commands without explicit state estimation. Policies trained entirely in simulation achieved zero-shot transfer to the real world, navigating through a sequence of gates at speeds up to 40 km/h with 100% success rate on a Figure-8 track.

## Problem

- Achieving robust and agile autonomous drone flight historically relied heavily on explicit state estimation (e.g., VIO/SLAM), which requires powerful onboard computing and complex sensor fusion.
- Traditional state estimation pipelines are susceptible to failures in challenging perception environments, like aggressive maneuvers causing motion blur, leading to crashes.
- Human drone pilots achieve exceptional agility by relying solely on FPV video streams, bypassing internal state estimates, a capability autonomous systems lacked.

## Method

- Training an end-to-end vision-based policy using ==reinforcement learning== (PPO) to directly map 84x84 pixel-level gate segmentation masks to low-level collective thrust and body rate commands.
- Employing an asymmetric actor-critic architecture where the critic has access to privileged simulation state information during training to guide the actor, which only sees visual input.
- Exclusively training policies in simulation with efficient visual observation generation (inner gate edges) and robust domain randomization to enable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- Demonstrated the first vision-based quadrotor system capable of autonomous navigation through gates at speeds up to 40 km/h and 2g accelerations, directly from pixels to low-level controls, without explicit state estimation.
- Achieved zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, with policies trained solely in simulation performing successfully on real hardware without fine-tuning.
- Achieved a **100%** success rate over multiple runs on a Figure-8 track in real-world flights, with performance comparable to state-based policies in terms of lap times and agility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Agile quadrotor flight directly from pixels without explicit state estimation is achievable, mirroring human pilot capabilities.
> - The asymmetric actor-critic architecture is indispensable for successful vision-based reinforcement learning in high-dimensional observation spaces for agile robotics.
> - Careful simulation design, including simplified visual abstractions and domain randomization, enables effective zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for complex aerial tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous quadrotor flight has seen remarkable advancements in recent years, largely driven by progress in computer vision, state estimation, and machine learning. Historically, achieving robust and agile autonomous drone flight has heavily relied on explicit state estimation pipelines, such as Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) or Visual SLAM. These systems fuse visual information from cameras with inertial data from IMUs to provide precise, low-latency estimates of the drone's position, orientation, and velocity. While effective, this approach necessitates powerful onboard computing hardware, careful sensor calibration, and can be susceptible to failures in challenging perception environments (e.g., poor lighting, featureless scenes, aggressive maneuvers causing motion blur).

This paper addresses a fundamental paradigm shift in autonomous drone navigation, moving away from this established state-estimation-centric approach. It draws a direct parallel to professional human drone racing pilots who achieve extraordinary agility and robustness by relying solely on a first-person-view (FPV) video stream and sending low-level control commands (collective thrust and body rates) without any internal "state estimate" of their exact position or velocity. The broader research landscape is exploring ways to simplify robotic perception and control, making systems more robust and less hardware-intensive. This work fits squarely into this trend by investigating whether a robot can learn to mimic human-like, intuitive control directly from visual input.

**Key distinctions and contributions to the landscape include:**
- **Departure from Explicit State Estimation:** Unlike the vast majority of agile autonomous drones (including many of the RPG's own previous works, e.g.,), this paper explicitly eliminates the need for VIO/SLAM. This reduces computational requirements and dependency on specialized sensors.
- **Direct Pixel-to-Control Mapping:** Prior vision-based drone control research, especially using reinforcement learning, often provided high-level velocity commands or relied on an underlying stabilizing controller (e.g.,), which inherently limited agility. This work directly maps pixels to low-level collective thrust and body rates, maximizing the drone's agility and demonstrating a more fundamental form of visuo-motor control.
- **Addressing Challenges of Vision-based RL:** Reinforcement learning directly from pixels is notoriously sample-inefficient and challenging due to high-dimensional observations, the sim2real gap (especially for flying robots with diverse viewpoints, motion blur, and non-linear aerodynamics), and the unstable nature of quadrotors. This paper contributes practical solutions, such as the "inner gate edges" abstraction, to make vision-based RL feasible for agile flight.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer without Real-world Policy Fine-tuning:** The paper achieves zero-shot transfer, a highly sought-after capability in robotics. This is facilitated by a well-designed simulation environment and robust domain randomization techniques, common in the RL landscape but particularly critical for complex robotic systems.
- **Hardware Efficiency:** By offloading computation to a powerful ground station, the approach enables the use of lighter and less expensive onboard hardware, opening possibilities for more scalable and cost-effective deployments, which is a major practical advantage in real-world robotics.

In essence, this research pushes the frontier of autonomous drone capabilities by demonstrating that agile, high-speed flight can be achieved with a more minimalistic, perception-action loop, mirroring the efficiency and robustness observed in biological systems and expert human operators. It provides a blueprint for building autonomous systems that are less reliant on complex, computationally expensive state estimation, potentially paving the way for a new generation of robust and agile robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to achieve **agile autonomous quadrotor flight purely from a first-person-view (FPV) video stream, without relying on explicit state estimation or IMU measurements**. The authors aim to demonstrate that a drone can navigate through a challenging environment, such as a drone racing track, at high speeds by directly mapping raw pixel observations to low-level control commands, analogous to how professional human drone pilots operate.

The key motivations driving this ambitious goal are multifold:

- **Mimicking Human Expertise:** Human FPV drone pilots exhibit exceptional agility and robustness by interpreting a live video feed and issuing low-level thrust and body rate commands. This paper is motivated by the desire to endow autonomous systems with similar perception-action capabilities, bypassing the complex, often brittle, and computationally intensive intermediate step of explicit state estimation.
- **Addressing Limitations of State-Estimation-Dependent Systems:**
 - **Hardware Requirements:** Traditional approaches require powerful, specialized onboard hardware to perform real-time visual-inertial fusion, limiting the drone's size, weight, and battery life.
 - **Computational Latency:** The fusion of visual and inertial information demands consistent and extremely low latencies, which is challenging to maintain, especially during aggressive maneuvers.
 - **Robustness Issues:** State estimation pipelines can fail or become unreliable in various scenarios, such as feature-poor environments, rapid lighting changes, or during extreme motion blur, leading to crashes. The authors specifically reference their previous work where their state-estimation-dependent champion drone still crashed in 40% of races against human pilots.
- **Enabling Offboard Computation and Scalability:** By shifting the primary computational burden (running large neural networks for perception and control) to a powerful ground station PC or cloud, the approach allows for simpler, lighter, and cheaper drones. This enables a one-to-one replacement of human pilots in specific applications, leading to a highly scalable solution for industrial use cases.
- **Maximizing Agility:** By directly controlling collective thrust and body rates (CTBR), the system can fully leverage the drone's dynamic capabilities, which is essential for agile maneuvers and high-speed flight in constrained environments.

In summary, the core objective is to achieve a significant breakthrough in drone autonomy by demonstrating robust, agile, and hardware-agnostic flight based purely on visual perception, reducing dependency on conventional state estimation pipelines and pushing towards more biologically-inspired control mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around training an end-to-end vision-based policy using reinforcement learning (RL) to directly map pixel observations to low-level control commands for agile quadrotor flight.

**A. Neural Controller Architecture (Asymmetric Actor-Critic):**
- **Observation Space (Actor):** The actor, which is the deployed policy, receives an 84x84 pixel-level gate segmentation mask. This "inner gate edge" abstraction is crucial: it encodes task-relevant information (the gate to fly through) and implicitly contains state information (how the gate appears depends on drone pose). Importantly, it is *not* raw camera pixels but a processed visual feature. The actor also receives a history of the last three actions to enable smoother control.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs a 4-dimensional continuous control command: collective thrust (mass-normalized acceleration) and 3D body rates. This low-level control modality maximizes agility and aligns with human pilot control, requiring no onboard state estimation from the drone's flight controller.
- **Reinforcement Learning Framework:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training.
- **Asymmetric Critic:** This is a key enabler. During training, the critic network, responsible for evaluating actions, has access to "privileged information"—the full 20-dimensional simulation state (position, orientation, linear/angular velocities, gate index, and distance to the next gate center). This rich, ground-truth state information allows the critic to provide a more accurate value estimate, guiding the actor's learning effectively in the high-dimensional and complex pixel observation space. The actor, however, never sees this privileged information.
- **Reward Function:** A dense reward function guides policy learning, composed of:
 - `r_prog`: Progress reward, encouraging fast flight towards the next gate.
 - `r_perc`: Perception reward, encouraging the camera to be oriented towards the next gate.
 - `r_cmd`: Penalty for large and abrupt changes in control commands, promoting smooth flight.
 - `r_pass`: Sparse reward for successfully passing a gate.
 - `r_crash`: Penalty for collisions or falling below a certain altitude.
- **Network Architecture:** A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) extracts features from the 84x84 pixel mask. These features, along with the past three actions, feed into a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) for the actor. The critic's MLP receives the CNN features plus the privileged simulation state.

**B. Simulation Environment for Policy Training:**
- **Simulation-Only Training:** Policies are trained exclusively in simulation to overcome the challenges and risks of real-world data collection for agile flight.
- **Efficient Visual Observation Generation:** Instead of computationally expensive photorealistic rendering, the simulation directly projects the inner gate edges onto the image plane. This simplified abstraction is extremely fast to compute (<100 μs per frame), allowing for millions of training interactions. To add robustness, 10% of the simulated edge segments are corrupted (drawn randomly).
- **Quadrotor Dynamics:** The simulator uses an accurate yet computationally lightweight augmented model (similar to previous work) for drone dynamics, enabling fast training compared to more complex first-principles models like BEM theory.
- **Sim2Real Gap Mitigation Strategies:**
 - **Initial State Buffer:** To prevent catastrophic forgetting and encourage exploration, the environment samples diverse initial drone states from a buffer, where successful gate passes contribute new, physically plausible states.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Critical for zero-shot transfer, this technique randomly varies physical parameters (e.g., thrust, drag, inertia, mass by ±5-20%) and environmental conditions (e.g., starting pose, velocity, body rates, and gate positions by ±5 cm) during training. This forces the policy to be robust to real-world uncertainties.

**C. Real-World Deployment Components:**
- **Offboard Control Setup:** The drone transmits a low-latency (33 ms at 60 Hz, 1280x720) video stream from its onboard camera to a powerful ground station PC. This PC performs all perception and control computations, sending low-level commands back to the drone via an RF receiver connected to the flight controller.
- **Gate Detector:** For real-world deployment, a robust SwinTransformerV2-based segmentation network processes the incoming video stream to generate the "inner gate edge" masks. This detector is trained on a diverse dataset comprising real-world images (from different cameras/environments), photorealistic renders with motion blur, and gates rendered on random ImageNet backgrounds. Importantly, it *does not* use real images from the specific deployment environment, highlighting its generalization capabilities. The detector achieves fast inference (4 ms on an RTX 3090).
- **Hardware:** A modified Agilicious platform, an open-source agile quadrotor, is used for real-world flights.

This comprehensive methodology, combining an efficient simulation environment with sophisticated RL techniques and a robust real-world perception pipeline, allows the system to bridge the sim-to-real gap and achieve agile vision-based flight without explicit state estimation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling evidence that agile quadrotor flight can be achieved directly from pixel observations without explicit state estimation. The key findings are:

- **Pioneering Vision-to-Control without State Estimation:** To the authors' knowledge, this work presents the first vision-based quadrotor system capable of autonomously navigating through a sequence of gates at high speeds (up to 40 km/h with accelerations up to 2g) by directly mapping pixels to low-level control commands (collective thrust and body rates) without using any explicit state estimation (e.g., VIO/SLAM) or IMU measurements for the control policy.
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies trained entirely in simulation, using the proposed methodologies, successfully transferred directly to the real world without any additional fine-tuning. This is a significant achievement given the complexities of real-world drone dynamics and visual perception.
- **Crucial Role of Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** Comparative analysis clearly demonstrated that the asymmetric actor-critic architecture is indispensable for successful learning. Symmetric pixel-based policies (where the critic does not have access to privileged information) consistently failed to learn to fly, underscoring the importance of using ground-truth state information during training to guide the learning process in a high-dimensional observation space.
- **Comparable Performance to State-Based Policies (with Nuances):**
 - In both simulation (BEM and augmented simulator) and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) experiments, the asymmetric pixel-based agent achieved success rates comparable to or very close to state-based agents. For instance, in HIL experiments on the Figure-8 track, it achieved a 100% success rate over multiple runs.
 - While state-based policies generally learn faster (up to 8 times quicker due to smaller, more task-relevant observation spaces), the pixel-based policy, given sufficient training (400 million environment interactions over approximately one day), converges to a similar performance level in terms of average reward.
 - Lap times for the pixel-based policy were very competitive with state-based policies, often being only marginally slower or even comparable depending on the track and specific state-based baseline.
 - The pixel-based policy tended to have a slightly larger Mean-Gate-Passing-Error (MGE) compared to state-based policies with perception-aware rewards, indicating it flew slightly less centrally through the gates. However, this did not compromise success rate.
- **Robustness to Track Layout Variations and Perturbations:** The pixel-based policy demonstrated robustness to variations in track layouts (Ellipse, Figure-8, Glasses) and also to random perturbations of gate positions (up to 50 cm in each direction) during evaluation in simulation. Even without direct access to ground-truth gate positions, the policy effectively inferred them from visual observations, showcasing its generalization capabilities.
- **Effective Gate Detector Performance:** The SwinTransformerV2-based gate detector proved robust, trained on a diverse synthetic and real-world dataset (crucially, *without* real images from the deployment environment). Its low inference time (4 ms) enabled real-time offboard processing.
- **Real-World Demonstration:** The supplementary video and real-world flight statistics for the Figure-8 track validated the system's performance, achieving a 100% success rate across 6 runs and 20 laps, with agile flight characteristics similar to state-based systems but *without* explicit state estimation.

These findings collectively represent a significant step towards more robust and hardware-agnostic autonomous quadrotor systems by demonstrating that high-performance, agile flight can be achieved through a direct pixel-to-action mapping, effectively bypassing the traditional reliance on computationally intensive state estimation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant milestone in autonomous robotics, particularly for agile aerial platforms. Its significance and potential impact extend beyond the immediate application of drone racing:

- **Challenging the Status Quo of State Estimation:** The most profound impact of this work is its direct challenge to the long-standing reliance on explicit state estimation (e.g., VIO, SLAM) for agile quadrotor flight. By demonstrating high-performance flight directly from pixels to low-level control, the paper opens a new paradigm where robots can perform complex tasks with a more minimalistic, human-like perception-action loop. This could fundamentally alter how autonomous systems are designed, moving towards more end-to-end, learning-based solutions.
- **Reduced Hardware Requirements and Cost:** By offloading heavy computation (neural network inference) to a ground station, the approach significantly reduces the need for powerful, expensive, and heavy onboard processors. This enables the deployment of lighter, cheaper, and more energy-efficient drones, making advanced autonomous capabilities accessible to a wider range of applications and fostering scalability in industrial settings.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** Traditional state estimation pipelines are vulnerable to sensor noise, calibration errors, environmental changes, or high-speed motion blur, leading to estimation drift or failure and subsequent crashes. By directly mapping pixels to control, this method bypasses these vulnerabilities, potentially leading to more robust systems that are less susceptible to perception failures in challenging real-world conditions (e.g., GPS-denied, visually degraded environments, or scenarios where IMU data might be unreliable).
- **Enabling New Applications in Structured Environments:** While demonstrated in drone racing, the methodology's focus on known "landmarks" (gates) and structured environments has broad implications.
 - **Indoor Navigation/Inspection:** For tasks like autonomous indoor navigation or industrial inspections (e.g., of power plants, warehouses), salient landmarks like doorways, pillars, or specific fixtures could serve as "gates." A pre-trained detector for these landmarks, combined with a navigation policy trained in simulation (potentially from building blueprints), could enable robust and diverse inspection tasks.
 - **Infrastructure Inspection:** The authors specifically mention powerline inspection. A drone could rely on detecting powerline pylons and navigating from one visible pylon to the next, especially in scenarios where IMU bias drift makes traditional state estimation unreliable (e.g., long, straight-line flights at constant speed).
- **Advancement in Reinforcement Learning for Robotics:** The paper contributes valuable insights into practical vision-based RL. The "inner gate edges" abstraction efficiently tackles the computational burden of high-dimensional visual observations during training, while the asymmetric actor-critic framework demonstrates an effective way to leverage privileged information for robust learning, even when that information is not available during deployment. This contributes to the growing body of knowledge on effective sim-to-real transfer techniques.

**Future Impact and Challenges:**
The authors acknowledge limitations, such as the neural controller's limited memory (only three past actions), which can lead to issues if no gate is visible for extended periods. They suggest recurrent architectures to address this. They also point to the need for future research into obstacle avoidance, dynamic objects, and unforeseen environmental changes for truly general real-world applications.

Despite these limitations, this work represents a major conceptual and practical leap forward. It demonstrates that highly agile autonomous flight can be achieved with a fundamentally different, perception-driven approach, paving the way for a new generation of robust, efficient, and versatile autonomous aerial robots.
