# World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> World Models learns a compressed, predictive model of an environment using generative neural networks (VAEs and MDN-RNNs) to facilitate efficient agent control. This framework enabled agents to achieve state-of-the-art performance on CarRacing-v0 and successfully transfer policies trained entirely within a self-generated "dream" environment to the VizDoom: Take Cover task.

## Problem

- Traditional model-free reinforcement learning struggles with high-dimensional sensory inputs and the credit assignment problem over long time horizons.
- Training reinforcement learning agents in complex, high-fidelity environments is computationally expensive and time-consuming, hindering rapid iteration and development.
- Developing general-purpose agents that can learn and act effectively in dynamic environments, mirroring biological intelligence that relies on internal world models.

## Method

- Decomposes the agent into three components: Vision (V) using a ==Variational Autoencoder== (**VAE**) for spatial compression, Memory (M) using an ==MDN-RNN== for temporal dynamics and predictions, and a compact Controller (C) for policy learning.
- Trains the V and M models unsupervisedly on collected environment data to learn a compact, predictive ==world model==, separating the complexities of representation and dynamics learning from policy optimization.
- Optimizes the small C model using ==Evolution Strategies== (**CMA-ES**), enabling training either within the actual environment or entirely within the learned =="dream" environment== generated by the M model in ==latent space==.

## Results

- Achieved an average score of **906±21** on CarRacing-v0, solving the task and setting a new state-of-the-art by providing the controller with both visual (VAE latent vector) and temporal (MDN-RNN hidden state) information.
- Successfully demonstrated policy transfer for VizDoom: Take Cover, with an agent trained entirely in a "dream" environment achieving **1092±556** time steps, surpassing the best prior leaderboard score of **820±58**.
- Showed that policies trained in a "noisier" dream environment (by adjusting the MDN-RNN's temperature parameter) are more robust and transfer better to the actual environment, preventing exploitation of model flaws.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a world model allows for decoupling complexity: a large world model can be trained unsupervisedly to understand environment dynamics, leaving a small, simple controller to learn the policy.
> - Training policies entirely within a learned latent-space "dream" environment is computationally efficient and can lead to robust policies, bypassing the need for computationally intensive interactions with the actual environment.
> - Introducing controlled stochasticity (e.g., via MDN-RNN temperature) during dream-based training helps prevent policy exploitation of model imperfections and improves transferability to the actual environment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned at the intersection of several critical areas in artificial intelligence and machine learning, particularly within the domain of Reinforcement Learning (RL), generative models, and bio-inspired AI.

#### 1.1. Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

The paper's core contribution lies in model-based reinforcement learning. Unlike model-free RL, which directly learns a policy or value function without explicit knowledge of the environment's dynamics, model-based RL aims to learn a predictive model of the environment. This model can then be used for planning, policy optimization, or generating synthetic data. The authors highlight a key challenge in traditional model-free RL: the "credit assignment problem," which makes it difficult to train very large neural networks due to the complexity of attributing rewards to distant past actions. By separating the learning of the world model from the learning of the control policy, they seek to alleviate this bottleneck.

#### 1.2. Generative Models and Neural Networks in RL

The architecture relies heavily on modern generative neural networks. The Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is used for efficient compression of high-dimensional sensory input (pixel images), a common technique to make visual RL tasks tractable. The Mixture Density Network - Recurrent Neural Network (MDN-RNN) serves as a powerful generative model for predicting future latent states, enabling the creation of dynamic, albeit "hallucinated," environments. This approach taps into the strengths of recurrent networks for sequential data modeling and generative models for data representation and synthesis.

#### 1.3. Historical Antecedents and Biological Inspiration

A distinguishing feature of this work is its deep historical grounding, particularly in the context of Jürgen Schmidhuber's decades-long research. The paper acknowledges that the concept of an "internal world model" that predicts future sensory data given actions dates back to early work on neural networks (e.g., Werbos, 1987; Schmidhuber, 1990a). The explicit connection to human cognitive systems, such as how humans develop mental models of the world, make decisions based on internal models, and perform fast reflexive behaviors (e.g., a baseball batter predicting a fastball), provides a compelling biological motivation for the proposed architecture. This continuous thread from early theoretical AI to modern deep learning implementations underscores the long-term vision of building truly intelligent agents.

#### 1.4. Evolution Strategies (ES) and Sim2Real

The choice to train the controller using Covariance-Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) situates the work within the growing interest in neuroevolution as a scalable alternative to traditional gradient-based RL methods. ES methods often require less precise feedback (only a final reward) and are highly parallelizable, making them attractive for complex optimization problems. Furthermore, the ability to train an agent entirely within a "dream" environment and then transfer that policy to the real environment directly addresses the "sim2real" challenge. This is a crucial area of research for robotics and real-world applications, where collecting data and training directly in the physical world is expensive and time-consuming. The paper posits that learning in a computationally cheap, fast-simulating dream environment offers a pathway to more efficient real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of "World Models" is to explore the viability and effectiveness of building generative neural network models of reinforcement learning environments. This overarching goal is broken down into several key aims:

1. **Learn Compressed Representations**: To train generative neural networks (VAE and MDN-RNN) in an unsupervised manner to learn a compressed spatial (via VAE) and temporal (via MDN-RNN) representation of complex RL environments. This addresses the challenge of high-dimensional sensory input, such as raw pixel data from games.
2. **Facilitate Policy Learning**: To demonstrate that features extracted from this learned "world model" (specifically, the VAE's latent vector `z` and the MDN-RNN's hidden state `h`) can serve as highly effective inputs for training a very compact and simple policy network. The motivation here is to simplify the credit assignment problem for the policy learner by providing it with a rich, pre-processed understanding of the world.
3. **Enable "Dream" Training**: A highly ambitious objective is to show that an agent's policy can be trained entirely within its own hallucinated environment, generated by its learned world model. Following successful training in this "dream world," the policy should be transferable back to the actual environment, still performing effectively. This aims to significantly reduce the computational cost and data requirements of RL training.

The motivations underpinning these objectives are manifold:
- **Biological Plausibility**: Inspired by human cognition, where internal mental models guide decision-making and enable instinctive, rapid actions without explicit planning. The authors posit that artificial agents could similarly benefit from such an internal model.
- **Overcoming RL Bottlenecks**: Traditional model-free RL struggles to efficiently train very large neural networks due to the credit assignment problem across long time horizons. By decoupling the large world model (trained unsupervisedly) from a small controller, the complexity of learning is partitioned, making the overall system more tractable.
- **Computational Efficiency**: Training directly in complex, high-fidelity environments (like game engines with physics and rendering) is computationally expensive. By building a compact, neural network-based world model, training in its latent space "dream" is significantly faster, allowing for rapid iteration and broader exploration of policies.
- **Robustness and Transferability**: The hypothesis is that a policy learned in a potentially "noisier" or "more uncertain" simulated environment (due to the stochastic nature of the MDN-RNN and adjustable temperature parameter) can be more robust and transfer better to the real environment, which might have its own imperfections or unexpected variations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "World Models" framework decomposes the artificial agent into three primary components: Vision (V), Memory (M), and Controller (C), designed to interact with the environment in a specific manner.

#### 3.1. Agent Architecture: Vision (V), Memory (M), and Controller (C)

1. **Vision (V) Model**: This component handles the high-dimensional sensory input, typically raw pixel images from the environment. A **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)** is employed as the V model. Its role is to compress each observed image frame into a small, abstract, low-dimensional **latent vector `z`**. The VAE consists of an encoder that maps the image to `z` (specifically, the parameters of a Gaussian distribution from which `z` is sampled) and a decoder that reconstructs the image from `z`. The VAE is trained in an unsupervised manner, minimizing reconstruction loss and a KL divergence term to enforce a Gaussian prior on `z`.
2. **Memory (M) Model**: While the V model processes spatial information at each timestep, the M model captures temporal dynamics and makes predictions about the future. It is implemented as a **Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)** with a **Mixture Density Network (MDN)** output layer, often referred to as an MDN-RNN. The MDN-RNN models the probability distribution of the next latent vector `z_{t+1}` given the current latent vector `z_t`, the action `a_t` taken by the agent, and the RNN's hidden state `h_t`. In the VizDoom experiment, it also predicts whether the agent dies (`d_{t+1}`). By outputting a mixture of Gaussian distributions, the MDN-RNN can model stochastic and multimodal transitions, which is crucial for complex environments. The `h_t` serves as a condensed representation of the agent's recent history and its predictive understanding of the future. The M model is also trained in an unsupervised manner on collected sequences of `z` and `a`.
3. **Controller (C) Model**: This component is responsible for decision-making, mapping the agent's internal state to actions that maximize cumulative reward. The C model is deliberately kept **simple and compact**, implemented as a single-layer linear model. It takes the concatenated latent vector `z_t` (from V) and the RNN's hidden state `h_t` (from M) as input to output the action `a_t`. The design philosophy is to put most of the complexity and parameters into the V and M models, allowing the C model to focus on the policy in a reduced, more abstract feature space.

#### 3.2. Training Regimen

The training process typically involves the following steps:
1. **Data Collection**: An initial dataset of environment rollouts is collected using a simple random policy. This data includes image observations and corresponding actions. (e.g., 10,000 random rollouts).
2. **Unsupervised World Model Training**:
 - The VAE (V model) is trained on the collected image frames to learn the compressed latent representation `z`.
 - The MDN-RNN (M model) is then trained on sequences of these latent vectors `z` and the recorded actions `a` to learn the environment's dynamics, predicting future `z` and `done` states. These two models (V and M) are trained independently and without access to reward signals, making their training "unsupervised" in the context of the RL task.
3. **Controller Optimization**:
 - The parameters of the compact C model are optimized using an evolutionary algorithm, specifically **Covariance-Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES)**. This choice is due to its effectiveness in optimizing parameter spaces up to a few thousand parameters and its ease of parallelization.
 - Crucially, the C model can be trained in two modes:
 - **In the Actual Environment**: The C model receives `z_t` from the VAE processing live observations and `h_t` from the M model, which updates its state based on true `z_t` and `a_t`.
 - **In a "Hallucinated" (Dream) Environment**: The M model is used to entirely simulate the environment. The C model takes `z_t` and `h_t` generated by the M model, and the M model's prediction for `z_{t+1}` (sampled from its MDN output) becomes the new `z_t` for the next step. This "virtual environment" operates entirely in the latent space, offering significant computational speedups. Reward signals for training C are obtained directly from this simulated environment.
 - A key aspect in the "dream" training is the ability to adjust a **temperature parameter (τ)** for the MDN-RNN's sampling process. This parameter controls the stochasticity or uncertainty of the generated dream, allowing for training policies in more challenging or robust virtual environments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents experimental results from two popular OpenAI Gym environments: CarRacing-v0 and VizDoom: Take Cover, demonstrating the effectiveness and unique advantages of the "World Models" approach.

#### 4.1. Car Racing Experiment

- **Task**: Continuously steer a car on randomly generated tracks, maximizing tiles visited in minimum time (target score > 900).
- **V Model Only (Handicapped C)**: When the controller (C) was limited to only seeing the latent vector `z_t` from the VAE (without `h_t` from the M model), the agent achieved an average score of 632±251. Although it could navigate, its driving was wobbly and unstable, failing to "solve" the task. Even with an added hidden layer to C, performance only marginally improved to 788±141. This suggests that raw compressed visual features alone are insufficient for robust control in dynamic environments.
- **Full World Model (V and M)**: By providing the controller with both the current latent state `z_t` and the MDN-RNN's hidden state `h_t` (which encodes temporal information and future predictions), the agent's performance significantly improved. It achieved an average score of 906±21 over 100 random trials, effectively "solving" the CarRacing-v0 task and setting a new state-of-the-art result at the time of publication. The authors note that the driving became much more stable, and the agent handled sharp corners effectively, suggesting that `h_t` provides crucial predictive information enabling more instinctive and reflexive actions, akin to a human expert.
- **Car Racing Dreams**: The experiment also showcased the M model's ability to "hallucinate" the car racing environment. Although not used for policy training in this specific task, it visually demonstrated the generative capacity of the world model.

#### 4.2. VizDoom Experiment: Training in the Dream and Policy Transfer

- **Task**: "Take Cover" scenario where the agent must avoid fireballs from monsters, with cumulative reward being survival time (target score > 750 seconds).
- **Enhanced M Model**: For this task, the MDN-RNN was extended to also predict a binary `done` state, indicating whether the agent had died, in addition to predicting `z_{t+1}`. This gave the M model sufficient information to act as a full RL environment simulator.
- **Training in the Virtual Environment**: The key finding here was the ability to train the controller (C) entirely inside this virtual environment generated by the M model. This process was significantly more computationally efficient than training in the actual VizDoom engine. The agent learned to navigate and avoid hallucinated fireballs, achieving a score of ~900 in the virtual environment.
- **Addressing Model Exploitation**: A critical observation was that policies trained in the dream environment could "exploit" imperfections in the learned world model (e.g., finding adversarial actions that prevent fireballs from being shot). To counter this, the authors leveraged the MDN-RNN's stochastic nature by increasing the **temperature parameter (τ)** during sampling from the mixture of Gaussians. A higher `τ` introduces more uncertainty into the dream environment, making it harder for the agent to find deterministic exploits. Training with `τ=1.15` (compared to `τ=0.1` which led to mode collapse and perfect, but unrealistic, dream scores) forced the agent to learn a more robust policy.
- **Policy Transfer to Actual Environment**: The policy learned in the "noisier" virtual environment (with `τ=1.15`) successfully transferred to the actual VizDoom environment. This agent achieved an average survival time of **1092±556 time steps** over 100 consecutive trials, far surpassing the required score of 750 and outperforming the best reported leaderboard score of 820±58. This demonstrates that training in a slightly more challenging, uncertain simulated environment can lead to policies that are more robust in the real world. Despite the V model not capturing every detail (e.g., number of monsters), the core dynamics necessary for the task were adequately learned and transferred.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "World Models" paper by Ha and Schmidhuber presents a compelling framework with significant implications for the field of reinforcement learning and broader AI research.

#### 5.1. Practical Advantages

1. **Computational Efficiency**: A paramount advantage is the ability to train policies entirely within a learned latent space "dream" environment. This bypasses the need for computationally intensive game engines (with their rendering, physics, and complex logic) and allows for much faster simulation and training, especially on GPUs. This could dramatically reduce the time and resources required for developing and iterating on RL agents.
2. **Data Efficiency (Implicitly)**: While the initial world model training requires a dataset (e.g., from random policies), the subsequent policy training in the dream environment is "data-efficient" in the sense that it doesn't require further interaction with the actual environment, which is often slow or costly (e.g., in robotics).
3. **Scalability of Policy Optimization**: By keeping the controller (C) model small and simple, the complex credit assignment problem is concentrated in the unsupervised training of the V and M models. This modularity, combined with the use of highly parallelizable Evolution Strategies (ES), enables more efficient optimization of the controller's parameters, potentially scaling to more complex tasks.
4. **Robustness via Stochasticity**: The use of MDN-RNNs allows for modeling stochastic environmental dynamics. By adjusting the "temperature" parameter (τ) during dream-based training, the authors show that introducing controlled uncertainty into the virtual environment can prevent the agent from exploiting model imperfections and lead to more robust policies that transfer well to the actual (and potentially less predictable) environment.

#### 5.2. Future Research Directions

The paper identifies several exciting avenues for future work:
1. **Iterative Training and Active Exploration**: For more complex tasks, a single dataset from a random policy might be insufficient to train a comprehensive world model. The authors propose an iterative training procedure where the agent actively explores the environment (guided, for example, by "curiosity" or intrinsic motivation signals based on the world model's prediction error) to continuously collect new data and refine its world model. This mirrors human learning, where exploration and model refinement are intertwined.
2. **Hierarchical Learning**: The concept suggests that complex motor skills (e.g., walking) could be "absorbed" into the large world model over time. This would allow the smaller controller to then focus on higher-level planning and navigation, abstracting away the low-level details of motor control. This aligns with ideas of hierarchical reinforcement learning.
3. **Enhanced World Model Capacity**: The current LSTM-based MDN-RNN might have limited capacity for extremely complex or long-term memories. Future work could explore more advanced sequence models (e.g., Transformers, attention mechanisms) or incorporate external memory modules to handle larger amounts of historical information and complex environments, potentially mitigating issues like catastrophic forgetting.
4. **Unified Architectures and Behavioural Replay**: The paper references Schmidhuber's "One Big Net" and "PowerPlay" concepts, which suggest collapsing the C and M models into a single network and using behavioral replay to prevent forgetting old skills while learning new ones. This points towards more integrated and general learning systems.
5. **Sim2Real Applications**: The framework provides a strong foundation for "sim2real" transfer learning, especially for robotic control where real-world data collection and training are exceptionally costly. Learning policies in a computationally cheap, self-simulated environment and deploying them to reality could revolutionize robotics and other physical world applications.

In essence, "World Models" demonstrates a powerful paradigm for agent learning by combining unsupervised representation learning with a predictive dynamics model. It not only achieves state-of-the-art results on challenging benchmarks but also offers a computationally efficient and conceptually rich approach inspired by human cognition, opening doors for more complex and general AI agents.
