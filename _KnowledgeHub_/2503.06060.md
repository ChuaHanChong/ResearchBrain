# STAR: A Foundation Model-driven Framework for Robust Task Planning and Failure Recovery in Robotic Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called STAR integrates Foundation Models with dynamically expanding Knowledge Graphs to achieve robust task planning and autonomous failure recovery in robotic systems. This approach yielded 86% task planning accuracy and a 78% recovery success rate, while continuously learning from experience.

## Problem

- Robotic systems struggle with autonomous task execution and graceful recovery from unforeseen failures in dynamic, unpredictable environments without human intervention.
- Foundation Models (FMs) offer generalization but are prone to hallucinations, computational inefficiency, and output inconsistencies, hindering reliable real-world deployment.
- Knowledge Graphs (KGs) provide precise, structured knowledge but lack the flexibility to adapt to novel scenarios and interpret natural language instructions.

## Method

- ==Integrates Foundation Models== (LLMs for planning, VLMs for failure detection) with dynamic Knowledge Graphs (FOON and FailNet) to create a hybrid architecture.
- Employs a hierarchical task planning pipeline where LLMs generate or modify task trees, which are then stored in the FOON KG for continuous learning and reuse.
- Develops an autonomous failure recovery mechanism utilizing progressive VLM-based failure detection, optimized with image grids, and adaptive re-planning via a specialized, expanding FailNet KG.

## Results

- Achieved **86%** task planning accuracy, outperforming GPT-4 (**78%**) and other baselines by leveraging continuous learning and Knowledge Graph integration.
- Demonstrated a **78%** success rate in autonomous failure recovery, surpassing GPT-4-only prompt engineering (**74%**) and KG-only search (**28%**) approaches.
- Recorded **90%** failure detection accuracy and **78%** explanation accuracy, significantly higher than single-frame detection methods and other Vision-Language Models like BLIP2.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The synergistic integration of Foundation Models' generalization and natural language understanding with Knowledge Graphs' structured, domain-specific reliability overcomes the individual limitations of both approaches for robotics.
> - Dynamic expansion of specialized Knowledge Graphs (FOON and FailNet) through experiential learning enables continuous adaptation, reducing future reliance on Foundation Models and improving system efficiency and accuracy over time.
> - Progressive, multi-frame failure detection using Vision-Language Models, enhanced by image grid optimization, provides high detection accuracy and crucial root cause identification for complex robotic failures at reduced computational cost.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly expanding, with systems being deployed in increasingly dynamic and unpredictable environments, from industrial settings to domestic assistance and even space exploration. A critical challenge for these robots is autonomous task execution and, more importantly, graceful recovery from unforeseen failures without human intervention. This paper directly addresses this challenge by integrating two powerful, yet distinct, paradigms: Foundation Models (FMs) and Knowledge Graphs (KGs).

Historically, robotic task planning has relied on **classical planning** approaches, which assume fully observable and deterministic worlds. Frameworks like STRIPS, PDDL, and Hierarchical Task Networks (HTNs) have been fundamental, providing robust solutions for structured environments. However, their deterministic nature limits their adaptability to the real world's inherent uncertainty and dynamism.

The advent of **Large Language Models (LLMs)**, such as GPT-3 and GPT-4, revolutionized task planning by offering remarkable generalization capabilities and the ability to process natural language instructions. LLMs can generate and adapt complex plans for long-horizon tasks, showing impressive zero-shot and few-shot learning. However, LLMs have their own set of limitations: they are prone to "hallucinations" (generating factually incorrect or illogical outputs), lack domain-specific precision, and can be computationally inefficient when repeatedly solving similar problems. Efforts like SayCan, Reflect, and LLM+P attempt to ground LLM outputs in physical constraints or integrate them with classical planners to enhance their reliability.

Complementing LLMs, **Multimodal Foundation Models (VLMs)**, like CLIP and BLIP, have emerged, integrating visual and textual data to enable robots to understand their environment visually while reasoning with language. These models are powerful for perception-focused tasks and offer a degree of visually grounded world understanding, moving beyond simple visual representations to broader reasoning.

On the other hand, **Knowledge Graphs (KGs)** provide a structured, symbolic approach to knowledge representation. KGs organize entities and their relationships, allowing for context-aware reasoning and efficient retrieval of procedural knowledge. The Functional Object-Oriented Network (FOON), a specific type of KG used in this research, explicitly models manipulation action sequences. KGs offer precision and reliability but are labor-intensive to build and lack the inherent flexibility and generalization of FMs for novel scenarios.

**Adaptive Error Management** in robotics is another critical area, focusing on enhancing system resilience and human-robot collaboration. Existing works often tackle specific failure scenarios (e.g., object-picking, navigation failures) and tend to be task-specific. While frameworks like CAPE and AutoTAMP address plan refinement and error identification, many struggle with translating virtual corrections to real-world scenarios or rely on predefined protocols.

STAR positions itself at the intersection of these fields, aiming to overcome the individual limitations of each. It specifically addresses the gap where LLMs are too unreliable and inefficient for real-world robotic deployment, and KGs are too rigid for dynamic environments. By synergizing FMs for their generalization and natural language understanding with KGs for structured, domain-specific, and reusable knowledge, STAR seeks to create a framework that is both adaptable to novel situations and reliable in executing known tasks and recovering from failures. Unlike traditional systems with rigid recovery protocols or purely FM-driven approaches prone to inconsistencies, STAR introduces a dynamic KG expansion and continuous learning mechanism, enabling robots to autonomously diagnose and recover from diverse failures by building experiential knowledge. This hybrid approach represents a significant step towards truly autonomous and resilient robotic systems in unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for the STAR framework stems from the critical challenges faced by modern robotic systems: the need for precision and adaptability in dynamic, unpredictable environments, coupled with the ability to autonomously recover from execution failures. Traditional robotic architectures, reliant on rigid, rule-based programming, are ill-equipped to handle the unpredictability of real-world tasks and struggle with autonomous failure recovery (e.g., sensor errors, grasping inaccuracies, environmental changes) without human intervention or costly task restarts.

While Foundation Models (FMs) offer promising avenues with their contextual reasoning and generalization capabilities, their inherent limitations pose significant obstacles to reliable deployment. These limitations include:
- **Computational inefficiency:** Repeatedly processing similar scenarios.
- **Hallucinations:** Generating plausible but incorrect outputs.
- **Output inconsistencies:** Lack of precision and reliability, especially in critical failure recovery scenarios.
- **Lack of domain-specific precision:** Inability to prioritize solutions based on historical success rates or domain constraints.

Conversely, while Knowledge Graphs (KGs) provide structured and reliable knowledge, they are limited to specific domains, require significant effort to build, and lack the flexibility to interpret natural language or adapt to novel situations.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of the STAR framework are:
1. **To develop a novel hybrid architecture:** Integrate the contextual reasoning of Foundation Models (specifically LLMs for planning and VLMs for detection) with dynamically expanding Knowledge Graphs (KGs) to achieve robust task planning.
2. **To enable autonomous and resilient failure recovery:** Design a mechanism that can autonomously diagnose root causes of failures, generate context-aware recovery strategies, and execute corrective actions without human intervention.
3. **To mitigate FM limitations:** Embed learned knowledge into structured, reusable KGs to streamline information retrieval, reduce redundant FM computations, and ground FM outputs in precise, scenario-specific facts, thereby reducing hallucinations and increasing consistency.
4. **To facilitate continuous learning and adaptation:** Allow the KG to dynamically expand with experiential knowledge (both successful plans and recovery strategies), ensuring continuous adaptation to novel scenarios and reducing future reliance on FMs.
5. **To validate effectiveness:** Demonstrate significant improvements in task planning accuracy and recovery success rates over existing baseline methods through comprehensive experimentation on diverse robotic tasks and failure scenarios.
6. **To contribute to the research community:** Provide a publicly shared dataset of robotic tasks, failure scenarios, and recovery benchmarks to standardize future research and foster further development.

In essence, STAR is motivated by the desire to build robotic systems that are not only capable of understanding and executing complex tasks from natural language instructions but can also intelligently adapt to unforeseen circumstances and recover from failures, continuously improving their performance through experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

STAR (Smart Task Adaptation and Recovery) employs a hybrid architecture that synergizes Foundation Models (FMs) with dynamically expanding Knowledge Graphs (KGs) to achieve robust task planning and autonomous failure recovery. The approach is organized into distinct pipelines for task planning and failure recovery, supported by continuous learning mechanisms.

#### 3.1. Hybrid FM-KG Architecture

The core of STAR is its dual reliance on FMs and KGs. FMs (specifically GPT-4 for LLM tasks and GPT-4 Vision for VLM tasks) provide generalized reasoning, natural language understanding, and adaptability to novel scenarios. KGs, primarily the Functional Object-Oriented Network (FOON), offer structured, reusable, and domain-specific knowledge for efficient retrieval and grounding FM outputs. A specialized KG called FailNet is introduced for failure recovery strategies.

#### 3.2. Task Planning Methodology

The task planning pipeline translates natural language instructions into executable robotic plans, leveraging a hierarchical approach inspired by human problem-solving.

1. **Task Tree Retrieval from KG:**
 - Given a natural language instruction (e.g., "Make a cheese pizza"), an FM interprets the input to identify the goal (e.g., "cheese pizza").
 - The system searches the FOON KG for a corresponding task tree, aided by a recipe classification system to categorize dishes.
 - Three scenarios are handled:
 - **Case 1 (No Match):** KG lacks any relevant task tree.
 - **Case 2 (Partial Match):** Similar task trees exist (e.g., corn soup instead of lentil soup).
 - **Case 3 (Exact Match):** A complete task tree for the requested dish is found.

2. **LLM-Assisted Task Tree Generation:**
 - For Cases 1 and 2, an LLM (GPT-4) is employed. If a partial task tree exists (Case 2), it's provided as context for the LLM to modify.
 - If no partial tree exists (Case 1), few-shot prompting is used to teach the LLM the syntax and structure of FOON task trees, enabling it to generate a new task tree. The paper notes that five examples are sufficient for this structural learning.

3. **Lifelong Learning and Knowledge Expansion (for Task Planning):**
 - Successfully executed task plans, whether retrieved from the KG or generated/corrected by the LLM, are stored back into the FOON.
 - This process reduces future reliance on LLM queries for repetitive tasks, improving efficiency, accuracy, and cost-effectiveness by leveraging accumulated structured knowledge. Corrections to plans are visualized and merged with the existing KG, preventing duplicates and expanding the functional unit repertoire.

4. **Low-Level Plan Generation:**
 - The final task tree (from KG, LLM, or combined) is converted into a PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language) plan for robotic execution.
 - LLMs are used with simple prompts to generate PDDL domain and problem files for each functional unit.
 - A classical planner, such as Fast Forward, then solves the PDDL problem to generate precise action sequences.

#### 3.3. Failure Recovery Methodology

STAR emphasizes continuous monitoring and adaptive recovery from execution failures, which are common in unstructured environments.

1. **Continuous Monitoring:**
 - During task execution, the robot continuously monitors its actions at regular intervals to detect unsafe actions or unintended collateral events (e.g., knocking over objects). This early detection prevents critical failures and ensures safety.

2. **Failure Detection through Vision-Language Models (VLM):**
 - GPT-4 Vision is used to detect failures by analyzing frames periodically sampled during execution.
 - Instead of relying on final-frame analysis, multiple frames are provided to the VLM, along with contextual information about the currently executing functional unit. This allows for precise detection of subtle and major issues, including root causes (e.g., overpouring water mid-action).
 - **Optimization with Image Grids:** To address the computational inefficiency and high token usage of sending multiple individual frames to the VLM, an optimization strategy is implemented: multiple frames (e.g., nine) are combined into a single 3x3 image grid. This significantly reduces API cost and improves response time without sacrificing detection accuracy.

3. **Recovery Mechanism:**
 - Upon failure detection, the system determines the appropriate recovery:
 - **Re-execution without Plan Modification:** Simple errors (e.g., failed grasp) can be resolved by retrying the action.
 - **Plan Adjustment and Adaptive Recovery:** More complex scenarios require modifications to the task plan (e.g., adding more flour after overpouring water).

4. **Hierarchical Re-planning with FailNet:**
 - **High-Level Recovery Plan:** A specialized KG called FailNet, structured similarly to FOON but focused on recovery strategies, is searched. FailNet contains functional units related to various failure types (e.g., "add extra ingredients" for overpouring).
 - **FailNet Knowledge Expansion and Lifelong Learning:** FailNet starts with a minimal set of recovery strategies and expands over time. When an LLM generates a novel and successful recovery plan, these functional units are added to FailNet. This continuous learning enhances future response efficiency, reducing the need for repetitive LLM queries. FailNet is kept separate from FOON to reduce search complexity but is designed for seamless merging.
 - **Low-Level Recovery Plan:** The high-level recovery plan from FailNet (or LLM) is converted into a PDDL plan for precise robotic execution.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Task Planning Evaluation:** A comprehensive dataset of cafeteria menus over one month was curated to test task planning for various dishes. Performance was measured by task tree generation efficiency and accuracy using a "progress line framework," compared against FOON, FOON + Substitution, GPT-4, and Tree Consolidation baselines.
- **Failure Detection and Recovery Evaluation:** A video dataset of robotic task failures (pouring, mixing, pick-and-place) was compiled from YouTube, robotics competitions, and lab experiments. Each video was manually annotated with failure causes and recovery plans.
 - Failure Detection: Compared against BLIP2, "Final Frame Only" detection, and Reflect, assessing the impact of progressive (multi-frame) detection.
 - Recovery Plan Accuracy: An ablation study evaluated KG-only, GPT-4-only, and STAR approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimentation conducted for STAR yielded significant results across both task planning and failure recovery, demonstrating the efficacy of the hybrid FM-KG framework.

#### 4.1. Task Planning Evaluation

- **Superior Accuracy:** STAR achieved a remarkable **86% task planning accuracy**, significantly outperforming all baseline methods. For comparison:
 - FOON: 5% (highlighting its limitation without external reasoning for natural language inputs)
 - FOON + Substitution: 54%
 - GPT-4: 78% (demonstrating the strength of FMs but also STAR's improvement by grounding FM outputs)
 - Tree Consolidation: 81%
- **Lifelong Learning Benefits:** A crucial advantage highlighted was STAR's ability to store corrected plans within its KG. This ensures that once a plan is refined for a specific task, the system will consistently produce the correct result for the same query in the future, improving reliability for repetitive tasks and making the system more robust over long-term deployment.

#### 4.2. Failure Detection and Recovery Evaluation: Failure Detection

- **High Detection and Explanation Accuracy:** STAR demonstrated strong performance in identifying and explaining failures:
 - **Detection Accuracy:** 90%
 - **Explanation Accuracy:** 78%
- **Outperformance of Baselines:** STAR significantly surpassed other detection methods:
 - BLIP2 Caption: 8% detection, 2% explanation (struggles with high-level semantic descriptions)
 - Final Frame Only: 82% detection, 52% explanation (underscores the importance of progressive detection)
 - Reflect: 88% detection, 68% explanation
- **Impact of Progressive Detection:** The use of sequence-based progressive detection (evaluating multiple frames) was crucial for STAR's high performance, enabling the system to detect subtle issues and identify root causes that single-frame analysis would miss.

#### 4.2.2. Recovery Plan Accuracy

- **Enhanced Recovery Success Rate:** STAR achieved a **78% recovery success rate**, showcasing the benefits of its integrated approach.
- **Ablation Study Insights:**
 - KG search only: 28% (emphasizing the limitations of static KGs for novel or complex failures)
 - GPT-4 prompt engineering only: 74% (demonstrates FM's capability but highlights the incremental improvement from KG integration)
- **Identified Limitations:** The study noted that some generated recovery plans were non-executable, such as instructing the robot to perform tasks beyond its physical capabilities (e.g., "collect scattered rice from the table"). This points to a need for better grounding of recovery actions within the robot's specific affordances.

#### 4.3. Knowledge Graph Initialization and Optimization

- **Dynamic KG Growth:** A key finding was the framework's ability to start with an initially empty KG and dynamically grow and update it as the robot interacts with its environment. This allows for learning new tasks and accumulating task-specific corrections in FailNet, increasing versatility.
- **Multi-Domain KG Merging:** The framework supports seamless merging of KGs from different domains (e.g., food preparation and cleaning), which is crucial for multi-domain task planning and broadening the robot's capabilities.
- **Image Grid Optimization:** The implementation of image grids for VLM input proved to be highly effective. It achieved comparable accuracy to processing individual frames for failure detection while significantly reducing API response times and operational costs. This optimization is vital for practical, real-time deployment of VLM-based detection.

In summary, STAR demonstrates a robust and effective approach to integrating the strengths of FMs and KGs, leading to measurable improvements in robotic task planning and autonomous failure recovery. The continuous learning mechanisms and practical optimizations contribute significantly to its real-world applicability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The STAR framework represents a significant advance in the field of robotics, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenges of achieving true autonomy and resilience in dynamic, unstructured environments. Its potential impact spans several crucial areas:

1. **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Reliability:** STAR directly tackles the limitations of traditional rigid robotic systems and the inconsistencies of purely Foundation Model-driven approaches. By providing a framework for autonomous task planning and self-correction from failures without human intervention, it paves the way for robots to operate more independently in real-world settings, from automated factories to assistive robotics in homes or extreme environments. The demonstrated 86% task planning accuracy and 78% recovery success rate are substantial improvements that translate directly into more reliable and dependable robotic operations.

2. **Mitigation of Foundation Model Limitations:** A critical contribution of STAR is its elegant solution for grounding and improving the reliability of Foundation Models. By embedding learned knowledge into structured Knowledge Graphs, STAR effectively reduces FM hallucinations, enhances domain-specific precision, and significantly curtails computational inefficiency due to redundant queries. This hybrid approach allows roboticists to leverage the powerful generalization capabilities of FMs while mitigating their known shortcomings, making them more suitable for safety-critical and high-precision robotic applications.

3. **Continuous Learning and Adaptability:** The dynamic expansion of both the FOON and FailNet Knowledge Graphs through experiential learning is a cornerstone of STAR's impact. Robots can continuously refine their understanding of tasks and accumulate effective recovery strategies over time. This "lifelong learning" capability means robots can adapt to novel scenarios, learn from their mistakes, and become increasingly proficient without requiring extensive retraining or human reprogramming for every new challenge. This is vital for long-term deployments where environments and tasks may evolve.

4. **Foundation for Multi-Domain and Multi-Agent Systems:** The framework's flexibility in supporting an initially empty KG and seamlessly merging KGs from different domains (e.g., cooking and cleaning) opens doors for highly versatile robots. In the future, this could lead to robots capable of performing a wide array of tasks across diverse environments by integrating specialized knowledge bases. Furthermore, the paper hints at extending STAR to multi-agent scenarios and distributed knowledge sharing, suggesting its potential to coordinate groups of robots or share learned experiences across a fleet, amplifying its impact in industrial and complex service settings.

5. **Practical Deployment and Cost Efficiency:** The optimization strategy using image grids for VLM-based failure detection is a practical innovation. By significantly reducing API response times and operational costs while maintaining high accuracy, STAR makes advanced perception and reasoning more feasible for real-time robotic applications, where computational resources and budget are often constraints.

6. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The release of a comprehensive dataset for robotic tasks and failure scenarios is a valuable contribution to the research community. It provides a standardized benchmark, enabling other researchers to evaluate and compare their methods, thereby fostering further innovation and accelerating progress in robust robotics. Future research directions outlined by the authors—such as integrating richer multi-modal inputs (sound, touch), advancing self-supervised learning, and exploring multi-agent scenarios—underscore the foundational nature of STAR and its potential to inspire new avenues of exploration in AI-driven robotics.

In conclusion, STAR pushes the boundaries of autonomous robotics by offering a well-engineered solution to the challenge of robust task planning and failure recovery. Its synergistic approach, emphasis on continuous learning, and practical optimizations lay a strong foundation for the next generation of intelligent, adaptable, and resilient robotic systems capable of operating effectively in the complexities of the real world.
