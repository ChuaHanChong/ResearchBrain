# Sample-efficient Low-level Motion Planning for Robotic Manipulation Tasks via Zero-shot Transfer Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework named iCEM+TL, developed by Cardiff University researchers, enhances low-level motion planning for complex robotic manipulation tasks like stacking and shelf placement by integrating Transfer Learning and Reward Redesign into the iCEM algorithm. This approach achieves success rate improvements of up to 23% in simulation compared to baseline iCEM and was successfully validated on a physical Franka Emika FR3 robot.

## Problem

- Increasing complexity of robotic manipulation tasks leads to computationally intensive motion planning models and extensive training times for high-level Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approaches.
- Existing low-level motion planners, such as iCEM, show performance limitations in complex, long-horizon manipulation scenarios (e.g., multi-object stacking, shelf placement).
- Current transfer learning research primarily focuses on DRL policies, leaving an opportunity to improve optimization-based low-level planners through knowledge reuse.

## Method

- Developed **iCEM+TL**, a model-based planning framework that integrates Transfer Learning (TL) and ==Reward Redesign== (RR) into the Sample-efficient ==Cross-Entropy Method== (iCEM).
- TL is implemented through zero-shot transfer of iCEM sampling distribution parameters (mean and standard deviation) and continuous transfer of elite trajectories from simpler "upstream" tasks.
- RR decomposes complex task objectives into elementary reward modules (e.g., for object placement, gripper proximity, and vertical lifting) to provide more granular guidance to the planner.

## Results

- The iCEM+TL framework achieved an absolute success rate gain of approximately **23.0%** on the FetchStack task compared to the vanilla iCEM baseline in simulation.
- iCEM+TL consistently outperformed other learning-based baselines like CEE-US and PointFlowMatch in the FetchSlide and newly introduced Shelf tasks, demonstrating superior generalization.
- Successful real-world validation on a Franka Emika FR3 robot for a stacking task confirmed the practical feasibility and deployment potential of the framework by executing simulation-identified optimal trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transferring knowledge, specifically iCEM sampling distribution parameters and elite trajectories, from structurally aligned "upstream" tasks significantly boosts performance in complex "downstream" tasks, guided by an "elite reward advantage" criterion.
> - The synergistic combination of Transfer Learning and Reward Redesign provides substantial performance gains for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks, outperforming either component applied in isolation.
> - Enhanced optimization-based low-level planners can effectively tackle challenging robotic manipulation tasks, demonstrating a computationally efficient alternative to methods that rely heavily on high-level learned policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing from foundational tasks such as pushing and placing objects to more intricate challenges, including object stacking, sliding, and shelf placement. High-level planning models, particularly those based on Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), have achieved reliable performance in these complex domains. However, DRL approaches typically necessitate substantial computational resources and extensive training periods, which can be a limiting factor for practical deployment.

To address these limitations, researchers have explored low-level motion planners. Evolutionary Strategies (ES), such as the Sample-efficient Cross-Entropy Method (iCEM), have demonstrated potential for real-time planning by incorporating efficient knowledge reuse. These low-level planners are often integrated with high-level learned models to achieve comprehensive planning across different scales. Nevertheless, as manipulation problems grow in complexity, even combined high-level and low-level planners can exhibit limitations.

Related work in DRL, such as Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC), has focused on improving exploration and stability, respectively. Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) has been employed to enhance sample efficiency, particularly in sparse-reward settings like FetchSlide. However, DRL methods often struggle with long-horizon dependencies and very sparse rewards characteristic of tasks like stacking or shelf placement.

Evolutionary algorithms, conversely, offer a gradient-free optimization paradigm, directly searching in the action space. CEM and its improved variant, iCEM, are prominent examples, leveraging structured exploration, colored noise, and elite reuse to enhance sampling efficiency. Other approaches, such as CEE-US, have further integrated iCEM with intrinsic motivation for multi-goal tasks. Beyond optimization, generative imitation learning methods (e.g., PointFlowMatch) and foundation models (e.g., Neural MP) are also being explored for low-level control and high-level planning, though they often focus on joint-space trajectories rather than comprehensive grasping and manipulation policies.

Transfer Learning (TL) is a recognized strategy for improving sample efficiency and cross-task generalization in robotics, encompassing task-to-task transfer and zero-shot transfer settings. Existing TL research often validates its effectiveness for DRL algorithms, focusing on reusing policy parameters or training models across progressively complex task sequences.

This work contributes to the landscape by specifically addressing the enhancement of *low-level motion planning* within an *optimization-based framework* (iCEM). Rather than relying on extensive DRL training or learning large neural policies, the proposed iCEM+TL framework aims to improve planning efficiency at inference time through task decomposition and knowledge reuse. It integrates TL and Reward Redesign (RR) directly into the iCEM algorithm, providing an alternative approach to managing complexity without necessarily combining with high-level learned models or undergoing lengthy training phases. This focus distinguishes it from approaches centered on DRL policy learning or purely generative models by concentrating on refining an online optimization planner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing complexity of robotic manipulation tasks and the associated challenges of longer training times and computationally intensive motion planning models. While existing methods, including DRL and low-level planners like iCEM, have made progress, they exhibit limitations in complex scenarios such as multi-object stacking, sliding, and shelf placement. The goal is to develop a method that can efficiently handle these complex tasks without relying on extensive training or high-level learned models.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

1. **Address Performance Constraints of iCEM in Complex Tasks:** Recognize that while iCEM offers promising real-time planning capabilities, its performance can be constrained in more complex manipulation tasks. The motivation is to enhance iCEM's capability to effectively address these scenarios.
2. **Leverage Task Decomposition:** Utilize task decomposition as a strategy to improve scalability and generalization for complex robotic manipulation problems. This involves applying complementary techniques: Transfer Learning (TL) and Reward Redesign (RR).
3. **Integrate Transfer Learning (TL) with iCEM:** Develop a novel framework, iCEM+TL, that explicitly integrates TL into the iCEM algorithm. The motivation is to reuse knowledge, specifically key iCEM parameters like sampling distribution attributes (mean and standard deviation) and elite trajectories, acquired from simpler "upstream" tasks to guide planning in more complex "downstream" tasks. This aims to accelerate the planning process and improve efficiency by providing a more informed initial search space.
4. **Incorporate Reward Redesign (RR) for Task-Specific Optimization:** Apply Reward Redesign (RR) through task decomposition to optimize task-specific performance, particularly for structured tasks like stacking and shelf placement. The motivation is to provide clearer, more aligned guidance for the planner by breaking down complex objectives into elementary reward modules.
5. **Achieve High Performance with a Low-Level Planner Only:** Demonstrate that challenging robotic manipulation tasks, including stacking, sliding, and shelf placement, can be performed effectively using only a low-level motion planner, without the necessity of high-level learned models. This addresses the computational and training cost associated with high-level DRL policies.
6. **Investigate Task Decomposition Modalities in Evolutionary Strategies:** Provide insights into "what to transfer" (e.g., sampling distribution parameters, elite trajectories) and "how to transfer" (e.g., zero-shot initialization, continuous guidance) within the context of ES-based planners like iCEM. Additionally, explore how reward functions can be effectively decomposed in the RR phase.
7. **Validate in Simulation and Real-World:** Prove the feasibility and practical applicability of the proposed iCEM+TL framework through extensive simulation experiments and a real-world validation on a physical robotic system.

In essence, the research aims to make low-level motion planning more sample-efficient and robust for complex manipulation tasks by intelligently reusing knowledge across tasks and structuring reward signals, thereby offering a computationally efficient alternative to purely learning-based methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces iCEM+TL, a model-based planning framework that integrates Transfer Learning (TL) and Reward Redesign (RR) into the Sample-efficient Cross-Entropy Method (iCEM). The framework is designed to address complex robotic manipulation tasks by optimizing trajectories to maximize expected cumulative reward.

**Problem Formulation:**
Each manipulation task is defined by a set of objects, an initial configuration, and a goal configuration. The objective is to determine an optimal sequence of actions `a_0:T` that moves the objects from the initial to the goal state, maximizing the cumulative task-specific reward `∑(t=0 to T) R(s_t, a_t)`.

**iCEM+TL Framework Components:**

1. **Transfer Learning (TL) Integration:**
 - **Zero-shot Parameter Transfer:** At the initial timestep (`t=0`), the mean (`μ`) and standard deviation (`σ`) of the Gaussian sampling distribution in iCEM are transferred. These parameters are derived from the elite trajectory distribution of a simpler "upstream" task. This provides a more informative starting point for the sampling process in the "downstream" (more complex) task, guiding exploration toward promising regions of the action space from the outset. This is termed "zero-shot" because no additional training on the downstream task is required; the transferred components solely initialize and guide the online iCEM optimization.
 - **Continuous Elite Trajectory Transfer:** At every subsequent timestep (`t`), in addition to the standard iCEM procedures of shifting elite trajectories from the previous timestep and sampling new candidates, the best-performing trajectories from the upstream task are incorporated into the current elite trajectory set. This mechanism offers ongoing guidance to the planner.
 - **Elite Selection and Distribution Update:** Candidate trajectories are sampled from a Gaussian distribution. Elite trajectories are selected based on their cumulative reward over a planning horizon (`h`). The distribution parameters (`μ`, `σ`) are then updated based on these selected elites, employing momentum to smooth the updates across iterations. The first action of the best elite sequence is executed, and the process reiterates. In the experimental setup, a fixed elite set size of 20 includes 6 shifted from the previous timestep, 10 transferred from an upstream task, and 4 newly generated through Gaussian sampling.

2. **Reward Redesign (RR):**
 - For the complex FetchStack and Shelf tasks, the overarching objective is decomposed into simpler sub-goals, and the reward function is redesigned to provide more granular guidance. The total reward `R` is formulated as the sum of three components:
 - `R_base`: Encourages accurate object placement. It penalizes distances between objects and their targets (`d_0`, `d_1`) and activates a second term when the lower object is near its goal.
 - `R_gripper`: Encourages the robot's gripper to remain close to the currently manipulated object, facilitating interaction.
 - `R_lift`: Specifically promotes correct vertical motion during stacking, activated when the lower object is near its goal. It rewards reaching a target stacking height and penalizes negative height differences between objects.

3. **Upstream Task Selection Criterion:**
 - A principled criterion is proposed to assess the potential benefit of transferring from a specific upstream task. It quantifies the structural alignment between tasks by evaluating whether trajectories from the upstream elite set maintain high quality under the downstream task's reward function.
 - The "elite reward advantage" (`∆R_U→D`) is calculated as the expected downstream reward of upstream elite trajectories minus the expected downstream reward of trajectories sampled from the initial downstream distribution. A positive `∆R_U→D` suggests good alignment and beneficial transfer, as upstream elites are already performing well under the downstream objective.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Simulation Environment:** All experiments are conducted in the MuJoCo simulation environment, utilizing Fetch robotic manipulation tasks. This includes FetchSlide (from OpenAI Gym), FetchStack (an adapted version with modified object placement and goal generation logic), and a newly created Shelf environment (with both single-object and multi-object versions).
- **Tasks:**
 - **FetchStack:** Involves picking an object, placing it at a target, and then stacking subsequent objects on top.
 - **FetchSlide:** Requires sliding an object to a target point.
 - **Shelf:** Involves picking multiple objects one by one and placing them on different layers of a shelf. A single-object version serves as the upstream task for TL.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Average success rates with standard deviations, computed over three experimental runs to mitigate randomness.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against Random Sampling, CEM, iCEM, TQC+HER, TQC+HER+TL, CEE-US, and Point-FlowMatch. For PointFlowMatch, task-specific expert demonstrations are generated to train the model, which is configured with 500 epochs based on observed superior performance compared to 1500 epochs.
- **Hardware:** Experiments were performed on a PC with an Intel Core i7-14700K CPU and a supercomputer with an Intel Xeon Gold 6148 CPU.

**Real-world Validation:**
The framework is validated on a Franka Emika FR3 robot for a stacking task. A simulated environment mirroring the real robot's initial end-effector and object configurations is created. The best-performing trajectory identified in simulation is saved and subsequently executed on the physical robot. The simulation uses a 4-DoF robotic arm, while the FR3 robot has 7-DoF; a RelaxedIK function is used to generate joint solutions, and linear interpolation is applied to joint movements for compliance with robot constraints.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the iCEM+TL framework, conducted across various simulated robotic manipulation tasks and validated on a real-world robot, yielded several key findings:

**Overall Performance Comparison (Simulation):**

- **Stack Task:** The iCEM+TL framework demonstrated a substantial performance improvement on the FetchStack task, achieving an absolute gain of approximately 23.0% in success rate compared to the best-performing baseline, vanilla iCEM. This improvement was also accompanied by reduced variance. Learning-based planners like TQC+HER and PointFlowMatch exhibited limitations in this long-horizon task, performing less competitively.
- **Slide Task:** For the FetchSlide task, learning-based baselines, specifically TQC+HER and its transfer variant, showed competitive performance. The iCEM+TL approach also proved effective in this task, achieving favorable results without requiring task-specific training.
- **Shelf Task:** On the newly introduced Shelf task, iCEM+TL again outperformed vanilla iCEM. Learning-based methods such as CEE-US and PointFlowMatch displayed limited generalization capabilities, with their performance substantially lower than that of iCEM+TL.
- **Conclusion on Overall Performance:** These results collectively indicate that the integration of Transfer Learning and Reward Redesign within an optimization-based planning framework offers a significant advantage, particularly for long-horizon and complex manipulation tasks.

**Ablation Study:**

- **Impact of Upstream Task Selection:** The study investigated the effect of transferring knowledge from different upstream tasks (Stack, Pick&Place, Push, Slide) on downstream task performance.
 - Transferring trajectories from a Pick&Place task provided the largest improvement for the Stack task (23.0% with a planning horizon of 1000 timesteps).
 - For the Slide task, transferring from a Push task resulted in approximately 10.0% improvement (with a 50-timestep horizon).
 - In the Shelf task, transferring best trajectories from a single-object version to a two-object version yielded a 9.9% improvement (from 0.717 ± 0.038 to 0.816 ± 0.052).
 - These empirical findings supported the proposed criterion for selecting beneficial upstream tasks, indicating that tasks with higher reward-component overlap and positive elite reward alignment (∆R_U→D > 0) consistently led to greater downstream performance gains. Conversely, less related task pairs (e.g., Slide to Stack) offered minimal or no improvement.
- **Individual and Combined Effects of TL and RR:**
 - On the Stack task, applying TL alone improved performance by approximately 5.8%, while RR alone slightly degraded performance by 1.6%. However, enabling both TL and RR together resulted in a significant 23.0% improvement over the baseline, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect.
 - A similar, though less pronounced, trend was observed for the Shelf task. TL and RR individually provided marginal improvements (around 1%), whereas their combination led to a notable 9.9% performance gain, suggesting their combined importance for structured, sequential tasks.
- **Sample Size Effects:** Different sample sizes (40, 100, 300) were evaluated. A sample size of 300 consistently yielded the best performance, with approximately 6.3% improvement for Pick-to-Stack and 14% for Push-to-Slide compared to smaller settings. This suggests that a moderately larger sample size facilitates broader exploration while still being effectively guided by the sampling distribution. When combined with the 23.0% gain from TL and RR, the total improvement on the Stack task reached approximately 29.3% over the vanilla iCEM baseline. However, further increases in sample size led to diminishing returns, potentially increasing inference time for long-horizon tasks or degrading performance in shorter-horizon tasks due to increased variance.
- **Elite Size Effects:** The analysis of elite set size showed that a relatively small elite set size (20 for Pick-to-Stack, 10 for Push-to-Slide) produced the best results. This indicates that a compact elite set is sufficient to effectively guide the optimization process. A fixed set of 10 elite trajectories was consistently transferred from the upstream task in these experiments.

**Real-world Experiment:**

- The iCEM+TL framework was successfully validated on a Franka Emika FR3 robot for a stacking task.
- By simulating the real-world setup and executing the best-performing trajectory identified in simulation, the practical feasibility of the approach for real-world deployment was demonstrated.
- Despite challenges such as differences in degrees of freedom between simulation (4-DoF) and the real robot (7-DoF), and the use of 4D actions (xyz movement, gripper open/close), the framework effectively controlled the FR3 robot. This validation was limited to executing a pre-planned trajectory rather than online replanning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces iCEM+TL, a framework that advances low-level motion planning for robotic manipulation by integrating Transfer Learning (TL) and Reward Redesign (RR) into the Sample-efficient Cross-Entropy Method (iCEM). The findings hold several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Performance in Complex Manipulation:** The framework significantly improves success rates (up to 23% in simulation) for challenging robotic manipulation tasks such as stacking, sliding, and shelf placement. This demonstrates that complex tasks can be managed effectively without relying on computationally intensive, long-horizon policy training, offering a more efficient alternative to purely learning-based approaches.
2. **Increased Sample Efficiency and Reduced Training Time:** By leveraging zero-shot transfer of iCEM parameters and elite trajectories from simpler upstream tasks, the framework accelerates the planning process and enhances sample efficiency. This could reduce the need for extensive data collection and training for new, complex tasks, thereby speeding up development cycles for robotic applications.
3. **Interpretable Task Decomposition:** The research provides practical insights into how task decomposition can be applied within optimization-based planners. It clarifies "what to transfer" (sampling distribution parameters, elite trajectories) and "how to transfer" (zero-shot initialization, continuous guidance), as well as how to effectively decompose reward functions. The proposed criterion for upstream task selection, based on elite trajectory consistency under downstream rewards, offers an interpretable and principled method for determining beneficial transfer.
4. **Practical Feasibility and Real-World Applicability:** The successful real-world validation on a Franka Emika FR3 robot for a stacking task confirms the practical feasibility of the iCEM+TL framework. This suggests its potential for deployment in industrial or service robotics where real-time performance and robustness are crucial.
5. **Guidance for Optimization-Based Planners:** The ablation studies offer valuable practical insights into optimizing iCEM-like planners. The findings highlight the importance of balancing exploration and exploitation—suggesting that moderately larger sample sizes can improve exploration, while a compact elite set, including transferred high-quality trajectories, maintains strong guidance. The necessity of Reward Redesign for complex tasks and appropriate planning horizons for effective optimization are also underscored.
6. **Foundation for Hierarchical Planning:** The work lays a foundation for future research in hierarchical planning, where iCEM+TL could be integrated with high-level learned models. This combined approach could benefit from learned policies providing coarse guidance, which the iCEM+TL planner then refines for low-level trajectory execution.
7. **Addressing Current Challenges in Robotics:** By focusing on improving low-level motion planning without extensive DRL training, the research addresses key challenges in robotic control, such as computational cost, data requirements, and generalization across tasks, making advanced robotic manipulation more accessible and deployable.

In summary, this research contributes a practical, efficient, and interpretable framework for complex robotic manipulation, pushing the capabilities of low-level planners through intelligent knowledge reuse and structured reward design, with demonstrated applicability in real-world scenarios.
