# GET-Zero: Graph Embodiment Transformer for Zero-shot Embodiment Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> GET-Zero introduces a Graph Embodiment Transformer (GET) that enables robot policies to generalize zero-shot to unseen robot morphologies, including structural and geometric variations. This approach, incorporating explicit graph encoding and a self-modeling loss, yields a 20% improvement in zero-shot generalization for complex dexterous manipulation tasks compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Existing robot policies require extensive retraining for even minor hardware modifications, limiting their adaptability and scalability.
- Learned robot policies often lack inherent mechanisms to adapt to structural variations (e.g., missing or added joints) within a robot's kinematic chain.
- Developing embodiment-aware policies for complex tasks like dexterous manipulation is challenging due to the difficulty in defining unified action spaces across diverse robot designs.

## Method

- Introduces the Graph Embodiment Transformer (GET) architecture, which explicitly encodes the robot's kinematic graph structure (spatial and parent-child relationships) into the self-attention mechanism.
- Utilizes an embodiment-aware distillation process, training GET with behavior cloning from multiple embodiment-specific expert policies to efficiently consolidate knowledge.
- Incorporates a self-modeling loss that trains the network to predict forward kinematics (3D joint positions), enhancing the understanding of robot spatial properties for improved generalization.

## Results

- Achieved a 20% improvement in zero-shot generalization for both new graph and new link length variations on dexterous manipulation tasks compared to baseline methods.
- Demonstrated successful transfer from simulation to a physical LEAP Hand, maintaining zero-shot generalization capabilities to unseen physical embodiments.
- Reduced the average per-joint forward kinematics prediction error to 0.82mm, confirming the efficacy of the graph encoding mechanism in understanding spatial relationships.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly embedding the robot's graph connectivity into a transformer's attention mechanism significantly improves zero-shot generalization to structural variations.
> - The self-modeling loss, when combined with robust graph encoding, enhances zero-shot performance by encouraging a strong internal representation of robot kinematics.
> - Linearized graph representations, such as those from Depth-First Search, are sensitive to structural changes, leading to poor generalization across varied robot morphologies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a critical challenge in modern robotics: the limited adaptability of learned robot policies to even minor changes in internal hardware. While robot learning has made strides in adapting to external environments, internal hardware modifications (e.g., missing links, added joints, varying link lengths) typically necessitate costly and time-consuming retraining of policies. GET-Zero directly confronts this limitation by proposing a novel approach for **zero-shot embodiment generalization**.

The broader research landscape includes several related areas:

- **Embodiment-Aware Policies:** A growing trend in robotics aims to develop policies that can control a "class" of robot embodiments rather than a single, fixed design. These policies condition their decisions on the robot's hardware configuration, allowing for data reuse across different embodiments. Previous methods have explored this, but often with limitations in their representation of embodiment, particularly regarding the **graph connectivity** of the robot's kinematic chain. This often restricts their ability to generalize to significant structural variations.
- **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Transformers in Robotics:** GNNs have been used to encode robot morphology, with nodes representing joints and edges representing links, to control stick-like characters. More recently, transformer architectures, initially successful in NLP and vision, have been adopted for robot control. Some prior work has explored transformers for embodiment-aware policies, but either lacked explicit graph encoding (Amorpheus) or used incomplete, linearized graph representations (MetaMorph) that proved sensitive to structural changes. GET-Zero builds upon these by integrating a more robust and explicit graph encoding directly into the transformer's attention mechanism.
- **Cross-Embodiment Pretraining and Fine-tuning:** Another stream of research focuses on large-scale pretraining on diverse robot data, followed by fine-tuning for new embodiments. These methods often rely on a unified action space (e.g., end-effector pose), which works well for tasks like navigation or simple arm manipulation. However, for complex tasks like dexterous manipulation, where precise, low-level joint actions are crucial and the number of fingers/joints can vary significantly, a unified action space is challenging or impossible to define. GET-Zero differentiates itself by operating at the joint-level and requiring **no fine-tuning** on the target embodiment for zero-shot generalization.
- **Self-Supervised Learning in Robotics:** The paper also touches upon self-supervision, a technique gaining traction in various domains. While less explored in cross-embodiment learning, some works have investigated it. GET-Zero's introduction of a "self-modeling loss" (predicting forward kinematics) leverages a general and robot-specific form of self-supervision to enhance generalization.
- **Dexterous Manipulation:** The chosen case study, in-hand object rotation with a multi-fingered hand, positions GET-Zero in the challenging domain of dexterous manipulation. This area demands fine-grained, low-level joint control and is particularly sensitive to variations in robot morphology, making it an ideal testbed for robust embodiment generalization. Many prior embodiment-aware methods focused on locomotion, where the action space considerations might be different.

In summary, GET-Zero advances the state-of-the-art by addressing the limitations of prior embodiment-aware policies in handling **structural variations** (graph connectivity) in a **zero-shot** manner, specifically for complex **dexterous manipulation** tasks. It achieves this by integrating a novel, explicit graph encoding into a transformer architecture and employing a practical behavior cloning strategy with a self-modeling loss.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind GET-Zero stems from a significant bottleneck in current robot learning paradigms: the inability of learned control policies to inherently adapt to variations in a robot's physical structure. Existing systems often require extensive retraining when hardware undergoes even minor modifications, such as losing a joint due to damage or gaining one for a design upgrade. This severely limits the flexibility, robustness, and scalability of robotic applications, especially in domains like manipulation and locomotion where diverse robot designs are common.

The central goal of this work is to overcome this limitation by introducing an **embodiment representation that explicitly leverages the graph connectivity of the robot** to enable **improved zero-shot control** for robots with variations in their graph structure. This means the policy should immediately function on a previously unseen robot configuration, including those with different numbers of joints or different kinematic layouts, without any additional training data for that specific configuration.

To achieve this overarching goal, GET-Zero sets out three specific objectives:

1. **Develop the Graph Embodiment Transformer (GET) architecture:** The core objective is to design a transformer-based model that can effectively encode and utilize the robot's kinematic graph structure. This involves representing joints as tokens and integrating the graph connectivity as a learned structural bias within the transformer's attention mechanism, differentiating it from prior transformer-based approaches that either ignored graph structure or used fragile linearizations.
2. **Propose an Embodiment-aware Distillation Training Procedure:** The aim is to create a practical and efficient method for training the GET model. This involves a teacher-student paradigm where behavior cloning is used to distill knowledge from multiple embodiment-specific expert policies into a single, generalizable GET model. This approach simplifies data generation and policy learning compared to jointly training an embodiment-aware policy with reinforcement learning across all embodiments.
3. **Introduce a Self-modeling Loss:** A complementary objective is to enhance the generalization capabilities of GET-Zero by incorporating a self-supervised learning signal. Specifically, the model is trained to predict the forward kinematics (3D joint positions), a fundamental and generally applicable task that encourages the network to develop a robust understanding of the robot's spatial and kinematic properties.

The case study on dexterous in-hand object rotation serves as a crucial testbed to validate these objectives, demonstrating the approach's effectiveness on a complex task where precise, low-level joint control and adaptability to diverse hand morphologies are paramount.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GET-Zero proposes a novel model architecture, the **Graph Embodiment Transformer (GET)**, and a distinct training procedure designed for zero-shot embodiment generalization. The methodology is structured in three key components: GET model architecture, embodiment-aware distillation, and a self-modeling loss.

#### 3.1. A. Graph Embodiment Transformer (GET)

GET is a modified transformer encoder specifically tailored to incorporate robot embodiment information.

- **Embodiment Tokenization:** For a robot with *J* joints, GET represents each joint as a distinct transformer token. These tokens encapsulate both **local sensory observations** (e.g., joint angle, velocity, with history) and **local hardware properties** (e.g., joint position in rest pose, joint limit ranges from URDF files). Additionally, **global observations** (e.g., global time encoding, task identifier) are included in each token. This comprehensive token format ensures that each joint's context, both dynamic and static, is fed into the transformer.
- **Graph Encoding as Structural Bias:** The core innovation of GET lies in its method of embedding the robot's kinematic graph structure directly into the transformer's self-attention mechanism, similar to Graphormer. This overcomes the permutation invariance of standard transformers and the limitations of linear graph representations.
 - **Spatial Encoding:** This computes the shortest path distance (SPD) between any two joints, treating all kinematic links as undirected edges. A learned scalar embedding, indexed by this SPD, is added to the attention score between the two joints. This allows the model to learn to modulate attention based on physical proximity in the robot's structure.
 - **Parent-Child Encoding:** Unique to GET, this introduces a directed bias. It computes parent distance (distance if joint *j* is a child of *i* along a forward kinematic chain) and child distance (distance if joint *j* is a parent of *i*). Separate learned scalar embeddings for these directed distances are added to the attention scores. This explicitly informs the model about the hierarchical and directional nature of kinematic chains, which is crucial for understanding how joint movements propagate.
 - **Attention Bias Computation:** The standard attention calculation is augmented with these learned spatial and parent-child bias embeddings, making the attention mechanism explicitly aware of the robot's graph structure in a permutation-invariant manner.
- **Output Heads:** After processing the joint tokens through the transformer encoder, GET uses two output heads:
 - **Policy Head:** Predicts per-joint delta actions, which are added to PD target states to control the robot's low-level joint controllers.
 - **Self-modeling Head:** Predicts the current 3D pose (position and orientation) of each joint in the robot's local frame, effectively performing forward kinematics.

#### 3.2. B. Embodiment-aware Distillation

The training of GET-Zero follows a teacher-student paradigm (Fig. 3), leveraging behavior cloning for efficiency and flexibility.

1. **Procedural Embodiment Generation:** The research uses the LEAP Hand, a highly configurable multi-fingered robot hand. Variations are procedurally generated by removing combinations of finger joints/links and introducing link length extensions, resulting in 236 unique graph variations. A subset of these (44) are selected as training embodiments, with only graph variations (no link length extensions during training).
2. **Train Embodiment-Specific Experts:** For each of the 44 training embodiments, an individual Reinforcement Learning (RL) expert policy is trained using PPO in the Isaac Gym simulation environment. These experts learn the dexterous in-hand object rotation task, using proprioceptive joint states to output delta joint targets for a PD controller. Only experts achieving a baseline performance (full 2π rotation) are kept.
3. **Distill Experts into Embodiment-aware GET:** Demonstration data (state-action pairs) is collected by rolling out these embodiment-specific experts. This data, combined with embodiment information (URDF-derived kinematic graph), forms the training dataset for the GET model. Behavior Cloning (BC) is then used to distill the collective knowledge of these diverse experts into a single GET policy. This contrasts with prior RL-based multi-embodiment training which can be complex due to varying learning rates.

#### 3.3. C. Self-modeling Loss

A crucial aspect of the training is the integration of a self-modeling loss. The self-modeling head predicts the 3D pose of each joint. This is supervised using an L2 loss against the ground truth forward kinematics (FK) from the simulator. This loss encourages the network to develop a strong internal representation of the robot's physical structure and how joint angles translate into spatial configurations, without requiring additional task-specific supervision. This is particularly valuable for cross-embodiment transfer, as understanding FK is fundamental to any robot control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted in simulation and real-world scenarios demonstrate the effectiveness of GET-Zero in achieving zero-shot embodiment generalization.

- **High Performance on Training Embodiments:** GET-Zero successfully learns to control 44 diverse training embodiments with a single set of network weights, achieving 99% of the performance of individual, embodiment-specific RL experts. This validates its ability to consolidate knowledge from multiple designs.
- **Significant Improvement in Zero-Shot Generalization:**
 - GET-Zero shows a **16% improvement** over the best baseline for zero-shot generalization to **new graph variations** (unseen numbers or configurations of joints).
 - It achieves a **10% improvement** for zero-shot generalization to **new link length variations** (unseen geometry).
 - Crucially, for the most challenging scenario of zero-shot generalization to **both new graph and new link length variations**, GET-Zero yields a **20% improvement**. These results highlight the robustness of GET-Zero's graph encoding to novel structural and geometric configurations.
- **Self-modeling Loss Enhances Performance with Graph Encoding:** The self-modeling loss (FK prediction) exhibits a nuanced effect. When added to a baseline without graph encoding (ET), it surprisingly *decreases* zero-shot performance (average 4.6% drop). However, when combined with the full graph encoding (ET+PE+SE), it leads to a consistent *increase* in performance (average 5.1% improvement). This suggests that self-modeling requires a sufficient structural understanding provided by graph encoding to generate a useful training signal; without it, the task is too ill-posed for the network.
- **Limitations of Depth-First Search (DFS) Linearization:** The ET+DFS baseline, which uses a linearized graph representation (as in MetaMorph), performs reasonably well for new *geometry* variations (11% improvement over ET) but significantly *worse* for new *graph* variations (26% drop compared to ET). This confirms that linearizing the graph via DFS is sensitive to structural changes, as the ordering becomes inconsistent across varied graphs, leading to poor generalization.
- **Contributions of Spatial and Parent-Child Encoding:** Both the spatial (SE) and parent-child (PE) graph encodings contribute positively to policy learning. Relative to ET+SL, adding PE improves performance by 19%, and adding SE improves it by 25% on average across zero-shot tasks. While adding PE when SE is already present showed no statistically significant *additional* improvement, both are essential components of the GET architecture.
- **Effectiveness with Fewer Training Embodiments:** The study demonstrates that GET-Zero can achieve reasonable zero-shot rotation performance even when trained on a smaller subset (e.g., 10) of embodiments, suggesting it does not strictly require an extremely large and diverse training set to generalize.
- **Improved Forward Kinematics Prediction:** Quantitatively, GET-Zero drastically improves the accuracy of forward kinematics prediction, reducing the average per-joint error to 0.82mm compared to 7.97mm (ET) and 9.03mm (ET+DFS) for baselines. This directly confirms the efficacy of the graph encoding mechanism in understanding spatial relationships.
- **Real-world Validation:** Real-world experiments on a physical LEAP Hand demonstrate that GET-Zero successfully transfers its policies from simulation to reality and maintains its zero-shot generalization capabilities to unseen physical embodiments with varied graphs and geometries. It outperforms the ET baseline significantly, particularly in maintaining stable object rotation. While a sim-to-real gap is observed, GET-Zero's relative performance and ability to handle challenging configurations highlight its practical utility.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GET-Zero represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and generalization, with several key implications:

- **Enabling Truly Adaptive Robots:** The ability of GET-Zero to perform zero-shot control on new robot designs with *structural variations* is a major step towards truly adaptive and robust robots. This means robots could instantly adapt to unexpected damage (e.g., a missing finger joint) or rapid design iterations (e.g., a prototype with different link lengths) without requiring laborious retraining.
- **Reduced Development and Deployment Costs:** By eliminating the need for retraining or fine-tuning for every new embodiment, GET-Zero can drastically reduce the data collection, computational resources, and human effort associated with deploying robots in diverse or evolving hardware configurations. This is particularly valuable for custom-built or specialized robots where large datasets for every configuration are impractical.
- **Enhanced Robot Resilience and Flexibility:** Robots capable of zero-shot generalization are inherently more resilient to unforeseen hardware changes and offer greater flexibility in deployment. This could accelerate prototyping cycles, allow for more agile robot manufacturing, and enable robots to operate in unpredictable environments where damage is a possibility.
- **Effective Use of Structural Priors:** The work elegantly demonstrates the power of explicitly leveraging the robot's kinematic graph as a structural inductive bias within a modern deep learning architecture (Transformer). This principle, by encoding fundamental physical relationships, makes the learning more efficient and the learned policies more generalizable.
- **Practical Training Paradigm:** The teacher-student distillation approach using behavior cloning provides a more practical and flexible training framework compared to complex multi-embodiment reinforcement learning setups. It allows researchers to leverage existing expert policies or data from various sources without needing to redesign the entire learning pipeline.
- **Generalizable Self-Supervision:** The successful integration of a simple, universal self-modeling task like forward kinematics further highlights the potential of self-supervised learning to improve cross-embodiment transfer. This approach could be extended to other fundamental robot properties, building more comprehensive and generalizable robot intelligence.
- **Impact on Dexterous Manipulation:** By validating its approach on dexterous in-hand object rotation—a notoriously challenging task—GET-Zero showcases its potential for high-precision, low-level control across diverse multi-fingered robot hands. This is an area where unified action spaces are often insufficient, and GET-Zero provides a compelling alternative.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** GET-Zero opens up new avenues for research, including exploring more complex self-modeling tasks (e.g., inverse kinematics, dynamics prediction), integrating robot design optimization directly into the learning loop, or extending the principles to multi-task and multi-modal settings for robots with vastly different morphologies (e.g., locomotion and manipulation with a single network).

In conclusion, GET-Zero offers a powerful and practical solution for zero-shot embodiment generalization, pushing the boundaries of what is possible in learning versatile and adaptive robotic control policies. Its focus on structural priors and a clever training methodology positions it as a significant contribution to the robotics community, fostering knowledge sharing and more resilient robot designs.
