# F4R: Failure-Driven Recognition, Reconstruction, Refinement, and Redeployment for Continual Robot Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> F4R (Failure for Rising) is a closed real-to-sim-to-real loop that improves vision-language-action manipulation policies using their own real-world failures. A VLM agent diagnoses each failure. The failed scene is then rebuilt as an interactive Gaussian-splat and mesh simulation, and randomization is applied only around the diagnosed causes. The policy is refined with MimicGen sim-real co-training and then PPO before redeployment. Without any new real demonstrations, it reaches 93.75% in-distribution and 90.0% OOD success.

## Problem

- Collecting real-world corrective demonstrations for each new VLA failure is costly, slow and potentially unsafe, and it cannot scale to cover rare long-tail deployment conditions.
- Existing real-to-sim reconstruction pipelines pick scenes without regard to the policy's observed failures, so the added diversity is not focused on the conditions where the deployed policy is weakest.
- Sim-real co-training and VLA reinforcement learning methods optimize over fixed or broadly randomized distributions instead of letting deployment failures decide where refinement effort goes.

## Method

- A ==VLM diagnostic agent== reads multi-view ==temporal evidence boards== and finds the earliest failed subtask. It outputs a structured record of failure time, objects, failure type, scene factors, evidence and confidence.
- ==Failure-case reconstruction== combines a one-time smartphone ==3D Gaussian Splatting== capture of the workspace with object assets scanned by the wrist camera (==TSDF==, ShapeR, Hunyuan3D PBR, P3-SAM articulation), assembled into Isaac Sim.
- ==Failure-aware domain randomization== varies only the diagnosed variables. It feeds Stage I MimicGen ==sim-real co-training== and Stage II ==PPO==, which keeps an auxiliary ==SFT loss== on real data, followed by iterative redeployment in **F4R**.

## Results

- On **four** real UR5 tasks with a pi0.5 policy, F4R reaches **93.75%** ID and **90.0%** OOD success. That beats budget-matched Targeted BC (**71.25%** OOD) and RLinf-Co (**77.5%** OOD).
- Ablations: swapping failure diagnosis for broad randomization drops OOD to **77.5%**. Co-training alone gives **83.75%** OOD, while RL without co-training reaches only **32.5%** OOD.
- Failure diagnosis is correct **86.25%** of the time on the first attempt and **92.5%** within two. Sim and real success correlate at **Pearson r=0.7625**, and **three** rounds raise Stack Bowls from **30%** to **95%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Letting observed deployment failures decide what to simulate beats broad domain randomization. At a matched budget, focusing the training distribution matters more than adding raw simulated diversity.
> - Sparse-reward RL alone rarely finds success in severe failure regions. Demonstration-based co-training has to seed the corrective behavior first, and RL then polishes closed-loop execution.
> - Keeping real-data supervision during simulation refinement mainly prevents forgetting of in-distribution skills. The failure-targeted simulation data drives most of the out-of-distribution gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Current robot policies can fail when real-world conditions differ from their demonstrations. Collecting a new corrective demonstration for every rare failure is costly and can be risky. The authors propose treating a failure as a reusable training case. The system diagnoses what went wrong, recreates the relevant conditions in simulation, and trains against that case and nearby variations. It then redeploys the policy and repeats. The authors call this F4R and frame it as failure-directed simulation and policy improvement, not simply adding more simulated data. On a block-in-drawer task, the policy's success rate under deployment failure conditions rose from 0% to 85%.

Success rates for Base, Targeted BC, RLinf-Co, and F4R on Pick Fruits, Place Cup on Coaster, Stack Bowls, and Place Block in Drawer, under ID and OOD conditions, with averages. On Place Block in Drawer OOD, Base is 0% and F4R is 85%.

### 2. Turning failure into practice

**Diagnosis.** A task-specific checker first separates failed from successful rollouts. A diagnostic agent then reviews synchronized wrist-camera and fixed external-camera evidence in temporal order. It identifies:

- the earliest visually supported failed subtask
- the relevant objects
- the possible failure type and scene factors
- the supporting evidence

F4R uses this record to decide what to reconstruct and which conditions to vary.

**Reconstruction.** A one-time smartphone capture supplies the shared static workspace. This capture is the only human-assisted stage of F4R. The robot gathers multi-view RGB-D observations of task-relevant objects. The reconstructed environment combines geometry for physical simulation with an aligned appearance representation for rendering.

**Failure-aware randomization.** Training varies the diagnosed, relevant conditions around the reconstructed case and holds unrelated conditions fixed. This keeps the training distribution centered on the observed failure.

**Refinement in two stages.** First, successful historical real-world trajectories seed simulated demonstrations adapted to the randomized scenes. Successful simulated rollouts are then co-trained with the original real-world data. Second, reinforcement learning runs in the same failure-centered simulation distribution, using the task's original reward. Real-data supervision helps preserve existing capabilities. Redeploying the updated policy yields new failures for later cycles.

The proposed F4R workflow in four stages: (A) failure analysis of real-world rollouts; (B) scene and object reconstruction; (C) generated trajectories, SFT, and parallel RL training; (D) recovery, new failure discovery, and return to deployment.

### 3. What the evidence tests

The main evaluation used the π₀.₅ policy on four tabletop tasks. Each task has a binary success criterion. Each task and setting was evaluated in 20 real-world trials. ID configurations are covered by the original data-collection distribution. OOD configurations are deployment-derived failure conditions absent from that distribution.

**Comparison with Targeted BC.** Across the four tasks, F4R averages 93.75% ID and 90.0% OOD success. The budget-matched Targeted Behavior Cloning (BC) baseline averages 91.25% ID and 71.25% OOD, an 18.75-point OOD gap. Targeted BC receives one hour of real-world data collection. F4R's matched preparation budget includes reconstruction and simulated experience instead of additional real-world corrective demonstrations. F4R still involves real data and people. It retains the original real-world data and seeds simulation with successful real trajectories. A human also remains available for hardware safety.

**Stage ablation.** Co-training without the RL stage reaches 90.0% ID and 83.75% OOD. Removing co-training and leaving RL to learn from sparse task-success rewards reaches 76.25% ID and 32.5% OOD. This supports the authors' account that simulated corrective demonstrations provide a useful starting point and that RL adds further improvement. It does not show that RL alone can never learn these behaviors.

**Diagnosis accuracy.** Diagnosis is imperfect. On 80 manually annotated failure trajectories, the system jointly predicted stage and cause correctly in 86.25% of cases on the first attempt and 92.5% within two attempts.

### 4. A loop that can continue

Redeployment allowed further refinement. On Stack Bowls, success rose from 30% for the base policy to 80%, 90%, and 95% over successive cycles. On Pick Fruits, it rose from 40% to 100% after the first cycle and stayed there for two more. These results come from two tasks, which improved in different patterns.

The contribution is a loop in which a diagnosed failure becomes a reusable simulation case and later failures direct the next update. The evidence covers predominantly rigid-object tabletop manipulation. Deformable objects, dynamic scenes, mobile manipulation, and long-horizon assembly are not validated.
