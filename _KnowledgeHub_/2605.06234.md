# RobotEQ: Transitioning from Passive Intelligence to Active Intelligence in Embodied AI

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tongji University and collaborators introduced "active intelligence," an embodied AI capability for unguided robots to understand and adhere to social norms, differentiating it from task-driven "passive intelligence." They developed RobotEQ, the first benchmark for this concept, comprising a dataset of 1,900 robot-view images and thousands of questions, revealing that state-of-the-art vision-language models like GPT-5.5 achieved a Macro-F1 of 66.45% but still struggle with nuanced social understanding, though Retrieval-Augmented Generation improved open-source models by up to 4.89 Macro-F1.

## Problem

- Current embodied AI systems predominantly rely on "passive intelligence," executing tasks based solely on explicit user instructions, which is insufficient for complex social integration.
- Robots lack an intrinsic ability to comprehend and adhere to social norms in diverse, unguided embodied scenarios, making it impractical for humans to define all permissible and prohibited actions.
- There is no standardized benchmark to systematically evaluate a robot's capacity for autonomous social compliance in the absence of explicit commands.

## Method

- The paper introduces "active intelligence," defining it as the ability of unguided AI to understand and adhere to social norms within embodied scenarios.
- **RobotEQ**, the first benchmark for active intelligence, was developed, comprising a comprehensive dataset (**RobotEQ**-Data) of robot-view images and associated action judgment and spatial grounding questions.
- A multi-stage data construction pipeline involving LLMs for scenario generation, image creation, and automated/human-verified annotation ensured the quality and diversity of the benchmark.

## Results

- Closed-source VLMs achieved the strongest performance on action judgment, with GPT-5.5 reaching a Macro-F1 of **66.45%**, yet all models remained significantly below human benchmarks across various active intelligence dimensions.
- Spatial grounding performance was notably lower, with Macro-F1 scores ranging from approximately **48%** to **59%**, indicating difficulties in precisely integrating visual perception and reasoning for socially appropriate spatial decisions.
- RAG improved the Macro-F1 performance of open-source models by 1.51 to **4.89** percentage points on action judgment, demonstrating its effectiveness in boosting social reasoning, while Chain-of-Thought prompting showed inconsistent results across different VLM architectures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models (VLMs) show significant limitations in active intelligence, particularly in fine-grained spatial reasoning and nuanced social understanding required for social compliance.
> - VLMs exhibit common failure patterns, including overly aggressive or cautious behavior, a lack of basic social knowledge (e.g., emotional awareness), and errors in spatial grounding that neglect social consequences.
> - Lightweight prompting strategies like Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), which incorporate external social norm knowledge, can effectively enhance active intelligence performance, especially for open-source models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) involves intelligent agents that perceive, reason, and act within physical environments. Prior research in this field has largely concentrated on developing agents capable of completing tasks based on explicit user instructions. This paradigm, which the authors refer to as "passive intelligence," focuses on whether robots can successfully execute predefined commands, such as navigation or object manipulation.

However, the paper identifies a significant gap as robots become more integrated into human society. In real-world interactions, it is not practical for humans to articulate every permissible and prohibited action for all potential scenarios. This necessitates a shift towards what the authors term "active intelligence," where robots autonomously understand and adhere to social norms and acceptable behaviors without explicit directives.

In the broader context of social intelligence research, existing efforts often aim to equip agents with the capacity to perceive, understand, and reason about human affect, behavior, and cognition. Examples include multimodal sentiment analysis, emotion recognition (e.g., CMU-MOSI, CMU-MOSEI), and understanding complex social situations and mental states (e.g., Social-IQ, Human Behavior Atlas). The current work distinguishes itself from these by focusing not on the interpretation of human internal states, but on the prescriptive question of *what actions a robot should or should not perform* in socially situated embodied scenarios. Thus, RobotEQ introduces a novel dimension to embodied AI, complementing existing work by addressing the unguided, socially aware behavior of robotic agents. This work aims to systematic explore the capacity of embodied AI to exhibit such autonomous social compliance, an area previously unaddressed by systematic benchmarks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing integration of robots into human society and the inherent limitations of current embodied AI systems. Existing systems primarily operate under "passive intelligence," meaning they require explicit user instructions to perform tasks. This reliance on direct commands presents several challenges for real-world deployment:
- **Unrealistic Expectations:** It is impractical to expect humans to provide explicit instructions for every conceivable scenario a robot might encounter, especially concerning social appropriateness.
- **Lack of Social Awareness:** Robots, without a built-in understanding of social norms, may perform tasks efficiently but in a manner that is socially awkward, intrusive, or even harmful, potentially hindering their acceptance and effectiveness in human environments.

To address these limitations, the paper introduces the concept of "active intelligence." This refers to the ability of an embodied agent to infer and adhere to socially acceptable behaviors, even in the absence of explicit commands. It involves understanding when to act, how to act, and which actions are permissible or prohibited based on the social context.

The key objectives of this work are:
1. **To define and systematically explore active intelligence:** As a nascent concept, active intelligence has not been thoroughly investigated. This paper aims to establish a foundational framework for its study.
2. **To create the first benchmark for active intelligence (RobotEQ):** The absence of a standardized evaluation tool hinders progress in this domain. RobotEQ is designed to assess whether current AI models can comprehend and adhere to social norms in embodied scenarios.
3. **To construct a dedicated dataset (RobotEQ-Data):** This involves developing a dataset of robot-view images representing diverse embodied scenarios, accompanied by detailed human annotations for judging action appropriateness and spatial relevance.
4. **To establish an evaluation framework (RobotEQ-Bench):** This framework is intended to benchmark the performance of state-of-the-art vision-language models (VLMs) on active intelligence tasks.
5. **To provide insights for future research:** Through comprehensive evaluation and error analysis, the research seeks to identify the limitations of current models and suggest strategies for enhancing active intelligence capabilities, thereby facilitating the transition of robotics from user-guided manipulation to socially compliant autonomy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **RobotEQ**, a benchmark designed to evaluate active intelligence in embodied AI. The methodology encompasses the construction of a novel dataset, **RobotEQ-Data**, and an evaluation framework, **RobotEQ-Bench**.

#### 3.1. RobotEQ-Data Construction Pipeline

The dataset construction follows a multi-stage pipeline as depicted in Figure 2:

- **Scenario Design (Section 3.1, Appendix A):**
 - **Taxonomy Development:** A hierarchical taxonomy was established, comprising 10 major scenario categories (e.g., Retail, Hospitality & Consumer Services; Healthcare, Caregiving & Rehabilitation; Public Spaces & Urban Infrastructure) and 56 fine-grained subcategories, drawing from surveys on socially aware robot navigation.
 - **Scenario Generation:** Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, were used to generate diverse textual scenarios for each subcategory. This involved a "beam-merge" strategy: 10 independent generation requests per subcategory, each producing at least 10 candidate scenarios, followed by deduplication using an expert model (GPT-5.4) to ensure diversity. Each scenario includes a title, a detailed description, and a rationale explaining why active intelligence is required.

- **Image Generation (Section 3.2, Appendix B):**
 - **Visual Prompt Synthesis:** LLMs (Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview) translated textual scenario descriptions into detailed visual prompts, specifying first-person robot viewpoint, spatial layout, key objects, human poses, and salient social cues.
 - **Image Generation:** These visual prompts were fed into an image generation model (Gemini-3-Pro-Image-Preview) to produce 1,894 robot-view images.
 - **Automated Quality Review:** An expert model (Doubao-Seedream) assessed generated images against seven quality criteria (e.g., scenario faithfulness, factual correctness, social cue visibility). It provided structured revision suggestions for images failing certain criteria.
 - **Image Revision:** Images were iteratively refined using the Gemini-3-Pro-Image-Preview model based on these automated suggestions.
 - **Human Verification:** Human annotators conducted a final quality check, comparing original and edited images to ensure realism, social plausibility, and fidelity to the intended scenario, providing further revision instructions if necessary.

- **Action Judgment Data (Section 3.3, Appendix C, D):**
 - **Candidate Action Generation:** For each validated image, LLMs generated a pool of approximately 10 candidate actions (5 proper, 5 improper) that were physically executable and covered various facets of active intelligence.
 - **Human Annotation:** Seven qualified undergraduate annotators (selected after a pilot study and training on labels: "proper," "improper," or "invalid") independently labeled each candidate action. The final label for each action was determined by majority vote, with invalid actions being excluded.

- **Spatial Grounding Data (Section 3.4, Appendix E):**
 - **Manual Questions Generation:** Five annotators proposed potential spatial grounding questions based purely on visual evidence for each image.
 - **Two-Stage Question and Image Editing:**
 - **Stage 1:** An LLM (GPT-5.4) converted human-proposed topics into standardized spatial grounding question titles and image editing instructions, specifying four labeled regions (A, B, C, D) overlaid on the original image. At least one region corresponded to a correct answer, and incorrect options were placed at plausible but suboptimal locations.
 - **Stage 2:** An image editing model (Gemini-3-Pro-Image-Preview) applied these instructions to create images with annotated regions.
 - **Human Annotation:** Seven annotators (from the action judgment task) labeled each spatial grounding question. They independently judged each option (A, B, C, D, Invalid), and options selected by a majority formed the final ground truth answer set, allowing for multiple correct regions. Questions deemed "Invalid" by majority were excluded.

#### 3.2. RobotEQ-Bench Evaluation Protocol (Section 4)

- **Action Judgment:** Evaluated as a binary classification task. The primary metric is Macro-F1 score, calculated by averaging F1 scores for "proper" and "improper" categories.
- **Spatial Grounding:** Evaluated as a multiple-choice task. Metrics include Accuracy (exact match of predicted set to ground truth), Macro-F1 (average per-option F1 scores), and Hit Rate (at least one correct option predicted).

#### 3.3. Benchmarking Candidates (Section 4, Appendix G)

The benchmark evaluates 28 vision-language models categorized into:
- **Closed-source VLMs:** Accessed via official APIs (e.g., GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.6/4.7, Gemini 2.5 Pro/3.1 Pro Preview).
- **Open-source general-purpose VLMs:** Deployed locally (e.g., LLaVA-OneVision, Qwen3-VL, Gemma-3, Llama-3.2-Vision).
- **Open-source task-specialized VLMs:** Optimized for fine-grained visual tasks like GUI grounding or OCR (e.g., GUI-Actor, GroundNext, Nanonets-OCR2).

#### 3.4. Experiment Settings (Appendix G.5)

Local models were deployed on a server with three NVIDIA L40 GPUs, using vLLM or HuggingFace Transformers for inference. Images were resized to a maximum of 768 pixels on the longest dimension. Decoding parameters included a temperature of 0 (greedy sampling), max_tokens of 1024 (standard/RAG) or 2048 (CoT), batch size of 16, and FP16 precision. Similar settings were applied for closed-source API models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Dataset Statistics (Section 3.5)

RobotEQ-Data comprises 1,894 unique scenarios, categorized into 10 major and 56 subcategories. It includes 4,944 action judgment questions and 1,157 spatial grounding questions. The largest scenario category is "Retail, Hospitality & Consumer Services" (25.8%). The dataset also structures active intelligence across eight evaluation dimensions, with "Non-verbal Signal Recognition" (27.2%) and "Proxemics & Spatial Norms" (19.3%) being the most frequent.

#### 4.2. Action Judgment (Section 5.1, Table 1, Figure 4)

- **Overall Performance:** Closed-source models generally achieved higher Macro-F1 scores. GPT-5.5 obtained the highest Macro-F1 of 68.92%, followed by Claude Opus 4.6 (66.11%). However, these scores remain notably below human performance.
- **Model Version Impact:** Performance did not consistently improve with newer model versions; for instance, Claude Opus 4.7 slightly underperformed 4.6, and Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview was below 2.5 Pro. This suggests that general model upgrades do not automatically translate to better embodied social judgment.
- **Dimension-Level Performance:** GPT-5.5 performed closest to human levels across the eight active intelligence dimensions, showing strong results on "Culture-Specific Norms" (74.00% Macro-F1 vs. 78.98% for humans) and "Resource & Ownership Norms" (71.46% vs. 79.45% for humans). Larger performance gaps were observed in dimensions such as "Contextual Volume & Behavioural Restraint," "Timing & Interruption Norms," and certain aspects of "Resource & Ownership Norms," which require understanding implicit, situation-dependent social knowledge.

#### 4.3. Spatial Grounding (Section 5.2, Figure 5, Table 7)

- **Performance Range:** Performance differences were narrower compared to action judgment, with Macro-F1 scores ranging from approximately 46% to 64%. Closed-source models did not exhibit a distinct advantage over open-source models.
- **Hit Rate vs. Accuracy:** Several open-source task-specialized VLMs (e.g., GUI-Actor, Gemma-3) achieved high Hit Rates (above 90%), indicating their ability to identify at least one correct spatial region. However, Accuracy (exact match) remained significantly lower for most models, falling far short of human performance (Human Accuracy: 66.49%). This suggests a limitation in precisely integrating visual perception with reasoning for robust spatial social compliance.

#### 4.4. Error Analysis (Section 5.3, Figure 6)

An analysis of GPT-5.5 errors identified four recurring patterns:
- **Overly Aggressive Behavior:** Models prioritized task completion, sometimes leading to inappropriate interventions (e.g., interrupting a focused student for a non-urgent delivery).
- **Overly Cautious Behavior:** Models exhibited excessive caution, misinterpreting normal human responses (e.g., stopping rehabilitation assistance due to a patient's normal pain expressions during training).
- **Lack of Basic Social Knowledge:** Models failed in emotionally sensitive social interactions, opting for passive silence when warmth or intervention was socially expected (e.g., during counseling).
- **Spatial Grounding Errors:** Models made spatially incorrect decisions, often selecting paths or regions without fully considering the social consequences of such spatial choices (e.g., choosing a "safest" path that would nevertheless expose the robot to a photographer's lens or intrude on a blind person's route).

These errors collectively suggest that current models struggle with balancing explicit task goals, human states, social norms, and their spatial implications in a unified decision-making process.

#### 4.5. Prompting Strategies for Improvement (Section 5.4, Table 2)

The research investigated two prompting strategies for the action judgment task:
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:** Guiding models through reasoning steps (scene analysis, demand recognition, role reflection, action assessment) showed a modest Macro-F1 improvement for GPT-5.5 (from 68.92% to 69.46%) but was less consistent or even detrimental for some open-source models.
- **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):** Incorporating a role-specific social norm knowledge base proved more consistently beneficial. All five tested open-source models showed Macro-F1 gains (2.51 to 5.05 percentage points). For closed-source models, RAG had a modest effect on stronger models (GPT-5.5, Claude 4.6) but led to substantial improvement for models with weaker baseline social reasoning (e.g., Doubao improved from 52.58% to 62.23%). Both strategies increased resource consumption due to longer input lengths. RAG appears to be a simple and effective method for improving active intelligence, particularly for models with limited implicit social knowledge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces "active intelligence" as a critical capability for embodied AI, moving beyond task execution based on explicit commands to include autonomous adherence to social norms. This concept addresses a foundational challenge for robots integrating into human society.

The **RobotEQ benchmark** is presented as the first systematic tool for evaluating active intelligence. It comprises **RobotEQ-Data**, a diverse dataset of 1,894 robot-view images across 10 categories and 56 subcategories, with 4,944 action judgment questions and 1,157 spatial grounding questions. The dataset's comprehensive annotation provides fine-grained insights into socially appropriate robot behaviors.

The evaluation conducted using **RobotEQ-Bench** reveals that state-of-the-art vision-language models currently exhibit significant limitations in active intelligence, particularly in spatial grounding tasks and understanding implicit social cues. The error analysis identifies recurring failure patterns, such as overly aggressive or cautious behavior, lack of social experience in sensitive interactions, and specific spatial grounding errors. These findings highlight the need for further research to enable robust social reasoning in embodied agents.

The exploration of prompting strategies, especially Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), demonstrates a practical approach to enhancing active intelligence by incorporating external social norm knowledge. This suggests that models can benefit from explicit normative context, particularly those with weaker inherent social reasoning capabilities.

Overall, RobotEQ is positioned to facilitate the transition of embodied AI from user-guided "passive intelligence" to socially aware "active intelligence." By providing a systematic evaluation framework and identifying current model deficiencies, this work can guide future research and development towards creating more adaptable, socially compliant, and ultimately more effective robotic systems for real-world human environments.
