# XSkill: Cross Embodiment Skill Discovery

> [!summary] Summary
> XSkill enables robots to acquire complex manipulation skills from unlabeled, unaligned human demonstration videos by discovering shared skill representations across different embodiments. This framework allows for one-shot learning of novel tasks through skill composition, achieving up to 70.2% success in simulation and 60% in real-world four-subtask compositions with human video prompts.

## Problem

- Enabling robots to acquire complex skills from readily available human demonstration videos despite significant differences in physical embodiment.
- Developing methods to decompose long-horizon tasks into reusable sub-skills directly from unlabeled human videos without requiring explicit task labels or robot-specific data for skill discovery.
- Allowing robots to generalize to unseen task compositions by leveraging a single human prompt video, without needing additional robot demonstrations or costly reinforcement learning.

## Method

- **XSkill**'s ==self-supervised learning== framework discovers a set of shared ==skill prototypes== from unlabeled and unaligned human and robot manipulation videos, creating a ==cross-embodiment skill representation space==.
- A ==skill-conditioned diffusion policy== is trained on robot teleoperation data to translate these abstract skill representations into concrete robot actions.
- A ==Skill Alignment Transformer (SAT)== enables the robot to compose learned skills for unseen tasks by adaptively aligning its execution with a single human prompt video, accounting for speed differences and potential failures.

## Results

- XSkill successfully learns a disentangled representation where skills executed by human and robot for the same object manipulation cluster together, confirming cross-embodiment alignment.
- The framework achieves **70.2%** success in simulation and **60%** success in real-world four-subtask compositions for unseen tasks, outperforming baselines when using cross-embodiment prompts.
- Ablation studies confirm that both shared skill prototypes (via prototype loss) and the Skill Alignment Transformer (SAT) are critical for robust cross-embodiment generalization and adaptive task execution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A shared, morphology-invariant skill representation space can be learned from unaligned human and robot videos by focusing on action effects rather than specific embodiments.
> - Decomposing complex tasks into reusable skill prototypes is essential for achieving cross-embodiment transfer and enabling generalization to novel task compositions.
> - An adaptive skill alignment mechanism is crucial for robust execution of human-demonstrated sequences, allowing the robot to handle variations in demonstration speed and recover from execution failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a crucial challenge in robot learning, specifically within the domain of **imitation learning (IL)**: enabling robots to acquire complex skills from easily accessible human demonstrations despite significant differences in physical embodiment. The broader research landscape in robot learning has long sought methods to teach robots new behaviors without laborious manual programming or extensive robot-specific data collection, which can be prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.

Existing imitation learning approaches often fall into several categories:
- **Direct Imitation Learning:** Many methods directly map observations to actions. However, these often struggle with long-horizon tasks, require large amounts of robot-specific data, and face significant domain gaps when transferring from human to robot. They also typically lack mechanisms for decomposing complex tasks into reusable sub-skills.
- **Single-Embodiment Skill Discovery:** A separate line of work focuses on discovering reusable "skills" or "options" from robot demonstrations. While effective for structuring robot behaviors, these methods are limited by requiring robot data for skill discovery, which contradicts the goal of leveraging ubiquitous human videos. They also often necessitate access to physical state information or action trajectories, which are not available in raw human videos.
- **Learning from Human Videos:** Some research has explored using human videos, often by extracting reward functions or state representations, but many of these approaches still rely on **reinforcement learning (RL)** in the loop to learn the policy. RL is notoriously sample-inefficient and often difficult to deploy in the real world, limiting scalability. Other methods might require explicit task labels or correspondence between human and robot data during training, adding a significant annotation burden.

XSkill positions itself by explicitly tackling the "cross-embodiment skill discovery" problem, which aims to bridge these gaps. It differentiates itself by:
- **Bridging the Embodiment Gap:** Unlike single-embodiment skill discovery, XSkill learns a shared skill representation space directly from *unaligned* human and robot videos, making it robust to morphological and kinematic differences.
- **Decomposition and Composition for Generalization:** It moves beyond direct imitation by decomposing long-horizon tasks into reusable "skill prototypes," which can then be composed to achieve novel tasks. This contrasts with methods that learn a monolithic policy for an entire task.
- **One-Shot Generalization from Human Prompts:** XSkill enables robots to perform unseen tasks by observing a *single* human prompt video, without requiring additional robot demonstrations or explicit task labels for new compositions. This is a significant step towards more intuitive and scalable robot programming.
- **Avoiding RL-in-the-Loop:** By focusing on imitation learning combined with learned representations, XSkill avoids the high sample complexity and instability often associated with RL, making it more practical for real-world deployment.

In essence, XSkill advances the field by offering a framework that synergistically combines self-supervised representation learning, skill discovery, and a robust policy for cross-embodiment skill transfer and composition, directly addressing key limitations of prior art and paving the way for more human-centric robot learning paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind XSkill is to enable robots to learn complex manipulation skills efficiently and scalably by leveraging readily available human demonstration videos. The authors identify three critical capabilities for a successful imitation learning algorithm from human demonstration:
1. **Discover:** The ability to decompose a demonstrated task into sub-tasks and identify common, reusable skills.
2. **Transfer:** The capacity to map observed skills from a human demonstrator's embodiment to the robot's own, often very different, embodiment.
3. **Compose:** The flexibility to perform novel compositions of learned skills to accomplish new tasks that were not explicitly demonstrated in their entirety.

XSkill's key objectives are built upon these capabilities:
- **To formulate and solve the task of "Cross-Embodiment Skill Discovery":** This involves identifying skills that are simultaneously robust to embodiment differences, transferable to robots, and composable for novel tasks.
- **To develop a self-supervised representation learning algorithm:** This algorithm must be capable of discovering a set of shared "skill prototypes" from unlabeled and unaligned human and robot manipulation videos. The goal is for video clips sharing similar *action effects* (i.e., the same skill) to have closer feature distances, regardless of the performer's embodiment.
- **To implement a skill-conditioned visuomotor policy:** This policy should effectively translate the discovered skill representations into concrete robot actions, facilitating the transfer of human skills to the robot's action space. The choice of a diffusion policy is motivated by its state-of-the-art performance and stability with limited data.
- **To create a robust skill composition framework:** This framework should allow robots to accomplish unseen tasks, specified by a single human prompt video, by sequencing the learned skills. A key challenge here is to robustly align the human-demonstrated skill sequence with the robot's execution, even in the presence of execution failures or speed mismatches.
- **To demonstrate the practicality and scalability of the approach:** This includes evaluating XSkill in both simulated and real-world environments and showing that it reduces reliance on costly robot-specific demonstrations, making robot learning more accessible and cost-effective.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to create a more general and scalable imitation learning framework that can bridge the human-robot embodiment gap and enable intuitive, one-shot robot programming from human video demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

XSkill's framework is structured into three distinct phases: Discover, Transfer, and Compose, each addressing a specific aspect of cross-embodiment skill acquisition.

#### 3.1. Discover: Learning Shared Skill Prototypes

This phase focuses on learning a cross-embodiment skill representation space (Z) and identifying K common learnable skill prototypes purely from unlabeled human and robot manipulation videos ($D_h$ and $D_r$). The goal is for the learned representations to be invariant to embodiment while capturing the underlying skill.
- **Skill Representations:** Video clips ($v_{ij}$) are sampled from human and robot videos and encoded into skill representations ($z_{ij}$) using a temporal skill encoder ($f_{temporal}$). This encoder comprises a vision backbone (3-layer CNN + MLP) and a transformer encoder, augmented with a learnable representation token to capture motion.
- **Skills as Prototypes & Soft-Assignment Clustering:** XSkill maps these continuous skill representations to a set of discrete, learnable skill prototypes ($c_k$). This is achieved via a self-supervised learning framework similar to SwAV. The prototypes are implemented as a normalized linear layer ($f_{prototype}$). The system predicts the probability ($p_{ij}$) of a skill being executed by projecting $z_{ij}$ onto these prototypes. A target distribution ($q_{ij}$) is obtained from an augmented version of the same video clip using the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm.
- **Learning Aligned Skill Representation with Sinkhorn-Knopp:** To ensure prototypes are shared across embodiments and prevent representation space segmentation, XSkill uses a specific training strategy. Batches are sampled from a single embodiment, and Sinkhorn-Knopp clustering is applied to regularize the target probabilities. This entropy-regularized clustering problem forces the skill representations from different embodiments to utilize the entire embedding space, thereby aligning them based on the effect of the skill rather than the agent's appearance.
- **Time Contrastive Learning:** An InfoNCE loss ($L_{tcn}$) is applied to ensure temporal coherence: skill prototype probabilities should be similar for clips close in time and dissimilar for those far apart within a demonstration, helping encapsulate the temporal effects of skills. The total discovery loss is $L_{prototype} + L_{tcn}$.

#### 3.2. Transfer: Skill-Conditioned Imitation Learning

Once the cross-embodiment skill representations ($z \in Z$) and prototypes are learned, this phase focuses on translating these abstract skills into concrete robot actions.
- **Skill-Conditioned Diffusion Policy:** A skill-conditioned visuomotor policy, $P(a|s, z)$, is trained using the robot teleoperation dataset ($D_r$). The authors specifically choose a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM)-based diffusion policy due to its ability to represent multimodal action distributions, stability, and effectiveness with small datasets. The policy takes the robot's current state ($s$, including proprioception and visual observation) and the identified skill representation ($z$) as input to predict an action sequence. The skill representation $z_t$ for policy training is derived from the robot's own video clips using the *already trained* $f_{temporal}$ encoder.

#### 3.3. Compose: Performing Unseen Tasks from One-Shot Human Prompt Video

This final phase enables the robot to execute novel tasks defined by a single human prompt video ($\tau^h_{prompt}$) by composing the learned skills.
- **Skill Identification from Prompt:** The human prompt video is first mapped into a sequence of skill representations, $\tilde{z} = \{z_t\}$, using the learned $f_{temporal}$ encoder. This sequence acts as a high-level task plan.
- **Skill Alignment Transformer (SAT):** To address issues like execution speed mismatches between human and robot, or unexpected failures during execution, a Skill Alignment Transformer (SAT), denoted as $\phi(z_t|o_t, \tilde{z})$, is introduced. SAT takes the robot's current visual observation ($o_t$) and the human-demonstrated skill sequence ($\tilde{z}$) as input. It aligns the robot's progress with the intended task plan by predicting which skill in the sequence should be executed next. This allows the robot to adaptively skip completed skills or re-attempt failed ones. SAT is trained using robot teleoperation data, minimizing the mean square error between its predicted skill and the actual skill at a given time step.
- **Execution:** During inference, the identified skill sequence from the human prompt is fed into SAT, which continuously provides the current relevant skill $z_t$. This $z_t$ is then passed to the skill-conditioned diffusion policy $P(a|s, z_t)$ to generate robot actions, enabling sequential and robust execution of the task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

XSkill's performance was rigorously evaluated in both a simulated Franka Kitchen environment and a newly introduced real-world Kitchen benchmark, using various baselines including goal-conditioned diffusion policies and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. XSkill Can Learn Cross-Embodiment Skill Representation

- **Qualitative Evidence (t-SNE):** The t-SNE visualization (Fig. 5a) effectively demonstrates that XSkill learns a disentangled representation where skills executed by different embodiments (human vs. robot) for the *same object manipulation* cluster together, distinct from other skills. This confirms the successful alignment of skill representations across embodiments.
- **Quantitative Evidence (Skill Prototype Distribution):** Visualization of skill prototype distribution over time (Fig. 5b) shows that despite significant differences in execution speed (human ~2x faster), XSkill successfully decomposes the task into meaningful and aligned skill prototypes for both in-contact manipulations and transitions, across human and robot demonstrations.
- **Performance Impact:** The ability to learn morphology-invariant representations means XSkill's performance drop when using cross-embodiment prompts, compared to same-embodiment prompts, is only about 5% (Table 2, Seen Tasks), showcasing its effectiveness in bridging the embodiment gap.

#### 4.2. XSkill Generalizes the Imitation Policy to Unseen Tasks

- **Superior Generalization:** XSkill significantly outperforms all baselines on unseen tasks with cross-embodiment prompts. In simulation, it achieves 70.2% success (Table 1), while in the real world, it reaches 60% success for four-subtask compositions (Table 2). This demonstrates its capability to decompose novel, long-horizon tasks into a sequence of executable skill abstractions and recompose them.
- **One-Shot Imitation:** The framework enables one-shot imitation learning from a single human prompt video, validating its practical utility for rapid task deployment.
- **Limitations in Real-World Generalization:** A slight performance drop in the real world is noted, attributed to novel transition dynamics not present in the robot teleoperation training data. For example, the robot struggles with "grasping cloth followed by closing drawer" if this specific transition was not in the robot dataset, highlighting the need for diverse robot data for robust generalization.

#### 4.3. Skill Prototypes are Essential for Cross-Embodiment Learning

- **Ablation Study (XSkill w.o proto. loss):** Removing the prototype loss ($L_{prototype}$) significantly degrades performance, especially with increasing cross-embodiment speed differences (Table 1). "XSkill w.o proto. loss" drops from 80.1% (same embodiment) to 3.75% (cross-embodiment, 1.5x speed), whereas full XSkill maintains 70.2%. This confirms that shared skill prototypes are crucial for learning morphology-invariant and speed-robust representations.

#### 4.4. Skill Alignment Transformer (SAT) Aligns Skills Based on Task Progress

- **Ablation Study (XSkill w. NN-composition):** XSkill (with SAT) dramatically outperforms "XSkill w. NN-composition" by over 50% with cross-embodiment prompts (Table 1). The nearest-neighbor composition approach fails significantly when human demonstration speed is faster, suggesting its reliance on precise visual state matching.
- **Robustness to Speed and Failures:** SAT enables adaptive adjustment of skills based on the robot's current state and task progress (Fig. 6). This provides robustness against variations in demonstration speed and allows the robot to re-plan in response to perturbations (e.g., manually turning off a light, Fig. 6e-f), ensuring task completion despite deviations.

#### 4.5. Sequential Input Benefits Cross-Embodiment Learning

- **Comparison to TCN:** Unlike GCD Policy with TCN (which uses single-image encodings), XSkill leverages sequential image input. While TCN partially bridges the embodiment gap, it fails to maintain the correct sub-task order, indicating it doesn't fully align representations or understand temporal progression. XSkill's use of temporal encoders for skill representation is critical for effective cross-embodiment transfer and successful task sequencing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

XSkill presents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly for manipulation tasks, by addressing critical bottlenecks that hinder widespread robot deployment. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key areas:

- **Enhanced Accessibility and Scalability of Robot Programming:** By enabling robots to learn complex, long-horizon tasks directly from unstructured, unlabeled human video demonstrations, XSkill drastically reduces the need for expensive and time-consuming robot teleoperation or manual programming. Human videos are abundant and can be generated by non-experts, making robot skill acquisition far more cost-effective and scalable. This has the potential to democratize robot programming beyond expert operators.

- **Robust Cross-Embodiment Skill Transfer:** The framework's ability to learn morphology-invariant skill representations and effectively transfer them across vastly different embodiments (human to robot) is a breakthrough. This directly tackles one of the most challenging problems in imitation learning, moving beyond the limitations of same-embodiment or domain-specific solutions.

- **Improved Generalization and Adaptability:** XSkill's hierarchical approach, involving skill discovery, transfer, and robust composition, allows robots to generalize to unseen task compositions. The Skill Alignment Transformer (SAT) further enhances robustness by enabling adaptive re-planning in response to execution failures or discrepancies in demonstration speed. This significantly improves the reliability of learned policies in dynamic and imperfect real-world environments.

- **Foundation for Future Research in Embodied AI:** By formulating and providing a strong baseline for "cross-embodiment skill discovery," XSkill lays a foundation for future exploration in this crucial area. The release of benchmark datasets and code will undoubtedly foster further research into more generalizable, autonomous robot learning systems.

- **Reduced Reliance on Reinforcement Learning:** By successfully implementing a pure imitation learning approach for skill acquisition and composition, XSkill bypasses the sample inefficiency and training complexities often associated with reinforcement learning. This makes the system more practical and efficient for real-world deployment.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge a limitation in the requirement of specifying the number of prototypes (K) as an input, although their ablation shows robustness to this choice. Future work includes investigating the applicability of XSkill in more diverse camera setups and environments (e.g., leveraging "in-the-wild" YouTube videos or multi-environment datasets like BridgeData), moving beyond the current single-laboratory camera setup. Expanding the diversity of robot teleoperation data is also crucial for better generalization to unseen transitions in real-world scenarios.

In conclusion, XSkill represents a pivotal step towards more intuitive, adaptable, and scalable robot learning. Its novel approach to cross-embodiment skill discovery has profound implications for how we teach robots, bringing us closer to a future where robots can readily acquire and perform complex tasks simply by observing human behavior.
