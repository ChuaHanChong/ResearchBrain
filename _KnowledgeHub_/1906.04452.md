# Continual Reinforcement Learning deployed in Real-life using Policy Distillation and Sim2Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Flowers Laboratory (ENSTA ParisTech, INRIA) developed a preliminary framework enabling a robot to continually learn sequential tasks in simulation and deploy them in real-life, using policy distillation to prevent forgetting and domain randomization for Sim2Real transfer. This allows a single, unified policy to perform multiple learned tasks by inferring context from visual input.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents struggle to acquire new skills sequentially in dynamic environments without suffering from catastrophic forgetting of previously learned tasks.
- Training RL policies directly on physical robots is often impractical due to high sample inefficiency, cost, and potential damage, necessitating efficient Sim2Real transfer.
- Real-world robotic applications, such as service robots, require agents to adapt to evolving tasks and contexts over time, a challenge for traditional single-task or multi-task RL approaches.

## Method

- The framework combines State Representation Learning (SRL) to extract compact features from raw observations, improving RL policy training efficiency in simulation.
- Policy distillation is used to transfer knowledge from sequentially trained task-specific 'teacher' policies into a single 'student' policy, effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
- Domain randomization is applied during simulation training to bridge the reality gap, making the learned policies more robust for deployment on physical robots.

## Results

- A single distilled policy successfully learned and performed two sequential 2D navigation tasks (Target Reaching and Target Circling) in simulation.
- The distilled student policy achieved performance comparable to individual teacher policies on both tasks, maintaining near-maximum rewards in simulation.
- Policy distillation effectively transferred learned behaviors from teacher policies to the student with minimal performance loss, closely matching the performance curve of the original RL training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy distillation can effectively aggregate knowledge from multiple teacher policies into a single student policy, allowing it to perform diverse tasks without requiring an explicit task indicator.
> - Learning compact state representations through SRL before RL training can significantly improve sample efficiency and aid in generalization for visual navigation tasks.
> - A combination of simulation-based learning with domain randomization and policy distillation offers a practical path toward deploying continually learning agents in real-life robotic scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of several subfields within artificial intelligence and robotics: Reinforcement Learning (RL), Continual Learning (CL), State Representation Learning (SRL), and Sim2Real Transfer.

Traditional Reinforcement Learning often assumes a static environment where an agent learns a single task or multiple tasks simultaneously by shuffling experience. However, real-world robotic applications frequently require agents to adapt to changing contexts or learn new skills sequentially without forgetting previously acquired knowledge. This challenge is addressed by **Continual Learning (CL)**, which focuses on developing models capable of acquiring new information over time while retaining performance on past tasks, thereby mitigating "catastrophic forgetting." The paper explicitly distinguishes its setting from multi-task RL, where tasks can be experienced concurrently.

To facilitate efficient learning in complex, high-dimensional environments, **State Representation Learning (SRL)** is employed. SRL aims to learn compact, relevant feature representations from raw sensory inputs (like images), which can then serve as input for RL policies. This approach is known to improve sample efficiency and interpretability compared to end-to-end learning from raw observations.

The ultimate goal of deploying learned policies on physical robots introduces the "reality gap," where policies trained in simulation may not transfer effectively to the real world due to discrepancies in physics, perception, or dynamics. **Sim2Real Transfer** techniques, such as domain randomization, are developed to bridge this gap, making simulation-trained policies more robust to real-world variations.

This paper combines these paradigms by presenting a preliminary method for deploying a continually learned RL policy on a real robot. It leverages SRL for efficient policy learning, policy distillation to achieve continual learning in a sequential task setting, and domain randomization for Sim2Real transfer. The approach aims to address the challenge of creating embodied agents that learn incrementally in real-life scenarios, a significant frontier in AI and robotics research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a preliminary framework for teaching a robot to solve tasks presented sequentially in a continual learning scenario, while deployed in a real-life setting. The core challenge is enabling the robot to acquire new skills without forgetting previously learned tasks, a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting.

The motivation behind this objective stems from the limitations of current RL approaches in realistic, real-life applications, particularly those involving service robots. In such scenarios, tasks are not static; they evolve over time, requiring agents to adapt to new contexts or learn entirely new tasks. The paper provides an illustrative example of a gift-wrapping robot that first learns to wrap cuboids and then must learn to wrap spheres while retaining its ability to wrap cuboids. Learning all possible tasks at once is often impractical due to the potential diversity and vast number of tasks. Therefore, continual, incremental learning is presented as a more natural and convenient paradigm.

Specific objectives and motivations include:

- **Addressing Catastrophic Forgetting:** The central aim of continual learning is to ensure that a robot, when learning a new task, maintains its proficiency in past tasks. The paper seeks to achieve this in an RL context.
- **Leveraging State Representation Learning (SRL):** Raw sensory data from real-world environments is often high-dimensional and complex. The motivation for using SRL is to extract compact, efficient, and relevant state representations, which can lead to better sample efficiency and faster policy learning compared to directly processing raw observations.
- **Enabling Real-life Deployment via Sim2Real Transfer:** Training policies directly on physical robots can be costly, time-consuming, and potentially damaging due to the sample inefficiency of RL. The research is motivated by the desire to leverage the efficiency of training in simulation and then successfully transfer these learned policies to real robots. This necessitates addressing the "reality gap" using techniques like domain randomization.
- **Developing a Single, Flexible Policy:** Instead of maintaining separate policies for each task, a key objective is to distill knowledge from sequentially learned task-specific policies into a single, unified policy that can solve all encountered tasks. This avoids the need for an explicit "task indicator" at test time, as the policy should be able to infer the current task from visual cues (e.g., color tags).
- **Sequential Task Learning:** The paper emphasizes a strict continual learning setting where tasks are presented one after another, and previous task environments or data are not immediately available for joint training. This differentiates the work from multi-task settings where experiences might be shuffled.

In summary, the motivation is to advance the state-of-the-art in robotics by developing an RL framework that allows robots to learn and adapt continually in dynamic real-world environments, without suffering from forgetting, and with the practical benefits of simulation-based training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology combines State Representation Learning (SRL) with Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Policy Distillation within a Sim2Real transfer framework for continual learning on a real robot.

#### 3.1. Overall Learning Process

The approach involves a sequential learning process for each task:
1. **Single Task Learning:** For each task $i$, a state representation encoder $E_i$ and a policy $\pi_i$ are learned in simulation.
2. **Distillation Dataset Generation:** After learning $\pi_i$, a distillation dataset $D_{\pi_i}$ is generated by executing this policy in its environment.
3. **Continual Learning via Policy Distillation:** As new tasks are encountered, the distillation datasets from all past tasks are aggregated, and a single "student" policy $\pi_D$ is trained via supervised learning to mimic the behavior of all "teacher" policies contained in these datasets.
4. **Real-Life Deployment:** The final distilled policy $\pi_D$ is intended for deployment on a real robot.

#### 3.2. Learning on One Task

For each task $i$, the following steps are performed:
- **State Representation Learning (SRL):**
 - **Data Collection:** A dataset $D_{Random,i}$ is collected from environment $Env_i$ using a random policy. This data includes raw observations.
 - **SRL Model Training:** An SRL model, composed of an inverse model and an auto-encoder, is trained on $D_{Random,i}$. The inverse model predicts actions given consecutive states, while the auto-encoder reconstructs observations. This architecture is designed to learn a compact and efficient state representation $s_t$ from raw observations $I_t$.
- **Reinforcement Learning Policy Training:**
 - **Policy Learning:** An RL policy $\pi_i$ is trained using the features (state representations) provided by the encoder $E_i$ of the trained SRL model as input. The paper mentions using PPO2 (Proximal Policy Optimization).
 - **Distillation Dataset Generation:** Once $\pi_i$ is learned, it is used to generate an on-policy dataset $D_{\pi_i}$. This dataset consists of sequences of observations and their associated actions executed by the expert policy. For task 1 (Target Reaching), the generation is limited to preserve frames associated with correct reaching behavior (10 contacts). For task 2 (Target Circling), it spans standard episode size (250 timesteps).
- **Resource Management:** Critically, after generating $D_{\pi_i}$, the dataset $D_{Random,i}$ and the environment $Env_i$ are considered unavailable for subsequent tasks, simulating a strict continual learning setting.

#### 3.3. Continual Learning

- **Policy Distillation:** To achieve continual learning without catastrophic forgetting, the method employs policy distillation (Rusu et al., 2015). When a new task $j$ is learned, its distillation dataset $D_{\pi_j}$ is added to the aggregation of all previous distillation datasets (e.g., $D_{\pi_0}..., D_{\pi_{j-1}}$). A new student model $M_D(\theta')$ is then trained in a supervised fashion using this aggregated dataset. This process distills the knowledge from all individual "teacher" policies ($\pi_i$) into a single "student" policy $\pi_D$.
- **Task Inference:** A notable aspect is that the system does not require a task indicator at test time. The policy is expected to infer the current task based on visual cues within the observation, such as differently colored target tags in the environment.
- **Efficiency:** The distillation process is presented as computationally efficient, requiring minutes to train a distilled policy from the collected distillation datasets, compared to hours for original RL training.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Robot:** A 3-wheel omni-directional robot is used for 2D navigation tasks.
- **Perception:** The robot's input is a fixed RGB image of the scene recorded from above. The robot itself is identified by a black QR code.
- **Actions:** The robot uses 4 high-level discrete actions (move left/right, up/down relative to the robot's Cartesian plane).
- **Environments/Tasks:**
 - **Task 1 (Target Reaching - TR):** The robot receives a positive reward (+1) for reaching a red square target, a negative reward (-1) for bumping into boundaries, and no reward otherwise.
 - **Task 2 (Target Circling - TC):** The robot aims to circle around a central blue tag. The reward function is designed to be highest when the agent maintains a specific distance from the target and has been moving. A penalty is applied for bumping into boundaries. The different color tags (red for TR, blue for TC) provide the visual cue for task inference.
- **Sim2Real Transfer Mechanisms:**
 - **Simulation Training:** All SRL and RL training is performed in simulation.
 - **Domain Randomization:** To cope with real-world complexities like illumination changes, domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017) is applied during RL training. The background color in the simulation is randomly changed at each timestep to make the learned policies more robust to variations in the real environment.

#### 3.5. Evaluation

- **Primary Metric:** The performance of the single, final distilled policy ($\pi_D$) is evaluated by reporting the mean and standard error over 5 runs on each task in simulation.
- **Distillation Effectiveness:** The learning process is analyzed by saving distillation datasets at various checkpoints during RL training. By distilling policies at these points and evaluating them on past and current tasks, the evolution of learning and potential forgetting can be assessed.
- **Real-Life Deployment:** The paper notes that real-life deployment and evaluation are part of future work, but the framework is designed with this goal in mind.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides preliminary results regarding the application of continual reinforcement learning to a real-life robotic scenario. The main findings are presented in the context of two 2D navigation tasks (Target Reaching and Target Circling) for an omni-directional robot.

#### 4.1. Continual Learning of a Single Policy for Multiple Tasks

The primary result is the successful training of a single student policy that can solve both tasks sequentially learned in simulation. The methodology involves learning two individual "teacher" policies, one for each task, and then using policy distillation to combine their knowledge into a unified "student" policy. This student policy is then capable of performing both the Target Reaching and Target Circling tasks without needing an explicit task indicator at test time, inferring the task from visual cues (e.g., target tag color).

#### 4.2. Performance of the Distilled Student Policy

The performance of the single student distilled policy was evaluated in simulation on both tasks. Figure 4 illustrates a comparison between the performance (normalized mean reward and standard error) of policies trained on one task only (teacher policies) and the single student distilled policy.
- **Target Reaching (TR) Task:** The student policy demonstrated performance comparable to the teacher policy specifically trained for the Target Reaching task.
- **Target Circling (TC) Task:** Similarly, the student policy achieved performance close to that of the teacher policy trained exclusively for the Target Circling task.
These results indicate that the distillation process effectively transferred the knowledge from the two individual teacher policies into a single student policy, allowing it to maintain high performance on both tasks simultaneously. The student policy achieved near-maximum reward in both scenarios.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Policy Distillation

An explicit evaluation of the distillation process was conducted for the Target Circling task. During the RL training of the teacher policy for this task, the policy was saved at regular intervals (every 200 episodes, or 50,000 timesteps). Each saved teacher policy was then distilled into a separate student policy and tested. Figure 5 compares the RL training curve of the teacher policy (PPO2) with the mean and standard deviation of the performance of the distilled student policies.
- The curves for the RL-trained teacher policy and the distilled student policy exhibit a close resemblance.
- This observation suggests that policy distillation effectively transfers the learned policy using a limited distillation dataset, incurring minimal loss in policy performance. This supports the utility of distillation as a mechanism for accumulating knowledge from individual tasks into a single, comprehensive policy.

The paper provides quantitative results showing that the proposed method enables a single policy to continually learn and perform multiple sequential tasks in simulation, paving the way for real-life deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the development of autonomous agents that can learn and adapt over extended periods in dynamic, real-world environments. The significance and potential impact of this work lie in several areas:

#### 5.1. Advancing Real-Life Continual Reinforcement Learning

The study provides a preliminary framework for deploying continually learned policies on physical robots. By integrating SRL, policy distillation, and Sim2Real transfer, it addresses key challenges in creating agents that can learn new skills sequentially without forgetting old ones, a critical capability for service robots and other long-lived autonomous systems. The ability to learn without task indicators, inferring the task from visual cues, adds to the practical applicability.

#### 5.2. Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting

The use of policy distillation as a continual learning mechanism demonstrates a method for accumulating knowledge from distinct tasks into a single policy, thereby counteracting catastrophic forgetting. This is significant for developing robust agents that can manage an expanding repertoire of skills over their operational lifetime.

#### 5.3. Practicality of Sim2Real Transfer

The methodology leverages simulation for efficient policy training, a common necessity in robotics due to the cost and time constraints of real-world data collection. The incorporation of domain randomization for robust Sim2Real transfer enhances the likelihood of successful deployment on physical hardware, making the approach more practical for real-world applications. This can reduce development cycles and increase the accessibility of advanced RL techniques for robotics.

#### 5.4. Improved Sample Efficiency and Interpretability through SRL

By utilizing State Representation Learning, the approach addresses the notorious sample inefficiency of RL by providing the policy with compact and meaningful input features instead of raw, high-dimensional sensory data. This can accelerate learning and potentially lead to more interpretable policies, although the latter is not explicitly explored in this paper.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Research and Complex Tasks

This preliminary work lays foundational groundwork for future advancements. The authors outline several promising directions, including:
- **Continual SRL:** Extending the continual learning paradigm to the SRL model itself, allowing the representation encoder to adapt to new tasks. This could further enhance robustness and adaptability.
- **Scaling to More Complex Tasks:** Investigating the scalability of the approach to a larger number and greater diversity of tasks, addressing potential issues like blind knowledge accumulation versus generalization and selective forgetting.
- **Direct Real-Robot Learning:** The ultimate goal of training policies directly on real robots is identified, potentially using model-based RL to improve sample efficiency in the real world.
- **Quantitative Real-Life Evaluation:** The paper explicitly mentions the future provision of quantitative results and videos from real-life robot deployment, which would solidify the real-world impact of the current findings.

In conclusion, this research presents a coherent strategy for addressing some of the most pressing challenges in contemporary robotics, particularly concerning lifelong learning and real-world deployment. Its potential impact lies in paving the way for more adaptable, capable, and autonomously evolving robotic systems.
