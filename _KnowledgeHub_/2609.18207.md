# Reinforcement Learning for Real-Time Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Real-Time EXPO-FT is a reinforcement learning fine-tuning framework for latency-bound VLA policies that splits control into two timescales: a large pretrained VLA asynchronously proposes multiple action chunks from a stale observation, then a lightweight edit policy corrects them using the latest observation and a Q-function selects the best chunk. With only 10 minutes of real robot data, it raises average success on four dynamic tasks from 42% to 97%.

## Problem

- Large VLA models incur high inference latency, so the observation used to choose an action is stale at execution, causing distribution shift that degrades reliability in dynamic tasks.
- Asynchronous execution methods like real-time chunking are built on imitation learning and offer no mechanism for improving beyond the demonstration distribution toward higher reliability.
- Standard RL fine-tuning assumes instantaneous inference; under latency the executed action depends on a delayed state, breaking the Markov property and biasing credit assignment for precise refinement.

## Method

- **Two-timescale asynchronous inference**: the π0.5 VLA samples 32 candidate chunks from a delayed observation, inpainting the in-flight action prefix via ==training-time real-time chunking==, and discards the first d delayed actions.
- **Fast synchronous edit-and-select**: a small ==tanh-Gaussian== edit policy adds bounded corrections to each candidate conditioned on the latest observation, and a ==REDQ== critic picks the highest-Q chunk among base and edited candidates.
- Training: ==chunk-level temporal-difference== critic backups with ==noise-space filtering==, where a lightweight noise critic scores 32 seeds so only one VLA decode is needed per backup, plus ==prefix-masked flow-matching== behavior cloning on successful episodes.

## Results

- On four real-world 30 Hz dynamic tasks with 10 minutes of online data, average success reaches **29/30** versus **25/30** for EXPO-FT with real-time chunking and **12.5/30** for the supervised fine-tuned policy.
- On Kinetix under a 4-step delay, it averages **96.2%** success, beating EXPO-FT with real-time chunking by **14.5** points and even the zero-delay RLPD baseline at **81.4%**.
- On Ball Balancing it reaches **28/30** where no baseline exceeds **23/30**; it stays near **100%** across **0.5x** to **1.25x** object-passing speeds and stable across delays where plain real-time chunking degrades.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Latency can be handled by decoupling timescales: let the big prior plan slowly from stale observations while a small reactive module corrects with fresh observations at execution.
> - Conditioning the last-stage correction on the current observation restores the Markov property for RL under delay, so value-based credit assignment stays valid without shrinking the base model.
> - A delayed expressive policy with fast edits can outperform an undelayed lightweight policy, suggesting pretrained priors plus local value-guided correction beat raw reactivity in sample-limited real-world RL.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are increasingly used as general-purpose robot policies because they can combine visual perception, language-level task conditioning, and motor control. These models are typically pretrained on large collections of robot demonstrations and related multimodal data. Their broad behavioral priors can allow them to perform complex tasks with relatively little task-specific data. However, their size and generative policy structure often produce substantial inference latency.

Latency is particularly problematic in dynamic manipulation. A robot may operate at 30 Hz or faster, while a large VLA may require tens or hundreds of milliseconds to generate an action sequence. Consequently, the observation used to generate an action can describe a state that is already outdated when the action is executed. This produces a distribution shift between training and deployment and can damage performance on tasks involving moving objects, unstable contacts, or narrow timing windows.

Several research directions address this problem. System-level methods attempt to accelerate VLA inference through model compression, optimized kernels, faster sampling, or speculative decoding. Other methods alter the execution procedure. Real-time chunking (RTC), for example, generates future action chunks asynchronously while the robot executes the current chunk, often using the already committed actions as a prefix for the next prediction. Training procedures such as Training-Time RTC and related future-state-aware approaches incorporate latency into policy learning.

A separate research line applies reinforcement learning (RL) to pretrained generative policies, including diffusion and flow-matching policies. These methods seek to improve reliability beyond the behavior represented in demonstrations. Off-policy approaches are especially relevant to robotics because they can reuse previously collected data and therefore reduce the amount of costly physical interaction.

The paper addresses the intersection of these areas. Prior asynchronous execution methods were primarily developed for imitation learning and generally did not provide a direct mechanism for policy improvement through online RL. Conversely, applying existing RL fine-tuning methods to a delayed VLA can produce biased credit assignment because the executed action is based on an old observation. Real-Time EXPO-FT is proposed as a way to retain both the expressive prior of a large VLA and the rapid state responsiveness of a lightweight controller.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to develop an RL fine-tuning framework for large VLA policies that can operate under realistic inference delays while maintaining high performance on dynamic tasks.

The authors identify three related technical problems. First, a large VLA may not produce actions quickly enough for direct, synchronous control. If a model takes approximately 167 milliseconds to generate an action at a 30 Hz control rate, the action may be five control steps behind the current environment state. Second, simply executing delayed actions can make the effective decision process non-Markovian with respect to the current state. Standard RL updates assume that the action depends on the current state, but delayed inference means that the executed action may depend on an earlier observation. This can bias value estimation and credit assignment. Third, existing real-time execution methods can preserve smooth asynchronous inference but do not necessarily improve the policy beyond the demonstration distribution.

The motivation is therefore to separate responsibilities between two policies. The large VLA should provide semantically and behaviorally meaningful candidate action sequences, taking advantage of its pretrained knowledge. A smaller policy should make rapid corrections based on the latest observation. RL should train the correction mechanism and value function, while also allowing controlled fine-tuning of the VLA itself.

The paper evaluates whether this design improves reliability under delay in both simulation and physical robotics. The experimental setting emphasizes tasks with stochastic dynamics, rapid state changes, and precise timing. The authors also investigate whether the method remains effective as inference delays increase and as the speed of the environment changes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

Real-Time EXPO-FT builds on EXPO and EXPO-FT, which combine a pretrained base policy with a smaller edit policy. The base VLA generates an action chunk, while the edit policy predicts a bounded residual correction. If the base action is \(a\) and the edit is \(\hat{a}\), the executed candidate is \(\tilde{a}=a+\hat{a}\). The edit is constrained to a task-specific range, which limits how far RL can move the pretrained behavior.

At inference time, the VLA operates asynchronously. When the currently executing chunk is approaching completion, the system queries the VLA using the current observation and the actions that will be executed during the remaining inference delay. These committed actions are inserted as a clean prefix, following the RTC formulation. The model samples multiple candidate chunks, but only the portion after the delay is retained for execution.

When the delay has elapsed, the latest observation is available. The lightweight edit policy then modifies each candidate chunk using this current observation. A learned action-value function evaluates both the original and edited chunks. The system deterministically selects the candidate with the highest estimated value and executes it.

In the real-world experiments, the VLA generates 32 candidate chunks, and the edit policy produces one edited version of each. The critic therefore evaluates up to 64 candidates at each replanning boundary. The VLA uses an action horizon of 16, while the execution window is eight actions. The base model is initialized from a task-specific, prefix-conditioned version of the π0.5 VLA, using LoRA adapters and a trainable visual pathway during supervised initialization.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning Components

The edit policy is trained to maximize the critic’s estimated value of the edited action while retaining an entropy regularization term. The critic is trained with a chunk-level temporal-difference objective. Each transition spans the entire execution horizon \(C\), rather than a single control step. The target value is based on the next observation and the highest-value candidate chunk available at that point.

The critic is implemented as a REDQ-style ensemble of ten Q-networks, with two networks sampled for each target estimate and their minimum used to reduce overestimation. A separate ResNet-based visual encoder processes the side and wrist camera images, while proprioceptive information is embedded separately and combined with the visual representation and action chunk.

The VLA itself is fine-tuned with a prefix-conditioned flow-matching behavior-cloning objective. During training, the first \(d\) actions of a chunk are treated as known, clean actions corresponding to the in-flight prefix, and the loss is applied only to the remaining postfix. This teaches the VLA to generate coherent continuations under delay. The initial supervised training uses demonstrations and randomized prefix lengths. During online training, the delay is set to zero for the first chunk of an episode and to the deployment delay for later chunks. The VLA is updated using demonstrations and successful online episodes.

#### 3.3. Computational Acceleration

Evaluating many VLA candidates during every Bellman backup would be expensive because each candidate requires flow denoising. The authors therefore introduce optional noise-level filtering. A lightweight noise-space critic scores multiple random noise inputs before full action decoding. Only the highest-scoring noise sample is decoded into an action chunk, after which the edit policy and main critic perform a final selection. The noise critic is trained to match the value assigned by the target action critic to the selected decoded candidate.

#### 3.4. Data and Experimental Setup

Training begins with a small offline demonstration dataset collected by teleoperation or scripted policies. The VLA is first supervised fine-tuned until it reaches approximately 30% success, after which online RL begins. All real-world tasks use sparse binary rewards based on task-specific success detectors. No human provides corrective interventions during training, although human assistance is used for some environment resets.

The simulation evaluation uses ten dynamic Kinetix environments with force-based control and Gaussian action noise. The pretrained flow policy experiences a four-step inference delay. The real-world robot operates at 30 Hz with two 224-by-224 RGB cameras and proprioceptive observations. Three tasks are evaluated with an effective delay of approximately 167 milliseconds, and Dynamic Picking uses approximately 67 milliseconds of latency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

Across the ten Kinetix environments, Real-Time EXPO-FT achieves an average success rate of 96.2% under a four-step delay. It is the best-performing delayed or non-delayed RL method in all ten environments according to the paper’s reported comparison.

The method substantially exceeds the delayed baselines. Its average success rate is 34.5 percentage points higher than DSRL, 20.1 points higher than DSRL with RTC, 21.3 points higher than EXPO-FT, and 14.5 points higher than EXPO-FT with RTC. It also exceeds the zero-delay RLPD baseline, which reaches 81.4% on average. The comparison is notable because RLPD receives the current observation for every action, whereas Real-Time EXPO-FT generates its base candidates from delayed observations and relies on the edit policy for current-state correction.

The detailed task results show particularly large gains on Catapult, Half-Cheetah, and Car Launch, where Real-Time EXPO-FT reaches 93%, 98%, and 98%, respectively. On H17 Unicycle and Grasp Easy, it reaches 99% in both cases. Performance is somewhat lower on Chain Lander at 92%, but it remains comparable to the strongest alternatives.

The noise-level filtering study indicates that filtering candidate noise before denoising improves learning efficiency under a fixed compute budget. This allows the method to use a larger candidate set without requiring full decoding of every candidate during each critic backup.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results

The real-world evaluation covers Dynamic Picking, Ball Balancing, Object Passing, and Soccer Kicking. With no more than ten minutes of online robot data per task, Real-Time EXPO-FT achieves 29 successes out of 30 trials on average.

The task-level results are:

- Dynamic Picking: 30/30.
- Ball Balancing: 28/30.
- Object Passing: 30/30.
- Soccer Kicking: 28/30.

The average performance improves from 12.5/30 after supervised fine-tuning to 29/30 after Real-Time EXPO-FT. The method also outperforms EXPO-FT with RTC, which averages 25/30, and all other baselines. Ball Balancing is identified as especially sensitive to small state perturbations; Real-Time EXPO-FT reaches 28/30, compared with 23/30 for the strongest prior baseline. RLPD performs poorly on several delayed tasks, including zero successes on Dynamic Picking and Object Passing, despite its low-latency policy structure.

Additional experiments support the latency-related explanation for the gains. In the H17 Unicycle simulation, Real-Time EXPO-FT maintains relatively stable performance as the delay increases, whereas RTC performance declines at longer delays. In the physical Object Passing task, the proposed method maintains near-100% success over the tested object speeds, while delay-unaware EXPO-FT variants degrade as the passing speed increases.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It presents a way to combine large, expressive VLA policies with lightweight reactive control and RL-based policy improvement. Rather than requiring the entire VLA to operate at the control frequency, the system uses the VLA for slower candidate generation and delegates immediate corrections to a smaller policy that can condition on the latest state.

If the results generalize, this design could make large pretrained robot models more practical on hardware where full-model inference is too slow. It may also reduce the amount of real-world data needed to adapt a model to dynamic task conditions. The reported gains under ten minutes of online interaction are relevant to robotics settings in which data collection is expensive or wear on hardware must be limited.

The work also illustrates that latency should be treated as part of the RL problem rather than only as an engineering issue. Prefix-conditioned training, chunk-level value backups, and current-observation action editing jointly address the mismatch between policy inference and action execution.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of the results. The real-world study uses four task-specific success detectors and sparse binary rewards, so the method’s behavior with more general or delayed rewards is not established. Human operators assist with environment resets, which reduces the degree of full autonomy. The simulation study uses state-based flow policies rather than visual-language-action models, so it primarily tests the real-time RL mechanism rather than the complete VLA pipeline. Finally, the baselines differ in update schedules: RLPD and DSRL receive more asynchronous learner updates, although the authors retain this advantage in the comparison. Broader evaluation across robots, environments, model sizes, latency distributions, and automated reset procedures would be needed to assess the framework’s generality.
