# Is Success All You Need? Investigating the Impact of Input Perturbations on VLA Behaviour in Tabletop Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> A benchmark-agnostic evaluation framework that measures how successful VLA trajectories behave under input perturbation, not just whether tasks succeed. Built on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, it scores successful rollouts on duration, jerk, path length and gripper movement, then compares them to unperturbed baselines with median shifts and MAD variability. Across three VLAs, perturbations substantially change successful behaviour even when task success rates are similar.

## Problem

- VLA robustness benchmarks such as LIBERO-Plus, LIBERO-PRO, Colosseum V2 and RobustVLA measure perturbation effects almost entirely through binary task success rate.
- Behaviour-quality evaluations such as RoboEval and BEHAVIOR report jerk, efficiency and path metrics but never pair them with systematic robustness perturbations.
- Because the two practices were never combined, current evaluation cannot tell whether a policy's execution quality has degraded while it still completes the task.

## Method

- ==Trajectory-level behaviour metrics==: duration, total gripper movement, mean and P95 ==Cartesian jerk==, mean joint jerk, and Cartesian and joint path length, computed only on successful rollouts.
- ==Typical-behaviour change score==: per-task percentage change in mean metric from baseline, median-aggregated across the suite, with ==Mann-Whitney U tests== and ==Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction==.
- Variability analysis using percentage change in ==median absolute deviation==, built into the open-source ==vla-eval harness==, with LIBERO-Plus's seven perturbation types matched to the original LIBERO tasks.

## Results

- On LIBERO-Spatial under camera perturbation, mean Cartesian jerk rose **24.1%** for pi0.5 (7/**10** tasks significant) and **35.3%** for OpenVLA-OFT (**10**/**10** tasks).
- pi0.5 and VLANeXt had near-equal camera-perturbed Spatial success (**69.7%** vs **68.0%**), but pi0.5 duration, jerk and gripper movement rose **22.5**/**24.1**/**37.8%** versus **4.2**/**3.2**/**2.5%**.
- TSR drop correlated only moderately with behaviour change (gripper movement rho=0.56, P95 jerk rho=0.54, n=63). Camera perturbation raised MAD for every model and suite.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Equal success rates can hide very different execution quality, so robustness reports should include smoothness, efficiency and gripper metrics alongside task success.
> - Camera-viewpoint and proprioceptive perturbations disturb how successful trajectories are executed far more than lighting, language or background changes do.
> - Narrower behaviour spread under perturbation may be survivorship bias: perturbation can remove messy recovery-based successes and leave only clean ones, which inflates apparent consistency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Vision-language-action (VLA) models turn camera images, language instructions, and robot state into actions for tasks such as picking up and placing objects. Their robustness is often summarized by task success rate (TSR), the percentage of episodes that meet the task's binary goal condition. A robot can still reach that goal by a substantially different route. A dropped object may be recovered, or a trajectory may become longer or jerkier. The authors therefore compare *how successful trajectories are executed* under perturbation, alongside whether tasks succeed.

In an illustrative LIBERO-Spatial pick-and-place example, π0.5 completes the task under a camera-angle perturbation but drops and re-picks the bowl. The authors use this example to show how a binary success outcome can miss altered execution. It is not meant to show that every successful trajectory behaves this way.

Paper Fig. 1: two successful π0.5 trajectories for "pick up the black bowl on the ramekin and place it on the plate" in LIBERO-Spatial. On the left is original LIBERO, and on the right is a LIBERO-Plus camera-angle perturbation. The goal is met in both cases, but the perturbed trajectory drops and re-picks the bowl. For the pictured trajectories, total gripper movement and mean Cartesian jerk are 0.077 m and 0.381 m/s³ (original) and 0.236 m and 0.596 m/s³ (perturbed).

### 2. Measuring the route, not just the destination

TSR records binary completion. The added measures describe successful trajectories:

- **Duration** is elapsed simulator time. It is not model inference latency.
- **Gripper movement** is cumulative gripper opening and closing.
- **Path length** is measured in Cartesian and joint space.
- **Jerk** measures abrupt changes in motion. The authors report mean jerk in both spaces, plus 95th-percentile Cartesian jerk.

Each perturbed LIBERO-Plus task variant is matched to its original LIBERO task. For each task and condition, the authors calculate a metric's mean across successful trajectories. They then express the perturbed mean as a percentage change from the unperturbed baseline. A suite is summarized by the median of these task-level changes. Task counts report statistically significant increases, using two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across tasks at α = 0.05.

### 3. Similar success, different execution

The strongest quantitative evidence is a same-condition comparison on the LIBERO-Spatial suite under the camera perturbation. There, π0.5 and VLANeXt had similar TSRs of 69.7% and 68.0%. Their successful trajectories, however, differed sharply from their respective unperturbed baselines:

Median task-level changeπ0.5VLANeXtDuration+22.5%+4.2%Mean Cartesian jerk+24.1%+3.2%Total gripper movement+37.8%+2.5%

The observed increases were significant on 10/10 versus 7/10 tasks for duration, 7/10 versus 5/10 for mean Cartesian jerk, and 9/10 versus 0/10 for gripper movement.

Under this perturbation, suite, and model comparison, TSR does not uniquely determine the reported trajectory metrics. The comparison does not identify the cause of the models' different behavior, and it does not show that one model is universally better. The changes describe simulator-measured successful-trajectory behavior. They do not measure wall-clock speed or added computation.

Shading marks a significant increase in at least six tasks. Decreases, and increases significant in fewer than six tasks, remain unshaded. OpenVLA-OFT appears only in the Spatial block.

### 4. What a success label leaves out

The established contribution is the combination of two things: comparing successful execution behavior between unperturbed LIBERO tasks and matched LIBERO-Plus variants, alongside success rates. The authors recommend that trajectory behavior and its variability complement TSR.

The study has clear limits. Behavior is measured only on successful trajectories, and the experiments are simulated tabletop manipulation. Complete suite coverage is for π0.5 and VLANeXt only. The study does not establish how failed trajectories behave, whether the patterns transfer to physical robots, or why a particular perturbation changes execution.
