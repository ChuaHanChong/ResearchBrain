# Beyond Action Residuals: Real-World Robot Policy Steering via Bottleneck Latent Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Z-Perturbation Reinforcement Learning (ZPRL) introduces a method for online adaptation of pretrained robot manipulation policies by steering a compact, task-aligned bottleneck latent. This approach enhances real-world robot behaviors with smoother motions, improved efficiency, and higher success rates across diverse manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Pretrained imitation learning policies frequently encounter failures in real-world deployment due to novel situations, execution errors, or distribution shifts between training and test environments.
- Existing online adaptation methods for robots are either computationally demanding (e.g., full policy finetuning) or lead to unstructured, potentially unsafe exploration (e.g., direct action-space residual reinforcement learning).
- There is a need for an efficient and structured interface for online reinforcement learning adaptation that preserves complex pretrained capabilities while enabling targeted, meaningful behavioral improvements.

## Method

- **ZPRL** employs a two-stage training pipeline: first, offline ==imitation learning== integrates a Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB) module to extract a compact, task-aligned latent representation from observations.
- During online ==reinforcement learning==, the entire base policy, including the VIB, is frozen. A lightweight RL policy learns to predict a small residual perturbation directly applied to this bottleneck latent.
- The perturbed latent then conditions the frozen generative action model, allowing online RL to indirectly steer the robot's behavior through a structured, low-dimensional space rather than by directly altering high-dimensional actions.

## Results

- ZPRL achieved strong final success rates and competitive sample efficiency across eight simulated manipulation tasks, outperforming several full finetuning and action-space residual baselines.
- The method consistently generated smoother robot motions during online finetuning, reducing end-effector translational velocity by **29%** and acceleration by **39%** compared to action-space residual approaches.
- In four challenging real-world tasks, ZPRL demonstrated improved success rates (e.g., **12.5%** higher on Flip Egg) and greater robustness to disturbances, producing visibly more coherent and stable trajectories than baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Steering a policy through a compact, task-aligned bottleneck latent provides a more structured and efficient exploration mechanism for online adaptation compared to direct action-space residuals or unconstrained latent space steering.
> - The Variational Information Bottleneck helps to distill task-relevant information into a low-dimensional latent space, which is crucial for focused online reinforcement learning that maintains the integrity of pretrained behaviors.
> - By freezing the large base policy and only learning a small perturbation network for the bottleneck latent, ZPRL achieves a balance between lightweight online adaptation and effective preservation of complex skills learned during offline pretraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation has seen significant advancements through imitation learning (IL) on large offline datasets. Modern policy architectures, including transformers, variational autoencoders (VAEs), and diffusion/flow models, have demonstrated the ability to reproduce complex behaviors with high fidelity. However, the performance of these pretrained policies is constrained by the coverage and quality of the offline data. In real-world deployments, policies often encounter novel situations, execution errors, or distribution shifts that lead to task failures. This necessitates mechanisms for online policy improvement beyond initial offline pretraining.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a framework for adapting and refining these pretrained policies through interaction with the environment. Existing approaches for RL adaptation can broadly be categorized into:
1. **Full policy finetuning:** This involves updating the entire policy end-to-end. While potentially expressive, it becomes computationally intensive and resource-demanding for large-scale visuomotor and vision-language-action (VLA) policies. Furthermore, for generative policies, it can be entangled with model-specific optimization designs.
2. **Action-space residual RL:** These methods freeze the pretrained base policy and learn a corrective policy that directly perturbs the output actions. This approach is lightweight as it avoids updating the entire model, thus preserving pretrained capabilities. However, direct action-space corrections can lead to low-level, unstructured exploration, often resulting in oscillatory, jerky, or potentially unsafe robot behaviors, especially in high-dimensional or chunked action spaces. Such exploration may modify motor commands without altering the underlying action pattern, limiting efficiency.
3. **Latent-space RL:** More recent work has explored moving RL intervention to internal latent spaces, such as the initial noise in diffusion policies. These methods suggest that the choice of steering interface is critical for effective RL post-training, enabling more structured exploration than action-space residuals. However, some latent spaces (e.g., diffusion noise) can still have dimensionality equivalent to the action space, which may limit efficiency as action horizons or robot degrees of freedom increase.

This research introduces Z-Perturbation Reinforcement Learning (ZPRL), which situates itself within the latent-space RL paradigm but with a distinct contribution. ZPRL aims to provide a lightweight yet structured interface for online adaptation by steering a *compact, task-aligned bottleneck latent*. This approach seeks to overcome the computational burden of full finetuning and the unstructured exploration issues of action-space residuals, while offering a more compact and task-relevant steering space compared to other latent-space methods like diffusion noise steering. The work builds upon ideas from variational information bottlenecks (VIB) for structured representations and on-manifold exploration, proposing that a compact bottleneck latent can serve as an efficient and interpretable interface for policy steering.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective and efficient method for adapting pretrained robot manipulation policies online using reinforcement learning. The authors aim to address the limitations of existing post-training approaches, particularly the computational expense of full policy finetuning and the unstructured, potentially unsafe exploration induced by action-space residual methods.

The motivation for ZPRL stems from several observations:
- **Persistent Failure Modes of Imitation Learning:** Despite the success of offline imitation learning (IL) in reproducing complex behaviors, policies often fail in real-world deployment due to factors such as execution errors, insufficient coverage of the task space by demonstrations, and discrepancies between training and deployment environments. Online adaptation through interaction, specifically RL, is recognized as a valuable mechanism to overcome these limitations.
- **Inefficiencies of Existing RL Adaptation Interfaces:**
 - **Weight-space finetuning:** Updating the entire policy's parameters is increasingly costly for modern large neural network policies, both in terms of computation and data. For generative policies, this process is also often intertwined with model-specific optimization designs, adding complexity.
 - **Action-space residuals:** While lightweight, direct corrections to output actions can lead to "low-level" exploration. This means the policy may modify motor commands without fundamentally altering the high-level action pattern. Such low-level modifications can result in oscillatory, erratic, or "jerky" robot movements, which are inefficient for exploration and potentially unsafe in physical environments. The intuition is that meaningful behavioral improvements require structured changes, not merely different motor commands.
- **Need for a Structured, Compact, and Efficient Interface:** The authors hypothesize that a more effective RL intervention space should possess two key properties:
 1. **Compactness:** To enable efficient online steering, avoiding the high dimensionality of policy weights or uncompressed latent spaces.
 2. **Structuredness:** To keep exploration within the realm of valid behaviors, contrasting with the unstructured nature of direct action-space modifications. This structure should ideally align with task-relevant features, preserving the underlying action patterns learned during pretraining.

Therefore, the central question driving this work is: "instead of correcting actions directly, can RL steer a policy through a different but more efficient interface that better captures the structure of pretrained behaviors?" ZPRL proposes a bottleneck latent as this interface, motivated by its potential to provide a low-dimensional, task-relevant space for structured exploration while preserving the strong action prior learned during offline pretraining.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ZPRL implements a two-stage training pipeline: first, offline imitation learning with a bottleneck latent, and second, online reinforcement learning through residual perturbations on this latent.

#### 3.1. Offline Bottlenecked Imitation Learning

1. **Base Policy Parameterization:** The method is instantiated with flow-matching (FM) policies, chosen for their expressive action generation capabilities and efficient inference. The base policy comprises an observation encoder `E` that maps observations `o` to a latent conditioning vector `c = E(o)`, and a conditional flow model `v_ϕ` that generates actions `a` given `c`. The FM policy is trained using an objective `L_IL` (Equation 1) that learns a velocity field to transport noise samples towards target actions, conditioned on `c`.
2. **Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB) Integration:** To create a compact, task-relevant steering interface, a plug-and-play VIB module is augmented to the policy. This module operates on the observation embedding `c`. The VIB aims to extract a bottleneck latent `z` that retains only information necessary for action prediction while discarding irrelevant details. The VIB objective `min -I(z; a) + βI(z; c)` (Equation 3) balances informativeness (maximizing mutual information with actions `a`) and compactness (minimizing mutual information with the observation embedding `c`).
3. **VIB Objective Implementation:** The VIB objective is implemented using a variational encoder-decoder structure. A VIB encoder `p_φ(·|c)` maps `c` to a latent posterior `z`, and a VIB decoder `g_φ(z)` reconstructs a feature `ˆc` for downstream action generation. The variational surrogate `L_VIB` (Equation 5) combines the flow-matching objective (with `ˆc` as condition) and a KL divergence term `β KL(p_φ(z|c)∥r(z))` to regularize `z` towards a standard Gaussian prior `r(z)`.
4. **Overall Offline Training:** The total offline loss `L_off = L_IL + L_VIB` (Equation 6) is used to train the base policy parameters (`ϕ` for `E` and `v_ϕ`) and the VIB module parameters (`φ` for `p_φ` and `g_φ`). A critical design choice is to block gradients from `L_VIB` to `E_ϕ` and `v_ϕ`, ensuring that the VIB module acts as an auxiliary path and does not interfere with the base policy's original imitation learning performance. The learned latent `z` is intended to encapsulate the steering potential for online RL.

#### 3.2. Online Z-Perturbation Reinforcement Learning

1. **Policy Freezing and Latent Perturbation:** During online finetuning, the entire base policy, including the observation encoder `E_ϕ`, the VIB encoder `p_φ`, the VIB decoder `g_φ`, and the flow model `v_ϕ`, is frozen. Only a new online RL policy `π_on` is learned. This `π_on` predicts a residual perturbation `∆z` to be added to the current bottleneck latent `z`.
2. **Perturbed Latent and Action Generation:** The perturbed latent is formed as `˜z = z + λ∆z`, where `λ` is a hyperparameter controlling the perturbation magnitude. This `˜z` is then passed through the frozen VIB decoder `g_φ` to produce a new conditioning embedding `˜c = g_φ(˜z)`. Finally, `˜c` conditions the frozen flow model `v_ϕ` to generate the online action `a_on ∼ π_base(·|˜c)`. This means online RL steers the base policy indirectly by modifying its internal task-conditioning variable, rather than directly outputting actions.
3. **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** The online RL policy `π_on` and a corresponding latent critic `Q_ψ` are trained using Soft-Actor-Critic (SAC).
 - **Actor Optimization:** The actor `π_θ` takes the observation embedding `c` and bottleneck latent `z` as input to predict `∆z`. Its objective `L_π` (Equation 9) aims to maximize expected returns while maintaining exploration (regulated by an entropy term).
 - **Critic Optimization:** The critic `Q_ψ` evaluates the value of the perturbed latent `(c, ˜z)`. It is trained with a temporal-difference objective `L_Q` (Equation 10, 11), using a target critic for stability. The critic is defined directly on `˜z` rather than `∆z` to account for the stochasticity of `p_φ(z|c)` and the downstream action generation process.
4. **Design Choices and Considerations:**
 - **Perturbation Scale `λ`:** A crucial hyperparameter determining the strength of RL steering. A small `λ` limits adaptability, while an excessively large `λ` can push `˜z` outside the data manifold learned by the VIB decoder and action prior, destabilizing finetuning. Practical guidance suggests setting `λ` such that `RMS(λ∆z)` is 10-20% of `RMS(z)`.
 - **Update-to-Data (UTD) Ratio:** The UTD ratio is balanced to ensure wall-clock efficiency, avoiding excessively high ratios (e.g., 1 or 2 in simulation/real-world, up to 5 for complex real-world tasks) to prevent the computational bottleneck from shifting from robot interaction to policy updates.
 - **Absence of Action-Space Critic:** An additional critic `Q_a(o, a)` in action space is avoided to prevent the computational overhead of iterative action denoising during critic optimization, thus preserving wall-clock efficiency.

This methodology aims to provide a compact, structured, and local steering mechanism for pretrained policies, leveraging the learned action prior to guide exploration towards meaningful and stable behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ZPRL across a range of simulation benchmarks and real-world manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against several baselines.

#### 4.1. Online RL Finetuning in Simulation

- **Benchmarks:** ZPRL was evaluated on eight tasks from Robomimic (can, square, transport), Adroit (door, hammer, pen), and Metaworld (box-close, push-wall). Observations included images and proprioception.
- **Baselines:** DPPO and ReinFlow (full policy finetuning), Policy-Decorator (Po-Dec, action-space residual), and DSRL (diffusion noise steering).
- **Performance:** ZPRL consistently achieved strong final success rates (SRs) across all eight tasks and demonstrated competitive sample efficiency. It was among the most effective methods for online adaptation speed.
 - ZPRL showed robust performance across parallel-jaw (Robomimic, Metaworld) and dexterous hand (Adroit) manipulation.
 - A notable exception was Metaworld tasks where Po-Dec sometimes learned faster in early stages. This was attributed to the smaller action chunk dimensions (8 dimensions) in Metaworld, where the advantage of ZPRL's 16-dimensional bottleneck latent was less pronounced. Nevertheless, ZPRL still achieved higher asymptotic returns.

#### 4.2. How the Bottleneck Latent Interface Shapes Online RL (Ablations on Robomimic square task)

- **Bottleneck Latent vs. Direct Observation Embedding Steering (RESEMB):** Directly learning residuals on the high-dimensional observation embedding (RESEMB) performed consistently worse and was less stable than ZPRL, especially as the residual scale increased. This indicates that the VIB bottleneck, by compressing representations into task-relevant latents, is crucial for focused and efficient RL exploration.
- **Perturbation Scale (λ):** The choice of `λ` significantly impacted learning speed and final SR.
 - Larger `λ` (e.g., 0.5) led to instability and poor performance (final SR 0.74, unable to reach SR=0.9 threshold), as it pushed the perturbed latent far from the pretrained distribution, weakening the action prior's support.
 - Smaller `λ` (e.g., 0.10, 0.15) resulted in slower learning (e.g., 2.40M steps to SR=0.9 for `λ=0.10`).
 - An intermediate `λ` (e.g., 0.20-0.25) provided the best balance, achieving high SRs (0.98) with efficient learning (1.16M steps to SR=0.9 for `λ=0.20`).
- **Bottleneck Dimension (dim(z)):** ZPRL exhibited robustness to a moderate range of bottleneck sizes.
 - Dimensions 8, 16, and 32 all achieved strong performance. The best result was with `dim(z) = 32`.
 - Even when `dim(z) = 64` (exceeding Po-Dec's `dim(a) = 40`), ZPRL still outperformed Po-Dec, suggesting the gain is not solely from dimensionality reduction but from operating in a *task-relevant space*.
 - Extremes: `dim(z) = 4` was too restrictive, leading to poor learning (final SR 0.68). `dim(z) = 128` made the steering space less compact and harder to optimize, reducing performance.
- **Amount of Offline Data:** Reducing the number of demonstrations (from 100 to 25 trajectories) led to a clear degradation in ZPRL's performance (more steps to reach SR=0.9, lower final SR). This highlights the dependence of ZPRL on a well-supported and covered latent manifold provided by the offline data.

#### 4.3. Smooth and Structured Action Generation

- **Metrics:** End-effector translational velocity (`Vel_EE`) and acceleration (`Acc_EE`) were measured for Po-Dec and ZPRL on the square task.
- **Comparison:** ZPRL consistently produced smoother motions than Po-Dec during online finetuning. At 2.4M steps, ZPRL reduced `Vel_EE` by 29% and `Acc_EE` by 39% compared to Po-Dec.
- **Qualitative Observation:** Po-Dec exhibited noticeable high-frequency oscillations in desired end-effector positions, while ZPRL maintained smoother and more structured steering, even when deviating from the base policy's trajectory. This supports the claim that latent perturbation, by leveraging the frozen flow model, constrains exploration to behaviorally meaningful directions, unlike direct action-space residuals.

#### 4.4. ZPRL in the Real World

- **Tasks:** Four challenging real-world tasks: Place Orange (single-arm pick-and-place), Flip Egg (dynamic, contact-rich), Open Box (bimanual coordination, contact-rich), and Insert Bills (bimanual, deformable object manipulation).
- **Performance:**
 - On Place Orange, Flip Egg, and Open Box, ZPRL achieved higher SRs and faster learning than Po-Dec. Significant gains were observed on more challenging tasks like Flip Egg (12.5% SR improvement) and Open Box (7.5% SR improvement). ZPRL also reduced episode length in Flip Egg and Open Box, indicating better real-world efficiency.
 - For Insert Bills (a complex bimanual DOM task), ZPRL alone improved the base policy's SR from 20% to 77.5%, demonstrating its applicability to highly complex scenarios.
- **Robustness:** ZPRL policies demonstrated non-trivial robustness to disturbances not seen during training, including human intervention, object replacement (e.g., different shaped/colored eggs), and out-of-distribution (OOD) initial layouts. An average SR of 69% was achieved across these disturbed settings.
 - Limitations were observed in cases of significant physical changes (e.g., real egg vs. model egg, SR dropped to 0.5) or highly sensitive tasks like Insert Bills under visual distractors/human disturbance (SRs 0.4-0.5), indicating robustness within the learned manifold rather than complete invariance to large shifts.
- **Real-World Smoothness:** ZPRL produced visually more coherent and smooth end-effector trajectories compared to Po-Dec in real-world rollouts (e.g., Place Orange), even when Po-Dec was augmented with temporal filtering for stabilization. This aligns with simulation findings and highlights ZPRL's benefit in reducing unsafe or unstable robot motions.

#### 4.5. Additional Analysis (Appendix)

- **UMAP Visualizations:** Projections of decoded observation embeddings and generated actions showed that ZPRL primarily remaps state-action associations within the existing action manifold rather than inventing entirely new behaviors.
- **Latent Space Shift:** Increasing `λ` consistently increased the Mahalanobis distance of perturbed latents from the offline distribution and the L2 displacement of decoded features. This confirmed that excessive `λ` can push latents out of the VIB decoder's training support, leading to less meaningful actions.
- **KL Weight β Ablation:** Offline KL regularization strength (`β`) in the VIB objective showed limited impact on online finetuning performance within a reasonable range (10⁻³, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁵).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Z-Perturbation Reinforcement Learning (ZPRL) as a method for online adaptation of pretrained robot manipulation policies. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Novel Adaptation Interface:** ZPRL proposes a bottleneck latent as a structured and compact interface for RL adaptation. This represents an alternative to both computationally intensive full policy finetuning and the unstructured exploration of action-space residual methods. By intervening in a low-dimensional, task-relevant latent space, ZPRL facilitates more efficient and targeted policy improvement.
- **Improved Performance and Efficiency:** The empirical results in both simulation and real-world scenarios demonstrate that ZPRL consistently improves sample efficiency and achieves higher final success rates compared to existing strong post-training baselines. This suggests a more effective use of online interaction data for refining robot skills.
- **Enhanced Behavior Quality:** A notable outcome of ZPRL is the generation of smoother and more coherent robot behaviors during online exploration, as evidenced by lower end-effector velocity and acceleration compared to action-residual approaches. This is particularly impactful for real-world robotics, where jerky or erratic movements can lead to failures, damage, or safety concerns. The inherent structure imposed by steering through a frozen generative model contributes to this behavioral stability, potentially reducing the need for post-hoc filtering or complex regularization.
- **Broad Applicability:** ZPRL's effectiveness across diverse manipulation tasks—including single-arm, bimanual, dynamic, contact-rich, and deformable object manipulation—underscores its generalizability. This suggests that the approach could be applied to a wide array of robotic applications where pretrained policies require refinement.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future exploration:
 - **Dependency on Base Policy:** The method's performance is inherently bounded by the capabilities of the frozen base policy. Future work could investigate strategies to expand the behavioral repertoire beyond re-composing existing skills, potentially by integrating mechanisms for novel behavior generation.
 - **Post-Hoc VIB Integration:** The current approach trains the VIB module jointly with the base policy offline. Exploring methods to attach a bottleneck latent interface to already trained, publicly available policies in a post-hoc manner could significantly broaden ZPRL's adoption and reduce setup costs.
 - **Extension to Complex Architectures:** Generalizing the steering principle to more complex policy architectures, such as multi-layer cross-attention models where task-relevant information might be distributed across various hidden states, is an important challenge. Identifying and perturbing the appropriate "task-relevant token" in such entangled models could be a promising direction.
 - **Beyond RL:** The concept of latent-space steering might extend beyond online RL to other forms of policy adaptation, such as iterative imitation learning, where lightweight steering could be used to target long-tail or failure-prone cases more efficiently than full policy retraining.

In conclusion, ZPRL contributes a method for post-training robot policies that balances lightweight adaptation with structured, task-aligned exploration, showing potential for advancing the deployment of robust and capable robots in complex real-world environments.
