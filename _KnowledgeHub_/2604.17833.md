# DART: Learning-Enhanced Model Predictive Control for Dual-Arm Non-Prehensile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The DART framework enables dual-arm robotic systems to precisely manipulate objects on a tray using non-prehensile techniques, integrating Model Predictive Control with learning-enhanced dynamics models. It demonstrates superior performance, including faster settling times and low steady-state errors (e.g., down to 2mm accuracy), and robust generalization across diverse object geometries, masses, and friction coefficients in simulation.

## Problem

- Current robotic manipulation largely depends on grasping, limiting handling of large, fragile, or awkwardly positioned objects.
- Dual-arm non-prehensile manipulation on a shared tray presents significant challenges due to dynamic arm coupling and uncertain object-tray dynamics.
- Accurately modeling the complex, varying dynamics between a tray and free-sliding objects (friction, inertia, contact) is difficult, hindering robotic adaptability.

## Method

- A two-layer **DART** framework integrating a high-level ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) for object trajectory generation with a low-level optimization-based dual-arm impedance controller.
- MPC computes optimal tray tilt trajectories, using desired end-effector poses derived for the dual arms.
- Three distinct dynamics models are evaluated within the MPC for object-tray interaction: a physics-based model (PMPC), an online regression model (RMPC), and a reinforcement learning model (LMPC).

## Results

- The LMPC variant achieved the shortest settling times (e.g., **0.82** s for a 2kg sphere) and generally lowest steady-state errors (e.g., **0.0026** m for a 1kg cylinder), demonstrating superior performance for rapid object repositioning.
- LMPC and RMPC exhibited superior generalization capabilities across 18 diverse object configurations (varying mass, geometry, and friction) compared to PMPC, which relied on accurate, object-specific physical parameters.
- While LMPC had higher control effort, RMPC showed lower effort for lighter objects and online adaptation, whereas PMPC provided consistent but moderate effort with variable performance depending on object contact dynamics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The DART framework pioneers dual-arm non-prehensile tray manipulation, addressing complexities of bimanual coordination and contact-rich object handling on a shared surface.
> - Evaluating physics-based (PMPC), online regression (RMPC), and reinforcement learning (LMPC) dynamics models reveals LMPC achieves the fastest settling times and lowest errors, while RMPC offers online adaptability.
> - Learning-enhanced models (LMPC and RMPC) significantly improve generalization across diverse object properties compared to purely physics-based approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper addresses a challenge at the intersection of non-prehensile manipulation and dual-arm robotic systems, a domain that has received limited attention in previous literature.

**Non-Prehensile Manipulation:** Historically, robotic manipulation has predominantly relied on grasping (prehensile manipulation). However, grasping presents limitations for objects that are large, fragile, or awkwardly positioned. Non-prehensile techniques, such as pushing, throwing, rolling, and sliding, offer alternative strategies by exploiting friction, inertia, and contact forces. Within this field, tray-based object manipulation, which involves moving objects through controlled surface tilting, is a human-inspired approach with applications in service robotics (e.g., restaurants, hospitals, laboratories). Prior research in non-prehensile manipulation has primarily focused on single-arm systems, with approaches ranging from maintaining object stability to exploiting sliding. Some studies have incorporated adaptive and learning-based methods to address uncertainties in friction and unconstrained environments.

**Dual-Arm Manipulation:** Dual-arm robotic systems expand capabilities beyond single-arm limitations, enabling tasks like coordinated object transport and bimanual assembly, particularly for heavier payloads or when enhanced stability is required. Traditional dual-arm control often employs impedance-based methods to manage dynamic coupling between manipulators, while more recent advancements utilize learning methods such as imitation learning, vision-language models, and reinforcement learning for coordinated tasks requiring precise force regulation and synchronization. A significant limitation in existing dual-arm research is the predominant assumption of rigidly grasped objects with well-defined contact dynamics. This assumption does not hold for scenarios where objects slide freely on a shared surface, as in tray-based manipulation.

**Bridging the Gap:** This paper aims to bridge the gap between these two areas by exploring non-prehensile manipulation within a dual-arm context. While single-arm non-prehensile approaches exist, they cannot leverage the benefits of distributed force and coordinated motion offered by dual-arm systems. The paper highlights the complexities of dual-arm non-prehensile tray manipulation, including the dynamic coupling between arms, the need for accurate modeling of uncertain tray-object dynamics (involving friction, inertia, and contact mechanics), and the requirement for compliant control to manage interaction forces. By proposing a framework for dual-arm non-prehensile tray manipulation, this work contributes to expanding the capabilities of service robots in dynamic and uncertain environments, moving beyond the constraints of traditional grasping and rigidly held objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework that enables dual-arm robotic systems to perform non-prehensile manipulation of objects on a tray with precision and adaptability. This addresses the challenge that while non-prehensile manipulation on a tray appears effortless for humans, it remains a significant hurdle for robots, especially in dual-arm settings where complexity is amplified due to dynamic coupling and uncertain object dynamics.

The key motivations driving this work are:

- **Addressing Limitations of Current Robotic Manipulation:** Most service robots rely on grasping, which is ineffective for large, fragile, or awkwardly positioned objects. Non-prehensile manipulation, particularly tray-based object transport, offers a human-inspired alternative with broad applications in service sectors like hospitality and healthcare.
- **Tackling Dual-Arm Coordination Challenges:** Extending tray-based manipulation to dual-arm systems introduces complexities. The shared tray dynamically couples the arms, demanding precise coordination. Simultaneously, the system must control object motion through uncertain dynamics, maintain compliance, and manage interaction forces. Traditional dual-arm research primarily assumes rigidly grasped objects, which does not apply to free-sliding objects on a tray.
- **Overcoming Object-Tray Dynamics Modeling Challenges:** Accurately modeling the complex dynamics between the tray and an object (involving friction, inertia, and contact mechanics that vary with object properties) is a fundamental challenge. Analytical models can oversimplify these mechanics, while purely data-driven approaches may lack interpretability and generalization. The goal is to develop robust dynamics models that can handle these uncertainties.
- **Enhancing Adaptability and Generalization:** Service robots need to handle diverse objects without extensive recalibration. The motivation is to develop a control framework that can generalize across varying object geometries, masses, and surface properties, reducing the need for object-specific fine-tuning.
- **Developing a Unified Control Solution:** The paper aims to integrate high-level trajectory planning (for object motion) with low-level compliant execution (for dual-arm control) into a cohesive framework that can manage the coupled dynamics of the robot-tray-object system.

To achieve these objectives, the paper proposes DART, a framework that integrates nonlinear Model Predictive Control (MPC) with an optimization-based impedance controller. A central objective is to systematically evaluate and integrate three complementary strategies for modeling the tray-object dynamics within the MPC formulation: a physics-based analytical model (PMPC), an online regression-based identification model (RMPC), and a reinforcement learning-based dynamics model (LMPC). This comparative evaluation aims to highlight the trade-offs and benefits of each modeling approach in terms of performance metrics like settling time, steady-state error, and control effort, especially concerning generalization capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DART framework employs a two-layer control architecture: a high-level Model Predictive Control (MPC) for object trajectory generation and a low-level optimization-based impedance controller for dual-arm execution.

**System Description:**
The system consists of two 7-DoF torque-controlled manipulator arms rigidly grasping a tray. An object of mass `m` is placed on the tray. The object's state `X` includes its position `p` and orientation `θ`, and linear `v` and angular `ω` velocities in the world frame `{W}`. The control input `u` consists of commanded roll (`uα`) and pitch (`uβ`) angles of the tray, constrained by actuation limits. The dual-arm model describes joint positions, velocities, accelerations (`qL/R`, `˙qL/R`, `¨qL/R`), joint-space mass matrix `M(q)`, and combined centripetal, Coriolis, and gravitational effects `h(q, ˙q)`. The arms rigidly grasp the tray at predefined poses, with desired end-effector poses (`yrefL/R`) derived from the desired tray pose.

**High-Level Controller: Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (MPC):**
The MPC component is responsible for computing optimal tray tilt trajectories to guide the object from its current state `X` to a desired state `Xref`. It solves a nonlinear optimization problem at each discrete time step `k` over a prediction horizon `N`.
The cost function penalizes:
- State tracking error `ek = (Xk - Xref)` along the horizon and terminal state error `eN`.
- Control effort `uk`.
- Control rate change `∆uk` to ensure smooth tray motion.
Constraints include:
- Object dynamics as a discrete-time state-transition function `Xk+1 = Φ(Xk, uk, ∆t)`.
- Velocity limits (`νmin ≤ νk ≤ νmax`).
- Tray tilt limits (`umin ≤ uk ≤ umax`).
- Control rate limits (`∆umin ≤ ∆uk ≤ ∆umax`).
The MPC uses weight matrices `Q` (for state error), `QN` (for terminal state error), and `QR` (for control input and rate of change). The state-transition function `Φ(·)` is the core component where different dynamics models are integrated.

**Object-Tray Dynamics Models (State Transition Function `Φ(·)`):**
The paper evaluates three alternative dynamics models for the state-transition function within the MPC:

1. **Physics-Based MPC (PMPC):**
 - This model uses analytical object dynamics, incorporating mass `Mo`, centrifugal/Coriolis `Co`, and gravity `go` terms.
 - The generalized contact wrench `Fo` due to tray contact interactions is modeled, including frictional effects via the Stribeck friction model.
 - Tray tilt `u` directly affects the gravity vector components acting along the tray's tangent plane.
 - The object's acceleration `˙νo` is computed, and numerically integrated to obtain `Φ(·)`.
 - This approach requires explicit modeling of the object's physical properties and contact dynamics.

2. **Regression-Based MPC (RMPC):**
 - This model augments a nominal physics-based dynamics model with an online Recursive Least Squares (RLS) estimator to capture unmodeled dynamics and contact effects.
 - It constructs 3x1 feature vectors (`ϕt`, `ϕr`) for translational and rotational components of unmodeled dynamics, which include velocity, hyperbolic tangent of velocity (to model Coulomb friction), and a constant (for bias).
 - Adaptive parameter vectors (`zt`, `zr`) are learned online to estimate the acceleration discrepancy `∆ˆao,k` between the observed acceleration and the nominal physics model prediction.
 - The RLS algorithm iteratively updates these parameters using observed acceleration discrepancies and feature vectors, employing a forgetting factor `λ`.
 - The dynamics prediction `˙νo = ˆao,k` is then integrated for `Φ(·)`.

3. **Reinforcement Learning-Based MPC (LMPC):**
 - This method uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a reinforcement learning algorithm, to learn a parameter vector `ψ`. This `ψ` collects state-independent components of `Mo`, `Co`, `Go`, and `Fo` within a structured dynamics model.
 - The learned dynamics model `˙νo = f(ψ, xo, νo, u)` accounts for unmodeled disturbances, parametric uncertainty, and model mismatch.
 - To ensure physical plausibility and stability, `ψ` is constrained using a `tanh` squashing function and a Logit-Sigmoid reparameterization, allowing adaptation in an unconstrained space while keeping parameters bounded.
 - PPO policy updates are driven by a reward signal `R` that penalizes high position error, high velocity error, and high control rate change (`∆u`).
 - Experience from state transitions and MPC commands is stored in a dense replay buffer, with a sparse buffer promoting generalization across diverse object configurations through domain randomization during training.

**Low-Level Controller: Optimization-Based Dual-Arm Impedance Control:**
The low-level controller translates the MPC-generated tray tilts `u` into joint torques for the two manipulator arms.
- It adapts a QP-based dual-arm impedance controller to compute optimal joint accelerations (`¨q∗L`, `¨q∗R`).
- The optimization minimizes a cost function that includes impedance-task error (`eimp`) and postural-task error (`epos`).
- `eimp` ensures desired end-effector dynamics, relating actual acceleration `¨y` to a desired impedance response based on position `(yref - y)` and velocity `(˙yref - ˙y)` errors, using stiffness `K` and damping `D` matrices.
- `epos` biases each arm towards its grasp configuration to maintain a stable posture, using joint stiffness `Knull`.
- Constraints include joint position, velocity, and torque limits.
- Finally, optimal joint accelerations `¨q∗` are converted to joint torques `τ` using the robot's dynamics (`τ = M(q) ¨q∗ + h(q, ˙q)`), which are then applied in the simulator.

**Implementation Details:**
The framework is implemented in Python, leveraging PyTorch for RMPC and LMPC, and CasADi with IPOPT solver for the nonlinear MPC and dual-arm controller. MuJoCo is used for simulation. Parallelization with Python's multiprocessing library ensures real-time performance, with separate processes for the CasADi solver, dual-arm controller, and MuJoCo environment, synchronized via shared memory buffers and mutex locks. LMPC training involves 500 episodes of 20,000 steps each in parallel environments, utilizing domain randomization across 18 unique object configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The DART framework was evaluated in a simulated dual-arm non-prehensile tray manipulation task using MuJoCo. Two UFactory xArm7 manipulators tilted a tray to move an object to a target state. The evaluation focused on robustness to variations in object mass, geometry, and tray-object friction.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Tray:** 1 kg mass, 40 cm x 30 cm x 1 cm dimensions.
- **Objects:** Three geometries (cube, cylinder, sphere), two masses (1 kg, 2 kg), and three friction coefficients (0.05, 0.10, 0.20), totaling 18 unique object configurations.
- **Task:** Objects started at the tray's center, and target positions were sampled radially between 0.08 m and 0.12 m from the center.
- **Hardware:** Simulated xArm7 manipulators with bases fixed 1.4 m apart.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Steady State Error (`ˆess`):** Mean position error over the last 4 seconds (20% of samples).
 - **Settling Time:** Time taken to reach steady state within a 1% tolerance of `ˆess`.
 - **Control Effort (`Jeffort`):** Sum of squared joint torques and torque rate changes.

**Quantitative Results (Table II analysis):**

- **Settling Time:**
 - **LMPC consistently achieved the shortest settling times** across almost all object configurations. For spheres, LMPC settling times were particularly low (e.g., 0.82 s for 2kg, 0.2 friction sphere).
 - **PMPC showed moderate settling times**, generally slower than LMPC but significantly faster than RMPC for most cases.
 - **RMPC exhibited the longest settling times**, often exceeding 15 seconds and sometimes over 20 seconds, indicating slower adaptation or convergence.
- **Steady State Error:**
 - **LMPC generally achieved the lowest steady-state errors**, particularly for cubes and cylinders, often reaching errors below 0.01 m (e.g., 0.0055 m for 2kg, 0.1 friction cube; 0.0026 m for 1kg, 0.1 friction cylinder). For spheres, LMPC's steady-state errors were higher compared to its performance on other geometries, but still competitive.
 - **RMPC also achieved relatively low steady-state errors**, often comparable to or slightly higher than LMPC for cubes and cylinders, but sometimes higher for spheres.
 - **PMPC exhibited variable steady-state errors.** It achieved very low errors for some cylinder (e.g., 0.0019 m) and sphere (e.g., 0.0047 m) configurations, often outperforming LMPC in these specific cases. However, for cube configurations, its errors were generally higher than both RMPC and LMPC.
- **Control Effort:**
 - **RMPC generally required the lowest control effort for 1kg objects**, demonstrating energy efficiency. However, for 2kg objects, its control effort significantly increased, often becoming the highest among the three methods.
 - **PMPC showed consistent control effort** across different object masses and friction coefficients, generally falling in the middle range.
 - **LMPC's control effort was relatively high and consistent** across various configurations, suggesting that its faster convergence and lower error come at the cost of more aggressive control actions.

**Generalization and Robustness:**
- **LMPC and RMPC demonstrated superior generalization** across objects, masses, and friction coefficients compared to PMPC. Their performance metrics remained relatively consistent without requiring object-specific tuning.
- **PMPC's performance varied more significantly**, underscoring its reliance on accurate, object-specific physical parameters.

**Trade-offs:**
- **LMPC:** Offers the fastest settling times and generally lowest steady-state errors, but requires pre-training and higher computational effort during operation.
- **RMPC:** Adapts in real-time without training, showing low control effort for lighter objects, but suffers from significantly longer settling times. Its control effort increases substantially with object mass.
- **PMPC:** Provides interpretability and good steady-state error for certain rolling contacts, but lacks generalization and can have longer trajectories due to simplified contact dynamics models.

**Qualitative Results (Fig. 4 & 5 analysis):**
- **Trajectory Visualization (Fig. 5):** The visualizations show distinct trajectory behaviors for the three methods across cube, sphere, and cylinder objects.
 - **LMPC:** Tends to take the shortest and fastest trajectories, confirming its superior settling time and overall performance.
 - **RMPC:** Converges towards the target while adapting online, but can exhibit overshooting in some scenarios before converging, consistent with its longer settling times.
 - **PMPC:** Often takes longer, smoother trajectories, which can lead to longer convergence times for objects like the cube, reflecting its simplified contact dynamics model. However, for objects with rolling contacts (cylinder, sphere), PMPC can achieve very good steady-state errors even with these trajectories.
- **Position Error and Tilt Commands (Fig. 4):** This figure illustrates the observed trends, with LMPC showing rapid convergence of position error and PMPC exhibiting smooth settling, sometimes to a low steady-state error, for certain objects. The control commands generated by each method also showed variations reflecting their underlying dynamics models and control philosophies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DART framework represents a significant advancement by introducing a novel approach to dual-arm non-prehensile tray manipulation, a previously underexplored area in robotics. The key contributions and their potential impact are:

- **Pioneering Dual-Arm Non-Prehensile Manipulation:** DART is presented as the first framework specifically designed for dual-arm non-prehensile manipulation of objects on a tray. This bridges a critical gap between bimanual coordination and contact-rich object manipulation, which has predominantly been studied in isolation or under the assumption of rigidly grasped objects. This opens new possibilities for robotic tasks that require handling objects without grasping, leveraging the benefits of two arms.
- **Enhanced Service Robotics Capabilities:** The ability to transport objects with an accuracy of up to 2 mm on dynamically controlled trays is highly relevant for service robotics applications. Domains such as hospitality (e.g., serving food), healthcare (e.g., transporting medical supplies), and domestic environments can benefit from robots capable of handling diverse items, including fragile, large, or awkwardly shaped objects, in a human-like, non-prehensile manner. This could lead to more versatile and adaptable service robots.
- **Comprehensive Dynamics Modeling Strategies:** The systematic evaluation of three distinct dynamics modeling strategies (physics-based, online regression-based, and reinforcement learning-based) within an MPC framework provides valuable insights into their respective strengths and trade-offs.
 - **Physics-based models (PMPC)** offer interpretability, which is crucial for understanding system behavior and debugging.
 - **Regression-based models (RMPC)** provide online adaptation capabilities, allowing robots to adjust to unmodeled dynamics in real-time without prior training. This is beneficial in dynamic environments where object properties might be unknown or change.
 - **Reinforcement learning-based models (LMPC)** deliver superior generalization and faster convergence. Integrating RL-learned state transition dynamics directly into MPC is a notable innovation, enabling the controller to adapt to varying object geometries, masses, and surface interactions without extensive fine-tuning. This significantly reduces the engineering effort required for deploying robots in varied environments.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The validation across a wide range of object configurations (varying mass, geometry, and friction) demonstrates the framework's robustness. The capability of RMPC and LMPC to generalize across these variations is particularly impactful, addressing a fundamental limitation in traditional model-based control which often requires precise object parameters. This adaptability is crucial for real-world deployment where object properties are often unknown or uncertain.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays the groundwork for several extensions. Future work outlined, such as handling surfaces with variable friction, uneven surfaces, complex object geometries, and incorporating active yawing for orientation control, suggests a roadmap for developing even more sophisticated non-prehensile manipulation capabilities. This framework can serve as a baseline for comparing new algorithms in these complex domains.

In conclusion, DART advances the field by demonstrating a practical and robust method for dual-arm non-prehensile manipulation, addressing critical limitations in current robotic capabilities and providing a flexible, learning-enhanced control strategy applicable to a range of service robotics challenges.
