# Reachable Sets for Safe, Real-Time Manipulator Trajectory Design

> [!summary] Summary
> The Autonomous Reachability-based Manipulator Trajectory Design (ARMTD) method enables real-time, provably safe motion planning for high-dimensional robotic arms. This approach achieves a 0% crash rate in complex simulations and hardware demonstrations by constructing conservative reachable sets from lower-dimensional joint reachable sets and integrating them into an optimization framework.

## Problem

- Robotic arms face a dilemma in complex, unconstrained environments: achieving both rigorous safety guarantees and real-time operational performance.
- Traditional motion planning methods often rely on approximations or large safety buffers to achieve real-time operation, potentially compromising performance or safety assurances.
- Applying reachable set analysis to high-dimensional manipulators has been historically limited by the 'curse of dimensionality' and complex nonlinear kinematics, hindering optimization over a continuous range of safe trajectories.

## Method

- **ARMTD** employs a ==receding-horizon planning== framework that constructs conservative workspace reachable sets for high-dimensional manipulators by composing lower-dimensional joint reachable sets using ==zonotopes== and ==rotatotopes==.
- It integrates ==sub-differentiable safety constraints== for collision avoidance, self-intersection avoidance, and joint limit adherence, derived directly from the reachable sets, into a real-time ==nonlinear optimization== problem.
- A parallelized algorithm, utilizing GPUs, enables rapid reachable set construction and constraint generation, ensuring the overall planning process operates within strict real-time budgets (e.g., 0.5 seconds per iteration) and incorporates a perpetual ==fail-safe braking maneuver==.

## Results

- ARMTD achieved a **0% crash rate** in extensive simulations (**100** random obstacle tasks, **7** hard scenarios) and hardware demonstrations, demonstrating superior safety compared to CHOMP, which had an **18% crash rate** in random scenarios and **100%** in hard scenarios.
- The method demonstrated real-time performance with a mean solve time of **0.273 seconds** per planning iteration (within a **0.5s** budget) for random obstacle scenarios, and successfully adapted to dynamic obstacles in hardware.
- Coupling ARMTD with a high-level planner (RRT*) enabled completion of **5 out of 7** challenging 'hard scenarios' while maintaining a **0% crash rate**, demonstrating its ability to enhance existing planners with safety guarantees.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-dimensional workspace reachable sets for robotic manipulators can be tractably and conservatively approximated by composing lower-dimensional joint reachable sets, overcoming the traditional 'curse of dimensionality' for such systems.
> - Sub-differentiable safety constraints, derived from these time-varying reachable sets, can be effectively incorporated into a real-time trajectory optimization problem, enabling mathematically provable collision avoidance.
> - A receding-horizon framework with a guaranteed fail-safe mechanism ensures continuous safety; if a new safe plan cannot be found, a predefined safe maneuver from the previous iteration is executed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: enabling robotic arms to operate safely and in real-time within complex, unconstrained environments, particularly those involving human interaction. A recurring issue in current motion planning algorithms is a trade-off between guaranteeing safety and achieving real-time performance. Generating plans with formal safety certificates can be computationally intensive, while rapidly generated plans may lack comprehensive safety assurances.

The broader landscape of motion planning is categorized into three paradigms based on where safety is primarily enforced:
1. **Path Planner Responsibility:** Many traditional methods fall into this category, where a collision-free path is first generated and then timed to create a trajectory. These often rely on discrete representations of the robot's configuration space, requiring approximations (e.g., buffering the robot's volume, linearizing collision penalties) to account for discretization and achieve real-time operation. This typically necessitates fine discretization or large safety buffers, which can compromise performance.
2. **Trajectory Planner Responsibility:** This paradigm, which ARMTD extends, focuses on computing a reachable set (RS) for a family of trajectories, rather than a swept volume for a single path. While methods in this category (e.g., using sums-of-squares programming or zonotope reachability analysis) can offer both safety and real-time performance in receding-horizon planning, they have historically been limited by the "curse of dimensionality." This limitation has prevented their direct application to the high-dimensional models typical of robotic manipulators. Existing uses of RS in manipulator planning have largely been restricted to collision-checking precomputed single trajectories or controlling to predefined setpoints.
3. **Tracking Controller Responsibility:** This approach aims to ensure safety at the lowest level, through supervisory controllers for predefined trajectories or states. Techniques like Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis are employed, but these also face scalability challenges with high-dimensional systems, similar to the second paradigm.

This work specifically positions itself within and extends the second paradigm. It identifies the gap where reachable sets have not been effectively used for *optimizing over a continuum of safe trajectories* for high-dimensional robotic arms due to the computational complexity arising from high-dimensional configuration spaces and nonlinear kinematics. ARMTD attempts to overcome these challenges by composing high-dimensional workspace reachable sets from lower-dimensional joint reachable sets, making it feasible to perform real-time, provably-safe trajectory optimization for robotic manipulators.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the necessity for robotic arms to reliably and safely operate in diverse, unstructured environments, especially when interacting with or being in proximity to humans. Current robotic motion planning solutions often present a dilemma: either prioritize rigorous safety guarantees, which can lead to slow planning times, or optimize for real-time performance, potentially at the cost of formal safety assurances. The paper aims to bridge this gap by developing a method that offers both real-time performance and provable safety.

The key objectives of the proposed Autonomous Reachability-based Manipulator Trajectory Design (ARMTD) method are:

1. **Develop a Conservative Reachable Set Construction Method:** To design a technique that can conservatively generate the reachable set (RS) for high-dimensional, redundant robotic manipulators. This includes addressing the complexities of their kinematic structure and workspace representation.
2. **Enable Real-Time, Provably-Safe Trajectory Optimization:** To implement a parallelized algorithm that performs receding-horizon trajectory optimization. This algorithm must incorporate safety guarantees and operate within a strict real-time budget.
3. **Formulate Novel Safety Constraints:** To derive sub-differentiable constraints for collision avoidance, self-intersection avoidance, and joint limit adherence directly from the reachable sets, allowing for their integration into an optimization framework.
4. **Demonstrate Effectiveness in Simulation and Hardware:** To validate the method's capabilities through extensive simulations in various environments (random obstacles, complex "hard scenarios") and on physical robotic hardware. This includes comparing its safety and performance against existing non-receding-horizon planners.
5. **Overcome Dimensionality Challenges:** To devise a strategy that effectively manages the curse of dimensionality often encountered when applying reachable set analysis to multi-degree-of-freedom robotic arms. This is achieved by composing workspace RS from lower-dimensional joint-level RS.
6. **Ensure Perpetual Safety:** To design the receding-horizon framework such that if a new safe plan cannot be found within the allotted time, a pre-defined fail-safe maneuver (braking to a stop) from the previous iteration ensures the robot's continued safety.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for robotic arms to be both agile and intrinsically safe, moving beyond ad-hoc safety mechanisms to mathematically guaranteed collision-free operation in dynamic, real-world contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ARMTD operates within a receding-horizon planning framework, which iteratively generates and executes short-duration trajectories while continuously updating its understanding of the environment. The methodology involves both offline pre-computation and online real-time planning phases.

**A. Arm, Obstacles, and Trajectory Parameters**

1. **Arm Model:** The method considers an arm with $n_q$ revolute joints and $n_q+1$ links. The configuration space $Q$ consists of joint angles $q = (q_1, \dots, q_{n_q})$. The forward occupancy (FO) function maps a configuration $q$ to the volume occupied by the entire arm in the 3D workspace $W$. Each joint has physical limits for position, speed, and acceleration.
2. **Obstacle Model:** Obstacles are represented as compact, static sets $O \subset W$. The method assumes access to a conservative estimate of their size and location.
3. **Trajectory Parameterization:** Trajectories are parameterized kinematically. Each trajectory $q(t; k)$ is defined over a time interval $T = [0, t_f]$, where $k \in K \subset \mathbb{R}^{n_k}$ is a set of trajectory parameters. The specific implementation uses $k = (k_v, k_a)$, where $k_v \in \mathbb{R}^{n_q}$ represents an initial velocity and $k_a \in \mathbb{R}^{n_q}$ a constant acceleration over an initial segment $[0, t_{plan})$. A critical aspect is that each parameterized trajectory includes a fail-safe maneuver, braking to a stop over the interval $[t_{plan}, t_f]$. The parameters $k_v$ and $k_a$ for each joint are chosen from compact intervals $K_i = K_{v_i} \times K_{a_i}$ to facilitate reachability analysis.

**B. Offline Reachability Analysis (Joint Reachable Sets - JRSs)**

The offline phase computes parameterized Joint Reachable Sets (JRSs) for each individual joint.
1. **Theory:** Since joint angles represent rotations, the method tracks the sine and cosine of each joint angle. For each joint $i$, a differential equation is formulated for $(cos(q_i(t;k)), sin(q_i(t;k)))$ with $k$ as a constant. The JRS $J_i$ is defined as the set of all such $(c,s,k)$ tuples reachable over time $t \in T$ for all $k \in K$.
2. **Implementation with Zonotopes:** Zonotopes are used to represent these JRSs. A zonotope is a convex set defined by a center vector and a set of generator vectors, with each generator having an indeterminate coefficient between -1 and 1. The time horizon $T$ is partitioned into discrete intervals. For each interval, a zonotope $J_i(t)$ is computed to overapproximate the reachable values of $(cos(q_i), sin(q_i), k)$ for joint $i$. An open-source toolbox is utilized for this overapproximation, leveraging the dynamics and initial set. A key property is that the zonotopes are structured to have specific "sliceable" generators corresponding to the trajectory parameters $k_v$ and $k_a$.

**C. Online Planning**

In each receding-horizon iteration, ARMTD performs the following steps (Alg. 3):

1. **Reachable Set Construction (of the full arm):**
 - **Slicing JRSs:** The estimated future initial velocity $\dot{\tilde{q}}$ from the previous plan is used to "slice" the precomputed JRSs $J_i(t)$. This operation evaluates the $k_v$-sliceable generators of each $J_i(t)$ based on $\dot{\tilde{q}}$, yielding new zonotopes $S_i(t)$ that represent reachable sine/cosine values for a *specific* initial velocity but *ranges* of acceleration parameters $k_a$.
 - **Matrix Zonotopes:** Each $S_i(t)$ is transformed into a matrix zonotope $M_i(t)$ that overapproximates the rotation matrix $R_i(q_i(t;k))$. This transformation also accounts for the initial joint angle $\tilde{q}_i$.
 - **Rotatotopes for Link Occupancy:** The link volumes $L_i$ and joint displacements $l_i$ (represented as zonotopes) are multiplied by sequences of matrix zonotopes $M_j(t)$ and then combined using Minkowski sums. This forms a rotatotope $V_i(t)$ that conservatively overapproximates the forward occupancy $FO_i(q(t;k))$ for each link $i$. Rotatotopes are a type of polynomial zonotope designed to handle products of zonotopes, representing the rotated and displaced link volumes.
 - **Generator Reduction:** To manage the exponential growth of generators during rotatotope multiplication, a reduction technique is applied after each product, keeping the largest generators and overapproximating the remainder with an axis-aligned box. This reduces conservatism minimally while maintaining computational tractability.
 - **Parallelization:** The construction of $V_i(t)$ for all links and time steps is designed to run in parallel, typically on a GPU.

2. **Constraint Generation:**
 - **Joint Limit Constraints ($K_{lim}$):** Analytic functions $h_{lim}(k_a)$ are formulated based on the piecewise quadratic nature of $q(t;k)$ and piecewise linear nature of $\dot{q}(t;k)$ to ensure joint positions and velocities remain within physical limits.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance Constraints ($K_{obs}$):** For each link $i$, time step $t$, and obstacle $O$:
 - The rotatope $V_i(t)$ is conceptually split into two parts: $V_{i,slc}(t)$ (containing only fully $k_a$-sliceable generators) and $V_{i,buf}(t)$ (containing remaining generators).
 - Obstacles $O$ are overapproximated by zonotopes.
 - An "inflated" obstacle $O_{buf}$ is computed by Minkowski summing $O$ with $V_{i,buf}(t)$.
 - The actual collision check is performed by evaluating $V_{i,slc}(t)$ at a particular $k_a$ (which becomes a point) and checking if this point is outside the inflated obstacle $O_{buf}$. This yields a sub-differentiable function $h_{obs}(i, t, O, k_a) < 0$ for collision-free trajectories. This approach is conservative, as the buffered part of the rotatope always contributes to the obstacle expansion, guaranteeing safety.
 - **Self-Intersection Constraints:** Similar to obstacle avoidance, self-intersection between specific link pairs is formulated using the relative positions of their rotatotopes, generating $h_{self}(i, j, t, k_a) < 0$.
 - **Parallelization:** Constraint generation is also parallelized across obstacles and time steps.

3. **Trajectory Optimization:**
 - **Nonlinear Program:** ARMTD solves a nonlinear program (NLP) over the acceleration parameters $k_a$ (as the initial velocity $k_v$ is fixed by slicing). The objective is to minimize an arbitrary user-specified cost function $f(k_a)$ (e.g., tracking a waypoint from a high-level planner) subject to all generated safety constraints ($h_{obs}<0$, $h_{self}<0$, $h_{lim}<0$).
 - **Solver:** IPOPT is used, leveraging analytic gradients/subgradients for the cost and constraints.
 - **Fail-Safe:** If the NLP cannot find a feasible solution within the allocated $t_{plan}$ seconds, the arm executes the fail-safe braking maneuver from the *previous* planning iteration, ensuring continuous safety.

This integrated methodology provides a framework for real-time, provably-safe motion planning by systematically constructing conservative reachable sets and using them to generate hard constraints for trajectory optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of ARMTD was evaluated through both simulations and hardware demonstrations, with comparisons against CHOMP, a prominent trajectory optimization planner.

**A. Simulation Demonstrations**

1. **Random Obstacles Scenarios:** This set involved 100 tasks with random start/goal configurations and varying numbers (4 to 40) of randomly placed box-shaped obstacles.
 - **ARMTD (with Straight-Line High-Level Planner - HLP):**
 - Reached 84 out of 100 goals.
 - Recorded 0 crashes (i.e., it stopped safely 16 times without finding a new safe trajectory).
 - Mean Solve Time (MST): 0.273 seconds per planning iteration (within the 0.5s `t_plan` budget).
 - Mean Normalized Path Distance (MNPD): 1.076.
 - **CHOMP:**
 - Reached 82 out of 100 goals.
 - Recorded 18 crashes. (CHOMP treats obstacles as cost penalties rather than hard constraints, allowing it to converge to unsafe trajectories if not tuned extensively).
 - MST: 0.177 seconds (total for the single optimization program).
 - MNPD: 1.511.
 - **Comparison:** ARMTD demonstrated a 0% crash rate compared to CHOMP's 18% crash rate, confirming its safety guarantees. ARMTD's MNPD was 24% smaller, suggesting more direct paths to goals when successful.

2. **Hard Scenarios:** This set consisted of seven complex, pre-defined tasks designed to challenge planners (e.g., navigating under tables, through narrow gaps, between shelves).
 - **ARMTD (with Straight-Line HLP):** Did not complete any tasks, but maintained safety in all 7 scenarios (stopping safely when no feasible plan was found).
 - **ARMTD (with RRT* HLP):** Successfully completed 5 out of 7 scenarios, demonstrating that coupling ARMTD with a more capable high-level planner (RRT* helps define better waypoints) improves task completion rates while still maintaining 0 crashes.
 - **CHOMP:** Converged to trajectories with collisions in all 7 hard scenarios, resulting in 7 crashes.
 - **Comparison:** ARMTD consistently ensured safety, even when a path to the goal could not be found, whereas CHOMP failed to find collision-free trajectories in these challenging environments.

**B. Hardware Demonstrations**

- ARMTD was deployed on a Fetch mobile manipulator (a 6-DOF arm) and successfully performed various tasks, including navigating around static obstacles and reacting to suddenly introduced dynamic obstacles (e.g., a box, a vase, a quadrotor).
- Dynamic obstacles were tracked using motion capture and treated as static within each planning iteration.
- The system demonstrated real-time reactivity to these changes, adjusting its trajectory without any observed collisions. This provides empirical evidence for ARMTD's ability to maintain safety and responsiveness in physical applications.

**C. Computational Performance**

- **Reachable Set Composition (Alg. 2):** Approximately 10–20 ms, utilizing a GPU with CUDA for parallelization.
- **Constraint Generation:** Approximately 10–20 ms for 20 obstacles, also parallelized on the GPU.
- **Trajectory Optimization (IPOPT):** 100–200 ms for scenes with 20 random obstacles, including analytic gradient/subgradient computations.
- The total planning time for a single iteration remained within the `t_plan = 0.5s` budget, confirming its real-time feasibility.

In summary, the results indicate that ARMTD provides strong safety guarantees, preventing any collisions in both simulation and hardware tests, which contrasts with CHOMP's observed crash rates in complex scenarios. ARMTD also demonstrates real-time performance and the ability to integrate with high-level planners to improve task completion while maintaining safety.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented by Holmes et al. makes several significant contributions to the field of robotic motion planning, with notable potential impacts:

1. **Bridging the Safety-Performance Gap:** The primary significance of ARMTD lies in its ability to simultaneously provide provable safety guarantees and real-time performance for robotic manipulators. This addresses a critical trade-off that often limits the deployment of robotic arms in complex, unstructured, and human-proximate environments. By offering certified collision-free trajectories within strict time limits, ARMTD advances the practical applicability of robots in areas such as collaborative robotics, service robotics, and manufacturing.

2. **Overcoming the Curse of Dimensionality for Reachable Sets:** The methodology introduces a novel approach to handle the high dimensionality of robotic manipulators within a reachable set framework. By composing high-dimensional workspace reachable sets from low-dimensional joint reachable sets using zonotopes and rotatotopes, ARMTD provides a tractable solution to a problem that has historically limited the application of exact reachability analysis to complex robotic systems. This approach could inspire similar decomposition strategies for other high-dimensional control problems.

3. **Enhancing Robot Reliability and Trust:** The formal safety guarantees offered by ARMTD contribute directly to increasing the reliability of robotic systems. In scenarios where human safety is paramount, provable non-collision is essential for building trust and facilitating broader adoption of autonomous robots. The demonstration of zero crashes in both extensive simulations and hardware experiments supports this aspect.

4. **Flexible Safety Layer for High-Level Planners:** ARMTD functions as a safety layer on top of arbitrary high-level planners. This flexibility allows designers to use existing path planners (even those not inherently safe, like RRT* for trajectory generation) for task guidance, while ARMTD ensures that the resulting trajectories are safe. This modularity simplifies the overall planning architecture and allows for the integration of various task-specific planning strategies without compromising fundamental safety.

5. **Foundation for Advanced Capabilities:** The use of time-varying reachable sets in ARMTD lays a foundation for future extensions to more complex real-world scenarios. The paper specifically highlights potential for:
 - **Dynamic Environments:** The framework can be extended to handle moving obstacles and other dynamic elements, drawing on prior work in this area.
 - **Uncertainty Management:** It provides a basis for incorporating and accounting for various forms of uncertainty, such as sensing noise and tracking errors, into the safety analysis.
 - **Planning with Grasped Objects:** The method can naturally adapt to changes in the robot's geometry due to grasping objects, by updating the link volume representations.
 - **Human-Robot Interaction:** While not yet demonstrated with human subjects, the principles of guaranteed collision avoidance are directly applicable to planning around people, a critical step towards truly collaborative robotics.

In conclusion, ARMTD represents a step forward in robust and safe robotic autonomy. By demonstrating a practical and provably safe real-time trajectory planning solution for multi-DOF manipulators, it paves the way for wider and safer deployment of robots in diverse and challenging environments.
