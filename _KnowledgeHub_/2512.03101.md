# ALARM: Automated MLLM-Based Anomaly Detection in Complex-EnviRonment Monitoring with Uncertainty Quantification

> [!summary] Summary
> ALARM adds uncertainty quantification to MLLM-based visual anomaly detection in ambiguous settings. It splits the reasoning chain into Data Comprehension, Analytical Thinking, and Reflection, scoring each stage from ensemble disagreement via matrix factorization plus a learned hypothesis-revision probability, then blends them with accuracy-optimized weights and defers high-uncertainty cases to humans under a cost-chosen rejection rate, reaching 84.34% smart-home accuracy.

## Problem

- MLLM anomaly detectors emit deterministic labels without confidence or abstention, so contextual smart-home ambiguity, normal in one household and abnormal in another, yields unflagged errors.
- Existing video anomaly detection studies report aggregate accuracy on apparent anomalies like crime, leaving robustness under ambiguous, context-dependent conditions largely unquantified and underexplored.
- Generic LLM uncertainty methods score a single final output and never decompose uncertainty across pipeline stages, so they cannot localize where in reasoning the uncertainty originates.

## Method

- A ==probabilistic decomposition== of the five-step reasoning chain into ==Data Comprehension==, ==Analytical Thinking==, and ==Reflection==, giving a final score that is a weighted sum of three stage-specific uncertainty scores.
- Data and task scores come from ==probabilistic matrix factorization== reconstruction error over pairwise cosine-similarity matrices across a five-MLLM ensemble; the reflection score is a ==logistic model== of hypothesis-revision probability.
- ==Selective classification== with a threshold set by rejection rate, weights fit by ==cross-validated sample-average approximation==, and optimal rejection rate solved from a cost objective balancing human labor against accuracy regret.

## Results

- On SmartHome-Bench (**1,203** videos) at **5%** rejection, non-deferred test cases reach **84.34%** accuracy, **90.36%** recall, **86.17** F1: **7.75** and **9.16** points over TRLC.
- On the ambiguous subset accuracy is **71.19%**, **9.65** points over TRLC and above LAC's **67.94**; wound classification (**432** images) reaches **91.72%** versus **88.53%** for random drop.
- Ablations: smart-home accuracy peaks near **25%** rejection and gains **6-8%** moving from two to three MLLMs; Gaussian-smoothed weights shift accuracy typically within **0.006**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Stage-wise uncertainty decomposition beats any single score because the sources are complementary; when one stage is uninformative, the optimally weighted combination still holds up.
> - Even random abstention lowers risk whenever the deferred-to expert is more accurate, so gains over random dropping, not over no abstention, are the honest baseline.
> - At least three MLLMs are needed before stage-wise uncertainty separates methods; value beyond three is domain-dependent, plateauing in one setting and still rising in another.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A clip description shows a young child in brown overalls outside in a snowy yard with a black dog that appears friendly. The first reasoning step treats the scene as normal. A rule about children outdoors without supervision then leads reflection to mark it abnormal. The example shows how a rule can change a judgment. It is not evidence that the child is in fact unsupervised.

The case points to a practical weakness in visual anomaly detection for settings such as smart homes. An event can look harmless in one household and risky in another. An MLLM's confident-sounding explanation also does not tell a user when its judgment is unreliable.

ALARM makes uncertainty a staged part of the decision process. It compares models' descriptions of the scene, assesses variation in their task reasoning, and estimates whether added rules or reference information would change their initial judgment. The authors frame this as a general approach to uncertainty in multi-stage LLM decisions. The evidence here comes from two visual case studies.

Data Comprehension, Analytical Thinking, and Reflection. The dog seems friendly and the child shows no fear or distress, so the first hypothesis is "Normal." Reflection applies a rule to identify children outdoors without supervision and produces an "Abnormal" final decision. Three component scores and their weights form an integrated uncertainty score.

### 2. Where uncertainty enters

ALARM follows a three-stage sequence:

1. **Data Comprehension** produces a scene description. Its uncertainty is semantic disagreement among multiple MLLMs describing the same input.
1. **Analytical Thinking** reasons from that description under the task instructions. Its uncertainty is variation in reasoning outcomes.
1. **Reflection** revisits the initial hypothesis using side information such as expert rules or reference examples. Its score estimates the probability that reflection changes the initial hypothesis.

ALARM combines the three scores using weights optimized on training data. The weights are learned using five-fold cross-validation.

High-scoring cases lead to *abstention*: the system withholds an automatic prediction for them. A threshold sets the rejection rate, and retained cases proceed to prediction.

The framework's decision rule assumes a human reviewer is more accurate than an MLLM and more costly. In the reported tests, rejected cases are excluded rather than sent to humans.

### 3. Does the score pick the right cases?

The sharpest test compares ALARM with random drop at the same rejection rate. Random drop discards the same number of cases at random. The question is whether ALARM's score identifies likely errors better than that.

The smart-home dataset has 1,203 videos. Of these, 91 are labeled ambiguous because even humans have difficulty deciding whether they are normal or abnormal.

The metric is overall accuracy: the fraction of retained test examples classified correctly. At the default 5% rejection rate, ALARM's overall accuracy is 84.34%. The same reasoning chain with random drop reaches 81.62%, a difference of 2.72 percentage points. Retained cases are classified by majority vote across five MLLMs, without any human involvement. ALARM also leads the listed methods on the ambiguous-video accuracy measure.

Figure 4 measures the proportion of rejected cases that were actually misclassified. The authors report that ALARM identifies a high proportion of errors at low rejection rates, while random drop remains flat. At higher rejection rates, ALARM's proportion falls because less-certain cases have already been rejected.

The pattern differs by domain. In wound classification, random drop is closer to some individual uncertainty components than it is in smart-home monitoring.

Ratio of rejected cases that are misclassified versus rejection rate P (%), for (a) smart home and (b) wound classification. In smart home, ALARM is highest at low rejection rates and declines as P increases, while Random Drop is roughly flat. The panels use different vertical scales.

### 4. A triage score, not a verdict

The snowy-yard example shows how reflection with a contextual rule can change a plausible first judgment. The random-drop comparison tests whether ALARM's uncertainty score can prioritize which automatic predictions to withhold. In the smart-home evaluation, it selects cases better than random drop.

The experiments measure a system that flags cases. They do not establish human-review accuracy or savings in cost, time, or computation. They also do not demonstrate a working human-review workflow.
