# Nexus: Same Pretraining Loss, Better Downstream Generalization via Common Minima

> [!summary] Summary
> The research introduces Nexus, a novel optimizer that improves Large Language Model generalization on downstream tasks and out-of-distribution data while achieving comparable pretraining loss to baselines. It leverages a dual-loop mechanism to encourage geometric closeness of task-specific minima during pretraining, a distinct implicit bias shown to enhance performance without requiring additional computational resources.

## Problem

- Standard LLM pretraining minimizes averaged loss across heterogeneous data sources, but often converges to parameter configurations where individual task-specific minima are geometrically distant.
- This 'distance' between task-specific minima can hinder a model's ability to generalize effectively to unseen downstream and out-of-distribution tasks, even when pretraining loss is low.
- Existing optimizers do not inherently encourage the geometric closeness of task-specific minima, and it was unclear if this specific geometric property could be leveraged for better generalization independently of pretraining loss.

## Method

- Proposed =='closeness of task-specific minima'== as a distinct ==second-order implicit generalization bias==, separate from factors like loss landscape flatness.
- Developed **Nexus**, a novel optimizer that uses a ==dual-loop mechanism== to implicitly maximize the ==cosine similarity== of gradients from different pretraining tasks.
- The inner loop performs multiple ==Normalized SGD== steps on an auxiliary model to compute a =='pseudo-gradient'== that encourages task-specific minima to converge closer, which is then used by a standard outer optimizer.

## Results

- Nexus consistently improved average downstream benchmark accuracy by up to **3.2%** on **2.3B** models and achieved up to **15.0%** higher accuracy on GSM8k, **8.0%** on MATH, and **4.0%** on HumanEval for **3B** models, despite achieving nearly identical pretraining validation loss.
- The generalization benefits of Nexus scale monotonically with increasing model capacity and persist with extended pretraining durations, demonstrating robust and stable improvements.
- Nexus exhibits dynamic balancing, shifting performance gains to general knowledge and reasoning domains when math data is abundant, and resists overfitting to contaminated public datasets, enhancing performance on uncontaminated, sparse domains like coding.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Improving the geometric closeness of task-specific minima during LLM pretraining directly enhances downstream generalization and out-of-distribution performance, even when the overall pretraining loss remains comparable to baselines.
> - Maximizing gradient similarity among diverse pretraining tasks serves as a tractable proxy for achieving geometric closeness of task-specific minima, avoiding computationally expensive direct optimization.
> - The multi-step inner loop of the Nexus optimizer is crucial for its 'consensus-seeking' property, dynamically balancing and implicitly prioritizing under-optimized tasks within a heterogeneous data mixture.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) rely heavily on pretraining, which consumes the majority of computational resources and data. This process involves learning from massive and diverse datasets, typically by minimizing an averaged loss across multiple data sources. The prevalent approach in LLM pretraining research often focuses on developing optimizers that accelerate convergence or reduce the pretraining loss itself. Examples include AdamW, Muon, Lion, and Sophia, which aim for faster optimization or improved pretraining error.

This work addresses a distinct aspect of LLM training: the geometric properties of the converged model parameters within the loss landscape and their influence on downstream generalization. While prior literature has explored implicit biases in deep learning, often focusing on concepts like sharpness or margins, this paper introduces and emphasizes the concept of "closeness" among task-specific minimizers. It investigates whether standard optimizers, despite achieving low pretraining loss, might converge to suboptimal geometric configurations for generalization.

The methodology draws inspiration from the nested inner-outer loop optimization structures found in meta-learning and decentralized learning. However, it repurposes this structure not for few-shot adaptation or communication efficiency, but for explicitly regularizing the geometric configuration of the loss landscape during large-scale LLM pretraining. This distinguishes it from other implicit bias studies by focusing on parameter and gradient closeness as a key factor for out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, even when the pretraining loss remains constant. It also offers a geometric explanation for the generalization benefits of Normalized SGD (NSGD) by interpreting it as a special case of the proposed method. The paper thus contributes to understanding and explicitly engineering the implicit biases of optimizers to enhance LLM capabilities beyond mere pretraining loss reduction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate a geometric question concerning the converged state of large language models during pretraining: whether the model converges to a common minimizer across all data sources (an "Intersection-type Minimizer") or merely a minimizer of the summed loss (an "Average-type Minimizer").

The core motivation is a hypothesis that the geometric "closeness" of task-specific minima is intrinsically linked to downstream generalization capabilities. The authors posit that even if two different converged parameter sets yield the exact same pretraining loss, their performance on unseen downstream tasks could vary significantly depending on this geometric closeness. They illustrate this with a simplified scenario (Fig. 2), where an Intersection-type Minimizer, where individual task minima are geometrically close, is hypothesized to lead to better downstream generalization than an Average-type Minimizer, where individual task minima are distant.

This hypothesis is substantiated through theoretical analysis:
- A second-order Taylor expansion (Eq. 4) of the downstream loss (L_T) around its task minimizer (θ*T) reveals that the loss at the pretraining minimizer (θ*train) is influenced by two factors: the local flatness of the landscape and the "closeness" (the squared Euclidean distance between θ*train and θ*T). The paper focuses on enhancing closeness.
- Theorem 2.1 provides a mathematical basis using strictly quadratic loss functions, demonstrating that for a fixed training loss, the expected downstream error is directly proportional to the variance of the task-specific minimizers (a measure of "distant" minima). This implies that trading intrinsic task loss for improved closeness leads to better out-of-distribution generalization.
- Theorem 2.2 extends this intuition to more general loss landscapes exhibiting local and directional strong convexity, showing an upper bound on the generalization error that scales with task variance.

The practical motivation arises from the observation that standard optimizers like AdamW often converge to configurations where task-specific minima are geometrically distant. The challenge lies in directly optimizing for this geometric "closeness," as it requires knowing the exact minimizer of each task (L_k) at every training step, which is computationally infeasible. Therefore, a key motivation is to devise a computationally tractable method to implicitly or explicitly encourage this closeness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology revolves around the concept of "closeness" and an optimizer named Nexus designed to promote it.

**Problem Formulation and Theoretical Basis:**
The pretraining corpus is defined as a union of *K* distinct data sources, D_train = ∪_k=1^K D_k, each with a sampling probability α_k. The weighted empirical loss for the *k*-th source is L_k(θ), and the total pretraining objective is L_train(θ) = (1/K) Σ_k=1^K L_k(θ). The goal is to improve performance on a downstream task T, measured by L_T(θ*train). The theoretical foundation, as described in the objectives, links downstream generalization to the geometric closeness between the converged pretraining parameters (θ*train) and the optimal parameters for the downstream task (θ*T), as well as the variance of task-specific minimizers (σ^2) across the pretraining tasks.

**Gradient Similarity as a Proxy for Closeness:**
To address the intractability of directly optimizing for parameter closeness, the authors introduce gradient similarity as a tractable proxy. Theorem 3.1 establishes that the sum of negative gradient dot products (Σ_i≠j -∇L_i(θ)⊤∇L_j(θ)) and, consequently, the sum of (1 - CosSim(∇L_i(θ), ∇L_j(θ))) upper bounds the average squared distance between the converged parameter θ and each task's minimizer θ*k. This implies that maximizing gradient similarity among training tasks encourages parameter closeness. Furthermore, if this gradient closeness generalizes to similarity between the training objective and the downstream task (CosSim(∇L_train, ∇L_T)), it directly leads to a reduction in downstream loss (Eq. 8), in a first-order sense.

**Nexus Optimizer (Algorithm 1):**
The Nexus optimizer is proposed to maximize this gradient similarity. Directly computing the gradient of cosine similarity involves complex Jacobian-vector products (Eq. 9). Nexus approximates this through a dual-loop mechanism:
- **Inner Loop**: For each outer iteration, *K* sequential Normalized SGD (NSGD) steps are performed. In each inner step *m*, a task index *s_m* is sampled, and the parameters θ_t,m-1 are updated using the normalized gradient of L_s_m(θ_t,m-1) with a small inner learning rate γ. This sequential update accumulates displacements.
- **Pseudo-Gradient Computation**: The total displacement of the parameters after *K* inner steps, (θ_t,0 - θ_t,K), is computed. This displacement, denoted as ˆg_t, serves as the pseudo-gradient for the outer optimizer.
- **Outer Loop**: A standard optimizer (e.g., AdamW) then uses this ˆg_t to update the main model parameters θ_t.
Theorem 3.2 demonstrates that the expected update direction of Nexus effectively minimizes a second-order objective J_2nd(θ) (Eq. 11), which includes the average pretraining loss and a term that explicitly maximizes the sum of cosine similarities between pairs of task gradients (CosSim(∇L_i, ∇L_j)). The sequential nature of the inner loop inherently approximates the Jacobian-vector product needed for this maximization.

**Adapting Nexus to Practical Pretraining (Algorithm 3):**
To make Nexus suitable for large-scale LLM pretraining with many data sources and mini-batch accumulation, an engineering adaptation is introduced:
- An auxiliary `inner_model` is maintained, a clone of the main model.
- For each mini-batch, an immediate NSGD update is performed on `inner_model`. This process approximates the Hessian-gradient product implicitly.
- At the accumulation step boundary (after a specified number of mini-batches), the displacement between `inner_model` and the main `model` (inner_model - model) is used as the pseudo-gradient ˆg for the outer optimizer (e.g., AdamW).
- The `inner_model` is then re-synchronized with the main `model`.
This adaptation allows Nexus to maximize cosine similarity between mini-batches, which are likely from different sources in a diverse pretraining corpus. Per-parameter normalization is used for NSGD, inspired by μP, to potentially improve stability and hyperparameter transfer. The authors state that this adaptation incurs negligible extra computational cost (same number of forward/backward passes) and minimal memory overhead, which can be further optimized.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of Nexus was conducted across various settings, including different model scales (130M to 3B parameters), data mixtures, learning rate schedules, and training compute allocations, primarily using an in-house cleaned pretraining dataset.

**1. "Same Pretraining Loss, Better Downstream Task":**
- Nexus consistently achieved nearly identical pretraining validation losses compared to standard AdamW baselines (e.g., a negligible difference of 0.004 on the 3B model).
- Despite this parity in pretraining loss, Nexus demonstrated consistent and substantial improvements across diverse downstream tasks and out-of-distribution (OOD) metrics. On the 3B model, Nexus reduced OOD validation loss by 0.012.
- Significant accuracy gains were observed on complex reasoning tasks: +15.0% on GSM8k, +8.0% on MATH, and +4.0% on HumanEval for the 3B model.
- Comparison with Muon revealed that while Muon achieved downstream improvements by significantly reducing pretraining loss, Nexus attained comparable downstream performance by introducing a beneficial implicit bias at a similar pretraining loss level as AdamW, indicating a distinct optimization pathway.
- Analysis of model outputs indicated that Nexus's performance gains were largely additive; it correctly answered questions that AdamW failed, while retaining most of AdamW's successes.

**2. Scaling Analysis (Model Size and Training Tokens):**
- **Model Size**: The "same pretraining loss, better downstream" phenomenon was observed across all tested model sizes, from 130M to 2.3B parameters. The relative performance gains of Nexus amplified with increasing model capacity, with average benchmark accuracy improvements ranging from +0.8% for the 130M model to +3.2% for the 2.3B model. This suggests that Nexus effectively leverages increased model expressive power.
- **Training Tokens**: Extending the training duration of the 3B model from 2x to 4x Chinchilla optimal tokens did not diminish Nexus's advantage. The performance gap between Nexus and AdamW was maintained or even slightly widened, suggesting that standard SGD's implicit bias is insufficient to reach optimal geometric closeness even with increased compute.

**3. Robustness to Data Mixing:**
- Experiments with varying proportions of math data (10%, 40%, 70%) demonstrated that Nexus acted as a dynamic data balancer. It yielded greater relative gains on domains that were comparatively sample-sparse or harder to learn within the mixture. For instance, when math data was sparse (10%), Nexus provided the largest gains on math reasoning. Conversely, when math data was dense (70%), Nexus shifted its advantages to general knowledge (MMLU) and broad reasoning (GPQA) domains, which became relatively scarcer.
- Nexus exhibited higher stability against drastic changes in data mixture compared to the baseline, mitigating performance fluctuations.

**4. Robustness to Learning Rate Schedule:**
- The observed generalization benefits of Nexus were robust across different learning rate schedulers, including Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) and Cosine annealing schedules. This indicates that the implicit bias introduced by Nexus is orthogonal to the specific learning rate dynamics.

**5. Training Dynamics and Implicit Biases:**
- Nexus was shown to effectively increase gradient similarity within the pretraining corpus, and this closeness generalized to unseen downstream tasks. This downstream gradient closeness translated into lower downstream losses and improved benchmark performance.
- Empirical loss landscape visualizations confirmed that Nexus reduced the geometric distance between the converged parameter and task-specific minimizers, aligning with the theoretical analysis.
- Ablation studies showed that other optimizers like Muon did not possess a superior implicit bias for downstream generalization beyond what was explained by pretraining loss reduction.
- Merely normalizing gradients (NSGD with K=1) did not introduce the favorable implicit bias, confirming that multiple inner steps are essential for Nexus's consensus-seeking property (Theorem 3.2).
- Directly optimizing dot product similarity, instead of cosine similarity, proved impractical as it disrupted pretraining loss minimization by incentivizing larger gradient norms rather than genuine consensus.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work provides empirical evidence and theoretical justification for the importance of geometric "closeness" among task-specific minimizers during large language model pretraining for enhancing downstream generalization. It challenges the conventional reliance on pretraining loss as the sole or primary metric for evaluating model quality, demonstrating that models can achieve the same pretraining loss yet exhibit substantially different downstream capabilities depending on the implicit biases of the optimization process.

The proposed Nexus optimizer offers a practical and computationally efficient method to explicitly engineer this beneficial implicit bias. By encouraging gradient similarity and, consequently, geometric closeness, Nexus consistently boosts downstream performance across a range of models, tasks, and training configurations without compromising the pretraining objective. The observation that Nexus's benefits amplify with model scale and persist with extended training budgets suggests its relevance for current and future large-scale LLM development. Furthermore, its ability to dynamically balance performance across heterogeneous data mixtures could simplify the complex process of data curation and mixing.

The findings indicate that as the LLM scaling paradigm shifts from being primarily compute-bound to data-bound, explicitly designing optimizers to induce favorable implicit biases, such as geometric closeness, could become a critical avenue for developing more robust and capable language models. The work contributes to a deeper understanding of generalization in over-parameterized models and offers a concrete strategy to leverage the geometric properties of the loss landscape for improved model utility.

A noted limitation is Nexus's current incompatibility with the Muon optimizer, which could be an area for future research to broaden its applicability.
