# LabVLA: Grounding Vision-Language-Action Models in Scientific Laboratories

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Zhejiang University, Shanghai AI Laboratory, and Harbin Institute of Technology developed LabVLA, a Vision-Language-Action model grounded in scientific laboratories using RoboGenesis, a simulation-based data engine. This framework enables robots to interpret written protocols and execute physical actions with 71.1% success in in-distribution and 70.0% in out-of-distribution simulated lab tasks, and demonstrates real-world transferability.

## Problem

- AI systems struggle with the physical execution of scientific protocols in laboratories, which demand precise manipulation of specialized instruments and liquids.
- A significant scarcity of relevant, high-quality, and diverse robot demonstration data exists for laboratory-specific tasks, making real-world data collection prohibitively expensive.
- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, primarily trained on household data, lack the ability to generalize across diverse robot embodiments and the unique visual-linguistic context of scientific labs.

## Method

- **RoboGenesis**: A programmable, simulation-based workflow and data engine built on Isaac Sim that generates high-fidelity, structured laboratory demonstrations, including generative asset creation, automated scene construction, and configurable domain randomization.
- **LabVLA**: A VLA policy that couples a ==Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct== backbone with a ==DiT== action expert, trained in two stages: VLM pretraining with ==FAST action tokenization== and ==flow matching== posttraining with Knowledge Insulation (KI).
- ==Knowledge Insulation (KI)== is applied during flow matching posttraining, preventing gradients from updating the VLM's visual and linguistic priors, thereby maintaining its broad knowledge while the DiT specializes in continuous action prediction.

## Results

- LabVLA achieved an average success rate of **71.1%** in in-distribution and **70.0%** in out-of-distribution settings on the LabUtopia benchmark, improving over the next best baseline by **7.8** and **6.8 percentage points** respectively.
- Finetuning an external VLA policy (X-VLA) with LabEmbodied-Data resulted in substantial improvements of **+15.0 percentage points** (ID) and **+19.3 percentage points** (OOD) in average success rates across five tasks.
- Successful simulation-to-real transfer was demonstrated on a physical Franka robot for composite tasks, with LabVLA performing comparably to or slightly better than baselines, achieving up to **80%** success in clean out-of-domain settings for a long-horizon task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scalable, simulation-based data generation, particularly with robust domain randomization and rich annotation, can effectively address data scarcity and enhance generalization for specialized robotic domains like scientific laboratories.
> - A multi-stage VLA training recipe, starting with action token pretraining and followed by flow matching with knowledge insulation, allows a VLM to become 'action-aware' and adapt to continuous control without corrupting its foundational knowledge.
> - The synthesized LabEmbodied-Data is highly transferable, providing valuable laboratory supervision that significantly improves the performance of external VLA architectures on lab-specific tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Artificial intelligence systems are increasingly integrated into scientific research, assisting with tasks such as literature review, hypothesis generation, and experimental planning. Recent advancements in self-driving laboratories and language model-driven laboratory agents have extended AI’s role from digital reasoning to aspects of physical experimentation. However, the physical execution of scientific protocols—involving manual manipulation of lab instruments, liquids, and delicate procedures—remains largely dependent on human operators.

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models offer a potential interface to bridge this gap, enabling robots to interpret written instructions and execute physical actions. Nevertheless, existing VLA policies are predominantly trained on demonstrations from household and tabletop environments. This training data typically lacks the specific instruments (e.g., pipettes, centrifuges, thermal cyclers), transparent liquids, precise contact requirements, and fixed protocol workflows characteristic of scientific laboratories. Consequently, current VLA models are not adequately equipped to ground written laboratory protocols in appropriate instrument usage and physical state transitions.

The paper identifies two primary bottlenecks: the scarcity of relevant **data** and the need to accommodate **diverse robot embodiments** in laboratory settings, alongside challenges in **model design**. Current large-scale robot corpora like Open X-Embodiment, DROID, and BridgeData V2 provide general manipulation priors but do not include lab-specific objects or protocol-level supervision. Collecting such specialized data directly in real laboratories is resource-intensive due to the need for specialized instruments, domain expertise, calibrated hardware, and stringent safety procedures. This work aims to address these identified limitations by developing a simulation-based data generation framework and a VLA policy specifically tailored for scientific laboratory automation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable AI systems to perform physical experiments in scientific laboratories by grounding Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in laboratory-specific tasks. The motivations are detailed as follows:

1. **Bridging the Digital-to-Physical Gap in Scientific Automation**: While AI excels at digital reasoning in science (e.g., hypothesis generation, literature analysis), the physical execution of experiments remains largely human-centric. VLA models are posited as a key technology to bridge this gap, allowing AI to translate written scientific protocols into robotic actions.

2. **Addressing Data Scarcity and Domain Specificity for Laboratory Tasks**:
 - **Lack of Laboratory-Specific Data**: Existing VLA models are trained on general household and tabletop manipulation datasets. These datasets lack the unique characteristics of laboratory environments, such as specialized instruments (pipettes, centrifuges, hot plates), transparent liquids, and the high precision required for tasks like liquid transfer, cap screwing, and button operation. This deficiency prevents current VLA policies from effectively understanding and executing scientific protocols.
 - **Cost of Real-World Data Collection**: Obtaining high-quality, diverse robot demonstrations in real scientific laboratories is prohibitively expensive due and requires specialized equipment, safety protocols, and expert supervision. This motivates the need for a scalable, cost-effective method for generating laboratory-specific training data.

3. **Enabling Cross-Embodiment Generalization**: Laboratory protocols often need to be executed by different robot platforms with varying kinematics, end-effectors, camera configurations, and action spaces (e.g., single-arm, mobile manipulators, dual-arm robots). Current VLA models struggle to generalize across such diverse embodiments, necessitating a framework that can unify learning across multiple robot profiles.

4. **Developing a Robust and Action-Aware VLA Policy**: Generic VLMs, while powerful in language and vision, are not inherently "action-aware" or optimized for generating continuous robot control actions. There is a need for a model architecture and a training strategy that can effectively integrate visual-linguistic understanding with continuous action generation, while maintaining the integrity of the underlying VLM's knowledge.

In summary, the motivation is to facilitate the physical automation of scientific discovery by overcoming the critical bottlenecks of data availability, embodiment diversity, and specialized policy design within the context of laboratory manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a two-pronged approach: **RoboGenesis**, a simulation-based workflow and data engine for generating laboratory-specific demonstrations, and **LabVLA**, a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy designed for executing these protocols.

#### 3.1. RoboGenesis: A Programmable Workflow and Data Engine

RoboGenesis is designed to address the data bottleneck by synthesizing high-fidelity, structured demonstrations for laboratory automation within an Isaac Sim-based environment. It comprises three stages:

1. **Environment Building**:
 - **Asset Generation Pipeline**: This stage converts text descriptions into physics-annotated 3D USD assets. It involves:
 1. Generating a structured product-photography prompt from text descriptions.
 2. Calling a text-to-image API to render a reference photograph.
 3. Feeding the image to TRELLIS 2.0 for textured mesh reconstruction (GLB format).
 4. Postprocessing to orient the mesh, export to USD with PBR textures, decimate triangle count, and generate collision meshes with URDF metadata (mass, friction, bounding box).
 5. This process populates the **LabAssetLibrary** with 2,947 annotated assets. For liquid-handling containers, colored mesh proxies represent liquid levels and transfer upon successful pour steps.
 - **Automated Scene Construction Pipeline**: A seed-driven, greedy placement algorithm assembles laboratory scenes from the asset library, adhering to numerical constraints (e.g., table dimensions, counter heights, aisle gaps). Assets are categorized (table, bench, floor) and placed sequentially in six passes, followed by ten validation checks (e.g., table clearance, robot aisle width, no overlap) to ensure physical executability and quality. The **LabTextureLibrary** (over 1,000 textures) is used for material assignment. This process generated 10,000 diverse laboratory scenes.
 - **Robot Profiles**: A separate pool defines 16 robot platforms (single-arm, bimanual, mobile manipulators, including Franka Panda, UR-series, Split ALOHA). Each profile stores kinematic parameters, gripper settings, camera frames, and skill level overrides, allowing the same workflow to run on different embodiments.
 - **Interactive Authoring**: A web-based Task Designer supports interactive creation of assets (from single images) and scenes (manual placement or drag-and-drop), exporting validated configurations.

2. **Agentic Workflow Generation**:
 - **Atomic Skill Library**: A library of laboratory-specific atomic skills (e.g., pick, place, pour, stir, shake, press, open, close, navigation) is provided. These operate at a laboratory action level, not controller level, allowing for reuse across different robots. The library is extensible.
 - **Workflow Authoring**: An agent-assisted path converts natural language instructions into executable YAML workflows by querying available robots and task specifications. An offline validator checks reachability and conflicts. Manual YAML definition is also supported for fine-grained control. Composite workflows exceeding 20 skill steps achieved collection success rates above 75%.
 - **Domain Randomization**: After scene and workflow validation, six configurable axes of randomization are applied: scene layout, visual clutter (non-protocol objects), camera views, object appearance (swapping compatible assets), lighting (intensity, color temperature, HDRI), and spatial perturbations of object poses. Language instructions are also paraphrased. This ensures visual, spatial, linguistic, and embodiment variation without altering the core experiment semantics.

3. **Knowledgeable LabEmbodied-Data Export**:
 - Only successful rollouts, verified by skill-specific success checkers and a contact safety monitor, are exported as **LabEmbodied-Data**.
 - Each episode includes multicamera RGB observations, robot joint states, executed actions, and the language instruction.
 - **Fifteen annotation providers** enrich the data with structured information, including robot state, camera intrinsics/extrinsics, step timing, instruction alignment (subinstructions per frame), object state, scene relations, object semantics, success explanation, collision events, temporal segments, subgoals, quality scores, intervention flags, and episode metadata. These annotations enable the model to learn both action trajectories and their context within the larger laboratory procedure, with a shared observation-action schema across embodiments.

#### 3.2. LabVLA Training Recipe

LabVLA is a VLA policy that couples a Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct backbone with a DiT action expert. Its training proceeds in two stages:

1. **VLM Pretraining with FAST Tokens**:
 - **Architecture**: The Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct backbone is trained first without the DiT action expert.
 - **Action Tokenization**: Continuous absolute action chunks are tokenized using the FAST tokenizer. Robot states are discretized into uniform bins, serialized as text, and prepended to the language instruction.
 - **Input Sequence**: The VLM processes a sequence concatenating image tokens, state-conditioned instruction tokens, annotation tokens, and discrete FAST action tokens.
 - **Objective**: The VLM is trained using a masked next token prediction loss (ℒFAST) to align the visual-language prefix with action semantics, combined with cross-entropy losses for any available annotation targets (ℒ (𝑗)CE). This makes the VLM "action-aware" before continuous control is introduced.

2. **Flow Matching Posttraining with Knowledge Insulation (KI)**:
 - **Architecture**: The pretrained VLM checkpoint is loaded, and an 18-layer DiT (width 1024, 8 attention heads) action expert is attached. The VLM maps visual observations, robot state, and language instructions to hidden states, which are linearly projected to the DiT. The DiT predicts a K-step continuous action chunk.
 - **Embodiment Agnostic Batch Format**: State and action vectors are padded to a maximum dimension, with an action valid mask to handle varying degrees of freedom across different robot types.
 - **Flow Matching Objective**: The DiT is trained using a flow matching objective on data from OXE-AugE and the newly synthesized LabEmbodied-Data. This objective maps Gaussian noise to a clean action chunk through a deterministic vector field, predicting the vector field (𝑈 𝜏) that transforms noisy actions (𝑋 𝜏) into clean ones. The loss is a masked Mean Squared Error (ℒFM).
 - **Knowledge Insulation (KI)**: A critical design choice is the use of a `stop-gradient` (sg) operation on the VLM's hidden states (`e 𝐻KI 𝜙,𝑝 = sg(𝐻KI 𝜙,𝑝)`) before they are fed to the DiT action expert. This prevents the flow matching loss gradients from updating the VLM's visual and linguistic priors, thereby insulating the VLM from interference while the DiT specializes in continuous action prediction. The VLM continues to learn from its FAST and annotation token losses during this stage.
 - **Inference**: At inference time, LabVLA computes VLM hidden states once, samples Gaussian noise, and integrates the predicted vector field over N=10 Euler steps to generate continuous action trajectories, which is faster than typical diffusion policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LabVLA was conducted primarily on the LabUtopia benchmark, supplemented by analyses of data transferability and real-world experiments.

#### 4.1. LabUtopia Benchmark Performance

LabVLA was evaluated on six laboratory operations within the LabUtopia benchmark: Pick Up, Press Button, Open Door, Pour Liquid, Heat Beaker, and Transport Beaker. Performance was assessed under both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) settings, where OOD involved perturbations to object placement, appearance, or scene configuration to test generalization.

- **Overall Performance**: LabVLA achieved the highest average success rate among all evaluated baselines, with 71.1% in ID settings and 70.0% in OOD settings. This represents an improvement of 7.8 and 6.8 percentage points, respectively, over the next best policy, 𝜋0.
- **Task-Specific Performance**:
 - LabVLA demonstrated strong performance in tasks requiring precise interaction, leading on **Pick Up** (49.2% ID / 48.3% OOD) and **Open Door** (65.0% ID / 65.8% OOD, tied for ID). It achieved a 100% success rate on **Press Button** in ID settings, indicating reliable interaction with simple device controls.
 - For **Heat Beaker** (83.3% ID / 87.5% OOD) and **Transport Beaker** (85.8% ID / 85.8% OOD), LabVLA remained competitive with or slightly behind other strong baselines such as GR00T N1.5 and 𝜋0.5, which achieved higher scores in specific instances.
 - **Pour Liquid** proved to be the most challenging task for all policies, with no baseline exceeding 50% success. LabVLA achieved 43.3% ID and 34.2% OOD, indicating that precise liquid transfer, particularly without explicit liquid level feedback, remains an open problem.
- **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Settings**: LabVLA exhibited robust generalization, with only a 1.1 percentage point drop in average success rate from ID to OOD (71.1% to 70.0%). This narrow gap suggests that the domain randomization strategies employed during data generation in RoboGenesis were effective in building visual and spatial invariances, enhancing transferability across varied laboratory scenes.
- **Balanced Performance**: Unlike some baselines that showed high performance on specific tasks but near-zero on others, LabVLA maintained a balanced performance, achieving over 48% on every task except Pour Liquid. This breadth is considered important for chained operations in scientific protocols.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results

Visual snapshots of representative rollouts (Figure 4) indicated that LabVLA generated smooth and task-appropriate trajectories across all six evaluation categories. Failure cases typically involved issues like premature grasp release during transport or insufficient precision in tilt angles during pouring, consistent with the quantitative results highlighting the difficulty of Pour Liquid.

#### 4.3. LabEmbodied-Data Transferability

To assess the broader utility of the synthesized LabEmbodied-Data, an external policy, X-VLA (a sub-1B baseline), was finetuned using this dataset.
- The finetuned X-VLA showed substantial improvements, increasing its five-task average (excluding Press Button due to saturation) by +15.0 percentage points in ID settings (from 49.3% to 64.3%) and +19.3 percentage points in OOD settings (from 43.7% to 63.0%).
- The most significant gains were observed in tasks requiring instrument-specific contact patterns, such as Heat Beaker (ID: 25.8% → 68.3%) and Pour Liquid (OOD: 25.0% → 65.0%).
- This analysis indicates that LabEmbodied-Data provides transferable laboratory supervision that is not specific to the LabVLA architecture.

#### 4.4. Real-world Experiments

LabVLA was deployed on a physical Franka robot platform to evaluate simulation-to-real transfer across four composite tasks (Shake Liquid, Pour Liquid, Magnetic Stir, Funnel Plug/Unplug). It was compared against DreamZero and 𝜋0.5 under varying conditions of target position (in-domain vs. out-of-domain) and workspace clutter (clean vs. cluttered).
- All three policies achieved over 70% success rates in most conditions, demonstrating that simulation pretraining can transfer to physical hardware.
- LabVLA and DreamZero performed comparably, with LabVLA slightly leading in clean out-of-domain conditions (80.0% vs. 78.0% average) and DreamZero slightly leading in cluttered settings.
- Both LabVLA and DreamZero consistently outperformed 𝜋0.5.
- Similar to simulation, Pour Liquid showed the most significant degradation under clutter and position shifts for all policies.
- Funnel Plug/Unplug, a four-step long-horizon task, was the most challenging, with LabVLA achieving 80% in clean out-of-domain settings, surpassing the baselines in that condition.

These results collectively indicate LabVLA's capability in laboratory automation, its robust generalization facilitated by RoboGenesis's data generation, and the practical transferability of its policies to physical robotic systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an approach to integrating Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models into scientific laboratory automation, addressing the critical bottlenecks of data availability and diverse robot embodiments. The contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enabling Embodied AI for Science**: The work represents a step towards extending AI's role in scientific research beyond digital reasoning (hypothesis generation, literature review) to include physical execution of experiments. By providing a grounded interface for AI to interact with laboratory equipment, it facilitates the development of embodied AI systems capable of performing physical scientific tasks.

2. **Scalable Data Generation for Specialized Domains**: The introduction of **RoboGenesis** provides a programmable, simulation-based workflow and data engine. This engine can generate high-fidelity, structured demonstrations specific to scientific laboratory protocols at scale, circumventing the high costs and logistical challenges of real-world data collection. Its capabilities for generative asset creation, agent-based scene and task generation, configurable domain randomization, success-filtered export, and long-horizon task composition across multiple robot platforms offer a novel solution for data scarcity in specialized robot domains.

3. **Advanced VLA Policy Design for Protocol Execution**: **LabVLA**, a Qwen3-VL-based policy, combines FAST action token pretraining, flow matching posttraining, and a knowledge insulation mechanism. This training recipe effectively adapts broad visual-language and manipulation priors to the precise and sequential demands of laboratory protocols. Its strong performance on the LabUtopia benchmark, particularly its generalization capabilities in out-of-distribution settings, demonstrates the efficacy of this design for complex scientific procedures.

4. **Transferability and Accessibility for Research**: The analytical results show that **LabEmbodied-Data** provides transferable laboratory supervision, benefiting even external VLA architectures. Furthermore, the demonstrated simulation-to-real transfer on a physical Franka platform indicates the practical utility of the learned policies in real-world benchtop manipulation. By providing RoboGenesis, LabEmbodied-Data, and the LabVLA training recipe as reusable artifacts, the research lowers the entry barrier for other groups to engage in laboratory VLA research, fostering further development in this specialized field.

5. **Addressing Key Challenges in Scientific Automation**: The detailed analysis of task performance, especially the identification of "Pour Liquid" as a persistently challenging task, provides clear directions for future research. This tiered view of laboratory competence, from "Apprentice" to "Scientist," frames current capabilities and future aspirations for embodied scientific assistants.

In the long term, this work envisions a future where embodied AI assists human scientists with routine aspects of protocol execution, from reagent preparation and simple instrument operation to observation logging, under human supervision. While acknowledging current limitations (e.g., primarily simulated validation, protocol-following only, lack of human-AI collaboration features), LabVLA represents a foundational step towards giving scientific AI systems a physical presence in the laboratory, thereby accelerating scientific discovery by offloading repetitive or precise manual tasks.
