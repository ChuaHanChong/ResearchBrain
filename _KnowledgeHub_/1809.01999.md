# Recurrent World Models Facilitate Policy Evolution

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Google Brain and NNAISENSE developed a modular agent architecture that learns an internal world model from raw pixels to facilitate policy evolution. It demonstrates successful transfer of policies trained entirely within the learned virtual environment to the actual environment, achieving state-of-the-art results on CarRacing-v0 and DoomTakeCover-v0.

## Problem

- Model-free reinforcement learning methods often require extensive data and interaction with the real environment, which can be inefficient.
- Developing robust models of environment dynamics from high-dimensional, stochastic visual inputs for model-based reinforcement learning is challenging.
- Agents trained in learned dynamics models can exploit imperfections in the model, leading to policies that perform poorly in the actual environment.

## Method

- A modular agent architecture comprising a Vision component (==VAE==), a Memory/**World Model** component (==MDN-RNN==), and a simple Controller component (==linear model==) is proposed.
- The components are trained sequentially: the ==VAE== encodes pixels to latent space, the ==MDN-RNN== predicts future latent states, and the Controller is optimized using ==Evolution Strategies== (==CMA-ES==).
- The Controller can be trained entirely within the environment simulated by the learned ==world model==, with a tunable ==temperature== parameter controlling the simulated environment's uncertainty.

## Results

- The world model approach achieved a new state-of-the-art on CarRacing-v0, solving the task with an average score of **906 ± 21**.
- A policy trained entirely within the self-generated `DoomRNN` virtual environment successfully transferred to the actual VizDoom, achieving an average score of **1092 ± 556**, surpassing the 'solved' threshold.
- Adjusting the `temperature` parameter to increase uncertainty in the virtual environment prevented the agent from exploiting model imperfections, leading to more robust and generalizable policies in the real environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a probabilistic world model from high-dimensional visual inputs can extract useful spatio-temporal features, simplifying policy learning.
> - Policies can be trained effectively and efficiently entirely within an environment simulated by the agent's own learned world model.
> - Controlling the uncertainty (via a temperature parameter) in the internally generated environment is crucial to prevent policy exploitation and facilitate robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several key areas within artificial intelligence: **model-based reinforcement learning (RL)**, **generative models**, **recurrent neural networks**, and **evolution strategies**.

Traditionally, RL is broadly categorized into model-free and model-based approaches. Model-free methods (e.g., Q-learning, Policy Gradients like A3C) learn policies directly from interaction with the environment without explicitly modeling its dynamics. While powerful, they often require vast amounts of interaction data, which can be computationally expensive and impractical in real-world scenarios. Model-based RL, in contrast, involves learning a model of the environment's dynamics, which can then be used for planning, simulation, or to assist policy learning. This paper firmly falls into the model-based paradigm, advocating for its data efficiency and interpretability.

The paper distinguishes itself by learning a **generative model** of the environment. Unlike earlier model-based approaches that might predict deterministic next states, this work uses a **Mixture Density Network-Recurrent Neural Network (MDN-RNN)** to model the *probability distribution* of future observations in a compressed latent space. This allows the model to capture the stochastic nature of many environments, which is a significant step beyond deterministic models that can be easily exploited by agents.

Furthermore, the research leverages **Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)** as a "vision" component. VAEs are a type of generative model used for unsupervised learning of compressed, disentangled representations. Their application here for high-dimensional pixel input demonstrates a common strategy in deep RL to reduce the complexity of raw sensory data, making it more tractable for subsequent processing by the world model.

A notable departure from much of the contemporary deep RL landscape is the use of **Evolution Strategies (ES)** for training the policy (controller). While gradient-based methods like backpropagation dominate the training of neural networks, ES offers an alternative, gradient-free optimization paradigm. ES is particularly effective for optimizing policies with a relatively small number of parameters and offers advantages in terms of parallelism and robustness to non-differentiable objectives. This choice is facilitated by the paper's design philosophy: shifting complexity and learnable parameters from the controller to the world model, allowing the controller to remain simple and amenable to ES. This contrasts with many model-free deep RL approaches where the policy network itself is large and trained via backpropagation.

Finally, the paper delves into the concept of **Sim2Real transfer** (or more accurately, "Sim-to-Sim-to-Real"). Instead of training in a pre-defined simulator, the agent learns its *own* internal simulator (the "world model"). A key innovation is demonstrating the ability to train a policy *entirely within this self-generated virtual environment* and then transfer it back to the real environment. The explicit management of uncertainty within the generated environment (via a "temperature" parameter) to prevent the agent from "cheating" or exploiting imperfections in the learned model is a critical contribution to the robustness of such transfer. This addresses a known weakness of learned dynamics models: policies trained on imperfect models can overfit to those imperfections and fail in the real world. The work thus highlights a path towards more flexible and potentially data-efficient RL by enabling agents to practice in self-generated, tailored environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of "Recurrent World Models Facilitate Policy Evolution" is to demonstrate a robust and efficient approach to training artificial agents that can learn complex behaviors in rich, pixel-based reinforcement learning environments. This is achieved by equipping the agent with a "world model" that captures the spatio-temporal dynamics of its environment, which then facilitates the evolution of a simple control policy.

The authors are motivated by several key insights:

- **Human Cognition as Inspiration:** Humans instinctively develop mental models of their surroundings, using abstract representations of sensory data to predict future states and inform actions. This internal predictive capability allows for both conscious planning and fast, reflexive behaviors. The paper posits that artificial agents could similarly benefit from an internal world model to navigate complex and partially observable environments.
- **Leveraging Foundational Work:** The paper aims to revisit and validate foundational concepts from earlier research on RNN-based world models, primarily by Jürgen Schmidhuber, which date back to the 1990s. The motivation is to show that these "key concepts" combined with modern deep learning tools (VAEs, MDN-RNNs) and computational power can lead to state-of-the-art results in modern RL benchmarks.
- **Addressing Limitations of Traditional RL:** Many deep RL methods are model-free, requiring extensive interactions with the actual environment, which can be computationally intensive and data-inefficient. Model-based approaches promise greater efficiency. The authors seek to overcome the challenge of learning robust dynamics models from high-dimensional, stochastic visual inputs.
- **Simplifying Policy Learning:** By offloading the complexity of understanding the environment's dynamics to a sophisticated world model (M) and a vision component (V), the authors aim to keep the control policy (C) intentionally simple and compact. This design choice is motivated by the desire to alleviate the "credit assignment problem" for the controller, making its training easier and allowing for the use of less conventional, yet highly parallelizable, optimization methods like Evolution Strategies.
- **Enabling Internal Simulation:** A crucial motivation is to explore the possibility of an agent training *entirely within an environment generated by its own learned world model*. This concept, if successful, could unlock significant efficiencies by removing the need for a separate, often computationally heavy, external simulator.
- **Mitigating Model Exploitation:** A known challenge in training agents within learned dynamics models is that the agent might exploit imperfections or "bugs" in the model, leading to policies that perform well in the simulated environment but fail in the real one. The authors are motivated to address this by explicitly incorporating and controlling uncertainty in the generated environment, thereby forcing the agent to learn more robust and generalizable policies.

In essence, the objectives are to build an intelligent agent with a robust, learned internal model of its world, use this model to extract useful spatio-temporal features for a simple controller, and ultimately enable the agent to learn effectively, potentially even entirely within its own internally simulated reality, for challenging pixel-based control tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around a modular agent architecture composed of three main components: a Vision module (V), a Memory/World Model module (M), and a Controller module (C). These modules are trained sequentially in a multi-stage process.

#### 3.1. Agent Architecture

- **Vision (V) - Variational Autoencoder (VAE):** The V component's role is to compress high-dimensional raw pixel observations (64x64x3 RGB images) into a compact, low-dimensional latent vector, `z`. A Convolutional Variational Autoencoder (ConvVAE) is employed for this purpose. The VAE is trained to reconstruct the input image from its latent representation, minimizing a reconstruction loss and a KL divergence loss to enforce a Gaussian prior over `z`. This compression makes subsequent processing by the world model more efficient.
- **Memory/World Model (M) - Mixture Density Network-Recurrent Neural Network (MDN-RNN):** The M component is a probabilistic generative model of the environment's dynamics in the latent space. It is implemented as an LSTM recurrent neural network whose output layer is a Mixture Density Network. Given the current latent observation `z_t`, the action taken `a_t`, and its own hidden state `h_t`, the MDN-RNN predicts the *probability distribution* `P(z_{t+1}|a_t, z_t, h_t)` of the next latent state `z_{t+1}`. By using a mixture of Gaussian distributions, M can capture the stochasticity and multi-modal nature of environment transitions. For environments like VizDoom, M also predicts a binary "done" state, indicating termination of an episode. A crucial aspect is the ability to adjust a `temperature (τ)` parameter during sampling from this distribution, which controls the level of uncertainty or noisiness in the generated environment.
- **Controller (C) - Simple Linear Model:** The C component is responsible for decision-making and selecting actions. It is deliberately designed to be as simple and compact as possible, implemented as a single-layer linear model. Its inputs are the current latent observation `z_t` from V and the current hidden state `h_t` of the MDN-RNN (M). This linearity keeps the number of parameters minimal, making it amenable to alternative optimization methods.

#### 3.2. Training Procedure

The training of the overall system is sequential and decoupled:
1. **Data Collection:** Initially, a dataset of 10,000 rollouts is collected by an agent taking random actions in the actual environment. This provides the raw image sequences and corresponding actions.
2. **Train Vision (V):** The VAE is trained on the collected image frames to learn to encode each frame into a latent vector `z`. This step is unsupervised.
3. **Train Memory/World Model (M):** Using the latent vectors `z_t` generated by the trained V and the corresponding actions `a_t`, the MDN-RNN (M) is trained to predict the probability distribution of the next latent vector `z_{t+1}`. This is also a form of self-supervised learning, where M learns to predict its future sensory input in latent space. V and M are trained without direct access to environmental rewards.
4. **Evolve Controller (C):** Finally, the parameters of the simple linear Controller (C) are optimized. The **Covariance-Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES)**, a gradient-free evolutionary algorithm, is used for this. C's objective is to maximize the expected cumulative reward during rollouts. The simplicity of C (low parameter count) makes CMA-ES a suitable and efficient optimizer, as it can run many parallel rollouts on multiple CPU cores.

#### 3.3. Training in a Generated Environment

A key experimental approach, particularly for the VizDoom environment, involves training the controller C *entirely within an environment simulated by its own internal world model M*.
- The trained M (which predicts `z_{t+1}` and `done_t`) is wrapped with an OpenAI Gym interface, effectively creating a "virtual environment."
- The agent (C) interacts with this virtual environment, receiving `z` and `h` from M and providing actions. The actual environment's rendering or physics engine is bypassed, leading to computational efficiency.
- To address the issue of the agent exploiting imperfections in M, the `temperature (τ)` parameter of the MDN-RNN is adjusted during sampling in the virtual environment. Increasing `τ` introduces more uncertainty and stochasticity, making the virtual environment more challenging and forcing C to learn more robust policies.
- After training in the virtual environment, the learned policy is transferred and tested in the *actual* environment to evaluate its generalization.

This modular, multi-stage approach, combined with the novel idea of internal simulation and uncertainty control, forms the core of the paper's methodology to facilitate robust policy evolution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling results across two distinct reinforcement learning environments: CarRacing-v0 and DoomTakeCover-v0, showcasing the effectiveness of their world model approach.

#### 4.1. CarRacing-v0: World Model for Feature Extraction

This environment is a continuous control task where the agent must navigate a randomly generated race track from pixel inputs. The task is considered "solved" if an average reward of 900 over 100 consecutive trials is achieved.

- **V without M (Handicapped Controller):** When the controller C only had access to the latent representation `z_t` from the VAE (V) but not the predictive hidden state `h_t` from the MDN-RNN (M), the agent performed poorly. It achieved an average score of 632 ± 251, exhibiting wobbly behavior and difficulty with sharp corners. Even with an added hidden layer to C, the score only improved to 788 ± 141, still below the solving threshold and comparable to traditional Deep RL methods like A3C (which scored around 591-652).
- **Full World Model (V and M):** When C was given access to both `z_t` and `h_t` (the hidden state capturing predictive information from M), the agent's performance dramatically improved. It achieved an average score of **906 ± 21**, successfully solving the CarRacing-v0 task and setting a new state-of-the-art result. Previous best solutions on the OpenAI Gym leaderboard were around 838 ± 11. The authors observed that the agent's driving became more stable and effective, particularly around sharp corners, attributing this to `h_t` providing an "instinctive" sense of future probabilities without explicit planning. This demonstrated that `M` extracts useful spatio-temporal representations for the controller.

#### 4.2. VizDoom Experiment: Learning Inside of a Generated Environment

This experiment explored the novel concept of training the agent's controller solely within an environment simulated by its own learned world model, specifically for the DoomTakeCover-v0 task (where the agent must avoid fireballs). The task is solved if the average survival time over 100 consecutive rollouts is greater than 750 time steps.

- **World Model Fidelity:** The MDN-RNN (M) successfully learned to mimic the complex Doom environment, including game logic, enemy behavior, physics, and 3D graphics rendering, by observing only raw image data and random actions. M also learned to predict the `done` (death) state.
- **Training in Virtual Environment and Transfer:** The controller C was trained entirely inside this `DoomRNN` virtual environment, which operates purely in latent space and is computationally more efficient than the actual VizDoom engine. The agent trained in this virtual environment achieved an average score of 918 virtual time steps. When this policy was transferred to the actual VizDoom environment, it achieved an average score of **1092 ± 556**, significantly surpassing the "solved" threshold of 750 and the Gym Leader's score of 820 ± 58. This is a crucial finding, demonstrating the successful transfer of a policy learned in a self-generated, latent-space simulation to the real environment.
- **The Role of Temperature (τ) and Preventing Exploitation:** The paper highlights the importance of the `temperature (τ)` parameter in controlling the uncertainty of the generated environment.
 - At a very low `τ` (e.g., 0.10), the MDN-RNN can suffer from "mode collapse," failing to generate fireballs. The controller then learns to exploit this deterministic imperfection, achieving a perfect score (2100) in the virtual environment but failing catastrophically in the real environment (performing worse than a random policy). This vividly illustrates the "cheating" problem of learned dynamics models.
 - Increasing `τ` (e.g., to 1.15 or 1.30) introduces more uncertainty into the virtual environment, making it more challenging and forcing C to learn robust, generalizable policies. The best actual score (1092 ± 556) was achieved at τ=1.15, demonstrating that training in a noisier, more uncertain virtual environment leads to better transfer. Higher `τ` values were also observed to yield less risky strategies with lower variance of returns.

These findings collectively underscore the power of learning a probabilistic world model from high-dimensional sensory data, utilizing its internal representations for policy learning, and the innovative approach of training policies within self-generated, controlled-uncertainty simulations to facilitate robust sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Recurrent World Models Facilitate Policy Evolution" holds significant implications for the field of reinforcement learning and artificial intelligence, offering several contributions that could shape future research directions:

- **Advancement in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning:** The paper unequivocally demonstrates the efficacy of model-based RL, particularly for tasks involving high-dimensional visual inputs. By successfully solving CarRacing-v0 from raw pixels, a task where traditional deep RL methods struggled, it showcases the power of learned spatio-temporal representations. This reinforces the argument for model-based approaches as a viable and often more data-efficient alternative to purely model-free methods.
- **Pioneering Self-Generated Simulation for Policy Learning:** The most impactful contribution is the successful demonstration of training a policy entirely within an environment *generated by the agent's own learned world model*. This goes beyond traditional "sim2real" transfer, where agents train in human-designed simulators. By learning its own internal simulator, the agent can potentially adapt to new environments more flexibly and efficiently. This could pave the way for agents that learn to build and exploit their own internal realities for accelerated skill acquisition.
- **Robustness through Controlled Uncertainty:** The innovative use of the MDN-RNN's temperature parameter to inject and control uncertainty in the generated environment is a crucial technique. This directly addresses the long-standing problem of agents exploiting imperfections in learned dynamics models, which often leads to policies that fail in the real world. By forcing the controller to learn in a more stochastic and challenging virtual environment, the method promotes the learning of robust and generalizable policies, significantly improving sim-to-real transfer.
- **Computational Efficiency Gains:** Training the controller in the latent space environment generated by the world model (e.g., DoomRNN) is significantly more computationally efficient than interacting with the actual high-fidelity environment (VizDoom). This efficiency gain has immense practical implications, potentially allowing for faster iteration, larger-scale experiments, and the training of more complex agents without prohibitive computational costs. This can democratize access to advanced RL research by reducing the reliance on massive GPU clusters for raw pixel rendering.
- **Simplicity of Controller and Scalability:** The design choice to keep the controller simple (a linear model) and shift complexity to the V and M components simplifies the policy optimization problem. This enables the use of Evolution Strategies (ES), which are highly parallelizable and do not require backpropagation through the environment, offering a robust alternative to gradient-based RL methods for certain problem types. This modularity could inspire new architectures where different components are optimized via different, specialized algorithms.
- **Conceptual Link to Neuroscience and Cognition:** The paper's inspiration from human mental models and predictive coding strengthens the connection between artificial intelligence research and cognitive science. The idea that agents build internal models to predict and act "instinctively" resonates with theories of brain function, suggesting a path towards more biologically plausible AI.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays fertile ground for several exciting future research directions, as explicitly discussed by the authors:
 - **Iterative World Model Refinement:** Moving beyond static data collection to active exploration where the agent constantly improves its world model. This connects to concepts of artificial curiosity and intrinsic motivation.
 - **Higher Capacity Models and Memory:** Addressing the limitations of current RNNs in storing long-term memories by exploring more powerful generative models or external memory modules, crucial for learning in more complex, open-ended worlds.
 - **Hierarchical Planning and Abstract Reasoning:** Integrating more sophisticated cognitive abilities, as proposed in Schmidhuber's "Learning to Think" framework, where the controller can strategically interact with and exploit the world model for abstract planning.

In summary, this research not only delivers state-of-the-art results on challenging benchmarks but also introduces a powerful paradigm for model-based RL that emphasizes internal simulation, robust transfer learning through uncertainty control, and computational efficiency. Its potential impact lies in enabling more autonomous and capable AI agents that can learn and adapt effectively in complex, dynamic environments, both virtual and real.
