# Reasoning over mathematical objects: on-policy reward modeling and test time aggregation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from FAIR at Meta, UCLA, and CMU developed a multi-faceted approach to improve large language models' mathematical and scientific reasoning, focusing on the derivation of complex mathematical objects. Their work introduced new benchmarks and datasets, a novel on-policy reward modeling framework (RLLM), and an online reinforcement learning method for test-time aggregation (ParaGator), leading to LLMs showing improved performance of 7.22-18.35% on their new PrincipiaBench and outperforming traditional RL baselines.

## Problem

- Current LLM evaluations for mathematical and scientific reasoning rely on simplified answer formats (e.g., numerical values, multiple-choice), potentially overstating true reasoning capabilities and not reflecting the need for formal expression of mathematical objects.
- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) post-training methods like RLHF and RLVR face limitations such as scalar reward models lacking transparency and being prone to reward hacking, or rule-based verifiers being restricted to easily verifiable tasks.
- Test-time aggregation strategies for LLMs often suffer from a mismatch between training and inference (due to off-policy generations) and a lack of diversity in generated solutions, limiting their effectiveness in enhancing reasoning.

## Method

- Introduced the ==Principia Suite==, comprising **PrincipiaBench** (an evaluation benchmark for complex mathematical object derivation), **Principia Collection** (a large-scale synthetic training dataset), and **Principia VerifyBench** (a meta-evaluation benchmark for verifiers).
- Proposed **RLLM** (==Reinforcement Learning with a Language Model as a Reward Model==), an ==on-policy== trained framework that leverages an LLM's own reasoning to provide flexible, explicit, and high-quality ==reward signals== across diverse task types.
- Developed **ParaGator**, an online, ==end-to-end reinforcement learning== framework that jointly optimizes the generation of diverse candidate solutions (via `pass@k` rewards) and their ==self-aggregation== (via `pass@1` rewards) for enhanced ==test-time reasoning==.

## Results

- Frontier LLMs like Qwen3-235B achieved **55.58%** on PrincipiaBench, significantly lower than on numerical or multiple-choice benchmarks, while RL training on the Principia Collection improved performance by **7.22-18.35%**.
- RLLM consistently outperformed traditional RLHF and RLVR across verifiable and non-verifiable tasks, with on-policy trained RMs improving AIME24 accuracy by **12%** over strong baselines, demonstrating its scalability and effectiveness.
- ParaGator, with its online end-to-end training, achieved the highest rewards in both generation and aggregation rounds, leading to the strongest overall `pass@1` rates on competition math and scientific reasoning benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evaluating LLMs on tasks requiring the derivation of complex mathematical objects reveals substantially lower performance compared to simplified formats, highlighting a critical gap in deep reasoning capabilities.
> - On-policy training of a language model as a 'thinking' reward model (RLLM) provides superior and more flexible reward signals, enabling effective post-training across easy-to-verify, hard-to-verify, and non-verifiable tasks.
> - Jointly optimizing diverse candidate generation and their aggregation through an online, end-to-end framework (ParaGator) effectively scales LLM reasoning at test time, with initial candidate diversity being critical for aggregation performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Current evaluations of language models for mathematical and scientific reasoning predominantly rely on simplified answer formats, such as numerical values or multiple-choice questions. This approach, while convenient for automated assessment, risks overstating models' true reasoning capabilities and does not accurately reflect the need for formal expression in domains like mathematics, physics, and chemistry, where reasoning culminates in precisely derived mathematical objects (e.g., equations, matrices, piecewise functions). This paper positions itself as addressing this gap by developing methodologies and resources specifically designed for the generation and evaluation of such complex mathematical objects.

The work also contributes to the field of LLM post-training, which traditionally employs Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). RLHF often utilizes scalar reward models that lack reasoning transparency and can be susceptible to reward hacking, while RLVR relies on rule-based verifiers that are limited to easily verifiable tasks with explicit "gold" answers. The paper proposes a new paradigm, Reinforcement Learning with a Language Model as a Reward Model (RLLM), which aims to overcome these limitations by leveraging LLMs' intrinsic reasoning and instruction-following abilities to create more flexible and effective reward signals.

Furthermore, the research integrates with existing efforts to scale test-time computation for LLMs, particularly methods involving parallel generation and aggregation of multiple solution attempts. Previous aggregation strategies often suffer from a mismatch between training and inference (due to training on off-policy generations) and a lack of diversity in generated solutions (due to mode collapse). This paper introduces an online, end-to-end training framework designed to optimize both the diversity of parallel generations and the effectiveness of subsequent aggregation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper is motivated by the observation that despite advances in LLM reasoning, a critical capability for scientific and mathematical discovery – the precise derivation of complex mathematical objects – remains underexplored and inadequately evaluated. The overarching objective is to develop both the tools and methods necessary to measure and improve this capability in LLMs. This breaks down into three core objectives:

**a. Improving reasoning over mathematical objects through new datasets and benchmarks:**
The primary motivation here is the perceived "overstatement" of LLM reasoning abilities by existing benchmarks. Current evaluations, focused on numerical or multiple-choice answers, do not compel models to generate entire mathematical expressions, which is fundamental to scientific communication and problem-solving at advanced levels. The authors argue that removing multiple-choice options often leads to significant performance drops and qualitative reasoning failures in even frontier LLMs, suggesting a reliance on "shortcuts" rather than deep derivational reasoning. There is a lack of training data and benchmarks that require graduate-level STEM knowledge and the generation of complex mathematical objects as answers. This motivates the creation of:
- **PrincipiaBench**: An evaluation benchmark specifically designed to assess an LM's ability to derive complex mathematical objects directly, without relying on simplified formats.
- **Principia Collection**: A large-scale synthetic training dataset to teach LLMs this specific ability.
- **Principia VerifyBench**: A meta-evaluation benchmark to ensure the reliability of verifiers used during training and evaluation, particularly for complex mathematical objects whose equivalence is challenging to check.

**b. Developing a unified post-training method via on-policy-trained Language Model as a Reward Model (RLLM):**
The motivation for RLLM stems from the limitations of existing RL post-training paradigms:
- **RLHF**: Scalar reward models provide a single numerical score without intermediate reasoning, making them prone to reward hacking and less effective for complex reasoning tasks.
- **RLVR**: Rule-based verifiers require "gold" answers that are often difficult to obtain or verify for complex problems, thus limiting their applicability to easily verifiable domains.
The objective is to establish RLLM as a single, flexible post-training framework that can function effectively across easy-to-verify, hard-to-verify, and even non-verifiable tasks. This is achieved by leveraging an LLM's own reasoning capabilities to serve as a "thinking" reward model, generating explicit reasoning traces to justify its judgments and allowing for flexible reward design through natural language prompts. A key motivation is to enable on-policy training of the LM-as-RM to produce more reliable and task-aligned reward signals.

**c. Developing a test-time scaling recipe for strong reasoning via on-policy-trained parallel aggregation:**
The motivation here addresses inefficiencies in current parallel thinking strategies, which aim to improve reasoning by generating multiple solutions and aggregating them. The identified limitations are:
- **Off-policy mismatch**: Aggregator models are often trained on solutions generated by a fixed policy, creating a discrepancy when encountering outputs from an evolving policy during inference.
- **Lack of diversity**: Standard RL training can lead to mode collapse, where the generator produces redundant solutions, thereby undermining the benefit of aggregation.
The objective is to create an online, end-to-end training framework that optimizes both the generation of diverse and complementary solutions and their subsequent aggregation into a single, high-quality answer. This aims to ensure that the generator produces outputs well-suited for aggregation, overcoming the issues of redundancy and off-policy misalignment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper introduces datasets, a novel reinforcement learning framework, and a test-time aggregation method.

**a. Principia Suite (Datasets and Benchmarks):**
- **PrincipiaBench**: An evaluation benchmark of 2,558 problems requiring complex mathematical objects (equations, inequalities, intervals, sets, matrices, piecewise functions). Problems are drawn from RealMath, Physics, ARB, and the Mathematics and Engineering subsets of SuperGPQA, with multiple-choice options removed. Problems were filtered for clarity, self-containment, and singularity of question.
- **Principia VerifyBench**: A meta-evaluation benchmark for verifiers. It consists of 168 instances where the judgments of a rule-based verifier (`math-verify`) and a model-based verifier (`o3`) disagreed. Human annotators determined the ground truth equivalence between model predictions and reference answers.
- **Principia Collection**: A synthetic training dataset comprising 248,748 graduate-level problems with answers as mathematical objects. Its construction involves:
 - **Topic Mining**: Extraction of subjects from Mathematics Subject Classification (MSC 2020) and Physics Subject Headings (PhySH) to ensure broad coverage.
 - **Problem Statement Generation**: GPT-OSS-120B generates problem statements based on subject entities, strategy descriptions, and a randomly selected mathematical object answer type. A refinement step ensures problems require genuine reasoning rather than simple knowledge probing.
 - **Filtering**: GPT-OSS-120B filters out invalid problem statements (e.g., compositional questions, non-self-contained, containing hints).
 - **Response Generation & Majority-Voting**: GPT-OSS-120B generates eight responses per problem. Self-consistency is applied, but with a novel conservative equivalence checking procedure for majority voting, which addresses issues like transitivity in mathematical object equivalence, using a GPT-OSS-120B as a verifier.
- **Training and Evaluation**: LLMs are trained using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on the Principia Collection. A model-based verifier (GPT-OSS-120B) is used to provide rewards during training and to evaluate models on PrincipiaBench, due to the fragility of rule-based verifiers for complex mathematical objects. Evaluation is also conducted on numerical (AIME) and multiple-choice (GPQA-Diamond, SuperGPQA) benchmarks to assess cross-format generalization.

**b. RLLM (Reinforcement Learning with Language Models as Reward Models):**
- **Framework**: RLLM is a two-stage process:
 - **Thinking LM-as-RM Training**: An LLM (e.g., Qwen3-32B) is trained using RLVR (J1 framework) to act as a "thinking" Reward Model. It generates an explicit reasoning trace and a scalar score. Crucially, this training is **on-policy**: the RM evaluates responses sampled from the policy model (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B) that RLLM aims to optimize, and synthetic labels are derived from a stronger teacher LLM (GPT-OSS-120B) or rule-based verifier.
 - **Policy Post-Training**: The LM-as-RM's generative rewards (scores) are used to optimize the policy model via reinforcement learning (GRPO).
- **LM-as-RM Variants**: RLLM supports reference-free pointwise, reference-based pointwise, pairwise, and listwise evaluation, allowing for flexible reward design.
- **Experimental Setup**: Qwen3-1.7B (Instruct) is a primary policy model. Experiments are conducted on verifiable tasks (competition math, Principia Physics) and non-verifiable instruction-following tasks (AlpacaEval 2.0, ArenaHard 2.0). Comparisons are made against RLHF (with scalar RMs) and RLVR (with rule-based or model-based verifiers for final answer equivalence). Ablation studies examine the impact of generator-verifier gap (relative strength of policy and RM) and on-policy vs. off-policy RM training.

**c. ParaGator (Learning to Aggregate through Online Reinforcement Learning):**
- **Unified Online Training Framework**: A single language model ($M_{\theta}$) is jointly trained for both candidate solution generation and self-aggregation using online RL (RLVR with GRPO).
- **Candidate Generation**: For a given problem, the model samples *m* candidate solutions. This stage is optimized with `pass@k` rewards, meaning the model is rewarded if *at least one* of its *k* samples is correct. This incentivizes the generation of diverse and complementary solutions.
- **Self-Aggregation**: The same model is prompted to act as an aggregator, taking the problem and the *m* candidate solutions as input to generate a final aggregated solution. This stage is optimized with `pass@1` rewards, meaning the model receives a reward only if the single aggregated answer is correct.
- **End-to-End Alignment**: Both `pass@k` generation and `pass@1` aggregation stages are trained online and end-to-end. This ensures the generator learns to produce candidates specifically beneficial for the aggregator.
- **Inference**: The framework allows for iterative multi-round aggregation, where refined aggregated solutions from one round become the candidate pool for the next.
- **Experimental Setup**: Training is performed on DeepScaleR (competition math) and Principia (scientific reasoning) datasets using Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 models. Comparisons are made against Dr.GRPO (optimizes initial round only), offline aggregation training (optimizes aggregation only), and online multitask training (jointly optimizes both, but not with pass@k for generation).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across its three main contributions:

**a. Principia Suite Findings:**
- **PrincipiaBench Challenge**: Frontier LLMs such as Qwen3-235B (55.58%) and o3 (62.90%) exhibit substantially lower performance on PrincipiaBench compared to their scores on numerical (AIME-2024: 83.33% and 85.63%) and multiple-choice (GPQA-Diamond: 74.34% and 82.29%) benchmarks. This indicates that PrincipiaBench serves as a more challenging testbed for assessing deep reasoning.
- **Effectiveness of Principia Collection**: RL training various base LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B-Base, OctoThinker-8B-Long-Base, Qwen3-4B-Base, Qwen3-4B) on the Principia Collection yields average performance improvements of 7.22-18.35% on PrincipiaBench.
- **Cross-Format Generalization**: Training on the Principia Collection, which contains mathematical object answers, also leads to improvements on numerical (AIME 2024: 7.5-17.5%) and multiple-choice (GPQA-Diamond: 12.31-25.47%) benchmarks. This suggests that learning to derive complex mathematical objects enhances general reasoning abilities that transfer to other answer formats.
- **Superiority of Model-Based Verifiers**: On Principia VerifyBench, `o3` demonstrates 94.05% agreement with human-labeled judgments, and GPT-OSS-120B shows 95.24% agreement. In contrast, the rule-based `math-verify` tool achieves only 5.95% agreement in adversarial cases. Ablation studies reveal that using a strong model-based verifier (like GPT-OSS-120B) is crucial for effective RL training on complex mathematical objects; imperfect verifiers can significantly degrade outcomes.
- **Importance of Complex Object Training**: Training directly on complex mathematical objects in the Principia Collection provides stronger transfer to reasoning tasks than training on datasets primarily containing numerical values or simple mathematical objects (e.g., DeepScaleR, WebInstruct-Verified).
- **Answer Format Combination**: Weight merging independently trained LLMs for different answer formats (mathematical objects, numerical values) consistently outperforms joint training on mixed datasets. Incorporating multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) data, whether in joint training or weight merging, consistently reduces performance compared to using only mathematical-object and numerical-value data.

**b. RLLM Findings:**
- **RLLM Outperforms Baselines**: RLLM consistently outperforms traditional RLHF (with scalar reward models) and RLVR (with rule-based verifiers) across a range of verifiable mathematical and physics reasoning tasks (easy-to-verify and hard-to-verify) and non-verifiable instruction-following tasks. Significant gains are observed, particularly on hard-to-verify problems where standard verifiers fail.
- **Scalability of LM-as-RM**: RLLM performance scales with the size and capability of the LM-as-RM. On-policy trained RMs (e.g., J1-Qwen3-32B-RM) achieve better results than prompted LMs-as-RMs or even larger prompted models (e.g., GPT-OSS-120B). For instance, J1-Qwen3-32B-RM improves AIME24 accuracy by 12% over a strong Qwen3-1.7B (Instruct) model.
- **Generator-Verifier Gap Importance**: A sufficient capability gap between the policy LLM and the LM-as-RM is identified as essential for effective downstream policy improvements. Training a weaker RM on its own responses leads to limited gains.
- **On-Policy RM Training**: On-policy training of the LM-as-RM (where the RM evaluates responses from the policy model it helps train) is shown to be critical, outperforming off-policy trained RMs.
- **RLLM Unifies Post-Training**: RLLM provides a unified framework effective across easy-to-verify, hard-to-verify, and non-verifiable domains, leveraging the LM's reasoning for higher-quality reward signals.

**c. ParaGator Findings:**
- **Inference-Only Aggregation Benefits**: Initial experiments show that basic self-aggregation of parallel generations improves performance on frontier open-source models (e.g., Kimi-K2-Thinking) across competition math benchmarks.
- **Role of Candidate Diversity**: The performance of iterative aggregation is bounded by the initial `pass@k` performance, highlighting that the maximum achievable quality depends on the diversity and quality of the initial candidate pool. Higher initial sampling temperatures, which increase `pass@k`, lead to better aggregation performance.
- **ParaGator Superiority**: ParaGator, with its online, end-to-end training using `pass@k` for generation and `pass@1` for aggregation, consistently achieves the highest rewards in both generation and aggregation rounds. This translates to the strongest overall `pass@1` rates across aggregation steps on competition math (DeepScaleR) and scientific reasoning (PrincipiaBench). For example, ParaGator-Zero-4B-Principia achieves the best average PrincipiaBench score (33.77) and competition-math average (23.72).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions that could influence future directions in artificial intelligence and its application in scientific domains.

The introduction of the **Principia Suite** (PrincipiaBench, Principia Collection, and Principia VerifyBench) directly addresses a critical gap in LLM evaluation and training. By providing benchmarks that explicitly require the derivation of complex mathematical objects, the work offers a more rigorous standard for assessing reasoning capabilities, moving beyond the limitations of numerical or multiple-choice formats. This is particularly significant for applications in STEM fields, where formal, structured expressions are essential for scientific discovery and communication. The Principia Collection provides a valuable resource for training models to acquire these capabilities, and Principia VerifyBench supports the development of reliable automated evaluation tools.

The **RLLM framework** introduces a flexible and robust paradigm for LLM post-training. By utilizing LLMs themselves as "thinking" reward models, RLLM overcomes the inherent limitations of scalar reward models (RLHF) and rule-based verifiers (RLVR). Its ability to provide explicit reasoning traces and function across diverse task types (easy-to-verify, hard-to-verify, non-verifiable) represents a step towards more capable and generalizable LLMs. The findings on the importance of the generator-verifier gap and on-policy training offer empirical guidance for designing effective reinforcement learning systems for LLMs. This could lead to more efficient alignment and fine-tuning processes, reducing reliance on costly human annotation or limited rule-based systems.

The **ParaGator method** contributes to the field of scaling test-time compute for LLMs. By explicitly optimizing both diverse solution generation (via `pass@k`) and effective aggregation (via `pass@1`) within a unified, online reinforcement learning framework, ParaGator provides a strategy to enhance LLM reasoning performance. The demonstration that aggregation performance is bounded by initial diversity underscores the importance of this integrated approach. This method has the potential to improve the reliability and accuracy of LLMs in complex problem-solving scenarios, where exploring multiple reasoning paths and synthesizing the best outcome is crucial.

Collectively, this work indicates that LLMs can be trained to acquire deeper, transferable reasoning skills. The observed cross-format generalization, where training on mathematical object derivation improves performance on numerical and multiple-choice tasks, suggests that these methods foster a more profound understanding rather than task-specific memorization or shortcuts. The artifacts and methodologies introduced in this paper could serve as foundational elements for future research in advanced reasoning, self-improving LLMs, and AI systems capable of autonomous scientific discovery.
