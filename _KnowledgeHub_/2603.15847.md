# FEEL (Force-Enhanced Egocentric Learning): A Dataset for Physical Action Understanding

> [!summary] Summary
> The University of Maryland, College Park, introduces FEEL (Force-Enhanced Egocentric Learning), a large-scale egocentric dataset that uniquely pairs video with direct force measurements from hand-object interactions. This resource enables the development of physically grounded AI models, demonstrating competitive performance in contact understanding and improved transferability for action representation learning across various benchmarks.

## Problem

- Existing egocentric video datasets primarily capture visual effects of human-object interactions, lacking direct measurements of underlying physical causes like force.
- Understanding contact and force in computer vision is difficult due to the scarcity of real-world force data and the labor-intensive, error-prone nature of manual annotation for contact events.
- Learning action solely from video necessitates inferring implicit action structures from high-dimensional pixel data without explicit physical grounding.

## Method

- Developed **FEEL**, a novel dataset integrating ==force data from a custom piezoresistive glove== with egocentric video from Meta's Project Aria glasses, providing 27 hours of synchronized, natural manipulation data.
- Implemented a methodology to derive precise, ==per-frame contact states== directly from filtered force sensor data, offering a scalable and objective alternative to manual annotation.
- Applied FEEL to ==contact understanding== tasks (temporal and spatial segmentation) and ==action representation learning== (using ==force prediction as a self-supervised pretraining objective==).

## Results

- The FEEL dataset was released, containing approximately **3 million** force-synchronized frames (**27 hours**) of egocentric manipulation, with **45%** involving hand-object contact and derived contact labels.
- Models trained on FEEL achieved **95.6%** Binary Contact accuracy and **88.9%** Binary Signed Contact accuracy, outperforming supervised baselines, and showed strong zero-shot transfer (e.g., **87.3%** BC on EPIC-VISOR) in contact understanding.
- Force pretraining on FEEL data improved action recognition on downstream benchmarks; for instance, Hiera-B showed gains of **4.85** percentage points for verb and **6.26** percentage points for noun prediction on EPIC-Kitchens, with improvements across SSV2, Ego-Exo4D, and Meccano, especially in the frozen evaluation setting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct force measurements provide a low-dimensional, physically grounded signal that can constrain and improve the extraction of action information from high-dimensional visual data.
> - Force-derived contact labels offer a scalable and precise alternative to manual annotations, overcoming issues of human subjectivity and imprecision in contact event detection.
> - Pretraining video backbones with force prediction as a self-supervised task instills more transferable and physically informed action representations, particularly beneficial for downstream tasks on smaller datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of physical action understanding in egocentric vision has seen substantial advancements, largely driven by the availability of large-scale egocentric video datasets. These datasets, such as EgoHands, VISOR, HOI4D, and Ego4D, have primarily focused on the visual modality, capturing the observable effects of human-object interactions. However, a critical limitation of these existing resources is the absence of direct measurements of the underlying physical causes of action, specifically force. Force is a fundamental primitive in physical interaction, driving changes that are only implicitly represented in pixel streams.

Prior work in contact understanding typically relies on extensive manual annotation of images or videos, which is a labor-intensive process prone to label noise and scalability issues. Some approaches use semi-automatic methods or controlled lab settings with motion capture systems, while others resort to synthetic data generation to overcome the challenges of real-world data collection. Similarly, force understanding in computer vision has been underexplored due to the scarcity of real-world force data, often leading researchers to depend on simulations. While robotics has begun to incorporate force-aware visual representations in real-world data collection, these efforts are often limited to simpler manipulation tasks.

The work presented in this paper addresses this gap by introducing FEEL, a dataset that integrates force measurements directly into egocentric video. This represents a shift from analyzing merely the visual effects of actions to understanding their physical causes. By providing a low-dimensional, physically grounded signal, FEEL aims to constrain how action is extracted from high-dimensional pixel data, offering a novel modality that existing large-scale egocentric datasets lack. This positions FEEL as a foundational resource for developing physically informed AI models, potentially improving performance in tasks like action recognition, contact understanding, and robot manipulation learning, which have historically struggled with the implicit nature of force in visual data. The dataset explicitly distinguishes itself from existing datasets by offering both contact annotations and force measurements in a real-world egocentric context (Table 1).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance physical action understanding by integrating force measurements into egocentric video datasets. The authors contend that force is a critical primitive for comprehending physical interaction, as it represents the underlying cause of actions, in contrast to vision, which primarily captures motion and appearance as observable effects. Learning actions solely from video necessitates inferring implicit action structures from high-dimensional pixel data. Incorporating force aims to transform this hidden structure into an intrinsically lower-dimensional, learnable signal.

The motivations for introducing FEEL are multifaceted:

1. **Addressing the limitations of existing datasets:** Current egocentric video datasets, despite their utility, do not directly capture the causal dynamics of action through force measurements. This absence is largely due to the difficulty and unreliability of obtaining force data through manual labeling. The physical responses of objects to applied forces can be subtle and challenging for human annotators to discern from visual changes alone, including the precise onset and offset of contact (Figure 3).
2. **Enabling scalable and precise contact understanding:** Manual annotation of contact events is resource-intensive and does not scale efficiently. The project aims to derive contact states directly from force measurements, providing temporally precise per-frame contact states and boundaries. This force-derived contact information is intended to offer a scalable alternative to manual annotation, which often suffers from imprecision, especially during transitional phases.
3. **Developing physically grounded action representations:** The authors hypothesize that using force as supervision can provide a low-dimensional, physically grounded signal that constrains the extraction of action information from high-dimensional pixel space. The goal is to leverage these force signals to learn action representations that are more physically informed than those derived from appearance-based features alone.
4. **Demonstrating utility through specific tasks:** The research sets out to demonstrate the practical utility of the FEEL dataset through its application to two families of tasks:
 - **Contact Understanding:** This involves jointly performing temporal contact segmentation (classifying per-frame contact state for each hand) and pixel-level segmentation (segmenting the object(s) in contact with each hand). Force measurements are expected to provide precise supervision for contact onset and offset, facilitating the learning of sharp interaction boundaries.
 - **Action Representation Learning:** This task aims to use force prediction as a self-supervised pretraining objective for video backbones. The objective is to encourage networks to infer physically grounded action structures from video, and then evaluate the transferability of these representations to downstream action recognition tasks on established benchmarks.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond purely visual action understanding by introducing a dataset that directly captures the physical forces involved in human-object interactions, thereby enabling more accurate, scalable, and physically grounded learning paradigms for various action understanding tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research involved the development of a novel dataset, FEEL, and the application of this dataset to two distinct learning tasks: Contact Understanding and Action Representation Learning.

#### 3.1. Dataset Collection (FEEL)

**Hardware:**
The data collection system combined two synchronized components:
- **Custom Piezoresistive Force Glove:** This glove features six piezoresistive sensors (Flexiforce A301, Tekscan) mounted on a glove, with one sensor at each fingertip and a sensor strip on the palm. These sensors measure forces up to 45 N. An Arduino Nano microcontroller, a 3.3V Lithium Polymer battery, and a toggle switch are housed in a wrist-mounted case (Figure 4).
- **Meta’s Project Aria Glasses (Gen 1):** This device captures egocentric visual and spatial audio streams. It includes a forward-facing RGB camera (1440 × 1440 pixels), two monochrome scene cameras (640 × 480 pixels) for peripheral coverage, a 7-microphone array, and dual IMUs sampled at 800 Hz and 1000 Hz.

**Sensor Calibration and Synchronization:**
A three-step calibration procedure was implemented at the beginning of each recording session to align the independent data streams from the force gloves and Aria glasses, which operate on separate internal clocks.

**Data Scale and Environment:**
FEEL contains approximately 3 million force-synchronized frames, totaling about 27 hours of natural, unscripted manipulation data, with roughly 45% of frames involving hand-object contact. The data collection focused on diverse manipulation activities within kitchen environments.

**Data Processing:**
- **Egocentric Pose and Hand Tracking:** Meta’s Machine Perception Services (MPS) were used to extract 6-DoF camera trajectories (1 kHz) from side camera and IMU data. MPS also performed 3D hand tracking, recovering hand keypoints for each frame.
- **Additional Processing:** A weak labeling strategy was employed to identify object nouns: for each 2-minute video segment, 8 uniformly sampled frames were annotated with the set of object nouns interacted with. Dense geometric cues were computed, including scaled depth maps (in meters) predicted using MLDepthPro and optical flow fields estimated between consecutive frames using SEA-RAFT.

#### 3.2. Contact Understanding

This task involved jointly classifying the per-frame contact state for each hand (temporal contact segmentation) and segmenting the object(s) in contact at a pixel level.

**Contact Detection:**
Raw piezoresistive sensor data underwent a multi-stage filtering pipeline to overcome baseline drift and high-frequency noise. This pipeline included Hampel filtering for outlier removal, Gaussian smoothing, time-varying baseline estimation via rolling percentile, baseline subtraction with negative clipping, and exclusion of regions with abrupt baseline changes or high RMS magnitudes. Each sensor’s signal was normalized, and the geometric mean across all six sensors produced a unified force signal. Dual thresholds were applied: signals exceeding an upper threshold indicated contact, signals below a lower threshold indicated non-contact, and values in between were excluded as ambiguous.

**Segmentation Pseudolabel Generation:**
A weakly supervised approach generated pseudo-labels for spatially segmenting contacted objects, eliminating the need for manual object segmentation. The inputs were:
1. Frames identified as containing contact by the force-based detection system.
2. Object mask proposals generated by SAM3 using concept prompts derived from the weakly labeled object nouns.
3. Pre-computed optical flow fields (SEA-RAFT).
4. Camera intrinsics/extrinsics from MPS.
5. Pre-computed depth maps (MLDepthPro).
The process involved: (1) extracting masked optical flow for each object proposal; (2) computing the fundamental matrix between consecutive frames using MPS-provided data; (3) evaluating the Sampson epipolar error within each mask (higher error indicates greater object motion); (4) selecting the proposal with the maximum mean epipolar error; and (5) accepting this as a contacted object if a sufficient number of its pixels were within a 3D distance threshold of the 3D hand centroid. This yielded 500K binary contact pseudolabels, of which 180K included binary segmentations of contacted objects.

**Training:**
A Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) model, built on top of frozen DINOv3 features, was trained. It received an RGB image as input and produced two prediction heads per hand (left/right): a binary contact classifier and a two-channel segmentation map for the object(s) in contact. Supervision for each hand's head was applied only when that hand was at least partially visible. Data augmentation included random crops and horizontal flips, mirroring target outputs during flips.

#### 3.3. Action Representation Learning from Video

This task utilized force prediction as a self-supervised pretraining objective for video backbones, aiming to learn physically grounded action representations transferable to downstream action understanding tasks.

**Self-supervised Pretraining:**
The model took a video clip as input and predicted per-hand forces. Training clips were selected only if at least one hand was visible for 50% or more of the frames, ensuring a relevant training signal.

**Transfer Learning:**
Video networks were initialized with weights pretrained on K710 (for Hiera-B) or Ego4D (for EgoVideo-B). These models were then fine-tuned on FEEL's force data, and the resulting force-aware representations were transferred to action recognition benchmarks: EPIC-Kitchens-100 (verb/noun prediction), SomethingSomething-V2, Ego-Exo4D, and Meccano (action prediction). Evaluation was performed in both frozen (feature extraction) and unfrozen (end-to-end fine-tuning) settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded a new dataset and demonstrated its utility across two distinct families of tasks, showcasing the benefits of force-enhanced learning for physical action understanding.

#### 4.1. Release of FEEL Dataset

The primary contribution is the release of FEEL, a large-scale egocentric force-video dataset. It comprises approximately 3 million frames collected over around 27 hours of natural, unscripted manipulation in kitchen environments. Notably, approximately 45% of these frames involve hand-object contact. The dataset provides scalable, force-derived per-frame contact states, with 1.35 million frames featuring hands and objects in contact (along with object segmentations) and 1.65 million frames depicting no contact. This dataset captures the physical causes of hand-object manipulation, rather than solely visual effects.

#### 4.2. Contact Understanding Results

**Temporal Contact Detection (Table 2):**
The model trained on FEEL, leveraging force-derived contact labels, demonstrated competitive performance in temporal contact detection.
- It achieved 95.6% Binary Contact (BC) accuracy and 88.9% Binary Signed Contact (BSC) accuracy on the FEEL validation set, substantially outperforming a zero-shot depth baseline and fully supervised baselines such as HOIRef, EPIC-VISOR, and 100DOH.
- In zero-shot transfer to other datasets, the FEEL-trained model achieved 82.2% BC and 81.3% BSC on HOI4D, and 87.3% BC and 79.7% BSC on EPIC-VISOR. These results were competitive with, and in several cases surpassed, fully supervised models (e.g., 100DOH achieved 81.3% BC and 71.9% BSC on VISOR, while the FEEL model achieved 87.3% BC and 79.7% BSC on VISOR).
- The model exhibited reliable left/right hand discrimination, indicated by a smaller gap between BC and BSC metrics compared to methods like 100DOH. Qualitatively, the model precisely localized contact onset, avoiding false positives due to mere hand-object proximity (Figure 6).

**Spatial Contact Segmentation (Table 3):**
The weakly supervised model achieved competitive spatial segmentation performance across the evaluation datasets.
- On the FEEL validation set, the model achieved an Intersection-over-Union (IOU) of 0.625, significantly higher than other baselines (e.g., 100DOH+SAM achieved 0.218 IOU).
- In zero-shot transfer, the model achieved 0.751 IOU on HOI4D and 0.399 IOU on EPIC-VISOR. While slightly trailing the 100DOH+SAM baseline on VISOR (0.402 IOU), this was achieved despite 100DOH+SAM relying on a powerful, Internet-scale pretrained segmentation model (SAM) and being fully trained on the EPIC-VISOR dataset, whereas the FEEL model was deployed zero-shot.
- Qualitative results (Figure 7) showed the model's ability to identify the correct hand side, avoid confusing proximity with contact, and handle simultaneous two-hand contacts. While some predictions might exhibit partial coverage, the model generally identified the correct contacted object more consistently than other methods that sometimes segmented incorrect objects.

#### 4.3. Action Representation Learning Results (Table 4)

Force pretraining over FEEL data generally improved action recognition performance on downstream benchmarks.
- **Frozen Evaluation:** In the frozen feature extraction setting, force pretraining consistently led to larger performance gains. For Hiera-B, pretraining on FEEL improved verb prediction by 4.85 percentage points (pp) and noun prediction by 6.26 pp on EPIC-Kitchens, action prediction by 2.96 pp on SSV2, 7.72 pp on Ego-Exo4D, and 3.15 pp on Meccano, compared to K710 pretraining alone. For EgoVideo-B, pretraining on FEEL improved verb prediction by 2.76 pp and noun prediction by 0.69 pp on EPIC-Kitchens, action prediction by 4.73 pp on SSV2, 1.75 pp on Ego-Exo4D, and 1.64 pp on Meccano, compared to Ego4D pretraining. These results suggest that force-aware features are more immediately transferable.
- **Unfrozen Evaluation:** While gains were smaller, force pretraining still resulted in improved or comparable performance in the unfrozen, end-to-end fine-tuning setting. For Hiera-B, it improved noun prediction by 0.9 pp on EPIC-Kitchens, action prediction by 1.13 pp on SSV2, and 2.61 pp on Meccano. For EgoVideo-B, it improved verb prediction by 0.35 pp on EPIC-Kitchens, action prediction by 0.36 pp on SSV2, and 1.02 pp on Meccano.
- **Dataset Size Correlation:** The performance benefits of force pretraining were more pronounced on smaller downstream datasets (e.g., Meccano, Ego-Exo4D) compared to larger ones (EPIC-Kitchens, SomethingSomething-V2). This observation aligns with the hypothesis that physically grounded representations may reduce the amount of labeled downstream data required for effective action classifiers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of FEEL represents a significant contribution to the field of computer vision and robotics, particularly in the domain of physical action understanding.

**Novelty and Dataset Contribution:** FEEL is distinguished as the first large-scale egocentric dataset to pair force measurements with video, directly capturing the physical causes of hand-object interaction. This addresses a critical gap in existing datasets that primarily provide visual observations, enabling a more direct study of the mechanics of human manipulation. The inclusion of approximately 3 million frames, with precisely localized contact information derived from force sensors, provides a rich resource for research.

**Scalability and Reduced Annotation Reliance:** The custom force-sensing glove and the methodology for deriving contact labels directly from force measurements offer a scalable alternative to labor-intensive manual annotation. This approach mitigates issues of label noise and human subjectivity that often plague contact and interaction datasets, paving the way for more robust and efficiently generated ground truth data.

**Enhanced Understanding of Physical Interaction:** By providing explicit force signals, FEEL facilitates the development of physically grounded AI models. This moves beyond inferring physical properties indirectly from visual cues, offering a more direct path to understanding the causal dynamics of actions. The demonstrated improvements in both contact understanding and action representation learning suggest that integrating force data can lead to more accurate and robust models.

**Improved Model Performance and Generalization:** The empirical results show that models pretrained on FEEL data achieve competitive performance in temporal contact detection and spatial contact segmentation, often surpassing or matching fully supervised methods without requiring manual contact or object segmentation annotations. For action recognition, force-aware pretraining generally enhances transfer performance, particularly for frozen representations and on smaller downstream datasets. This indicates that force supervision instills a qualitatively different and more transferable representational prior.

**Potential Future Applications:** The FEEL dataset and the methodologies explored open several avenues for future research and applications. These include:
- **Robotics:** Development of more capable and force-aware visuomotor control systems for robots, enabling them to learn complex manipulation policies directly from human demonstrations.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** Improved understanding of human intent and interaction dynamics for more intuitive and effective human-robot collaboration.
- **Virtual and Augmented Reality:** Creation of more realistic and interactive virtual environments where physical interactions are accurately simulated.
- **Fine-grained Hand Understanding:** Detailed analysis of hand kinematics and force exertion, leading to advancements in understanding dexterous manipulation.

While the work identifies hardware limitations (e.g., glove discomfort, sensor reliability, obstruction of fine dexterity) and algorithmic limitations (e.g., reliance on object motion for pseudolabel generation), it lays a foundation for future improvements. The overarching impact is the acceleration of research towards AI systems that possess a deeper, physically informed understanding of the world, bridging the gap between observable visual effects and their underlying physical causes.
