# Latent Memory Palace: Reasoning for Control as Autoregressive Variational Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> The Latent Memory Palace (LMP) framework re-conceptualizes iterative and adaptive reasoning for continuous robotic control as an autoregressive variational inference problem in a latent space. This approach yielded improved performance over state-of-the-art baselines in real-world and simulated manipulation tasks, while also demonstrating adaptive computational allocation during test time.

## Problem

- Robotic control policies lack an inherent mechanism for adaptive, iterative "reasoning" akin to human deliberation or large language models.
- Directly applying language-based reasoning (e.g., Chain-of-Thought) to continuous robotic control is challenging due to the insufficient granularity of linguistic tokens for precise spatial and motor actions.
- Existing control policies often use fixed-computation architectures, limiting their flexibility and generalization across tasks of varying complexity.

## Method

- Formulate reasoning as ==autoregressive variational inference== in a learned latent space, where the latent variable is a variable-length sequence of discrete tokens (**Latent Memory Palace**).
- Introduce a ==compression mechanism== via an exponentially decaying variance schedule for the action decoder, encouraging the policy to adaptively terminate latent sequence generation using an ==End-of-Sequence (EOS) token==.
- Optimize the non-differentiable sampling process by reframing the ==ELBO== as a latent-space ==reinforcement learning== problem, using a ==PPO-style clipped surrogate objective== and per-step ==KL divergence==.

## Results

- LMP-π achieved superior performance over Diffusion Policy on real-world DROID tasks, including a **95%** success rate for picking at least two objects in 'clean-table', and showed higher success (**0.645%** vs **0.463%**) on the 10 most challenging LIBERO-90 tasks.
- LMP-tok significantly outperformed alternative action tokenizers (VQ-VAE, FAST, OAT) in downstream policy performance across RoboMimic and LIBERO, notably achieving non-trivial success on the high-precision 'tool-hang' task where others failed.
- The policy demonstrated adaptive test-time compute allocation, using fewer latent reasoning steps during critical gripper actions (e.g., grasping) and more steps during general movements, with a negative correlation (**r=-0.518**) between reasoning steps and action uncertainty.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Latent space can serve as a principled, end-to-end learned medium for iterative and adaptive reasoning in continuous control policies, circumventing limitations of language tokens.
> - An autoregressive latent structure with an explicit length penalty (via decaying decoder variance) is effective in encouraging policies to adaptively allocate computational effort based on the task's precision requirements.
> - Iterative computation in the latent space significantly enhances performance compared to fixed-length latent representations, particularly in complex manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenge of enabling adaptive, iterative "reasoning" in continuous control policies, such as those used in robotics. This capacity for flexible decision-making, where computation time and effort vary with task complexity, is observed in human cognition and has been demonstrated by large language models (LLMs) through techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting. However, directly transferring such language-based reasoning to robotic control is problematic because linguistic tokens may lack the necessary granularity for spatial understanding and precise motor actions.

The paper positions its contribution within several related research areas:
- **Reasoning for Foundation Models**: Previous work on foundation models has shown that iterative inference and adaptive test-time computation can improve performance, for example, through Chain-of-Thought prompting or RL-based reasoning models. Concepts like latent reasoning aim to shift multi-step inference to implicit representations for increased bandwidth. This work specifically builds on recent variational latent reasoning approaches, differentiating itself by addressing the fundamental issue that robotic policies lack an inherent reasoning medium. It proposes latent space as a principled framework for iterative and adaptive computation in robotics.
- **Behavior Cloning via Generative Control Policies**: The field of behavior cloning (BC) often frames policy learning as generative modeling of actions. Existing methods include energy-based models (IBC), diffusion models (Diffusion Policy), and discrete action token approaches within transformer policies (BeT, VQ-BeT). This work introduces a discrete latent-variable model that can dynamically adjust its computational depth for action prediction, aiming to combine the benefits of expressivity with adaptive computation.
- **Reasoning for Robotics**: Contemporary robotics research is exploring iterative reasoning and test-time computation through various avenues. Some approaches use explicit intermediate reasoning traces or sub-steps (e.g., ECoT, ECoT Lite, OneTwoVLA). Others introduce structured intermediate representations, such as spatial abstractions or hierarchical guidance. Recent work like RD-VLA also explores latent iterative reasoning with a recurrent-depth transformer. In contrast to these methods that often involve manually designed reasoning interfaces or stopping criteria, this paper proposes an approach that reasons in an autoregressive latent space trained end-to-end for action prediction, without explicit design choices for reasoning steps.

The work contributes a novel perspective by integrating autoregressive variational inference to facilitate iterative and adaptive latent reasoning specifically for robotic control, thus bridging concepts from general foundation models and adapting them to the unique requirements of embodied decision-making.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether iterative and adaptive computation, analogous to human deliberation or LLM "reasoning," can be effectively applied to sequential decision-making problems in robotics. The authors hypothesize that such an approach could offer two primary benefits for robotic policies: more efficient decision-making when faced with task variability and improved generalization capabilities.

The motivation stems from observing the flexibility in human decision-making, which ranges from instantaneous, reflexive actions to deliberate, multi-step processes, with computational effort scaling with task complexity. Similarly, modern LLMs have demonstrated "reasoning" capabilities by generating intermediate tokens before producing a final answer, leading to enhanced performance in complex problems.

However, a direct transfer of LLM techniques to continuous control policies for robotics presents challenges. Language tokens, by their nature, might not possess the sufficient granularity or expressiveness required for nuanced spatial understanding and precise motor control, which are critical in robotic manipulation tasks. To overcome this limitation, the authors propose that robotic policies should reason iteratively within a *latent space*. This latent space would be learned end-to-end for action prediction, allowing intermediate representations to capture control-relevant information at an appropriate level of detail, thereby preserving the advantages of iterative and adaptive computation.

To formalize this latent-space reasoning, the research builds upon variational inference, a framework for learning latent-variable models. This perspective reframes an action not as a direct prediction from an observation but as the outcome of an intermediate latent computational process. A key innovation and motivation for this work is to move beyond standard variational inference, which typically employs fixed-dimensional latent variables, by introducing an *autoregressive latent distribution*. This variable-length structure, termed "Latent Memory Palace" (LMP), allows the policy to adapt its computation to each specific input. The "memory palace" analogy describes this: the autoregressive latent space is the palace, each latent token is a location, and generating the latent sequence is akin to traversing a path to retrieve control-relevant information. Crucially, the inclusion of an End-of-Sequence (EOS) token enables the policy to terminate the latent sequence generation as needed, facilitating the desired iterative and adaptive allocation of computation.

Furthermore, a specific motivation is to elicit adaptive behavior by introducing a compression mechanism. This is achieved by parameterizing the action decoder with a variance schedule that decays with the length of the latent sequence. This design choice implies a length penalty, encouraging the model to use more latent steps only when a high degree of precision in action prediction is genuinely required.

Finally, the work is motivated by the potential for this framework to generalize to action tokenization. By removing observation conditioning, the same variational inference formulation can be adapted to create a variable-length sequential action tokenizer (LMP-tok), which can compress continuous actions into discrete latent tokens for use by downstream autoregressive policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on formulating reasoning for sequential decision-making within an imitation learning context, where the goal is to learn a control policy from expert demonstrations. The core of the approach is the Latent Memory Palace (LMP), which re-conceptualizes reasoning as autoregressive variational inference.

#### 3.1. Reasoning as Variational Inference with Autoregressive Latents

The policy $\pi_\theta(a|o)$ is parameterized through a latent variable model where an action $a$ is generated from an observation $o$ and an intermediate latent variable $z$. Policy learning involves maximizing the likelihood $p(a|o)$. Since marginalizing over $z$ is intractable, the true posterior $p(z|o,a)$ is approximated by a variational distribution $q_\theta(z|o,a)$, and the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is maximized:
$L = E_{z \sim q_\theta(z|o,a)}[\log p_\phi(a|o,z)] - D_{KL}(q_\theta(z|o,a) || p_\theta(z|o))$.
Here, $p_\theta(z|o)$ is the prior distribution, $q_\theta(z|o,a)$ is the posterior distribution, and $p_\phi(a|o,z)$ is the action decoder. The prior and decoder together define the control policy (LMP-π) at test time.

The key innovation is to define $z$ not as a fixed-dimensional variable but as a **variable-length autoregressive sequence** $z = (z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_{T(z)})$, where $z_t$ are discrete tokens from a vocabulary $V \cup \{\text{EOS}\}$. The prior $p_\theta(z|o)$ and posterior $q_\theta(z|o,a)$ are factorized autoregressively, allowing for iterative generation of latent tokens until an EOS token is produced, enabling adaptive computation.

To elicit adaptive behavior, **compression** is introduced through the action decoder's variance. The decoder $p_\phi(a|o,z)$ is parameterized as an isotropic Gaussian $N(\mu_\phi(o,z), \sigma^2(T(z))I)$, where the variance $\sigma^2(T(z))$ decays exponentially with the latent sequence length $T(z)$ (i.e., $\sigma(T(z)) = \gamma^{T(z)}\sigma_0$ with $\gamma \in (0,1)$). This implies a length penalty, forcing the model to only extend the latent sequence if it can achieve a proportionally sharper (more precise) action prediction, effectively allocating more compute when uncertainty is reducible.

#### 3.2. Tractable Optimization via Latent-Space Reinforcement Learning

Optimizing the ELBO with variable-length autoregressive sampling presents a challenge due to non-differentiable sampling processes. The authors address this by reframing the ELBO as a reinforcement learning (RL) objective over latent-token trajectories.
For a given observation-action pair $(o,a)$, a "latent episode" is defined where tokens $z_t$ are sampled from the posterior $q_\theta(\cdot|o,a)$ until EOS. The "return" for a latent trajectory $z_{1:T(z)}$ comprises a reconstruction reward ($\log p_\phi(a|o,z_{1:T(z)})$) and a penalty from the KL divergence between the posterior and prior. Notably, this RL is applied to optimize *inference over latent reasoning chains*, not for direct interaction with the physical environment.

To reduce variance in gradient estimation, the sequence-level KL term is decomposed into **per-step contributions** using the autoregressive structure. Optimization is then performed using a **PPO-style clipped surrogate objective**, maximizing a combination of the reconstruction likelihood and the per-step KL divergence. The objective includes a length-aware clipped likelihood ratio for stabilization.

Two additional stabilization techniques are employed to prevent latent collapse:
1. **Uniform-interpolated regularization**: The prior is regularized toward a length-aware uniform prior over the vocabulary.
2. **Per-step free-nats clipping**: The KL contribution at each timestep is capped by a free-nats threshold, preventing overly aggressive distillation early in training.

#### 3.3. Connection to Action Tokenizer (LMP-tok)

The same variational inference framework can be adapted to create a variable-length sequential action tokenizer (LMP-tok). This is achieved by dropping the observation conditioning from the model. The tokenizer consists of an encoder $q_\theta(z_{1:T(z)}|a)$ that autoregressively generates tokens, and a decoder $p_\phi(a|z_{1:T(z)})$ that converts token sequences back into continuous actions. The prior $p(z_{1:T(z)})$ is a uniform distribution over the tokens. This allows sequences of arbitrary length to be decoded into valid actions, contrasting with some existing tokenizers that require exact reconstruction of fixed-structure outputs.

#### 3.4. Practical Instantiation

The LMP-π architecture utilizes a shared causal transformer with cross-attention for both the prior $p_\theta(z|o)$ and posterior $q_\theta(z|o,a)$. The posterior is conditioned on image and action embeddings, while the prior is conditioned only on observation embeddings, with actions masked out. The action decoder is a bidirectional transformer that generates action chunks from learnable query tokens conditioned on observation and sampled latent tokens. Latent sequences are truncated at a fixed maximum length during training. For LMP-π, the decoder variance schedule is defined by $\sigma_{max}, \sigma_{min}$ with exponential decay, and the decoder can also predict a per-dimension scale. For LMP-tok, a scalar learned variance is used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across real-world (DROID) and simulated (LIBERO, D3IL, RoboMimic) visuomotor control benchmarks to evaluate the performance, effectiveness of tokenization, adaptive compute allocation, and contributing factors of the proposed Latent Memory Palace (LMP) framework.

#### 4.1. LMP-π as a Generative Control Policy

- **Real-world DROID tasks**: LMP-π demonstrated stronger empirical performance compared to Diffusion Policy (DP), a state-of-the-art generative control policy. In zero-shot settings, LMP-π outperformed DP on 'block-bowl' and 'marker-mug' tasks. For finetuned, challenging tasks requiring precision ('peg-hole') and compositional generalization ('clean-table'), LMP-π also showed higher success rates, achieving 95% success for picking at least 2 objects in 'clean-table'.
- **Simulated LIBERO-90 multitask benchmark**: LMP-π achieved a slightly higher overall success rate (0.933 ± 0.003) than DP (0.909 ± 0.004). More notably, on the 10 most challenging tasks (bottom-10), LMP-π exhibited a significantly higher success rate (0.645 ± 0.023) compared to DP (0.463 ± 0.011), suggesting reduced cross-task interference and improved retrieval capabilities.
- **D3IL and RoboMimic single-task benchmarks**: LMP-π generally achieved comparable or better performance than Diffusion Policy in these domains, particularly in tasks requiring precise manipulation.

#### 4.2. LMP-tok as an Effective Action Tokenizer

- LMP-tok was evaluated against three alternative action tokenizers: VQ-VAE, FAST, and OAT, by training the same downstream autoregressive transformer policy on the tokenized actions.
- Across RoboMimic and LIBERO tasks, LMP-tok significantly outperformed all baselines in downstream policy performance.
- Notably, LMP-tok was the only method to achieve non-trivial performance on the high-precision RoboMimic 'tool-hang' task, where others largely failed. The paper attributes the baselines' limitations to factors such as insufficient precision (VQ-VAE), demanding reconstruction requirements (FAST), or the lack of a variational objective to maintain latent diversity (OAT).

#### 4.3. Analysis of Contributing Factors and Adaptive Reasoning

- **Iterative vs. Non-Iterative Computation**: Comparison of LMP-π with non-iterative latent-variable baselines (VAE Policy, VQ-BeT) on D3IL and RoboMimic tasks showed that LMP-π consistently achieved higher success rates. This indicates that fixed-length latent representations may lack the granularity necessary for complex manipulation tasks, highlighting the benefit of iterative computation.
- **Contribution of Compression**: Ablation studies on the LIBERO-90 benchmark, varying the strength of the decoder's variance schedule (compression strength), revealed that stronger compression (lower $\sigma_{min}$) led to improved policy performance. This suggests that parsimonious allocation of latent compute is beneficial for generalization. Analysis of reconstruction error indicated that insufficient compression causes latents to be ignored, while excessive compression can increase overall reconstruction error by forcing shorter traces. A balanced configuration of variance decay was found to be empirically effective.
- **Qualitative Analysis of Test-Time Compute Allocation**: Visualizations of latent step allocation during policy rollouts showed that LMP-π adaptively uses fewer reasoning steps during critical gripper movements (e.g., grasping, releasing, hand-over) in tasks like DROID 'clean-table' and RoboMimic 'tool-hang'/'transport'. More steps were used during general movement or alignment phases. This pattern is hypothesized to reflect irreducible timing variability in expert demonstrations for gripper commands.
- **Mode-Seeking Behavior**: When the maximum number of latent steps was manually truncated at test time, the policy exhibited mode-seeking behavior, meaning it became more deterministic but still produced valid actions, rather than suffering from mode-averaging. Performance decreased only marginally, suggesting robustness.
- **Quantitative Analysis of Test-Time Compute Allocation**: A quantitative study correlated the average number of latent reasoning steps with non-parametric estimates of action uncertainty (KNN action variance) in a LIBERO multitask. A negative correlation (r = -0.518) was found: fewer reasoning steps were used when expert actions near the current state exhibited higher variance, indicating that the model allocated less compute when facing irreducible uncertainty, such as gripper timing variations.
- **Training Dynamics**: During training, the average latent length was observed to increase gradually, similar to "reasoning length" trends in other models, demonstrating that the model learns to effectively utilize more latent steps over time. More aggressive variance decay in the decoder resulted in shorter average reasoning lengths.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Latent Memory Palace (LMP) framework introduces a novel perspective on developing control policies capable of adaptive and iterative reasoning by formulating it as autoregressive variational inference. This approach addresses a fundamental limitation in transferring reasoning capabilities from language models to robotics, specifically the challenge of achieving fine-grained spatial and motor control with symbolic language representations.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Principled Framework for Latent Reasoning**: LMP provides a principled, end-to-end learning framework for iterative reasoning in a latent space, which is critical for robotic policies that lack a natural "reasoning medium" like language.
- **Enhanced Control Policy Performance**: The resulting policy, LMP-π, demonstrates strong empirical performance across a variety of complex robotic manipulation tasks in both simulated and real-world environments. Its ability to outperform state-of-the-art baselines like Diffusion Policy, particularly in challenging, high-precision, and multitask scenarios, suggests a significant advancement in robot learning.
- **Effective Action Tokenization**: The derivative framework, LMP-tok, offers a variable-length action tokenizer that substantially improves the performance of downstream autoregressive policies compared to existing tokenization schemes. This capability is important for learning expressive policies from continuous actions.
- **Adaptive and Interpretable Compute Allocation**: A key contribution is the demonstration that LMP-π can adaptively allocate test-time computation. The observed correlation between reasoning steps and action uncertainty, particularly the reduction in steps during highly variable gripper actions, provides a degree of interpretability regarding the policy's internal deliberation process. This adaptive allocation is also shown to be crucial for the overall performance of the policy.
- **Foundational Insights into Iterative Computation**: The ablation studies rigorously establish that both iterative computation and adaptive compute allocation are essential factors contributing to the method's performance, providing insights into the design of future robotic learning systems.

The potential impact of this research is broad, extending to the development of more intelligent, flexible, and efficient robotic systems. By enabling robots to adaptively "think" or "deliberate" in a continuous latent space, this work could contribute to:
- **More Robust and Generalizable Robotic Skills**: Policies that can dynamically adjust their computational effort are likely to be more robust to variations and generalize better to novel task instances.
- **Improved Efficiency**: Adaptive computation can lead to more efficient decision-making by avoiding unnecessary deliberation for simpler actions while allocating sufficient resources for complex ones.
- **Advancements in Multimodal Robotic Learning**: The framework's flexibility could be beneficial for integrating diverse sensory inputs and generating complex, multi-modal actions.
- **Next-Generation Foundation Models for Robotics**: By demonstrating the efficacy of autoregressive variational inference, the work opens avenues for developing more sophisticated, "reasoning-capable" foundation models that can learn and execute a wider range of robotic behaviors.

Future directions include exploring continuous latent chains akin to diffusion processes and investigating mechanisms for maintaining persistent latent states across an entire episode, rather than regenerating them at each step, to enhance long-term coherence in robotic behaviors. While the current training process has noted sensitivities to hyperparameters due to its sampling-based RL nature, addressing these stability and scalability aspects in future work could further amplify its impact.
