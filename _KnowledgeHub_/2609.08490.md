# Multi-bounce Drum Roll with Optimized Active Tricks to Leverage Soft Embodiment

> [!summary] Summary
> A dual-arm drumming robot with 3D-printed elastic end-effectors performs multi-bounce drum rolls via two feedforward stroke tricks: Tap-Pull, which retracts the stick horizontally after impact to prolong rebounds, and Micro-Pulse, which injects small vertical oscillations to counter amplitude decay. Bayesian optimization tunes both. The soft arm reached 12.25 bounces per stroke and 6.8-times higher acoustic efficiency than rigid.

## Problem

- Elasticity alone is passive: softness sets bounce count and interval but cannot shape the sound waveform between strokes into a desired uniform profile.
- Multiple bounces from one strike dissipate kinetic energy, so successive impact amplitudes decay, trading bounce count against amplitude uniformity.
- Prior reinforcement-learning stiffness tuning is sample-inefficient, needs real-world audio rewards computed after each performance, and cannot close the loop at roll frequencies.

## Method

- **Tap-Pull** trick: after the vertical step strike, a horizontal ramp command pulls the stick by displacement dx_pull following a wait t_down, injecting energy into passive rebounds.
- **Micro-Pulse** trick: the downstroke is split into 2(N-1)+1 segments with small-amplitude pulses A_mu inserted, exciting elastic end-effector dynamics without altering stroke period.
- ==Bayesian optimization== with a ==Tree-Structured Parzen Estimator== (Optuna) minimizes per-trick objectives built from ==Hilbert-envelope peak detection==: bounce count, interval coefficient of variation, decay ratio, motor power.

## Results

- Soft arm with Tap-Pull achieved **12.25** bounces per stroke and the best interval uniformity (**0.290**), versus **8.100** for soft Tap and **7.125** for rigid Tap-Pull.
- Soft Micro-Pulse cut amplitude decay to **0.143** from **0.539** (soft pulse) and **0.312** (rigid Micro-Pulse), holding bounce count at exactly the **5-bounce** target.
- Acoustic efficiency was **0.403** for soft Micro-Pulse against **0.059** for rigid, a **683%** gain, though motor power rose to **0.058 W** from **0.011 W**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Small feedforward motions timed within a stroke can resonate with a compliant body and produce large behavioral change without any real-time sensory feedback loop.
> - Softness raises parameter sensitivity: rigid hardware tolerates manual tuning, while exploiting elastic embodiment genuinely requires black-box optimization.
> - Efficiency ratios can mislead when uncontrolled: the highest efficiency came from an unconstrained excessive bounce count, so constrain the confound before comparing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This study belongs to the fields of **robotic musicianship, soft robotics, physical human–robot interaction, acoustic control, and data-driven robot learning**. Robotic performance has previously been demonstrated for instruments including violins, saxophones, pianos, and drums. However, percussion differs from many other instruments because sound is generated through brief physical impacts. The timing, velocity, position, and rebound of a drumstick directly affect both rhythm and acoustic output.

Drum rolls are especially demanding. Human drummers can produce rapid sequences of impacts by combining active strokes with passive rebounds of the drumstick. At high frequencies, this technique allows a performer to create many drum sounds from relatively few deliberate arm motions. For robots, reproducing this behavior through repeated active swings is difficult because of actuator speed, mechanical friction, backlash, sensing delays, and energy consumption.

Earlier robotic-drumming research established that **elasticity and passive impedance** can generate secondary or multiple bounces after an initial active strike. Studies using flexible joints, spring mechanisms, and dielectric elastomer actuators showed that mechanical compliance can increase drumming speed. However, passive rebounds generally lose energy over time, causing the acoustic amplitude to decay. Moreover, the stiffness and elasticity that determine bounce count and timing are difficult to tune analytically because the system includes nonlinear interactions among the arm, drumstick or end-effector, drum membrane, and actuator dynamics.

Previous work also used learning-based methods to tune stiffness or learn drumming patterns. Reinforcement learning demonstrated that robots could exploit passive rebounds, but the authors of the present paper identify limitations in sample efficiency and the difficulty of obtaining real-time acoustic feedback during high-frequency movements. The current study therefore takes a different approach: it uses **small feedforward motions**, rather than state-dependent feedback, to actively shape passive drumstick rebounds. These motions are tuned through **Bayesian optimization**, which is suited to expensive real-world experiments.

The paper’s contribution is consequently twofold. First, it proposes two active motion patterns designed specifically to exploit a soft robotic structure. Second, it demonstrates that optimization is particularly useful when the effects of small control changes are amplified by nonlinear soft-body dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. General Objective

The main objective is to develop a robotic drumming system capable of producing fast, continuous, and acoustically consistent drum rolls while using a limited number of active arm strokes. The researchers seek to exploit the elasticity of a soft end-effector rather than relying solely on repeated motor-driven impacts.

The paper identifies two desirable forms of drum-roll behavior:

1. **A maximum number of bounces with uniform time intervals.**
 This behavior is intended to extend the duration of a roll and create continuous sound from a single stroke.

2. **Stable sound amplitude and uniform time intervals for a fixed number of bounces.**
 This behavior addresses the natural decay in sound level that occurs as passive bounces lose kinetic energy.

These objectives involve a trade-off. Increasing the number of rebounds can improve continuity and efficiency, but each successive bounce tends to become quieter. Conversely, injecting energy to maintain amplitude may increase motor effort and disrupt temporal regularity.

#### 2.2. Proposed Active Tricks

The first technique, called **Tap-Pull (TP)**, adds a horizontal pulling motion after the initial downward tap. The purpose is to inject energy into the system and prolong the sequence of rebounds. The authors aim for TP to increase bounce count without producing irregular inter-bounce intervals.

The second technique, called **Micro-Pulse (MP)**, introduces small vertical oscillations during the downward stroke. These oscillations are intended to excite vibrations in the elastic arm and increase the impact velocity of later rebounds. MP is designed to compensate for amplitude decay while maintaining a fixed bounce count and regular timing.

The motivation for both techniques is that even small active motions may produce relatively large effects when coupled with a compliant mechanical structure. Because these effects are difficult to model precisely, the paper uses data-driven optimization to identify useful parameter values.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Robotic Hardware

The experimental platform is a dual-arm robot, with each arm containing three joints. The motors are Koala BEAR V2 actuators, and joint-angle commands are updated at **200 Hz**. The arms alternately move toward the drum so that one impact is produced during each period of the basic stroke cycle.

Two end-effector configurations are compared:

- A **soft arm**, 3D-printed from PLA with a thickness of 2 millimeters and fitted with a highly elastic rubber ball at its tip.
- A **rigid arm**, using vertical reinforcing ribs to prevent bending and suppress passive bouncing.

The soft arm is intended to store and release elastic energy after impact. The rigid arm provides a comparison condition in which the rubber tip remains present but the end-effector itself contributes substantially less flexibility.

#### 3.2. Basic Stroke

The basic movement is a vertical step-function trajectory. Each arm alternates between an upper position and a lower position, and the two arms move in opposite phases. The principal baseline parameters are the upper and lower positions, denoted \(A_U\) and \(A_L\), and the stroke period \(T\).

The vertical movement produces the initial tap, while the proposed tricks modify the trajectory during or immediately after the downward phase.

#### 3.3. Tap-Pull Control

TP uses four parameters:

- Upper amplitude, \(A_U\).
- Lower amplitude, \(A_L\).
- Delay before the pulling motion begins, \(t_{\text{down}}\).
- Horizontal pull distance, \(d x_{\text{pull}}\).

After the downward tap, the end-effector waits for a specified time and then moves horizontally toward the robot along a ramp trajectory. This retraction is intended to provide additional kinetic energy to the elastic system and sustain the rebound sequence.

The TP objective function combines two terms: the negative bounce count and a penalty for nonuniform inter-bounce intervals. The optimization therefore favors more detected impacts while discouraging irregular timing. Interval uniformity is measured using the **coefficient of variation**, defined as the standard deviation of the inter-impact intervals divided by their mean.

#### 3.4. Micro-Pulse Control

MP uses two parameters:

- Micro-pulse amplitude, \(A_\mu\).
- Number of micro-pulses, \(N_\mu\).

The downward phase is divided into multiple segments. The end-effector alternates between the lower position and a slightly higher micro-pulse position. The overall stroke period remains unchanged, but the small oscillations modify the motion and energy of the elastic arm.

The MP objective includes several terms:

- The decay ratio of impact amplitudes after the first impact.
- The coefficient of variation of bounce intervals.
- A penalty for deviating from a target bounce count.
- Motor power consumption.

For MP experiments, the upper and lower amplitudes were fixed at 0.04 meters and 0 meters, respectively, so that the initial kinetic conditions would be comparable across methods.

#### 3.5. Acoustic Measurement and Signal Processing

A Sony ECM-PCV80U condenser microphone recorded the drum sound at **44.1 kHz**, using blocks of 1,024 samples. After each episode, the researchers removed the DC component and applied a discrete Fourier transform. Frequencies between **1 and 100 Hz** were retained to reduce high-frequency noise.

The filtered signal was processed with a Hilbert transform to obtain an amplitude envelope. This envelope was smoothed using a 0.02-second moving-average window. Local maxima were detected as impact peaks using SciPy’s `find_peaks` function. The number of peaks represented the bounce count, while the intervals between peaks were used to calculate temporal uniformity.

For MP, the researchers also calculated acoustic power and motor power. Because some constants required for absolute acoustic-power estimation were difficult to identify accurately, they used a relative acoustic measure based on the mean squared microphone signal. Acoustic efficiency was then estimated as this relative acoustic power divided by motor power.

#### 3.6. Bayesian Optimization and Evaluation

The TP and MP parameters were optimized separately using Bayesian optimization with the **Tree-Structured Parzen Estimator**, implemented in Optuna. TP optimization used 200 iterations, while MP optimization used 100 iterations. Optimization was conducted independently for the rigid and soft configurations because the best parameters were expected to depend on the hardware.

After optimization, each condition was evaluated over **10 trials**. TP trials used four strokes involving both arms to determine whether the roll continued continuously. MP trials used a single stroke with one arm and targeted five bounces.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Tap-Pull Results

TP improved the number of bounces for both arm types. The mean bounce counts were:

- Rigid arm with Tap: **5.45 bounces per stroke**.
- Rigid arm with TP: **7.125 bounces per stroke**.
- Soft arm with Tap: **8.10 bounces per stroke**.
- Soft arm with TP: **12.25 bounces per stroke**.

The soft TP condition also produced the most uniform inter-bounce intervals, with a coefficient of variation of **0.290**, compared with **0.668** for the soft arm without TP. The rigid TP condition reached a coefficient of variation of **0.364**, while rigid Tap produced **0.876**.

These results indicate that the active pull was more effective when combined with a flexible end-effector. With the rigid arm, the rebound behavior was less natural and the acoustic waveform sometimes resembled dragging. With the soft arm, TP extended the rebound sequence and reduced periods of silence between impacts. The optimized soft TP therefore achieved both a higher bounce count and more regular timing.

#### 4.2. Micro-Pulse Results

MP was optimized to produce five bounces. For both rigid and soft arms, the optimized settings achieved the target count exactly, with **5.0 bounces per stroke** on average.

The optimized micro-pulse amplitudes were approximately:

- Rigid arm: \(A_\mu = 0.028\) meters, \(N_\mu = 5\).
- Soft arm: \(A_\mu = 0.032\) meters, \(N_\mu = 5\).

The amplitude decay ratio was reduced from **0.539 to 0.143** for the soft arm when MP was applied. For the rigid arm, the ratio decreased from **0.569 to 0.312**. Thus, MP substantially stabilized the acoustic amplitude in the soft configuration.

MP also improved interval uniformity. The soft arm’s coefficient of variation decreased from **0.225** without MP to **0.029** with MP. The rigid arm’s value decreased from **0.133** to **0.025**.

The soft MP condition required more motor power, increasing from approximately **0.011 W** to **0.058 W**. This increase is consistent with the technique’s purpose: energy lost through passive impacts must be replenished by active motion. Nevertheless, the relative acoustic-efficiency measure was **0.403** for soft MP and **0.059** for rigid MP, corresponding to an approximately **6.8-fold advantage** for the soft configuration under the MP comparison.

The soft arm without MP showed a higher efficiency value of **0.454**, but it also produced an excessive average of **9.2 bounces**, rather than the target of five. The authors therefore interpret soft MP as the better-organized condition when bounce count, amplitude stability, timing, and energy use are considered together.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The study shows that soft embodiment can be used not only to generate passive rebounds but also to amplify the effects of carefully timed active motions. TP demonstrates that a small post-impact pull can extend a roll, while MP shows that small oscillations can compensate for acoustic energy loss. These results support a control strategy in which robots deliberately combine passive mechanical dynamics with low-dimensional feedforward commands.

The use of Bayesian optimization is also important. Soft robotic systems often have nonlinear and difficult-to-model behavior, making manual tuning unreliable. The results indicate that parameter sensitivity was much greater for the soft arm than for the rigid arm. This suggests that optimization is particularly relevant when the objective is to exploit, rather than suppress, complex mechanical dynamics.

Potential applications include robotic musical performance, automated percussion, musical interfaces, and general-purpose robots that use compliant mechanisms for high-speed contact tasks. The approach may also be relevant to manipulation or locomotion problems in which small control inputs can activate useful elastic behavior.

The evidence remains limited to one drum configuration, a restricted tempo range, and a specific robot and end-effector design. The study does not include human perceptual ratings, so acoustic metrics such as bounce count and amplitude decay cannot yet be equated directly with perceived drum-roll quality. Future work could evaluate multiple drums, tempos, materials, and stroke patterns, while using multi-objective optimization to balance bounce count, temporal regularity, amplitude consistency, acoustic efficiency, and perceptual quality.
