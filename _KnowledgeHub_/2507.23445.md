# Quantifying and Visualizing Sim-to-Real Gaps: Physics-Guided Regularization for Reproducibility

> [!summary] Summary
> DENSO IT Laboratory researchers developed a physics-guided regularization method combined with parameter-conditioned domain randomization to improve sim-to-real transfer for robotic control, particularly on systems with large sim-to-real gaps. The approach demonstrated enhanced transfer fidelity and reduced anomalous oscillations on a high-gear-ratio balancing robot, achieving closer alignment between simulated and real-world angular velocity recovery compared to traditional domain randomization.

## Problem

- Standard sim-to-real transfer methods using domain randomization (DR) often fail for robots with wide sim-to-real gaps, such as those with high-gear-ratio mechanisms or requiring precise maneuvers.
- Naïve DR can lead to "overly conservative policies" and reduced success rates when randomization ranges are expanded to cover larger discrepancies, compromising performance at nominal parameters.
- Existing alternatives like system identification are data-intensive and costly, while classical controllers are linear and cannot achieve the nonlinear performance needed for complex robotic tasks, often lacking reproducibility.

## Method

- A ==physics-guided gain regularization loss== was formulated, constraining the neural network controller's ==input-output partial derivatives (equivalent gains)== to match ==empirically measured hardware proportional gains==.
- ==Parameter-conditioned domain randomization== was implemented by appending current plant parameters to the controller's input, allowing the network to specialize its output for different plant configurations.
- A single-layer **Elman Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)** was used as the controller, trained with a ==continuous curriculum learning== strategy to progressively increase simulation complexity and noise.

## Results

- The proposed **GC-DR-RNN** maintained equivalent gains below **2.0**, consistent with empirical hardware constraints, unlike the DR-RNN which showed unconstrained gains fluctuating up to **4.0**.
- On a physical balancing robot (**110:1** gearbox), the DR-RNN exhibited persistent **5 Hz** oscillations, while the GC-DR-RNN achieved smoother corrections and remained within tighter angular velocity bounds.
- The GC-DR-RNN demonstrated nearly identical decay envelopes and peak timings between simulated and real-world angular velocity recovery responses, confirming effective sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unconstrained neural network policies trained solely with domain randomization in simulation tend to develop unrealistically high equivalent gains, which manifest as unstable oscillations on physical hardware.
> - Naïve domain randomization can bias the training process towards 'easy' low-loss regions in the parameter space that may not correspond to the real hardware, leading to degraded performance at nominal plant parameters.
> - Integrating lightweight empirical physics guidance (measured proportional gains) into the learning process is effective for explicitly embedding real-world actuation characteristics and closing sim-to-real gaps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics extensively utilizes simulation-to-real (sim-to-real) transfer, particularly with reinforcement learning (RL) combined with domain randomization (DR), to develop controllers for robotic platforms. This approach has demonstrated success on systems equipped with low-gear-ratio, backdrivable actuators, where modest parameter perturbations in simulation can enable operation on physical hardware without requiring highly detailed models of friction, backlash, or sensor delays. Examples include the locomotion of small humanoids.

However, the paper identifies a limitation: this success does not consistently extend to scenarios characterized by wider sim-to-real gaps. Such scenarios include systems with high-gear-ratio mechanisms, tight object-insertion tasks, or requirements for aggressive maneuvers. When the range of randomization in simulation is increased to address these complex requirements, two primary issues emerge: the probability of a sampled model accurately matching the real plant decreases, and the learned policy tends to be marginalized over numerous inaccurate models, leading to "overly conservative policies." The authors characterize the current RL+DR methodology in these challenging contexts as more akin to "gambling" than engineering, highlighting a lack of reproducibility and certifiable performance necessary for industrial applications.

Alternative approaches in the landscape include real-to-sim system identification or iterative simulator refinement. While these methods can enhance fidelity, they demand extensive data collection, are constrained by safety considerations, and contribute to hardware wear. Furthermore, unmeasured or hazardous operating regimes often remain poorly modeled, potentially leading to simulator divergence during aggressive robot motions. Another option is to bypass physics modeling and directly tune classical controllers, such as Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controllers. However, PID control is inherently linear, limiting its applicability for tasks requiring nonlinear performance, such as wide-amplitude swing-up or contact-rich manipulation.

This work positions itself as a complementary approach that addresses the limitations of existing methods by integrating lightweight physics guidance into the learning process. It aims to bridge the gap between purely data-driven, black-box RL approaches and purely model-based control, specifically for challenging hardware conditions like high-gear-ratio systems where traditional sim-to-real methods struggle. The core idea is to reinterpret classical proportional gains as surrogates for complex, unmodeled plant dynamics and to explicitly incorporate these real-world characteristics into the neural network training loop. This contrasts with approaches that simply expand domain randomization ranges or rely on computationally intensive system identification, offering a path towards reproducible, high-performance control on more affordable robotic hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the limitations of existing sim-to-real transfer methods, particularly when facing a significant discrepancy between simulated and real-world robot dynamics. The work is motivated by several identified shortcomings in the broader research landscape:

1. **Breakdown of Sim-to-Real with Wide Gaps:** Standard domain randomization (DR) techniques, effective for systems with low-gear-ratio and backdrivable actuators, tend to fail when the "sim-to-real gap" is substantial. This occurs in high-gear-ratio mechanisms, precise assembly tasks, or dynamic, aggressive maneuvers.
2. **Inefficiency and Conservatism of Naïve Domain Randomization:** When the randomization range is expanded to cope with wider gaps, the probability of the simulated model matching the real plant decreases, leading to a lower "hit rate." Moreover, the policy learned under such broad randomization can become "overly conservative" as it averages over many potentially inaccurate models, compromising performance.
3. **Lack of Reproducibility and Engineering Principles:** The authors articulate that the prevalent RL+DR methodology often relies on chance for successful transfer, which is unsuitable for industrial applications requiring certified and reproducible performance. This highlights a need for more principled and predictable approaches.
4. **Limitations of Alternative Approaches:**
 - **System Identification:** While capable of improving simulator fidelity, this process is data-intensive, costly, time-consuming, limited by safety concerns, and contributes to hardware wear. It also struggles to model hazardous or unmeasured operating regimes accurately.
 - **Classical PID Control:** Although interpretable and suitable for direct gauging, PID controllers are inherently linear and cannot achieve the nonlinear performance required for complex robotic tasks.
5. **Need for Lightweight and Affordable Solutions:** There is a motivation to develop methods that enable robust sim-to-real transfer on readily available, low-cost robotic hardware, which often feature high-gear-ratio systems that complicate control.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of this work are articulated through specific contributions:

1. **Develop Physics-Guided Gain Regularization:** To formulate a training loss function that constrains the input-output partial derivatives (local sensitivities) of a neural controller to match empirically measured hardware proportional gains. This is intended to prevent the simulator from rewarding unrealistically large virtual gains and to keep the learned policy within the feasible actuation limits of the physical robot while retaining the neural network's nonlinear capabilities.
2. **Implement Parameter-Conditioned Domain Randomization:** To mitigate the issue of "overly conservative policies" resulting from na¨ıve domain randomization. By appending current plant parameters to the controller's input, the network can specialize its output for different plant configurations rather than converging to a single, suboptimal compromise.
3. **Provide Quantitative Analysis and Visualization of the Sim-to-Real Gap:** To offer a clear visualization of how unconstrained policies inflate equivalent gains and to quantitatively compare simulated and real-world responses, thereby exposing discrepancies in na¨ıve domain randomization.
4. **Validate on Challenging, Low-Cost Hardware:** To verify the proposed methodology on an off-the-shelf, two-wheeled balancing robot (Balboa 32U4) equipped with a high-gear-ratio (110:1) gearbox, a platform considered difficult for conventional RL+DR approaches.

In essence, the research aims to introduce a reproducible and effective framework for closing significant sim-to-real gaps by judiciously incorporating lightweight physics guidance and parameter awareness into deep learning-based control, thereby making high-performance control accessible on affordable robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper integrates physics-guided regularization and parameter-conditioned domain randomization into the training of a neural network controller for sim-to-real transfer.

#### 3.1. Robot Modeling

A two-wheeled balancing robot is selected as the experimental platform, modeled as a planar cart-pole system without wheel slip. This simplification is intended to capture key nonlinear control challenges while enabling analytical modeling. The nonlinear state-space model is defined by equations that describe the horizontal position (x), velocity (v), angle (θ), and angular velocity (ω) of the system, considering cart and pole masses (m, m_c), pole length (l), inertia (J), gravity (g), and viscous damping terms (D_c, D_p). Nominal parameters are derived from empirical tuning.

#### 3.2. Controller Design and Training

A single-layer Elman Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) is employed as the controller, chosen for its ability to handle nonlinear dynamics with minimal computational requirements. The RNN receives the plant state `x = [x, v, θ, ω]⊤` and its hidden state `h`, outputting a force `f`. The network's output is scaled by `c_out` to match the force range, and trainable weights (`A, B, C, b1, b2`) along with an activation function (`σ`) define its internal operations. A specific decomposition of matrix `A` is used to mitigate training loss divergence.

The training process is driven by a combination of control objectives and regularization:
- **State Tracking Loss (L_s):** This loss function minimizes the deviation of the robot's state (`s ∈ {x, v, θ, ω}`) from a target state (upright and stationary, `ˆs =⊤`). States are normalized by reference scales during calculation.
- **Continuous Curriculum Learning (CCL):** To stabilize and improve training, a CCL strategy is implemented. An initial "Pretrain" phase uses short simulation horizons and low noise levels. As training progresses to the "Finetune" phase, simulation length, noise levels, and plant parameter variations are gradually increased. Gaussian noise is added to the initial state variables and as a time-consistent external force, and state/force values are clamped within defined maximums. The system dynamics are discretized using a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method.

#### 3.3. Physics-Guided Regularization

This constitutes one of the core contributions of the paper.
- **Gain-Constrained Learning:** The concept draws inspiration from classical proportional control, where force `f_prop` is a linear combination of state variables scaled by empirically tunable gains (`k_x, k_v, k_θ, k_ω`). The paper reinterprets these proportional gains as surrogates for complex, unmodeled plant characteristics (e.g., bandwidth, friction, gear-train dynamics).
- **Experimental Gain Measurement:** Simple real-world experiments are conducted on the robot to measure its effective proportional gains. These measured hardware gains (`k_s`) serve as targets for regularization.
- **Loss Formulation:** Using automatic differentiation, the partial derivatives of the RNN's output force (`f`) with respect to its input states (`∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂v, ∂f/∂θ, ∂f/∂ω`) are computed. These derivatives represent the RNN's equivalent gains. A regularization loss (`L_grad`) is formulated to minimize the deviation of these RNN-equivalent gains from the experimentally observed hardware gains (`k_s`). The total training loss (`L_total`) combines the state tracking loss (`L_s`) and the gain regularization loss (`L_grad`). This constraint implicitly embeds real-world actuation characteristics, preventing the RNN from developing unrealistically high sensitivities in simulation.

#### 3.4. Parameter-Conditioned Domain Randomization

This is the second core contribution, designed to address the "overly conservative policies" problem of na¨ıve DR.
- **Domain Randomization:** Plant parameters (mass, length, damping) are randomized during the finetuning phase of training to improve generalization across hardware uncertainties.
- **Parameter Conditioning:** To prevent the controller from averaging over all randomized variations and becoming conservative, the current plant parameters (e.g., `M, m, l, D_c, D_p`) are explicitly appended to the input state of the RNN. This allows the neural network to specialize its output based on the specific plant configuration it is currently interacting with, rather than seeking a single, compromise solution.

#### 3.5. Deployment Setting

During deployment, sensor smoothing (exponential moving average) is applied to velocity estimates. A simplified DC motor model is used to convert the RNN's output force into a PWM duty ratio for motor control. Empirical constants (`c_emp`) are calibrated for different controllers to account for modeling errors. The control system operates with a nominal control period of 10 ms, although actual periods vary due to communication overhead.

#### 3.6. Hardware Setup

The experimental platform is a "Balboa 32U4 Balancing Robot Kit" with 50:1 gearboxes, assembled with a Raspberry Pi 3B+. The final gear ratio is 110.2:1. The architecture involves state estimation from the Balboa's IMU and encoder, computation on the Raspberry Pi (using NumPy), and feedback to the Balboa's Arduino via Timer3 interrupts.

#### 3.7. Pre-experiments

Prior to main experiments, empirical determination of nominal proportional gains and their working ranges (lower and upper bounds) was conducted through trial-and-error on the real hardware. These values were used in the gain regularization loss.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents findings from both simulated and real-world experiments, comparing a traditional proportional controller, a purely domain-randomized RNN (DR-RNN), and the proposed gain-constrained, parameter-conditioned DR-RNN (GC-DR-RNN).

#### 4.1. Simulated Results

- **Training Loss Convergence:** Both DR-RNN and GC-DR-RNN models achieved comparable normalized state tracking loss levels after 4000 epochs. However, the GC-DR-RNN exhibited faster convergence during the initial 400 epochs, suggesting a quicker acquisition of fundamental inverted pendulum control strategies.
- **Equivalent Gains:** Analysis of the RNN's equivalent gains (e.g., `∂f/∂ω`) showed a notable difference. The GC-DR-RNN maintained these gains below 2.0, aligning with the empirically derived hardware constraints. In contrast, the baseline DR-RNN's equivalent gains fluctuated between approximately 1.0 and 4.0, indicating the simulator rewarding unrealistically high sensitivities in the absence of explicit regularization.
- **Settling Time Contours (Mass Parameters):**
 - A standard proportional controller displayed fast convergence (dark blue regions) confined to a narrow band around nominal parameters in the cart mass (M) vs. pole mass (m) plane, indicating limited robustness to parameter variations.
 - The DR-RNN demonstrated a substantially wider stable region, illustrating the effectiveness of domain randomization in broadening robustness. However, the nominal operating point often resided near the edge of this feasible zone, suggesting potentially reduced local stability.
 - The GC-DR-RNN produced a broad dark-blue region surrounding the nominal point, indicating a balance between global robustness and high performance at the true hardware parameters.
- **Bias in Domain Randomization:** Visualizations of final minibatch sample locations overlaid on angular velocity loss contours revealed a bias in the DR-RNN. Training was pushed towards "easy" low-loss regions that were often distant from the true hardware parameters (e.g., optimal M around 2.0 kg while the maximum randomized value was 0.8 kg). This bias led to degraded performance near nominal plant parameters. The GC-DR-RNN suppressed this tendency, centering the loss minimum closer to the nominal hardware parameters.
- **Friction Parameter Conditioning:** Varying friction coefficients (D_c, D_p) in simulation showed that the GC-DR-RNN's performance landscape deformed as expected. Counter-intuitively, the in-domain robustness in the friction plane was sometimes best when the RNN was conditioned on "out-of-domain" plant context values for friction, leading to their selection for hardware experiments.

#### 4.2. Experimental Results (Hardware Validation)

- **Basic Balancing Capability:** All three controllers (proportional, DR-RNN, GC-DR-RNN) demonstrated the ability to balance the physical robot.
- **Anomalous Oscillations in DR-RNN:** A closer inspection of the real-world angular velocity traces revealed that the DR-RNN exhibited persistent oscillations (approximately 5 Hz) that were visibly apparent. The GC-DR-RNN and the proportional controller, however, maintained smoother corrections and remained within tighter angular velocity bounds. This observation aligns with the higher equivalent angular velocity gains observed in the DR-RNN's simulated results, which exceeded the empirically determined working range of proportional gains for the hardware.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap Visualization (Angular Velocity Recovery):**
 - **Proportional Controller:** The simulated and real-world angular velocity recovery responses showed close alignment in peak times and decay rates, indicating high transfer fidelity.
 - **DR-RNN:** A clear discrepancy was observed. While simulation predicted fast convergence, the real system displayed pronounced oscillations, highlighting a substantial sim-to-real mismatch.
 - **GC-DR-RNN:** This model achieved nearly identical decay envelopes and peak timings between simulation and real hardware, confirming effective sim-to-real transfer with physics-guided regularization. Its angular settling times in hardware closely matched those observed in simulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research offer several significant implications for the development of robust and reproducible robot control systems, particularly in the context of sim-to-real transfer.

**Reproducibility and Engineering Principles:** The work proposes a framework that moves beyond the "gambling" aspect of purely data-driven RL+DR, emphasizing a more structured, engineering-oriented approach. By embedding physically meaningful constraints into the learning process, it contributes to developing controllers with more predictable real-world performance. This is crucial for industrial applications where certifiable performance and reproducibility are paramount.

**Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer for Challenging Hardware:** The successful validation on a low-cost, off-the-shelf balancing robot with a high-gear-ratio (110:1) gearbox demonstrates the method's capability to address platforms that typically pose significant challenges for na¨ıve RL+DR. Such hardware is prone to wider sim-to-real gaps due to unmodeled dynamics like friction and backlash, making the proposed solution relevant for affordable robotics.

**Mitigation of Domain Randomization Drawbacks:** The research identifies and addresses two key problems associated with na¨ıve domain randomization: the tendency to generate "overly conservative policies" and the bias towards "easy" samples in the parameter space that may not correspond to the real hardware. Physics-guided gain regularization prevents the neural controller from developing unrealistic sensitivities, while parameter conditioning allows it to adapt specifically to different plant configurations, enhancing both robustness and performance around nominal parameters.

**Lightweight Physics Guidance:** The study suggests that incorporating lightweight physics guidance, in the form of empirical gain measurements, is a practical and effective strategy for closing sim-to-real gaps. This approach offers an alternative to simply enlarging randomization ranges, which can degrade performance and lead to conservative behaviors. It indicates that strategic, minimal physical insights can yield substantial improvements.

**Quantitative Analysis and Visualization:** The ability to quantitatively visualize the sim-to-real gap, by comparing simulated and real responses and analyzing equivalent gains, provides a diagnostic tool for understanding controller behavior and identifying discrepancies. This enhances the scientific understanding of sim-to-real challenges.

**Limitations:** The paper acknowledges several limitations:
1. **Mismatch in Conditioned Physical Parameters:** The optimal performance sometimes occurred when the controller was conditioned on "out-of-domain" plant parameter values, rather than the measured hardware parameters directly. This suggests a more complex interaction than simple one-to-one mapping and requires further investigation for the direct use of measured values as conditioning inputs.
2. **Performance Parity with Proportional Control:** Quantitatively, the RNNs' settling times generally matched those of a well-tuned proportional controller rather than significantly exceeding them. While qualitative advantages were observed in some runs, achieving reliably measurable advantages over classical control may necessitate further refinement of loss functions or training strategies.
3. **Remaining Seed Sensitivity:** Despite improved fragility compared to pure RL+DR, the GC-DR-RNN still exhibits some seed sensitivity inherent to deep networks. Although manageable with a few reruns to filter out instabilities, this indicates that the method is not entirely immune to the training complexities of neural networks.

In conclusion, this work contributes a method for enhancing the reproducibility and transferability of learned robot controllers by integrating empirical physical knowledge into the neural network training process. It offers a promising direction for developing reliable control solutions for a broader range of robotic hardware, particularly those with challenging dynamics, while acknowledging avenues for future refinement.
