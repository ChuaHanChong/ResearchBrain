# Evaluating Cognitive Maps and Planning in Large Language Models with CogEval

> [!summary] Summary
> Microsoft Research introduced CogEval, a cognitive science-inspired protocol, to systematically evaluate large language models' (LLMs) cognitive mapping and planning abilities. The study found that current LLMs, including GPT-4, display "apparent competence" in simpler tasks but consistently fail on complex planning problems requiring the inference of latent relational structures, exhibiting common issues like hallucinating invalid paths and generating suboptimal routes.

## Problem

- Claims about emergent cognitive abilities in large language models (LLMs) often lack rigorous, controlled evaluation and may be susceptible to data contamination.
- Existing AI evaluation methods can conflate linguistic fluency with deeper, functional cognitive capacities like planning or causal inference.
- There is a need for unbiased assessment of whether LLMs can genuinely grasp and utilize latent relational structures for planning, rather than simply retrieving memorized patterns.

## Method

- Introduced ==CogEval==, a ==cognitive science-inspired protocol== for systematic evaluation of LLM cognitive abilities, adhering to practices like latent construct operationalization and using non-linguistic human experiment paradigms.
- Systematically evaluated eight state-of-the-art LLMs across diverse ==graph structures==, domains, and planning task conditions (e.g., reward revaluation, shortcuts) to assess cognitive maps and planning.
- Investigated the effect of ==Chain of Thought instructions== on GPT-4's performance and conducted supplementary experiments on systematic graph explorations.

## Results

- LLM version, graph structure, task condition, and domain all significantly contributed to performance variations (all p-values **< 0.001**) in planning tasks.
- LLMs, including GPT-4, showed apparent competence in simpler tasks but significantly failed on tasks requiring genuine understanding of latent relational structures, particularly in community graphs.
- Chain of Thought instructions resulted in only marginal and inconsistent performance improvements, and common failure modes included hallucinating invalid edges, getting trapped in loops, and generating suboptimally long routes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs' planning capabilities are highly sensitive to graph structure, task condition, and domain, but the temperature parameter had no significant effect on overall performance in broad tasks.
> - Apparent competence in simpler planning tasks stems from "route memorization" of explicitly provided information rather than a genuine understanding of latent relational structures.
> - CogEval provides a robust, cognitive science-inspired framework for assessing LLMs' cognitive capacities, challenging claims of emergent intelligence by demonstrating systematic failure modes under controlled conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in large language models (LLMs) have led to a surge in claims about their emergent cognitive abilities, ranging from sophisticated reasoning to human-like understanding. These claims often rely on impressive but sometimes anecdotal demonstrations of LLM performance on various tasks, frequently overlooking potential data contamination (where test data inadvertently appears in training sets) or the lack of systematic, controlled evaluation. This has created a critical need for rigorous methodologies to assess what LLMs truly understand and what capabilities they genuinely possess, rather than merely mimic.

This paper directly addresses this gap by positioning itself as a counter-narrative to the hype surrounding emergent intelligence in LLMs. It argues for a more cautious and systematic approach to evaluating cognitive capacities. Historically, AI evaluation has sometimes suffered from methods that conflate linguistic fluency with deeper understanding or genuine cognitive functions. This research draws a clear distinction between "formal linguistic ability" (e.g., grammatically correct sentence formation) and "functional cognitive capacities" (e.g., planning, theory of mind, causal inference), aligning with a long-standing debate in cognitive science about the relationship between language and thought.

The paper is part of a growing body of work that seeks to apply cognitive science and psychological methods to probe the internal workings and capabilities of AI models. It builds upon foundational concepts from cognitive mapping, originating from Tolman's work on latent learning in rodents and later substantiated by neuroscientific discoveries like hippocampal place cells and entorhinal grid cells. By adapting experimental paradigms used in human and animal cognition research (which were originally non-linguistic), the authors aim to circumvent the data contamination issues that plague many text-based benchmarks. This approach aims to provide a more authentic assessment of whether LLMs can genuinely grasp and utilize latent relational structures for planning, a core aspect of intelligence, rather than simply retrieving memorized patterns from their vast training data. In essence, this work represents a crucial step towards developing a more scientifically rigorous framework for evaluating AI, moving beyond superficial performance metrics to probe underlying cognitive mechanisms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper sets out two primary objectives, driven by the current landscape of LLM research, characterized by enthusiastic claims of emergent cognitive abilities that often lack rigorous validation:

1. **To propose CogEval: A Cognitive Science-Inspired Protocol for Systematic Evaluation:** The first objective is to develop and introduce "CogEval," a structured, cognitive science-inspired protocol designed for the systematic and robust measurement and evaluation of various cognitive abilities in LLMs. The motivation here is to provide a standardized, rigorous framework that can be universally applied to assess complex cognitive capacities (such as planning, theory of mind, or causal inference) in a way that is systematic, involves control conditions, multiple iterations, and statistical robustness tests. This addresses the current reliance on anecdotal evidence or less controlled evaluations that may be susceptible to data contamination.

2. **To systematically evaluate cognitive maps and planning ability in LLMs using CogEval:** The second objective is to apply the newly proposed CogEval protocol to a specific, fundamental cognitive domain: cognitive maps and planning ability. The authors aim to empirically investigate whether current state-of-the-art LLMs (including GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, Google Bard, Cohere, Claude, LLaMA, and Alpaca) genuinely understand the latent relational structures necessary for planning, often referred to as cognitive maps. The motivation stems from the hypothesis that a deficit in understanding these underlying structures might explain observed failures in planning. By grounding their tasks in established human cognitive experiments that assess planning in non-linguistic contexts, they seek to ensure construct validity and minimize the risk of data contamination, thereby providing a clear, unbiased assessment of these "out-of-the-box" capabilities.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to move beyond superficial demonstrations of LLM intelligence towards a deeper, empirically grounded understanding of their true cognitive capacities, with significant implications for their safe and effective application.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-faceted methodology centered around their proposed **CogEval protocol**, which adheres to four best practices from cognitive science:

1. **Latent Construct Operationalization:** The study focuses on evaluating "cognitive maps" (representations of latent relational structures) and "adaptive planning" (requiring internal representation of task structures).
2. **Operationalization with Construct Validity:** Planning ability is operationalized through unique variations of established human cognitive experiments that measure comprehension and use of cognitive maps in multi-step planning. Crucially, these original human experiments were *non-linguistic*, mitigating data contamination risks when adapted to text prompts for LLMs.
3. **Multiple Tasks and Response Generations:** A diverse set of tasks were generated, varying across:
 - **Graph Structure:** Six distinct graph structures (e.g., simple line, tree, community graphs) inspired by human experiments were used.
 - **Domain:** Tasks were presented using items from different domains (spatial with ordered states like room numbers, spatial with unordered states, social ties, object relations, random letters) to test robustness to surface variations.
 - **Task Conditions:** Multiple planning tasks were designed for each latent graph structure, including value-based planning, reward revaluation, transition revaluation, and the introduction of shortcuts and detours. These conditions probe flexible adaptivity to local changes, analogous to model-based reinforcement learning.
 - **Multiple Generations and Temperatures:** For OpenAI models, each task prompt was run 30 times across three temperatures (0, 0.5, 1) to assess consistency and the impact of response diversity. Other LLMs were run once per task and temperature due to API access limitations.
4. **Control Conditions for Inference:** The variations across graphs, domains, and task conditions serve as control factors to robustly measure the latent construct and attribute performance variations.

**LLMs Evaluated:** The study evaluates eight LLMs: OpenAI's GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo-175B, text-Davinci-003-175B; Google Bard; Anthropic Claude-1-52B; Cohere-52.4B; LLaMA-13B; and Alpaca-7B.

**Experiments:**
- **Experiment 1: Systematic Cognitive Science-Inspired Evaluation:** This main experiment systematically compares the performance of all LLMs across the varied graph structures, domains, and task conditions. Statistical analysis (logistic regression with analysis of deviance) was performed to quantify the contribution of each factor (LLM, graph, domain, condition, temperature, and their interactions) to performance variance.
- **Experiment 2: Effect of Chain of Thought (CoT) Instructions:** This experiment explored whether simple instructions about graph traversal algorithms (Breadth-First Search/BFS and Depth-First Search/DFS) could mitigate observed failure modes in GPT-4. These instructions were appended to existing prompts, and performance was evaluated across different temperatures.
- **Supplementary Experiment (in Supplementary Materials): Systematic Graph Explorations:** This additional experiment specifically investigates GPT-4's performance on community graph structures with varying edge densities, asking the LLM to assign partitions to maximize modularity. This provides a fine-grained analysis of how graph structure impacts performance, measured by Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) against a Leiden modularity maximization process.

**Failure Mode Analysis:** The methodology also includes qualitative analysis of specific failure modes observed in LLM responses, such as hallucinating non-existent edges, getting trapped in loops, or finding sub-optimal long trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The systematic evaluation conducted using the CogEval protocol yielded several key findings regarding LLMs' cognitive mapping and planning abilities:

**Experiment 1: Out-of-the-Box Planning Performance**
- **Significant Factors:** Statistical analysis (logistic regression and analysis of deviance) revealed that LLM version, graph structure, task condition, and domain *all* significantly contributed to variations in performance (all p-values < 0.001). This indicates that LLMs' planning capabilities are highly sensitive to the problem's structure and presentation.
- **Non-Significant Temperature:** Surprisingly, across the broad range of tasks in Experiment 1, the temperature parameter (0, 0.5, 1) and its interaction with the LLM version did *not* show a statistically significant effect on overall performance. This suggests that for these complex planning tasks, increasing the "creativity" or diversity of outputs does not inherently lead to better planning solutions.
- **Apparent Competence vs. Underlying Deficits:** LLMs, including GPT-4, showed "apparent competence" in simpler tasks, particularly those involving smaller, sparser graphs (like line graphs or simple trees) where the entire trajectories were explicitly laid out in the prompt. In these cases, the models seemed to succeed by "route memorization" or piecing together readily available information.
- **Failure on Complex Structures:** However, performance significantly deteriorated on tasks requiring genuine understanding of *latent relational structures*, especially in larger "community graphs." When multi-step traversal and planning necessitated inferring connections beyond explicit memorization, LLMs consistently failed.

**Experiment 2: Effect of Chain of Thought (CoT) Instructions**
- **Marginal Improvements:** Providing GPT-4 with high-level instructions on graph traversal algorithms (BFS and DFS) resulted in only *marginal* improvements in performance, primarily on community graphs.
- **Inconsistent Effects:** The benefits of CoT were not consistent across all tasks or temperatures. For instance, in a "cluster counting" task, BFS/DFS helped at lower temperatures (0, 0.5) but less so at temperature 1. For "shortest paths within a cluster," BFS/DFS helped across temperatures, with BFS being most effective at temperature 0. However, for "shortest path 1-cluster away," only BFS at temperature 0.5 yielded slight improvements. This indicates that CoT prompting is not a universal panacea for these planning deficits.

**Supplementary Experiment: Systematic Graph Explorations**
- **Sparsity vs. Density:** GPT-4 performed *worse* on sparser community structures and *better* as edge density increased within the community blocks. This further supports the idea that LLMs struggle with inferring connections in less explicit or more complex graph structures, even when the task changes from local traversal to optimization (like modularity maximization).
- **Temperature Impact:** In this specific experiment, higher temperatures generally resulted in poorer performance with higher variance, contrasting with the non-significant effect observed in Experiment 1's broader task set.

**Common Failure Modes:** The study identified three consistent failure modes when tasks involved underlying graphs with dense community structures, suggesting a fundamental lack of structural understanding:
1. **Hallucination of Invalid Trajectories/Edges:** LLMs frequently generated connections or paths that did not exist in the defined environment.
2. **Getting Trapped in Loops:** Instead of finding optimal or shortest paths, LLMs often proposed trajectories that repeated states, indicating a failure to maintain a coherent state representation or explore efficiently.
3. **Suboptimally Long Routes:** Even when they found a correct path, it was often not the shortest or most efficient one, suggesting a reliance on superficial matching rather than true path optimization. This included instances where LLMs failed to identify 1-step paths, suggesting multi-hop trajectories instead.

In summary, the results strongly suggest that current LLMs, including GPT-4, do not possess out-of-the-box emergent cognitive map comprehension or planning competence. Their apparent success in simpler tasks is more likely attributable to memorization of explicitly provided routes rather than a generalized ability to extract and utilize latent relational structures for adaptive planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for both the fundamental understanding of Large Language Models and their practical applications:

**Challenging "Emergent Intelligence" Claims:** The most significant impact of this study is its rigorous refutation of widespread claims about LLMs possessing emergent human-level cognitive abilities, particularly in planning and cognitive mapping. By demonstrating systematic failure modes under controlled conditions, the paper offers a crucial reality check against the growing hype, urging the AI community to adopt more cautious interpretations of LLM capabilities. This contributes to a more grounded and scientifically responsible discourse around AI.

**Methodological Advancement (CogEval):** The introduction of CogEval is a major methodological contribution. It provides a blueprint for future systematic and robust evaluations of various cognitive capacities in LLMs. By emphasizing non-contaminated task designs (based on non-linguistic human experiments), multiple measurements, control conditions, and statistical analysis, CogEval sets a higher standard for assessing LLM intelligence. This protocol can guide researchers in designing more rigorous experiments, moving beyond anecdotal successes.

**Implications for LLM Applications:** The observed failure modes (hallucinations, loops, suboptimal paths) in planning tasks, especially those requiring inference from latent structures, highlight critical limitations for real-world applications. Industries looking to deploy LLMs in domains requiring reliable, multi-step planning (e.g., logistics, robotics, gaming, complex decision-making systems) must exercise extreme caution. Relying on "out-of-the-box" LLMs for such tasks could lead to inefficient, incorrect, or even unsafe outcomes. This paper suggests that augmentation with external planning algorithms or specialized architectures might be necessary for robust performance in these areas.

**LLMs as "Programmable Machines":** The findings reinforce the view of LLMs as sophisticated "programmable machines" rather than inherently intelligent agents with emergent cognitive capacities. Their strong performance when explicit routes are provided, contrasted with failures when deeper structural inference is required, aligns with the idea that natural language acts as a programming language to execute instructions, rather than indicating genuine understanding of the underlying world model. This perspective influences how researchers and engineers should approach LLM design and deployment.

**Future Directions in AI Research:**
- **Augmentation Strategies:** The paper implicitly advocates for augmenting LLMs with modules inspired by cognitive functions, such as memory (like a hippocampus) and executive control/planning (like a prefrontal cortex). This aligns with hybrid AI approaches that combine the strengths of LLMs (language understanding, vast knowledge recall) with algorithmic components for robust reasoning and planning.
- **Representational Analysis:** Future work could analyze the internal representations (embeddings) of LLMs to understand *why* they fail, mirroring neuroscientific approaches to understanding cognitive maps in the brain.
- **Efficiency and Architecture:** The "programmable machine" view suggests that simply scaling models might be a computationally expensive shortcut. More "brain-inspired" architectures with inherent efficiency constraints could lead to more adaptive and capable AI, potentially reducing the need for massive models and their associated environmental costs.
- **Transitive Inference and Schemas:** Investigating LLMs' ability for transitive inference from pairwise associations and their capacity to utilize "schemas" (generalized cognitive maps) are proposed as promising research avenues.

In conclusion, this research significantly shifts the narrative around LLM intelligence, emphasizing the need for scientific rigor in evaluation. It provides a robust framework (CogEval) for future studies and offers practical warnings and directions for augmenting LLMs to achieve true cognitive capabilities in real-world applications.
