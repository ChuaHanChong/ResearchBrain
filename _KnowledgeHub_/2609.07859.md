# M3-Tele: A Unified Multimodal Teleoperational Framework for Compliant Whole-Body Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> M3-Tele is a teleoperation and data-collection system for compliant whole-body mobile manipulation. Retargeted iPhone or Xbox end-effector commands pass through directional force admittance control, closed-loop GelSight tactile grasp regulation, and QP-based whole-body inverse kinematics coordinating base and arm. It records aligned vision, tactile, force, and proprioception. Force-tracking error drops from 4.132 N to 0.346 N and contact loss from 2.46 to 0.02 events per trial.

## Problem

- Existing teleoperation and data-collection frameworks treat multimodal perception and coordinated whole-body operation separately, so demonstrations become inconsistent and downstream policy learning suffers.
- Whole-body inverse kinematics methods optimize geometric tracking only; they do not adapt the commanded motion to measured interaction forces during contact-rich tasks.
- Visuo-tactile imitation-learning systems are evaluated on fixed-base tabletop arms, and hybrid force-position loco-manipulation work lacks dense optical tactile feedback at the gripper.

## Method

- **M3** unified high-level controller: the measured force projected onto the end-effector z-axis drives an ==admittance-plus-PI correction== along the contact normal only, leaving tangential position and orientation stiffness intact.
- ==Closed-loop tactile gripper control==: GelSight Mini marker-displacement magnitude acts as an uncalibrated deformation proxy, and a PI law scaled by a mixing coefficient corrects gripper position toward desired deformation.
- ==QP-based whole-body inverse kinematics== following HoMeR: velocity-level costs for end-effector tracking, arm posture, base damping, under joint, velocity and ==non-holonomic differential-drive constraints==; interfaces include feedback-enabled iPhone and Xbox.

## Results

- Removing admittance control raises wiping force-tracking error from **0.35 N** to **4.12 N** and normal-force variance from **0.56** to **19.1** N squared.
- Dropping tactile feedback increases deformation RMSE from **2.23e-3** to **6.37e-3**; decoupled base-arm control raises force error to **0.762 N** and completion time from **58.73 s** to **72.37 s**.
- Across **300** user-study trials the feedback-enabled iPhone scores **8.4/9** ease of use versus **5.8** plain; cross-task completion spans **80-94%**; vision-tactile-force Diffusion Policy gives highest coverage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compliance works best when directional: relax stiffness only along the estimated contact normal and keep tangential tracking rigid, so force adaptation costs no positional accuracy.
> - Tactile and force sensing are complementary rather than redundant; either modality alone underperforms both, and the gain is largest under limited demonstration data.
> - Uncalibrated tactile deformation serves as a usable closed-loop control signal without force-unit calibration, and additionally protects the sensor surface from excessive grasp deformation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of mobile manipulation, which integrates the extended workspace of a mobile base with the interaction capabilities of a robotic manipulator. Recent advancements in this domain, as exemplified by systems such as HomeRobot, Mobile ALOHA, and TidyBot++, demonstrate increasing capabilities for robots in realistic environments. A significant trend in this area involves utilizing mobile manipulators not only as task-execution platforms but also as data collection systems for learning visuomotor policies.

Imitation learning (IL) is a prominent approach for acquiring complex manipulation skills. Its effectiveness relies heavily on the quality, coverage, and consistency of collected demonstrations. Inconsistent operator actions or insufficient state coverage can lead to issues such as distribution shift and compounding errors during autonomous policy deployment. Teleoperation, a robot-in-the-loop data collection method, remains relevant for acquiring precise and directly executable mobile manipulation data, contrasting with robot-out-of-the-loop methods that require transfer across different embodiments.

Various teleoperation interfaces exist, including smartphone-based, gamepad-based, gesture-based, and homologous leader-follower devices, each presenting trade-offs in intuitiveness, cost, motion correspondence, and operator workload. However, comparative evaluations across these interfaces on different robots and tasks make it challenging to isolate their effects on specific mobile manipulation task classes.

A key aspect of mobile manipulation is the coordination between the mobile base and the robotic arm. Whole-body control approaches address this by treating the base and manipulator as a unified kinematic system, distributing end-effector commands across their combined degrees of freedom while respecting constraints. Nevertheless, kinematic coordination alone is insufficient for tasks involving physical contact. Compliant motion generation, where interaction forces can modify commanded trajectories, is necessary for contact-rich tasks. While hybrid force-position and impedance-aware loco-manipulation have been explored, these often lack dense optical tactile feedback at the gripper or integration into a complete teleoperation-to-learning workflow.

Vision alone provides indirect information about contact, and local contact phenomena such as object deformation, pressure, and incipient slip can be visually ambiguous or occluded. Tactile sensing has been recognized as a crucial modality for physical interaction. Prior work has shown that combining vision and touch can enhance state estimation and contact-rich manipulation. More recent systems, including 3D-ViTac, TactileAloha, and Reactive Diffusion Policy, demonstrate that tactile observations can improve imitation learning performance in fine-grained and contact-rich tasks, primarily in the context of fixed-base tabletop manipulators. TACT extended tactile imitation learning to humanoid broad-area contact, but did not focus on fingertip manipulation.

This work aims to bridge the identified gaps by integrating multimodal learning (specifically vision, tactile, and force sensing) with compliant whole-body mobile manipulation within a unified teleoperational framework. This unification is presented as a novel approach in the current research landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop M3-Tele, a unified multimodal teleoperational framework designed for compliant whole-body mobile manipulation. This framework aims to facilitate stable physical interaction and capture aligned multimodal observations (visual, tactile, force, and proprioceptive) during task execution.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in existing teleoperation and data-collection frameworks:
- **Inefficient Data Collection:** Current frameworks frequently overlook the joint consideration of multimodal perception and coordinated whole-body operation, which can reduce the efficiency and quality of demonstration collection.
- **Impact on Downstream Learning:** Suboptimal demonstration quality directly affects the effectiveness of downstream policy learning, a critical component for acquiring complex robot skills.
- **Requirements for Contact-Rich Tasks:** Efficient execution of tasks involving physical contact necessitates both seamless whole-body coordination and precise regulation of physical compliance.
- **Limitations of Unimodal Sensing:** Vision-only systems provide indirect information about physical contact, missing critical local details such as object deformation, pressure, and incipient slip that tactile sensing can provide.
- **Lack of Integration:** Previous research in multimodal learning and compliant whole-body mobile manipulation has largely progressed as separate directions, without a unified system that integrates these capabilities for data collection and policy learning on mobile platforms.

In response to these issues, M3-Tele is designed to:
- Enable human operators to intuitively control a mobile manipulator for complex, contact-rich tasks.
- Provide real-time multimodal feedback to the operator, enhancing awareness and control quality during teleoperation.
- Collect high-quality, temporally aligned datasets comprising multimodal observations and coordinated whole-body actions.
- Demonstrate that these collected multimodal datasets can effectively improve the performance of downstream imitation learning policies, particularly for contact-rich mobile manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system, M3-Tele, integrates an interaction-aware control framework (M3) with a multimodal teleoperation interface for data collection. The methodology can be broken down into problem formulation, controller design, hardware setup, and evaluation procedures.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system considers a mobile manipulator with a planar mobile base, an n-degree-of-freedom arm, and a gripper. At each timestep *t*, it records a multimodal observation `o_t = {I_v_t, I_t_t, w_t, s_p_t}`. This includes visual images (`I_v_t`), tactile images (`I_t_t`), a 6-dimensional interaction wrench (`w_t`) comprising force and torque, and proprioceptive observations (`s_p_t`) of the base, arm, and gripper states (positions and velocities). The corresponding task-space action `a_t` consists of the target end-effector position (`p*_t,ee`), orientation (`q*_t,ee`), and a scalar gripper command (`q*_t,g`). The objective is to collect temporally aligned multimodal observations and whole-body actions as demonstrations for downstream policy learning.

#### 3.2. Teleoperation Controller Formulation (M3)

The core of the system is the M3 control framework, which includes leader-to-follower retargeting, a unified high-level controller, and a QP-based whole-body inverse kinematics component.

- **Leader-to-Follower Retargeting:** This method maps the motion of a leader device (e.g., a handheld iPhone) to the robot's end-effector target. It calculates leader motion relative to its initial pose, then transforms and scales this motion to apply it to the initial follower-side end-effector pose. This non-recursive approach is used to prevent accumulated retargeting drift.

- **Unified High-level Controller:** This controller combines force-based admittance control and tactile-adaptive gripper control.
 - **Compliant Admittance Control:** After contact detection, the measured 6-dimensional interaction force is transformed into the world frame. The force relevant for compliance is projected onto the end-effector's z-axis, defined as the contact-normal direction. An admittance controller then modifies only the position reference along this axis, allowing tangential position components and end-effector orientation to maintain their original tracking stiffness. This helps maintain a desired contact force.
 - **Tactile Adaptive Gripper Control:** Optical tactile deformation, specifically its magnitude (`S_t`), is used for closed-loop grasp regulation. An error `e_t` is defined, combining the normal-force error (`F_t,d - F_t,n`) and the tactile-deformation error (`S_t,d - S_t`). A proportional-integral (PI) controller converts this error into corrections for both end-effector displacement along the contact direction (`δp*_t,ee`) and gripper position (`δq*_t,g`), dynamically adjusting the gripper based on tactile feedback.

- **QP-based Whole-Body Inverse Kinematics:** This component converts the compliant end-effector target (from the high-level controller) into coordinated base and arm velocities. It formulates a velocity-level constrained inverse-kinematics problem with the following objectives and constraints:
 - **Objectives:** Minimize end-effector pose error, regularize arm posture towards a reference configuration, and suppress unnecessary whole-body motion. These are formulated as quadratic functions of the generalized velocity.
 - **Constraints:** Enforce maximum velocity limits for the generalized velocity, ensure arm joint positions remain within limits, and apply a non-holonomic constraint for the differential-drive mobile base (preventing lateral motion).
 The optimized generalized velocity is then decomposed into base velocity commands and arm joint position references.

#### 3.3. Hardware and Software Setup

- **Physical Platform:** Comprises a differential-drive mobile base, a 7-DoF Kinova Gen3 robotic arm, and a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper.
- **Sensing Modalities:** Two Action 5 RGB cameras (external and wrist-mounted) for visual input. A Robotiq FT300 force-torque sensor measures global interaction wrench. Two GelSight Mini optical tactile sensors provide local contact observations at the gripper pads.
- **Sensor Frequencies:** Force-torque sensor (100 Hz), RGB cameras (30 Hz), tactile sensor (25 Hz). High-level teleoperation and policy commands are updated at 10 Hz. Low-level base and arm controllers operate at 100 Hz and 1 kHz, respectively, using asynchronous operation.
- **Tactile Representation:** Raw GelSight Mini streams are processed into three tactile representations: FEATS-based force-distribution estimates, NormalFlow-based contact-motion estimates, and PCA-compressed marker-deformation features for policy learning.
- **Visual Representation:** ResNet-18 backbone encodes RGB observations into compact visual features.
- **Proprioceptive Data:** Includes arm end-effector pose, mobile-base velocity, and gripper state.
- **Teleoperation Interface:** An operator-facing mobile interface (FE-iPhone) provides episode initialization, communication round-trip-time monitoring, selection of predefined compliance profiles (soft, medium, hard), and configurable visualization of wrist-camera and tactile streams, along with real-time warning feedback.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design and Baselines

The evaluation addresses six research questions across three aspects: controller performance, teleoperation system effectiveness, and demonstration data utility.
- **Baselines:** Six configurations were compared, including M3-Tele and five ablations:
 - **W/O AC:** Without admittance control.
 - **W/O TC:** Without tactile feedback control.
 - **W/O WBC:** Without whole-body coordination (independent base-arm control).
 - **W/O FE:** Without feedback-enabled interface (standard iPhone).
 - **Xbox-Control:** Uses an Xbox gamepad for direct velocity inputs.
- **Tasks:** Four tasks of varying complexity: Pick-and-Place, Bar Rotation, Drawer Opening, and Wiping. The wiping task, which is contact-rich and long-horizon, served as the primary benchmark for controller performance and policy learning.
- **Metrics:** Evaluated using Contact Loss Count, Force-Tracking Error, Normal-Force Variance, Tactile-Deformation RMSE, Tactile-Deformation Variance, Completion Time, Ease of Use (user-rated), Completion Rate, Grasp Failure Count, and Coverage Ratio (for wiping).
- **User Studies:** Five participants were recruited for interleaved experiments across various configurations and tasks to mitigate learning effects and environmental variations.
- **Policy Learning:** Diffusion Policy was used to train and evaluate policies on collected wiping demonstrations with varying observation configurations (vision-only, vision+tactile, vision+force, vision+tactile+force) and dataset sizes (25%, 50%, 75%, 100%).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded findings regarding controller performance, teleoperation system effectiveness, and the utility of collected multimodal data for policy learning.

#### 4.1. Controller Performance Comparison (Q1-Q3)

- **Q1: Force-based admittance control:** M3’s admittance control demonstrated improved contact behavior during board wiping. It reduced the force tracking error from 4.132 N (without AC) to 0.346 N. Contact loss events were significantly reduced from 2.46 to 0.02 events per trial, and normal-force variance decreased from 19.1 N² to 0.56 N². This indicates stable interaction with rapid convergence to the desired force.
- **Q2: Tactile-based gripper control:** The integration of tactile feedback into gripper control improved contact stability. Without tactile control, the tactile deformation RMSE increased from 2.230 x 10⁻³ to 6.369 x 10⁻³, and tactile deformation variance increased from 2.608 x 10⁻⁶ to 1.933 x 10⁻⁵. This also led to increased force tracking error (from 0.346 N to 0.906 N) and force variance (from 0.559 N² to 2.722 N²). The results indicate that tactile feedback allows the gripper to actively regulate contact deformation and maintain stable interaction.
- **Q3: Whole-body coordination:** Whole-body coordination enhanced execution efficiency and interaction quality. Decoupled base-arm control resulted in a higher force tracking error (0.762 N vs. 0.346 N) and force variance (2.026 N² vs. 0.559 N²). Tactile deformation error also increased (3.718 x 10⁻³ vs. 2.230 x 10⁻³). The completion time was longer for decoupled control (72.37 s vs. 58.73 s), attributed to increased operator workload.

#### 4.2. Teleoperation System Evaluation (Q4-Q5)

- **Q4: Feedback-enabled interface:** The FE-iPhone interface achieved a higher user-rated ease of use score (8.4 ± 0.9) compared to a standard iPhone interface (5.8 ± 0.8), while maintaining a high completion rate. The Xbox-based interface, while achieving slightly shorter completion times (e.g., 35.4 ± 3.9 s for bar rotation), lacked the physical interaction feedback provided by the FE-iPhone. This indicates that multimodal feedback improves intuitiveness and operability, especially for tasks requiring precise contact.
- **Q5: Cross-task reliability and usability:** M3-Tele demonstrated reliable teleoperation across four diverse tasks. Completion rates were 94.0% for pick-and-place, 92.0% for bar rotation, 86.0% for drawer opening, and 80.0% for wiping. Completion times increased with task complexity, from 34.87 ± 3.60 s for pick-and-place to 59.63 ± 6.13 s for wiping. Users consistently reported favorable usability scores (above 7.5/10 for most tasks).

#### 4.3. Policy Learning from Multimodal Demonstrations (Q6)

- **Q6: Utility of multimodal demonstrations:** Policies trained with the complete vision–tactile–force observations achieved the highest coverage ratio across all tested training-set sizes for the board-wiping task. This configuration also generally provided the best or near-best performance in grasp success, normal-force stability, and contact continuity. The performance advantage was more pronounced with limited data (25% or 50% of demonstrations), suggesting that tactile and force measurements provide complementary interaction cues. While vision with tactile sensing occasionally outperformed the complete multimodal input in specific metrics or data sizes, these differences did not translate into better overall task execution (as indicated by lower coverage ratios). Vision-only policies consistently produced lower coverage ratios. The results indicate that the joint integration of tactile and force information is more effective for learning complete contact-rich manipulation behaviors than incorporating either modality alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The M3-Tele framework represents a notable contribution to the field of mobile manipulation and robot learning by addressing the integrated challenge of multimodal perception, compliant control, and whole-body coordination.

**Significance:**
- **Unified Framework:** The work provides a unified framework (M3-Tele) that bridges the previously separate research directions of multimodal learning and compliant whole-body mobile manipulation. This integration is crucial for enabling robots to perform complex tasks that require both broad spatial navigation and fine-grained physical interaction.
- **Enhanced Contact Interaction:** By explicitly incorporating interaction-aware admittance control and tactile-adaptive gripper regulation, M3-Tele enables more stable and reliable physical interactions. The quantitative reductions in force tracking error, contact loss, and tactile deformation error demonstrate the effectiveness of this compliant control strategy.
- **High-Quality Data Collection:** M3-Tele provides a validated system for collecting high-quality, temporally aligned multimodal demonstration data (visual, tactile, force, proprioceptive). This is critical for improving the robustness and effectiveness of data-driven policy learning approaches.
- **Improved Whole-Body Coordination:** The research highlights the benefits of whole-body inverse kinematics for coordinating mobile base and manipulator movements, leading to increased task efficiency and interaction stability by reducing operator workload and ensuring motion consistency.
- **Validation of Multimodal Learning:** The experiments with Diffusion Policy empirically demonstrate the downstream utility of the collected multimodal data, showing that integrating tactile and force observations significantly improves policy learning for contact-rich tasks, especially in data-limited scenarios.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancements in Autonomous Robotics:** The framework could contribute to developing more capable and general-purpose mobile manipulators for a wider range of contact-rich tasks in unstructured environments, such as household assistance, logistics, and service robotics.
- **Robust Robot Learning:** By providing a method for collecting rich, high-fidelity multimodal demonstrations, M3-Tele can facilitate the development of more robust and data-efficient imitation learning policies. This could accelerate the deployment of learned skills in real-world applications.
- **New Research Avenues:** The work suggests future research directions, such as extending compliant control to dexterous robotic hands and exploring high-dimensional hand control with richer tactile sensing, which could lead to even more sophisticated manipulation capabilities.
- **Reproducible and Accessible Research:** The stated aim for aligned simulation and real-world interfaces supports reproducible evaluation, and the context of emerging open-source projects suggests potential for broader accessibility and adoption within the robotics research community.
