# Are In-Context Images Worth 10 Dimensions?

> [!summary] Summary
> A mechanistic study of how vision-language models classify images from in-context examples. A meta-learned linear probe finds a Shared Discriminative Geometry: a roughly 10-dimensional subspace, shared across tasks, where in-context images become linearly separable. Early layers compress vision tokens through image-to-image attention, then copy the result into the label-predicting text token. Ablating 15 dimensions cuts accuracy by 12.9 points, and theory shows linear attention can perform PCA by gradient descent.

## Problem

- Interpretability work on in-context learning has focused on induction circuits that compare linear representations, but rarely explains how those class-separable representations are formed in the first place.
- In-context classification studies have mostly used text, whose discrete inputs and narrow task variety make it hard to trace feature formation across many diverse classification tasks.
- The raw hidden state of the label-predicting token classifies close to chance by nearest centroid, so it is unclear where and how its discriminative visual information is stored.

## Method

- Meta-learned **SDG** projections: per-layer linear maps, trained on a frozen model with a ==centroid-alignment loss== over 32k few-shot episodes, that expose class-discriminative subspaces in text and vision tokens.
- Two-step circuit analysis: ==participation-ratio== dimensionality tracking, plus ablation and boosting of SDG directions and of vision-to-text attention, separating early vision-token compression from transfer at peak layer.
- Theoretical construction: non-causal ==linear self-attention== with chosen KQ and OV weights takes one gradient step per layer toward matching the in-context Gram matrix, projecting examples onto their principal components.

## Results

- The probed label-predicting token peaks at layer 16 with about 10 effective dimensions, beating model accuracy; the top 10 directions hold **91%** of variance versus **45%** for input vision features.
- Ablating the top 15 SDG dimensions cuts 3-shot accuracy by **12.9 points** and boosting them adds 3.9, while ablating a random 128-dimensional subspace costs only 1.9.
- Ablating image-to-image attention in the **2%** of heads matching the theoretical OV circuit removes the low-dimensional accuracy gain; on Open MI, boosting the SDG adds **11.5 points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Few-shot visual representations form before labels matter: shuffling support labels leaves early geometry formation intact, so compression is unsupervised and label-driven induction only comes later.
> - Attention between in-context samples can work as dimensionality reduction, keeping only the pairwise similarities a downstream comparison needs, one explanation for why contextualization helps.
> - One small shared subspace can be a test-time control knob: boosting it helps similarity-based classification, but it hurts matching tasks where support similarity misleads the model.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In-context learning asks a model to classify a query image using a handful of labeled examples in its prompt, rather than updating the model's weights for each new task. One might expect the text position about to produce the answer to already hold a useful summary of the images. Its raw representation is a poor classifier, even though image-token representations are much more informative.

The authors find that a compact, shared geometry for comparing images emerges inside a large vision-language model and makes class information visible. The learned projection is 128 dimensions wide, but its *effective* dimensionality is about 10. That number refers to representation geometry. It does not refer to fewer generated tokens, faster elapsed time, or less computation, none of which the paper measures.

Right: 3-shot accuracy by layer. Projected forerunner-token NCC rises from near chance toward the model-accuracy region around layer 16. Vision-token NCC in token space is already much higher across much of the layer range. These are probe results; the model's output is a separate quantity.

### 2. Finding the compact geometry

The main experiment uses Ministral 3 (3B) on 3-way, 3-shot image-classification tasks drawn from 10 image-classification datasets. Each prompt contains nine labeled support images and one unlabeled query. The labels are abstract: "Rud," "Baz," and "Gip." This discourages the model from solving the task from label semantics alone.

Representation quality is measured with a Nearest Centroid Classifier (NCC) probe. The probe averages the support representations within each class, then assigns the query to the class whose centroid has the largest dot product with it. This probe accuracy is distinct from the LVLM's own output accuracy.

The SDG is a learned projection, shared across tasks, that makes this kind of comparison class-discriminative. Its effective dimension estimates how many directions substantially contribute to those similarities. At layer 16, the Text SDG has about 10 effective dimensions. NCC in that learned geometry reaches about 90% accuracy across the 10 image domains.

An intervention tests whether the model uses these directions. Removing the top 15 SDG dimensions from all hidden states at layer 16 decreases the model's 3-shot classification accuracy by 12.9 percentage points. Removing a 128-dimensional random control subspace decreases it by 1.9 points. Boosting the top 15 dimensions increases it by 3.9 points. This supports a causal contribution of these dimensions to this task. It does not show that they are the only cause of the model's performance.

Randomizing support-label assignments has little effect on the *formation* of the Text SDG, even though correct labels matter to subsequent prediction. The authors interpret this as evidence that early feature compression is not driven by induction over the labels.

### 3. What the mechanism evidence supports

The authors propose a two-stage explanation. First, early layers use comparisons among in-context images to make vision representations more compact while retaining useful similarities. Second, the resulting information is transferred to the text token that predicts the label, and later induction heads use it.

They support the first stage analytically. They show that a simplified linear-attention transformer can update compressed representations toward preserving the original examples' pairwise-similarity matrix, one gradient step per layer. The derivation assumes non-causal attention, no softmax, and no normalization. It also assumes a suitable full-rank initialization and a sufficiently small step size. This is a theoretical construction. It does not directly identify that exact circuit in the trained LVLM.

To test the trained model, the authors select 2% of attention heads by how well their output-value circuit maps one layer's Vision SDG geometry to the next. These candidate dimensionality-reduction (DR) heads concentrate before layer 15, where vision-token compression is observed. The authors then ablate those heads' attention from vision tokens to tokens in *other* images. The NCC gain in the top 10 dimensions disappears, and an equal-number random-head control is included. This is evidence for a mechanism resembling the theory. It does not prove that these heads implement the theoretical circuit.

### 4. Contribution and boundary

The authors frame the contribution as explaining how the representations used by the well-studied induction circuit are formed, rather than replacing that circuit. They report five tested model configurations in total, with Text-SDG effective dimensions at peak NCC ranging from 7.46 to 10.28. This broadens the observed pattern without establishing its universality. The real-model evidence supports a *similar* mechanism. The paper explicitly says it does not directly identify the theoretical circuit in trained models.
