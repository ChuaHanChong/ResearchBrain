# Towards Capability-Aware Traversability Navigation for Unstructured Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Capability-Aware Traversability (CAT) predicts dense traversability maps for robots in unstructured environments by conditioning visual features on robot-specific physical capabilities. The approach demonstrated improved accuracy in identifying traversable regions and enabled real-time, embodiment-aware navigation on both legged and wheeled robots.

## Problem

- Existing traversability methods struggle with conflicting ground-truth labels when combining experience from different robot platforms due to their distinct physical capabilities.
- Robot-specific physical limits are often not intrinsically embedded within perception models, relying instead on static semantic mappings or late-stage trajectory filtering.
- Self-supervised learning provides sparse supervision, and zero-shot Vision-Language Models (VLMs) typically apply to robot-agnostic scene context rather than embodiment-specific feasibility.

## Method

- **CAT** integrates frozen visual embeddings with a robot-modulated semantic representation, utilizing a ==Spatially-Adaptive Denormalization (SPADE) decoder== to condition predictions on robot-specific constraints.
- Dense traversability labels are generated through an interactive annotation pipeline that combines ==zero-shot segmentation== (GroundingDINO and SAM 2) with human refinement for low-confidence frames, grounded in robot trajectories.
- Traversability is estimated as the ==cosine similarity== between local features from the decoder and unit-norm, robot-specific ==prototypes== maintained in the latent space, updated via ==Exponential Moving Average (EMA)==.

## Results

- CAT improved Path-vs-Rest AUROC by **11.0%** over W-RIZZ and AUPRC by **15.8%** over WayFAST (RGB-D) on held-out physically executed robot trajectories.
- The framework ran at **4.8 Hz** on a Jetson Orin Nano, demonstrating real-time capability and successful deployment on a Boston Dynamics Spot (legged) and a TerraSentia skid-steer (wheeled) for embodiment-aware obstacle avoidance.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both spatial conditioning and profile-specific prototypes are crucial for capability awareness, with sharing a single prototype reducing legged Embodiment AUROC by **8.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Encoding robot physical limits directly within the perception layer, rather than through late-stage filtering, resolves conflicting traversability labels across diverse robot platforms and improves policy transferability.
> - Spatially-adaptive conditioning via SPADE blocks and the use of distinct, profile-specific prototypes are critical for achieving true capability-aware traversability and differentiating robot-specific navigation costs.
> - The modulation effect on traversability prediction is more pronounced for terrain specific to less capable robots (e.g., legged robots on stairs vs. wheeled robots), indicating effective response where platform capabilities diverge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traversability estimation is a fundamental component of autonomous navigation, particularly in complex and unstructured environments where robots must assess the feasibility and safety of traversing various terrains. The existing research landscape for traversability prediction faces several challenges that this work aims to address.

Traditional methods often rely on semantic perception, incorporating semantic understanding to provide contextual features for navigation. While effective for outdoor navigation, these approaches typically treat semantics as a static input, leading the network to learn a fixed mapping from visual features to traversability costs. This static mapping struggles to account for robot-specific physical capabilities, meaning a terrain labeled "grass" might be traversable for a tracked vehicle but impassable for a small wheeled robot.

Another prevalent approach involves self-supervised learning from a robot's physical interaction with the environment. These methods leverage data from traction, IMUs, and various sensors to map safe regions. However, robot experience inherently provides sparse supervision signals. Efforts to densify this supervision often propagate labels based on visual similarity, which can be unreliable in truly unstructured environments where visual appearance does not always correlate with actual traversability for a given robot.

More recently, the advent of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has spurred research into embodiment-agnostic navigation and zero-shot generalization. These models can interpret high-level scene context or generate end-to-end paths. However, VLM-based zero-shot approaches primarily apply to robot-agnostic scene context, meaning embodiment-specific feasibility is often not directly encoded within the learned representation. Consequently, current VLM pipelines often integrate a secondary filter into the perception system to discard unfeasible paths, rather than embedding robot constraints directly into the traversability prediction process itself. Weakly-supervised frameworks using human annotations also exist, but human judgment can introduce biases that do not accurately reflect the specific embodiment limits of robotic platforms. Furthermore, the computational cost and inference latency of large VLMs can be a barrier for real-time, time-critical tasks like collision avoidance.

The central issue across these existing paradigms is the difficulty in merging interaction experiences across multiple robot platforms due to conflicting ground-truth labels arising from their distinct physical capabilities. A trajectory illustrating a quadruped navigating stairs is not suitable for training a wheeled robot, preventing the creation of a unified traversability representation and limiting policy transferability.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitation of encoding robot embodiment directly within the perception layer. Instead of late-stage filtering or static semantic mappings, it proposes a framework that internalizes physical limits by actively modulating visual features based on robot-specific profiles. This approach seeks to integrate physical constraints directly into the spatial features, aiming to provide a unified foundation for multi-embodiment traversability estimation that is sensitive to the specific capabilities of different robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework, termed Capability-Aware Traversability (CAT), for predicting traversability across diverse robot platforms operating in unstructured environments. This framework aims to generate dense navigation costs from a single observation while conditioning on the specific embodiment of the robot, allowing different robot types to assign distinct traversability costs to the same terrain.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several identified limitations in current traversability estimation methods:

- **Embodiment-Specific Contradictions:** Existing methods struggle with the fundamental problem that the same terrain can be traversable for one robot (e.g., a legged quadruped ascending stairs) but impassable for another (e.g., a wheeled robot). This leads to conflicting ground-truth labels when attempting to combine experience from multiple platforms, hindering the creation of a shared traversability representation and limiting policy transferability.
- **Lack of Capability Encoding in Representation:** Many approaches either rely on static semantic mappings or employ late-stage trajectory filtering to adapt predictions to robot morphologies. This means that robot-specific physical limits are not intrinsically embedded within the learned representation itself. The authors argue that perception models should internalize these physical limits by dynamically modulating visual features based on robot-specific profiles.
- **Limitations of Self-Supervised and Zero-Shot Methods:** While self-supervised learning from robot experience provides reliable ground truth, it often generates sparse supervision signals and can degrade outside specific training domains. Zero-shot approaches using VLMs show impressive semantic reasoning but typically apply only to robot-agnostic scene context, failing to integrate embodiment-specific feasibility directly into the learned representation.
- **Need for Robust Annotation:** Generating dense, accurate, and robot-specific traversability labels for diverse environments and robots is challenging. Relying on generic semantic segmentation is often insufficient, as semantic classes do not strictly correlate with safe passage, which depends on specific robot capabilities and local geometric variations.

To address these motivations, CAT defines specific objectives:

1. **Develop a Capability-Conditioned Feature Space:** Create a perception layer that directly embeds platform-specific physical limits, thereby eliminating the need for late-stage trajectory filtering.
2. **Incorporate Dynamic Modulation with Semantic Maps:** Integrate semantic terrain maps into the decoding process using Spatially-Adaptive Denormalization (SPADE) blocks. This allows for the dynamic adaptation of visual embeddings based on a robot’s specific traversability profile.
3. **Structure Latent Space with Robot Prototypes:** Organize the learned latent space by maintaining a distinct prototype for each robot profile, enabling traversability to be estimated as the similarity between spatial features and the relevant robot's prototype.
4. **Design an Interactive Annotation Pipeline:** Develop a method to generate dense, per-robot supervision masks by grounding zero-shot segmentation (using models like GroundingDINO and SAM 2) in robot trajectories, augmented with temporal propagation and human refinement for low-confidence frames. This aims to create high-quality, dense ground-truth data that reflects physical traversals.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to provide a practical and effective solution for multi-robot traversability estimation in unstructured environments, enhancing safety and autonomy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CAT (Capability-Aware Traversability) is presented as an embodiment-conditioned traversability estimation framework designed for unstructured environments. It predicts a dense traversability map for a target robot given an RGB-D observation. The core of the approach lies in conditioning this prediction on robot-specific constraints by merging frozen visual embeddings with a robot-modulated semantic representation, utilizing a SPADE decoder.

The methodology is structured around three main components: generating traversability labels for training, creating semantic terrain mappings with robot-specific costs, and the capability-conditioned traversability inference pipeline.

**A. Generating Traversability Labels**

To create a training dataset across multiple robotic navigation scenarios, CAT leverages the robot's past physical trajectories as the primary source of supervision.
1. **Sparse Trajectory Projection:** Ground-truth robot poses, obtained from state estimation, are projected into the image space of an aligned RGB-D camera. This accounts for the robot's physical footprint and sensor configuration, yielding sparse positive trajectory pixels.
2. **Densification via Interactive Annotation:** To overcome the limitations of sparse annotations, the trajectory labels are expanded into dense positive masks through an interactive process. This pipeline combines zero-shot mask proposals with human verification on low-confidence frames.
 - **Initial Mask Generation:** GroundingDINO extracts bounding boxes from an RGB image using textual prompts (e.g., "path," "traversable"). These textual prompts, combined with positive point prompts sampled from the robot’s projected traversal area, condition Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM 2). SAM 2 then generates an initial dense traversability mask. This process grounds visual segmentation in the robot’s demonstrated traversals.
 - **Temporal Propagation and Refinement:** The generated segmentation masks are propagated through video sequences. Label integrity is maintained by continuously monitoring SAM 2 confidence scores and temporal consistency (Intersection-over-Union between consecutive masks). If either metric falls below a predefined threshold, the frame is sent for human refinement.
3. **Robot-Agnostic Masks with Robot-Dependent Specialization:** The pipeline generates general traversability masks, which are later refined by robot-specific vectors during capability conditioning. This ensures that a region marked traversable by segmentation but impassable by a robot’s specific traversability vector is correctly demoted to a negative sample.

**B. Semantic Terrain Mapping and Traversability Costs**

CAT captures terrain-dependent traversability by decomposing it into a robot-agnostic spatial semantic map and a robot-specific vector.
1. **Semantic Map Generation:** A frozen CLIPSeg model is used to produce per-pixel terrain class probabilities. Given a set of `C` terrain prompts, CLIPSeg outputs logits, which are then softmax-normalized at each pixel to create a semantic map `S`.
2. **Robot Traversability Vector:** Each robot `r` is characterized by a traversability vector in `R^C`, quantifying the suitability of each terrain class for that platform. Instead of manual engineering, a VLM (Qwen3-VL) is prompted with dataset RGB images and a natural language description of the robot’s physical profile to automatically generate these scores. These scores serve as a coarse prior, with the network primarily fitting its predictions to recorded trajectories.

**C. Capability-Conditioned Traversability**

This module integrates visual features with the robot-modulated semantic representation to generate robot-specific dense traversability maps.
1. **Visual Feature Encoding:** DINOv3, a frozen feature encoder `f`, processes the RGB image `x` to produce a dense spatial feature map `F`. For 3D geometric information, the single-channel depth image is replicated across three channels and processed by `f` to extract depth features `F_depth`. These are then concatenated along the channel dimension to form a unified visual representation `F_vis`. A learnable linear layer projects `F_vis` to a fused dimension `d'`, resulting in `F_fused`, which serves as the initial input to the decoder.
2. **Semantic Modulation:** The semantic map `S` is modulated by the robot’s traversability vector through a per-channel multiplication. This operation scales each terrain channel by its suitability for the platform, suppressing impassable terrains and preserving traversable ones, yielding a modulated map `ˆS_r`.
3. **SPADE Decoder:** The `F_fused` visual features and the `ˆS_r` modulated semantic map converge in a progressive SPADE decoder. The modulated map acts as a spatially-varying semantic condition. The decoder consists of four SPADE residual blocks that upsample the feature map. Within each block, SPADE replaces standard batch normalization, dynamically adapting the feature map `u` based on `ˆS_r` through learned convolutional functions `γ` and `β`. This process ensures that different robots produce distinct modulations and output features from the same visual input. The decoder outputs an `L_2`-normalized `D`-dimensional feature map.
4. **Traversability Estimation with Prototypes:** To structure the latent space produced by the decoder, a contrastive learning approach is employed. For each robot profile, a unit-norm traversability prototype `z_r` in `R^D` is maintained, updated via an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of positive feature embeddings. Traversability at each spatial location is evaluated as the cosine similarity between the local feature `u_j` and the robot-specific prototype `z_r` ( `T_j = z_r^⊤ u_j` ).
5. **Combined Loss Function:** The network is trained by minimizing a combined loss `L = (1 − ω) L_traj + ω L_mask`. Both `L_traj` and `L_mask` are pixel-level InfoNCE losses.
 - `L_traj` samples positive features exclusively from robot path pixels.
 - `L_mask` samples from the dense, trajectory-grounded mask pixels.
 - Negative samples are drawn from pixels outside positive masks and pixels demoted during the refinement step. The InfoNCE objective pulls positive spatial features closer to `z_r` and pushes negative features away.
 - A crucial step is refining the positive mask: if a pixel's modulated suitability (derived from the robot’s traversability vector) falls below a fixed threshold, it is demoted to a negative, ensuring consistency with the target robot’s physical constraints.

**Implementation Details:** The CAT framework has 8.74 M trainable parameters, with DINOv3 and CLIPSeg frozen. It supports joint training for multiple robot profiles (wheeled, legged, differential, ATV). Training uses an AdamW optimizer with specific hyperparameters for InfoNCE temperature and loss weights, trained for 35 epochs on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates CAT's performance across quantitative metrics, qualitative observations, ablation studies, and real-world deployments.

**A. Quantitative Performance**

CAT's performance was assessed on two distinct data distributions: NaviTrace (human-annotated dataset) and a held-out test split of physically executed robot trajectories. The evaluation metrics included Mean Traversability, Path-vs-Rest AUROC, Path-vs-Rest AUPRC, and Exterior Activation at 90% Recall.

- **Held-Out Trajectory Split (Trajectory Alignment):** CAT demonstrated strong performance, leading all metrics. It improved AUROC by 11.0% over W-RIZZ and AUPRC by 15.8% over WayFAST (RGB-D). This indicates that CAT generalizes well to unseen sequences and effectively differentiates traversed terrain from the surrounding environment.
- **NaviTrace (Human-Trace Alignment):** CAT achieved the highest AUROC and AUPRC, alongside the lowest exterior activation. The AUPRC improvement over WayFAST (RGB-D) suggests that CAT's high responses are more concentrated around the sparse human traces. WayFAST (RGB-D) showed higher average scores within trace masks but suffered from lower AUPRC and higher exterior activation, indicating broader, less precise activation.

**B. Qualitative Performance**

Qualitative comparisons (Figure 4) demonstrated CAT's ability to resolve ambiguities in unstructured environments and produce clearer traversable corridors.
- In a rocky passage, CAT confined high traversability scores to the uneven ground, suppressing surrounding rock walls. Baselines often extended high responses onto side surfaces or activated much of the surrounding structure.
- In an urban scene, CAT maintained a continuous traversable corridor along open pavement while reducing responses for pedestrians, construction barriers, and adjacent structures. Baselines tended to break the corridor into disconnected regions or broaden responses beyond the open pavement.

**C. Capability-Conditioned Modulation**

The study analyzed CAT's capability-specific behavior using NaviTrace scenes with distinct wheeled and legged annotations.
- When switching from the wheeled to the legged profile, the mean traversability assigned to legged paths increased by 14.2%.
- In contrast, the same profile change resulted in only a 2.8% difference on wheeled paths.
- This indicates that the modulation effect is significantly larger on terrain specific to legged robots, suggesting CAT primarily responds where platform capabilities diverge. This directional pattern is consistent with legged robots generally being able to traverse terrain accessible to wheeled robots, while wheeled robots cannot traverse terrain specific to legged platforms (e.g., stairs).

**D. Ablation Studies**

Ablation studies (Table IV) investigated the contribution of CAT’s capability-conditioning components, focusing on Path-vs-Rest AUROC and Embodiment AUROC (Emb-W for wheeled, Emb-L for legged).
- **Conditioning Mechanism:** Replacing the traversability vector with a uniform input, using concatenation, or global modulation all weakened capability-specific discrimination while generally preserving Path-vs-Rest alignment. This indicates that the conditioning mechanism primarily affects profile sensitivity rather than generic path prediction.
- **Prototypes:** Sharing a single prototype resulted in the largest degradation in capability awareness, reducing legged Embodiment AUROC by 8.0% compared to the full CAT model. Expanding to three prototypes per robot did not recover the capability signal, reducing legged Embodiment AUROC by 3.5%. These results suggest that spatial conditioning and profile-specific prototypes are critical for achieving capability awareness.

**E. Real-World Deployments**

CAT was deployed on two physical platforms: a Boston Dynamics Spot (legged) and a TerraSentia skid-steer (wheeled).
- **Performance:** CAT ran at 4.8 Hz on a Jetson Orin Nano, demonstrating real-time capability. The frozen DINOv3 and CLIPSeg backbones were the primary contributors to runtime.
- **Legged Robot (Spot):** In a forested area, Spot successfully segmented a tree as non-traversable, following a safe bypass trajectory in 10 out of 10 trials.
- **Wheeled Robot (TerraSentia):** On a staircase scenario, CAT correctly masked the elevation drop as a severe hazard for the wheeled robot, causing TerraSentia to calculate an evasive route rather than attempting an unfeasible traversal in 7 out of 10 trials. The failures were attributed to the greedy pure-pursuit planner rather than perceptual errors.
- **Demonstration:** These deployments highlight embodiment-aware obstacle avoidance, with robots adapting their navigation based on their specific capabilities.

**F. Limitations**

The authors identified several limitations:
- **Semantic Map Dependence:** CAT's performance is linked to the quality of the semantic map and the fixed terrain prompts. Unrepresented terrain inherits the score of the nearest class, and grouping errors can merge unrelated regions or reduce spatial detail.
- **Limited Capability Evaluation:** Comprehensive capability evaluation was limited to wheeled and legged profiles due to the lack of trajectory data for the differential profile and specific annotations/distinctions in NaviTrace for ATV or varying wheeled robot sizes.
- **Prototype Readout:** The study did not demonstrate that additional prototypes per robot improve capability discrimination, or verify their distinctness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Capability-Aware Traversability (CAT), that addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic navigation: the need for embodiment-aware traversability estimation in unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Novelty in Capability Encoding:** CAT introduces a novel approach by reformulating traversability estimation as capability-conditioned representation learning. Instead of applying late-stage trajectory filtering, it directly embeds platform-specific physical constraints within the spatial features of the perception layer. This represents a shift towards integrating robot embodiment at a more fundamental level within the perception pipeline.
- **Addressing Conflicting Labels and Policy Transferability:** By dynamically modulating visual embeddings based on robot-specific traversability profiles and structuring the latent space with per-robot prototypes, CAT effectively resolves the issue of conflicting ground-truth labels that arise when training different robot morphologies. This approach enhances the potential for shared traversability representations and improves policy transferability across diverse robotic platforms.
- **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** The quantitative results demonstrate CAT's ability to outperform existing state-of-the-art baselines on both human-annotated and physically executed trajectory datasets. Its improved AUROC and AUPRC metrics indicate more accurate and concentrated predictions of traversable regions, suggesting better generalization to unseen environments and effective separation of traversable terrain.
- **Demonstrated Embodiment Awareness:** The ablation studies confirm that spatial conditioning and per-robot prototypes are key drivers of capability sensitivity, enabling CAT to differentiate between terrain traversable for a legged robot but not a wheeled one. This is a critical step towards robots exhibiting more "intuitive" and safe navigation behaviors tailored to their physical limitations.
- **Real-World Applicability:** The successful deployment on physical legged and wheeled robots, operating in real-time on embedded hardware, demonstrates the practical feasibility and robustness of the CAT framework. This shows its potential for direct integration into autonomous navigation systems for various applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By establishing a robust method for capability-conditioned feature learning, CAT opens avenues for future research. This includes collecting more diverse and profile-specific supervision data for a wider range of robots, further characterizing the learned latent space, and exploring joint optimization of segmentation and traversability tasks.
- **Broader Impact on Autonomous Systems:** The ability of robots to intelligently assess terrain based on their unique physical characteristics is crucial for robust autonomous navigation in complex, dynamic, and unstructured real-world environments. This work contributes to enhancing the safety, efficiency, and versatility of autonomous robots in fields such as exploration, inspection, agriculture, disaster response, and urban mobility.

In conclusion, CAT represents a significant step towards enabling robots to understand and navigate their environments with an inherent awareness of their own physical capabilities, moving beyond generic perception towards truly embodiment-aware autonomy.
