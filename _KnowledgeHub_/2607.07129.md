# Compositional Motion Generation from Demonstration with Object-Centric Neural Fields

> [!summary] Summary
> A generative learning-from-demonstration framework enables compositional robotic behavior by integrating object-centric neural fields for scene modeling with a temporal mixture-of-experts for motion generation, conditioned on shared object-level latent representations. This approach achieves superior data efficiency in learning complex manipulation tasks from few demonstrations, demonstrating robustness and generalization on simulation benchmarks and real-robot tasks, such as 25/25 successful pick-and-place and 10/10 successful box-in-drawer with only four training examples.

## Problem

- Representing diverse, long-horizon robotic behaviors from a limited number of demonstrations in Learning from Demonstration (LfD) due to monolithic motion sequences.
- Movement Primitive (MP) based methods often require manual compositionality and reliance on hand-designed, low-dimensional features, limiting scalability and data efficiency.
- Learning robust visual features directly from images for LfD typically necessitates large datasets, undermining the data-efficiency advantage of MP-based approaches.

## Method

- Developed a framework integrating ==object-centric neural fields== to learn smooth, compact latent representations for individual objects directly from raw visual input.
- Designed a temporal ==Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)== model for motion generation, which conditions different trajectory segments on specific object-level latent representations, enabling sequential compositionality.
- Introduced a ==latent relabeling== technique using 'corner' latent vectors to canonicalize object representations, effectively expanding the training data support and constraining generalization to observed variations.

## Results

- Achieved consistently low error and high success rates (e.g., **0.97±0.02** on Cube Stacking) with significantly fewer demonstrations in simulation benchmarks compared to CNN-conditioned and diffusion policy baselines.
- Demonstrated robustness and category-level generalization in real-robot tasks, successfully completing **25/25** pick-and-place and **15/15** cup-to-target trials, even with visually distinct distractor objects or unseen object instances.
- Successfully generalized complex multi-stage tasks using 3D scene representations (ESDFs), completing **10/10** Box-in-drawer tasks with only four training demonstrations, showing average position errors of **0.32 cm** and **0.35 cm** at key poses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compositional modeling at both the perceptual (object-centric neural fields) and action (temporal MoE) levels, linked by shared latent representations, significantly enhances data efficiency and generalization in LfD.
> - Learning smooth, interpretable object-centric scene representations directly from few images provides a robust visual grounding for motion generation without requiring extensive manual feature engineering.
> - Leveraging temporal gating in motion generation allows for sequential compositionality, where different phases of a task are conditioned on distinct subsets of object-specific latent variables.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD) for robotic manipulation. LfD is a widely utilized approach for robots to acquire task-specific skills by observing human examples. A central challenge in LfD involves representing diverse, long-horizon behaviors from a limited number of demonstrations. Traditional approaches often treat motion as a monolithic sequence, which can complicate the learning process and limit scalability.

Movement Primitives (MPs) are recognized as a dominant representation in LfD, offering compact and smooth trajectory encodings. While MPs can be employed compositionally to model complex behaviors from atomic skills, this compositionality is frequently imposed manually, for example, by concatenating primitives or specifying via-points. Furthermore, MPs typically rely on hand-designed, low-dimensional features (e.g., object positions), which can be challenging to identify and estimate consistently across varied tasks. Learning features directly from images for MPs often necessitates large datasets, thereby diminishing the data-efficiency advantage of MPs. Previous neural field-based methods for scene and motion modeling have been explored, but these often operate at a scene-level, which can scale poorly with the number of objects or scene parameters.

In the broader context of imitation learning and generative policies, recent developments include diffusion-based and transformer-based methods, as well as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and generalist robot policies that leverage pretrained vision-language representations and large datasets. Some works further explore object-centric and few-shot adaptation strategies for pretrained VLAs, such as ControlVLA, which integrates online segmentation with visuomotor policies.

This research distinguishes itself by focusing on few-demonstration, task-specific trajectory generation from single static scene observations. It aims to address the limitations of existing MP-based methods by overcoming the need for manual compositionality and hand-designed features, and by improving data efficiency for visual feature learning. Unlike large-scale VLA models, this work is tailored for scenarios with limited demonstrations, where object-centric latent representations are inferred from the scene to condition full trajectory generation. It also differentiates from unsupervised scene decomposition methods, which generally require large image datasets, by learning object-centric representations from fewer images and integrating them with compositional motion modeling. The proposed approach further extends neural field applications by offering a framework for compositional scene and motion modeling at the object level, enabling efficient full-trajectory generation directly from visual input without sacrificing object-level compositionality or data efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generative learning-from-demonstration framework that facilitates compositional modeling of robotic behavior by integrating perception and motion through shared object-level representations. The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in existing LfD approaches:

1. **Addressing Monolithic Behavior Representation:** Current LfD methods frequently treat robot behavior as a single, indivisible sequence. This monolithic approach can overlook the inherent compositional structure of complex tasks, which typically involve sequential, object-level interactions (e.g., reaching, grasping, moving, placing). The authors contend that recognizing and leveraging this compositionality is crucial for scalable and data-efficient robot learning.
2. **Enhancing Movement Primitive Compositionality:** While Movement Primitives (MPs) are effective for encoding demonstrated skills, their compositional application often requires manual intervention (e.g., concatenating primitives or explicitly defining via-points). The goal is to enable a more autonomous and integrated form of compositionality that does not rely on manual imposition.
3. **Grounding Motion in Visual Structure:** MPs typically depend on hand-designed, low-dimensional features such as object positions. Identifying and consistently estimating these features across diverse tasks can be challenging. Alternative approaches that learn features directly from images often demand extensive datasets, undermining the data efficiency traditionally associated with MPs. The authors aim to ground motion generation in rich, object-centric visual structures inferred directly from raw images, thereby making the system more robust and less reliant on manual feature engineering.
4. **Improving Data Efficiency and Generalization:** A significant challenge in LfD is the ability to generalize from a limited number of demonstrations to novel scene configurations. The proposed framework seeks to maintain the data efficiency of primitive-based methods while enabling systematic generalization by leveraging compositional representations. This involves learning smooth object-level parameterizations from few examples and integrating them with motion modeling.
5. **Integrating Perception and Action:** The work is motivated by the desire to create a unified framework where perception of the scene and generation of motion are intrinsically linked through shared object-level representations. This integration is designed to enable task representations based on compact latent parameters, preserving the efficiency and compositionality of MPs while ensuring perceptual grounding.

In summary, the key objectives include learning smooth, interpretable object-centric scene representations, developing a motion modeling approach that supports sequential compositionality through temporal gating, and integrating these components into a data-efficient LfD framework capable of learning movement primitives directly from visual input to support generalization from few demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for compositional motion generation from demonstration integrates object-centric scene modeling with a temporal mixture-of-experts for motion generation. The methodology is structured around learning object-specific latent representations and conditioning trajectory generation on these representations.

**Problem Formulation:**
The training data consists of sets of images, corresponding coarse object masks, and robot motion trajectories. Each image $I^{(k)}$ has $N$ object masks $\{M_i^{(k)}\}_{i=1}^N$ and a trajectory $\tau^{(k)} = \{q^{(k)}(t)\}_{t=1}^T$. The masks are assumed to cover objects sufficiently without requiring precise boundary alignment. Trajectories are assumed to be approximately time-aligned across demonstrations, implying that each time step corresponds to a similar task phase. The learning objectives are twofold: (i) to learn a scene representation model that decomposes a scene into a fixed background and objects whose variations are captured by smooth latent vectors $\{z_i\}$, and (ii) to train a motion generation model that maps these latent vectors to demonstrated trajectories. During inference, latent vectors for new scenes are extracted and used by the motion model to generate trajectories.

**Framework Overview:**
The system comprises three main stages: training a spatial Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) for object-centric scene modeling, latent relabeling to canonicalize object latent representations, and training a motion MoE for trajectory generation. Inference involves latent search for novel scenes, followed by motion generation.

**A. Compositional Image Modeling (Spatial MoE):**
The scene is modeled as a spatial MoE, where each image $\hat{I}(x|\{z_i\}_{i=1}^N)$ is a composition of object appearances and a background.
- **Equation (2):** $\hat{I}(x|\{z_i\}_{i=1}^N) = \sum_{i=1}^N M_i(x_i) F_i(x_i) + M_{bg} F_{bg}(x)$.
- Each object $i$ has an appearance field $F_i(x)$ and a deformation $\Delta_i(x|z_i)$ conditioned on its latent vector $z_i$, which modifies the coordinate $x$ to $x_i = x - \Delta_i(x|z_i)$. $M_i(x_i)$ are object masks. $M_{bg}$ and $F_{bg}$ represent the background mask and appearance model.
- All fields ($F_i$, $F_{bg}$, $\Delta_i$, and mask logit functions $l_i$) are implemented as coordinate-based Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs), also known as neural fields.
- **Equation (3):** Object masks $M_i(x_i)$ are derived using a spatial softmax across objects and background, ensuring coherent spatial partitioning.

**B. Object-Centered Training of Experts:**
Training occurs in stages:
1. **Background Modeling:** $F_{bg}$ is trained using non-masked pixels from all demonstrations, providing a consistent background estimation.
2. **Object-Centric Training:** Individual object models $F_i$ are pretrained using coarse masks. Importance sampling emphasizes target object appearance within its mask, and pixels within other objects' masks are sampled from the background model to prevent cross-object capture. A position-aware zoom-based curriculum is employed for stability, particularly for small objects.
3. **Joint Compositional Training:** All components ($F_{bg}$, $F_i$, $\Delta_i$) are jointly optimized using the full compositional model (2). An $\ell_2$ reconstruction loss is used. Lipschitz regularization is applied to $\Delta_i$ to promote smooth dependence on latent variables.
4. **Extension to 3D Representations:** The framework supports replacing 2D appearance fields $F_i$ with 3D occupancy fields to construct compositional 3D scene representations.

**C. Latent Relabeling:**
Each object's latent vector $z_i^{(k)}$ from each demonstration $k$ is canonicalized by representing it as a convex combination of a small set of "corner" latent vectors $C_i$, which span the observed variation.
- **Algorithm 1 (Latent Search):** For a given reference image, this algorithm samples convex combinations of corner latents, calculates reconstruction error, refines low-error candidates via gradient descent, and retains the top-L candidates. This process effectively expands the training set.
- **Corner Set Estimation:** $C_i$ is iteratively expanded by initializing with two maximally distant latents from the training set, then adding the latent that is most distant from the current convex hull until all training instances can be reconstructed. This constrains generalization within the demonstrated latent support.

**D. Motion Generation (Temporal MoE):**
Trajectory generation assumes sequential compositionality, where different trajectory segments depend on different subsets of the relabeled object latents $\{\hat{z}_i\}$.
- **Equation (4):** A time-dependent conditioning signal $c(t)$ is formed by blending object-specific conditioning variables $c_i(\hat{z}_i)$ using softmax mixture weights $w_i(t)$, which are derived from learnable relevance scores $s_i(t)$.
- **Equation (5):** The robot state $q(t)$ is predicted as a Gaussian distribution $N(\mu(t), \text{diag}(\sigma^2(t)))$.
- **Equation (6):** The mean $\mu(t)$ and standard deviation $\sigma(t)$ are estimated by a network $f$ conditioned on $t$ and $c(t)$.
- **Equation (7):** The model is optimized by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of demonstrated trajectories.
- **Regularization:** Latent dropout is applied during early training to encourage reliance on informative variables. The probabilistic formulation mitigates overfitting to spurious correlations.
- **Orientation Modeling:** Quaternion trajectories are converted to axis-angle form in the tangent space of the initial quaternion for continuous rotation modeling.

**E. Latent Conditioning with FiLM:**
Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) is used to condition both the image model and trajectory generator on latent vectors.
- **Equation (8):** For a latent $z_i$, FiLM generates scale ($\gamma_i^{(\ell)}$) and shift ($\beta_i^{(\ell)}$) parameters for each layer $\ell$ of a base network.
- **Equation (9):** These parameters modulate the base network's weights and biases.
- **Equation (10):** For the trajectory generator, the object-specific FiLM parameters are aggregated using the temporal softmax weights $w_i(t)$, implementing sequential compositionality.

**F. Inference:**
At test time, latent search (Algorithm 1) is performed using the pre-trained corner sets. Objects are processed sequentially, with larger or more distinct objects potentially prioritized to resolve ambiguity. The method tolerates moderate lighting variations and visually distinct distractor objects. Task-relevant object structures are favored during latent inference, allowing visually similar distractors to be ignored if they do not align with learned representations. Inference time is approximately 1.4 seconds (RGB) and 0.8 seconds (3D) per object.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed compositional motion generation framework was evaluated through both simulation benchmarks and real-world robotic experiments.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
Four manipulation tasks of increasing complexity were used: Wall Avoidance (easy), Incline Pick-and-Place (medium), Cup Stacking (medium), and Cube Stacking (hard). These tasks featured scene variations including geometric changes and diverse object placements.

- **Baselines:** The proposed method was compared against CNN-conditioned Neural Movement Primitives (CNN-CNMP), Diffusion Policy (DP), DINO-FiLM (pretrained DINO features), CNN-FiLM (end-to-end learned CNN features), and a modified Neural Field Movement Primitive (NFMP) baseline (on Wall Avoidance only). An ablation without temporal gating ("Ours w/o gating") was also included.
- **Metrics:** Mean Euclidean Distance (MED) between predicted and demonstrated trajectories, task success rate, and data efficiency across varying training sizes.
- **Key Results (Table I):**
 - The proposed method consistently demonstrated superior performance in the low-data regime across all tasks. For instance, in Cube Stacking (hard), "Ours (Low)" achieved an MED of 0.72±0.05 and a success rate of 0.97±0.02, while the best baseline, CNN-FiLM, required a "High" data regime (100 demonstrations vs. 30 for Ours) to achieve an MED of 1.32±0.03 and 0.66±0.10 success.
 - Even when baselines were trained with substantially more demonstrations (High-data regime), the proposed method in its Low-data regime often matched or outperformed them.
 - The CNN-FiLM baseline, using global image features with FiLM conditioning, generally performed better than CNN-CNMP and Diffusion Policy, suggesting the inductive bias of FiLM-conditioned trajectory generation is beneficial.
 - The "Ours w/o gating" ablation demonstrated that temporal gating contributes to performance improvements, particularly in more complex tasks.
 - **Data Efficiency (Figure 6):** The proposed method achieved consistently low error with a limited number of demonstrations across tasks, whereas baselines typically required significantly more data to approach similar performance levels. An ablation with median-image-based background supervision showed comparable average performance but sometimes exhibited less stable behavior.
- **Robustness:** On the Cube Stacking task, the method maintained comparable MED and success rates with up to 3 out of 30 demonstrations containing incomplete masks. Moderate temporal warping within sub-task segments increased MED to 2.03 and reduced success rate to approximately 0.90, indicating reasonable performance under some timing variation.

**B. Real-Robot Experiments:**
Four real-world tasks validated the framework's robustness to perception noise, category-level generalization, and compatibility with 3D scene representations. Kinesthetic teaching (except one task) and gripper-state-based temporal alignment were used.

- **Task 1 (Pick-and-Place to bowl):**
 - Required 1-2 cm horizontal grasping accuracy.
 - With 30 training demonstrations, it achieved 25/25 successful task completions.
 - Demonstrated robustness to visually distinct distractor objects, completing 5/5 additional test cases.
- **Task 2 (Ball in container) & Task 3 (Cup to target):**
 - Evaluated category-level generalization using language-guided segmentation (Lang-SAM) with prompts like "ball," "box," "cup," "rectangle paper."
 - Task 2 (20 demos) succeeded in 14/15 trials, with one failure attributed to a novel, harder baseball requiring higher precision.
 - Task 3 (30 demos) achieved 15/15 successful completions, generalizing to unseen objects like mugs and a watering can with similar geometry.
 - **Multi-object, Language-conditioned Setting:** Both tasks were successfully completed in a multi-object, language-conditioned setting across five trials each (three executions per scenario), where prompts specified the target object.
- **Task 4 (Box in drawer):**
 - Involved opening a drawer, retrieving a box, and placing it inside, with varying drawer opening and box positions. Required approximately 1 cm grasping accuracy for the drawer handle.
 - Scene represented using a 3D Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF) converted to an occupancy field.
 - Trained with only four occupancy fields and corresponding trajectories.
 - Achieved 10/10 successful task completions on novel test configurations.
 - Average position errors at key drawer-handle and box-grasp poses were 0.32 cm and 0.35 cm, respectively, on held-out demonstrations, indicating effective generalization across unseen configurations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses several challenges in robot learning from demonstration, offering contributions with potential implications for the development and deployment of robotic systems.

One significant aspect of this work is its **data efficiency**. The proposed compositional framework enables robots to learn complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks from a comparatively small number of human demonstrations. This is particularly relevant in real-world scenarios where acquiring large datasets can be time-consuming, expensive, or impractical. By requiring significantly less training data than image-based baselines, the method lowers the barrier to entry for teaching robots new skills.

The framework introduces **compositional modeling at both perceptual and action levels**. Through object-centric neural fields, the system learns smooth and compact latent representations that capture object-level variability directly from visual input. This spatial compositionality provides an interpretable latent structure. This is complemented by a temporal Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model for motion generation, which exploits these latent representations to model motion as a sequence of object-conditioned primitives. This dual-level compositionality allows for the systematic generalization of learned behaviors across diverse scene configurations and even different object categories, as demonstrated by the use of vision-language models for segmentation.

The work's ability to **ground motion in visual structure** without relying on hand-designed, low-dimensional features is another key contribution. By directly connecting perception and motion through shared object-level representations, the framework allows for robust generalization to novel object positions, orientations, and even previously unseen objects within a semantic category. This reduces the manual engineering effort typically associated with task parametrization in LfD.

Furthermore, the research demonstrates **real-world applicability and robustness**. The successful completion of various manipulation tasks on a physical robot, including those involving perception noise and generalization across object categories, suggests the method's potential for practical deployment. Its compatibility with 3D scene representations (using ESDFs) further expands its utility for tasks requiring understanding of full three-dimensional geometry and variations in scene surfaces.

In conclusion, this compositional framework represents a step towards more scalable and adaptable robot learning. Its ability to learn from few demonstrations, generalize systematically, and integrate perception and action in an interpretable manner could facilitate the broader application of LfD in complex real-world manipulation tasks, potentially accelerating the development and deployment of intelligent robotic systems.
