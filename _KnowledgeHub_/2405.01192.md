# Imagine2touch: Predictive Tactile Sensing for Robotic Manipulation using Efficient Low-Dimensional Signals

> [!summary] Summary
> Imagine2touch predicts the tactile signal a robot would feel before touching a surface, mapping a 48x48 depth patch to a 15-dimensional ReSkin magnetic-sensor reading via a small MLP autoencoder with a 5-unit bottleneck and an auxiliary depth-reconstruction head. Applied to object recognition, ensembled independent touch likelihoods reach 58% accuracy after ten touches, beating a proprioception baseline.

## Problem

- Vision alone is unreliable for manipulation because objects become partially or fully occluded during motion, unlike humans who identify objects by touch under occlusion.
- Prior cross-modal visuo-tactile work relies on high-resolution optical tactile sensors that are bulky and expensive, limiting adoption on general robot platforms.
- Magnetic skins were only shown to recover contact location and force, leaving open whether low-dimensional readings support higher-level concepts like shape or object identity.

## Method

- **Imagine2touch** model: ==MLP encoder== (200 units) to a 5-unit ==bottleneck== to a 500-unit decoder, regressing a 15-dimensional tactile vector from a depth patch under ==MSE loss==.
- ==Auxiliary decoding head== with a separate 2000-unit layer reconstructs the input depth patch, forcing ==modality fusion== at the bottleneck; low capacity chosen to avoid overfitting the small dataset.
- Data-collection procedure pairs wrist-camera depth crops with ReSkin readings via end-effector pose alignment, yielding 1630 samples across geometric primitives and a tool.

## Results

- Shape classification on stamp indentations reached **0.92** mean accuracy across five shapes (**0.90-0.95**), confirming the low-dimensional skin encodes multi-contact geometry.
- Object recognition over ten touches averaged **58%** for Imagine2touch versus **46%** for the proprioception baseline, on five out-of-training-distribution objects.
- Gains split by set: **50%** versus **23%** on similar-looking primitives, but **70%** versus **80%** on tools, where proprioception alone was stronger.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A cheap 15-dimensional magnetic skin carries enough signal for shape and identity inference, so tactile prediction need not depend on high-resolution optical sensors.
> - Treating each touch as an independent binarized weak classifier and averaging turns noisy low-dimensional readings into a usable recognition signal.
> - Nearest-point proprioception is a surprisingly strong baseline when objects differ in extent; predicted touch helps mainly when geometric extent is nearly identical.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous robotic manipulation commonly relies on vision-based methods for tasks such as object detection, pose estimation, and tracking. However, visual sensing can be inherently unreliable in scenarios involving partial or complete occlusion of objects during manipulation. Human manipulation capabilities, in contrast, often integrate proprioceptive and tactile senses, allowing for tasks like identifying objects by touch without direct visual input. This work addresses the challenge of equipping robots with a similar visuo-tactile skill, termed "Imagine2touch," which involves predicting tactile signals from visual information.

The paper positions itself within the broader context of multi-modal sensing in robotics, particularly the fusion of vision and touch. Prior research has explored various aspects of cross-modal learning for robotic tasks, including active visuo-haptic object shape completion, 3D shape reconstruction from combined vision and touch data, and cross-modal object recognition. Efforts have also been made in generating synthetic tactile data from visual scenes, such as leveraging Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), and generating visual scenes from touch. More recent work has focused on learning unified multimodal tactile representations. This research specifically contributes to this field by developing a cross-modal model that predicts low-dimensional tactile readings from visual depth-image patches, and demonstrates its utility in an object recognition task.

A key aspect of this work is its use of ReSkin, a magnetic-based tactile sensor characterized by its low cost and compact design. This distinguishes the approach from those employing more complex or vision-based tactile sensors like GelSight, DIGIT, or DiTAC. By demonstrating efficacy with an inexpensive sensor, the research aims to contribute to the wider adoption of accessible tactile sensing solutions in robotics. The "Imagine2touch" concept directly seeks to emulate human-like predictive tactile perception, thereby enhancing robot autonomy and robustness in complex manipulation scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and implement "Imagine2touch," a capability for robots to predict expected tactile signals from visual input. Specifically, this involves predicting a touch signal based on a visual patch representing the area of an object that is anticipated to be touched.

This objective is motivated by observations of human manipulative behavior. Humans possess the ability to manipulate objects even when visual information is limited or absent, for instance, locating an object within a container without looking. This ability relies on the anticipation and interpretation of tactile feedback. Current robotic systems, predominantly reliant on vision, are susceptible to failure when objects become occluded or when environmental conditions hinder visual perception. By enabling robots to "imagine" touch, the researchers aim to mitigate these limitations and enhance robotic dexterity and robustness in diverse manipulation tasks.

A secondary motivation involves leveraging and validating the utility of accessible tactile sensing technology. The researchers utilize ReSkin, a recently developed magnetic-based tactile sensor, noted for its low cost and compact form factor. This choice aims to demonstrate that effective predictive tactile sensing can be achieved without relying on expensive or complex sensor modalities, potentially increasing the practicality and scalability of such systems for broader robotic applications.

Furthermore, the research seeks to validate the efficacy of the Imagine2touch concept through its application to a downstream robotic task. Object recognition under conditions of uncertainty, where a robot must identify an object from a known set without complete visual access, serves as a practical demonstration. This application specifically aims to show that despite utilizing low-dimensional sensor readings from ReSkin, the proposed method can achieve competitive performance. The overarching motivation is to encourage the more frequent integration of inexpensive tactile sensors into robotic systems by showcasing their potential for complex perceptual tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for Imagine2touch involves developing a cross-modal neural network model, collecting a paired visual-tactile dataset, and evaluating the model's performance in object recognition.

### 4. Imagine2touch Model

The core of the approach is a function, `IT`, which maps a processed depth image patch `z_d ∈ R^(48x48)` to a predicted tactile reading `~τ ∈ R^15`. This function is implemented as a neural network with a low-capacity architecture to prevent overfitting given the dataset size and the imbalance between input and output dimensions. The architecture consists of:
- An MLP encoder with a single layer of 200 neurons.
- A 5-neuron bottleneck layer.
- An MLP decoder with a single layer of 500 neurons, which generates the predicted tactile output `~τ`.
- An auxiliary input-decoding head with a separate 2000-neuron layer decoder. This head is designed to encourage modality fusion at the bottleneck stage, ensuring that the compressed representation integrates information from both modalities.
The model is trained using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function, minimizing the difference between the predicted and actual tactile readings.

### 5. Data Collection

A dataset of 1630 paired tactile and visual samples was collected using a robotic setup (Fig. 1a). The setup included a robotic arm equipped with a ReSkin sensor and a wrist-mounted depth camera. Data collection involved the robot performing random touches on various objects. An alignment vector guided the robot's movement to ensure accurate pairing of wrist camera images and ReSkin readings for each touch. The dataset comprised 11 distinct objects: five basic geometric shapes (cube, cylinder, pyramid, sphere, cone) used for training the Imagine2touch model, one tool (screwdriver) for model validation, and additional primitive shapes and tools for the object recognition experiments. A t-SNE plot of the collected data indicated a distributed sensor activation and a correspondence between depth patches and tactile signals, suggesting the feasibility of learning the mapping.

### 6. Object Recognition Application

Imagine2touch is applied to the downstream task of object recognition, where the robot identifies an object from a predefined set `O` of possible objects, each with a known 3D representation (e.g., meshes). The recognition process involves `N` sequential touches and an ensemble probabilistic model:
- For each touch `i` (up to `N`), the robot gathers an actual tactile reading `τ_i`.
- It then considers each possible object `o' ∈ O`. For each `o'`, the robot uses its known 3D model and a heuristic (not detailed in the paper) to sample a location `~l` on the object's surface that is intended to distinguish it from other objects.
- The Imagine2touch model `IT` is then used to predict a hypothetical tactile signal `~τ_i` for that location `~l` on object `o'`.
- An ensemble probabilistic model calculates the likelihood `P(o = o' | τ_i, ~τ_i)` for each possible object `o'` based on the difference between the actual `τ_i` and the predicted `~τ_i`. This is modeled as `e^(-(τ_i - ~τ_i))`.
- The model assumes independence between touches for robustness against noise. Probabilities are binarized among possible objects (winner-take-all) to balance the weight of each individual touch.
- The final probability for an object `o'` is calculated as the average of its likelihoods across all `N` touches: `P(o = o') = (1/N) * Σ P(o' | τ_j, ~τ_j)`.

### 7. Experiments

Two experiments were conducted:
- **Shape Classification (Feasibility Check):** This preliminary experiment aimed to verify that the ReSkin sensor could distinguish between different contact shapes, beyond just touch location and force. A statically mounted ReSkin sensor was indented by five distinct stamp shapes (T, circle, angle, triangle, cross). An MLP classifier (500-neuron hidden layer, 10-neuron bottleneck, 5-class head) was trained on 1280 data points using cross-entropy loss.
- **Object Recognition:** This experiment evaluated Imagine2touch's performance against a proprioception baseline. The task involved identifying an object from a possible set, using 3D models for hypothetical touch predictions. Objects were fixed to wooden bases to remove the need for pose estimation. The robot performed 10 touches per object in locations sampled by the heuristic. The experiment included out-of-training distribution primitive shapes and tools.
 - **Proprioception Baseline:** Defined as the Euclidean distance between the real contact location and the nearest point on a possible 3D model. This baseline represents a minimal form of tactile feedback.
 - **Evaluation Metric:** Success rate per touch in recognizing the correct object.

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the ReSkin sensor's capabilities and the performance of the Imagine2touch model in object recognition.

### 9. Shape Classification Results

The preliminary shape classification experiment demonstrated the ReSkin sensor's ability to distinguish between various contact shapes. The MLP classifier achieved an average accuracy of 92% across five different shapes. Individual shape accuracies were recorded as 91% for "T", 90% for "Circle", 90% for "Angle", 95% for "Triangle", and 94% for "Cross" (Table I). These results indicate that the low-dimensional data from the ReSkin sensor can be effectively used to extract higher-level features pertaining to contact geometry, thereby validating its suitability for the proposed predictive tactile sensing task.

### 10. Object Recognition Results

The primary object recognition experiment compared the performance of Imagine2touch (I2T) against a proprioception baseline (prop.). The evaluation was conducted on objects not used in the Imagine2touch model's training, demonstrating generalization.
- **Overall Performance:** Imagine2touch achieved an object recognition accuracy of 58% after ten touches per object, which exceeded the proprioception baseline's average accuracy of 46% (Table II).
- **Primitives Set:** For the set of primitive shapes, Imagine2touch achieved 50% accuracy, significantly outperforming the proprioception baseline, which recorded 23% accuracy. The paper notes that primitive objects were more challenging for both methods due to their similar extent and tactile features.
- **Tools Set:** For the set of tool objects, the proprioception baseline achieved 80% accuracy, while Imagine2touch achieved 70%. In this specific subset, the proprioception baseline showed higher performance.
- **Generalization:** The results indicated that Imagine2touch was capable of generalizing to predict touch signals for objects outside its training distribution.

In summary, the Imagine2touch model demonstrated an ability to improve object recognition performance when compared to a minimal proprioception baseline, particularly for objects that were challenging to differentiate based solely on contact location. Despite the low-dimensional nature of the ReSkin sensor's output, Imagine2touch achieved recognition rates that surpassed random chance and the simple proprioceptive measure across the varied object sets.

### 11. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing "Imagine2touch," a method for equipping robots with a predictive tactile sense. The significance of this work can be understood through several points:

Firstly, it addresses a fundamental limitation of vision-centric robotic systems: their susceptibility to performance degradation under object occlusion or adverse visual conditions. By enabling robots to anticipate tactile feedback from visual cues, Imagine2touch offers a pathway to more robust and dexterous manipulation, mirroring human-like capabilities in navigating objects by touch.

Secondly, the work demonstrates the effective utilization of ReSkin, a low-cost and compact tactile sensor. This choice highlights the potential for advanced tactile sensing capabilities to be integrated into robotic systems without requiring prohibitive costs or complex hardware. This accessibility could broaden the adoption of tactile feedback in various robotic applications, from industrial tasks to service robotics. The generalization of the model to out-of-distribution objects further suggests its potential for practical deployment in dynamic environments without extensive retraining for every new object.

Thirdly, the development of a cross-modal model for predicting tactile readings from depth images contributes to the growing area of multi-modal perception in robotics. By establishing a robust mapping between visual and tactile modalities, the research provides a foundation for more integrated sensory processing, where different senses can complement each other to form a comprehensive understanding of the environment.

Finally, the paper identifies clear avenues for future research, such as developing an inverse model to predict depth images from tactile signals. This concept, leading to tactile 3D reconstruction, could enable robots to create 3D representations of objects solely through touch, further enhancing their exploratory and manipulative capacities in visually ambiguous or dark environments. The overall impact of this work is to advance robotic perception by integrating predictive tactile sensing, making robots more adaptable and capable in complex interaction scenarios.
