# When Should a Failing Robot Ask? Initiating Corrective Human-Robot Dialogue from Audited Sensor Evidence

> [!summary] Summary
> This work builds a LIBERO-simulated benchmark of 2,200 injected-cause failures per family (grasp, detection, placement) and audits what each sensor reveals about the cause, then casts corrective dialogue as a three-action choice: act, consult onboard sensors, or ask a human, with an expected-cost threshold. Six open vision-language models track prompt layout rather than evidence, yet one human reply lifts them to roughly the answerer's reliability.

## Problem

- Failure-diagnosis systems name causes from robot observations but never verify the cause is actually recoverable from those observations, leaving high scores unchecked against data leakage.
- A separate line builds ask-for-help mechanisms assuming causes are sometimes unrecoverable, yet neither line tests which assumption holds for a given failure.
- Existing ask-timing methods give coverage guarantees or information-gain heuristics without measured sensor informativeness or measured agent accuracy, so no cost-optimal reference policy is computable.

## Method

- A LIBERO-based benchmark injects known causes into grasp, detection and placement failures, 2,200 episodes per family, logging camera frames plus ==wrist force-torque== and ==gripper telemetry==.
- A **three-check audit** (==shuffled-label==, ==held-out viewpoint and appearance transfer==, ==run-separated splits==) certifies each sensor's classifier accuracy as a lower bound on the information it carries.
- An **expected-cost decision rule** over act, sense and ask yields a threshold from stated repair and question costs and a noisy human answerer, scoring models by ==regret==.

## Results

- Grasp causes reach **0.986** from force data but plateau at **0.545** from images; detection reaches **0.910**, while placement's **0.998** collapses to **0.605** on held-out camera angles.
- Moving the refusal option first collapses refusal from **78-100%** to **0-6%** in three model-family pairs; frames-only accuracy never clears majority-class baselines and confidence is uninformative.
- Telemetry text lifts four of six models above baseline (**0.457-0.543** vs **0.400**); one human answer yields **0.70-0.81**, yet ask rates ignore a fourfold cost change.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A high diagnosis score means nothing until it survives shuffled-label, held-out-viewpoint and run-separation checks; shortcut features can make an uninformative sensor look near-perfect.
> - Apparent model caution or recklessness may be option-position bias; sweep answer-option order before reading refusal rates as epistemic behavior.
> - Ask a human only when no robot channel carries the answer; wire the decision to measured accuracy and stated costs, not self-reported confidence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A simulated pick-and-place robot has just failed to pick up an object. It must choose whether to act on its diagnosis, check another onboard sensor, or interrupt a person. A question costs attention and time, but a wrong repair can cost more. This paper measures what the robot's sensors actually reveal about each failure before deciding whether asking is warranted. In its benchmark, some grasp failures look ordinary in the camera while the robot's force data nearly identify the cause. These are simulation results, with a scripted task policy and a scripted answerer.

Example episodes from three failure families: A grasp, B detection, C placement. In the grasp row, the injected causes (grip force, object mass, and friction) are not rendered, and the frames look unremarkable. The placement row illustrates the disqualified 0.998 score.

### 2. Auditing what the sensors reveal

The benchmark is a simulated tabletop pick-and-place task with three failure families: grasp, detection, and placement. Each family has 2,200 failures, with 2,000 in the audit pool and 200 held out for model evaluation. The true cause is known because it is injected.

To measure what a sensor's data contain, the authors train a classifier to identify the cause. A high score alone does not establish genuine evidence, so the classifier must also pass three checks: shuffled labels, held-out appearance and viewpoint, and a separate simulation run. A classifier that passes earns a "certificate," a demonstrated lower bound on what the sensor data contain.

The decisive comparison concerns grasp causes. From force-torque and gripper data, a classifier scores 0.986. A stricter severity-matched force test still separates causes at 0.97 against a 0.35 chance level. The checked image-classifier sequence plateaus at 0.545. That plateau holds for this sequence and simulator and is not a proven ceiling for every image method.

Placement shows why the checks matter. An image classifier scores 0.998 but falls to 0.605 on held-out camera angles. That apparent result is therefore not accepted as evidence that the cause is recoverable from the image.

### 3. Pricing the next move

The paper states costs in units of one retry: a correct repair costs 1, a wrong repair 10, and asking 3. The scripted answerer is correct with probability 0.80, vague with probability 0.15, and wrong with probability 0.05. Under those assumptions, asking pays off when diagnosis accuracy falls below a break-even point of about 0.588. The resulting policy is a computed reference built from stipulated costs and measured model accuracy. It is not a real deployment guarantee.

The test sweeps the question cost across 1, 3, 6, and 10. On grasp failures, Qwen's measured forced-answer accuracy is about 0.32. The reference policy therefore asks at costs 1 and 3 and acts at 6 and 10. Qwen asks on 100% of episodes at every cost. More broadly, no tested model's ask rate tracks the fourfold cost sweep.

Ask rate for six labeled models on family A (grasp) and family B (detection) as stated asking cost $C_{a}$ varies over 1, 3, 6, and 10. The dashed black step is the optimal fixed policy; the shaded region is its break-even uncertainty band. No model tracks the cost.

When the four models with ask data do ask, a human answer raises final task accuracy to 0.70–0.81. This result depends on a scripted answerer whose informative replies name causes in the answer-option vocabulary. Indirect or narrative phrasing can lower performance for some models.

### 4. What the paper establishes

The contribution is to audit sensor evidence and measure model accuracy first, and then use them to define a reference policy for acting, sensing, or asking. Existing models can be assessed against that policy. Under this framing, the question shifts from whether an uncertain robot should ask to which available source of information the model can use, and whether that source is worth its cost. The tested models generally do not follow the rule.

The findings are simulation results with a scripted task policy and answerer. They are not evidence that real people, robots, or open-ended dialogue will behave the same way.
