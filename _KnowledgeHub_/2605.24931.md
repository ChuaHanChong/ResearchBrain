# Learning High-Frequency Continuous Action Chunks in Latent Space

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a method for robots to learn high-frequency continuous actions by using a VAE to encode action chunks into a continuous latent space. Their Reuse-then-Refine strategy further improves action chunk continuity during asynchronous execution, leading to smoother real-robot movements and higher task success rates.

## Problem

- Existing action chunking policies produce imprecise and jittery trajectories at high frequencies (e.g., 60 Hz), making it difficult to achieve smooth robot execution.
- Training at lower frequencies and interpolating actions often amplifies errors and fails to recover the fine-grained motion details essential for continuous high-frequency control.
- Asynchronous inference in real-time robot execution introduces discontinuities at action chunk boundaries, leading to stalls, jerky motions, or rollbacks that degrade execution quality.

## Method

- A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is employed to compress high-frequency action chunks into a continuous, low-frequency latent representation, which a separate policy then learns to predict.
- The Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) strategy addresses asynchronous inference discontinuities by reusing recently executed actions and feeding a combined sequence through the pre-trained VAE decoder for seamless refinement.
- The VAE enforces continuity in the latent space and reconstructs smooth action chunks, simplifying the policy's learning task for high-frequency control.

## Results

- Latent-space training reduced Cartesian position deviation by up to **80%** (e.g., from 7.59mm to 1.47mm for OpenVLA-OFT) and consistently lowered jerk in synchronous execution across tasks.
- The Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) strategy reduced asynchronous chunk-to-chunk Cartesian position overlap difference by up to **79%** (e.g., from 1.575mm to 0.331mm for PI0.5) compared to original methods.
- Real-world experiments demonstrated improved task success rates (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT from **28%** to **74%** for 'Peel Cucumber') and reduced end-to-end latency, indicating smoother and more reliable execution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning high-frequency actions in a continuous latent space simplifies the policy approximation problem, leading to inherently smoother and more precise trajectories compared to direct action-space learning.
> - A training-free refinement mechanism, Reuse-then-Refine (RTR), can effectively maintain chunk-level continuity under asynchronous inference, mitigating common issues like stalls and jerky movements.
> - The co-design of a robust action representation (latent space via VAE) and an efficient execution strategy (RTR for continuity) is crucial for achieving high-quality, real-time robotic control in complex tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation, specifically in the area of imitation learning and policy execution. Modern robotic policies, particularly Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, increasingly employ action chunking, where a policy predicts a sequence of actions rather than single-step commands. This approach has been shown to improve the modeling of complex trajectories and long-horizon dependencies, enhancing generalization across diverse manipulation tasks.

However, the existing research landscape faces challenges regarding the action frequency at which these policies operate. While action chunking is effective at moderate frequencies, its benefits diminish at high frequencies (e.g., 60 Hz). At such rates, policies trained directly in the action space frequently generate imprecise and jittery trajectories, hindering smooth and continuous real-world robot execution. A common alternative, training at lower frequencies and interpolating, has also shown limitations, as interpolation can amplify errors and fail to reconstruct fine-grained motion.

Simultaneously, the pursuit of real-time robot execution in long-horizon tasks has led to the development of asynchronous inference strategies. These methods overlap policy computation with action execution to mitigate inference latency. However, asynchronous execution can introduce discontinuities at the boundaries between consecutively inferred action chunks, leading to stalls and degraded execution quality. While some prior work has attempted to address chunk-level continuity, these methods primarily operate in the action space and have not fully explored solutions within latent representations.

The present work addresses these limitations by introducing a method for learning high-frequency actions in a continuous latent space and developing a strategy to maintain chunk-level continuity under asynchronous inference. It builds upon existing imitation learning architectures (Diffusion Policy, OpenVLA-OFT, PI0.5) by integrating a variational autoencoder (VAE) for action representation and proposing a training-free refinement mechanism, thus bridging gaps in achieving precise, smooth, and continuous high-frequency robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to enable the learning and smooth execution of high-frequency continuous action chunks for robotic manipulation. The motivation stems from several identified limitations and challenges:

1. **Challenge of High-Frequency Action Learning:**
 - **Imprecision and Jitter:** When action frequency increases (e.g., to 60 Hz), existing action chunking policies, particularly those trained directly in the action space, tend to produce imprecise and jittery trajectories. This issue is particularly pronounced in methods relying on discrete action tokenization, where quantization errors become significant at small action strides.
 - **Difficulty for Policy Approximation:** High-frequency actions possess high temporal information density and fine-grained spatial variation. Directly learning these highly detailed sequences places a substantial burden on policy function approximation, making it difficult for models to accurately capture the underlying motion structure.
 - **Inadequacy of Interpolation:** Training policies at lower frequencies and interpolating actions to higher frequencies during execution is an intuitive alternative. However, this approach is limited because interpolation can amplify small prediction errors and often fails to recover the fine-grained motion details essential for continuous high-frequency control, resulting in trajectories that remain imprecise and jittery.

2. **Desirability of High-Frequency Actions:**
 - **Smooth and Continuous Motion:** High-frequency actions are crucial for achieving smooth, continuous robot execution. They provide dense target sequences with small spatial steps and short temporal intervals, allowing the robot controller to maintain non-zero velocities between steps. This avoids the "stop-and-go" behavior characteristic of low-frequency control, where each action implicitly enforces a zero-velocity boundary, leading to repeated acceleration and deceleration.
 - **Preservation of Fine-Grained Motion Details:** High-frequency actions implicitly encode velocity information and capture subtle motion nuances, which are vital for complex contact-rich tasks requiring precise and stable interaction with the environment.

3. **Challenge of Chunk-Level Discontinuity in Asynchronous Inference:**
 - **Real-time Execution Requirement:** For long-horizon tasks, policies must repeatedly generate new action chunks. Real-time deployment often relies on asynchronous inference to hide latency by overlapping computation and execution.
 - **Boundary Discontinuities:** Under asynchronous execution, directly switching between consecutively inferred action chunks can lead to significant spatial and temporal misalignments at chunk boundaries. These "gaps" or "jumps" manifest as visible stalls, jerky motions, or even rollbacks in robot execution, undermining the desired smoothness. Existing continuity solutions in the action space have shown limitations, and their applicability to latent spaces is unexplored.

To address these motivations, the research proposes a dual approach: shifting high-frequency action learning to a continuous latent space and developing a novel strategy, Reuse-then-Refine (RTR), to ensure continuity across action chunks during asynchronous inference.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology focuses on two main components: learning high-frequency actions in a continuous latent space and ensuring chunk-level continuity through a novel refinement strategy during asynchronous inference.

#### 3.1. Learning High-Frequency Actions in Latent Space

The core idea is to transform the learning target from raw high-frequency action chunks to a more compact and continuous latent representation.

- **Action Chunk Representation:** An action chunk $A_t = [a_t, a_{t+1}, \dots, a_{t+H-1}]$ represents a sequence of $H$ actions, where each action $a_i$ consists of Cartesian positions (xyz), orientations (roll-pitch-yaw), and gripper width.
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE):** A VAE is employed to compress the high-frequency action chunk $A_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times c}$ (where $c$ is action dimension) into a low-frequency, continuous latent representation $z$.
 - **Encoder ($\mathcal{E}$):** The encoder maps an action chunk $A_t$ to a latent vector $z = \mathcal{E}(A_t)$. This latent representation is regularized towards a Gaussian prior using a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence term.
 - **Decoder ($\mathcal{D}$):** The decoder reconstructs the action chunk from the latent representation, yielding $\hat{A}_t = \mathcal{D}(z)$.
 - **Temporal Downsampling:** To reduce the temporal information density, the encoder downsamples the input action chunk by a factor of $f = H/h$, producing a latent $z \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d}$, where $h$ is the latent horizon and $d$ is the latent dimension. This process effectively summarizes dominant motion trends over multiple timesteps, rather than modeling every fine-grained fluctuation.
- **Latent Policy Training:** After the VAE is trained and its parameters are fixed, a separate latent policy is trained. This policy learns to predict latent action chunks ($z$) conditioned on observations ($o_t$). During inference, the latent policy outputs a latent vector, which is then passed through the VAE decoder to reconstruct the high-frequency action chunk. This approach leverages the VAE's ability to enforce continuity in the latent space and reconstruct smooth trajectories, thereby simplifying policy learning for high-frequency control.

#### 3.2. Improving Continuity via Reuse-then-Refine (RTR)

This strategy addresses discontinuities that arise between consecutive action chunks during asynchronous inference.

- **Asynchronous Inference Problem:** In real-time execution, policy inference often overlaps with action execution. When a new action chunk is inferred, some of its initial actions may already be "outdated" (i.e., the robot has progressed beyond those timesteps). Directly switching to the non-outdated part of the new chunk can create abrupt gaps, leading to jerky motion or stalls.
- **Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) Strategy:** RTR is a training-free method designed for latent policies to ensure seamless transitions.
 1. **Reuse Stage:** During asynchronous inference for a new action chunk, some actions from the *previously executed* chunk are still being performed. These *recently executed actions* that overlap with the new inference window are "reused." They are then concatenated with the *non-outdated actions* from the *newly predicted* (decoded) action chunk to form a combined, but potentially misaligned, intermediate action sequence.
 2. **Refine Stage:** This concatenated action sequence is then fed into the *pre-trained VAE encoder*. The encoder generates a compressed latent representation from this combined sequence. Subsequently, the VAE *decoder* reconstructs a new, refined action chunk from this latent representation. Because the VAE is trained to map continuous latents to smooth action chunks, this refinement step smooths out inconsistencies and ensures a continuous transition from the previously executed actions into the newly generated sequence.
- **Efficiency:** The VAE encode/decode process for refinement introduces a negligible overhead (approximately 2 ms), minimizing its impact on overall policy latency.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Base Policies:** The methodology is evaluated using three representative imitation learning policies: Diffusion Policy (DP), OpenVLA-OFT (OFT), and PI0.5. For each, both an original (action-space) and a latent-space variant are trained.
- **Hardware:** Experiments are conducted on a 7-DOF xArm 7 robotic arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive gripper. Policy inference occurs on a remote server with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, communicating with the robot.
- **Tasks:** Three real-world, contact-rich manipulation tasks are used: "Peel Cucumber," "Wipe Vase," and "Write Board."
- **Action Frequency:** All experiments use a high action frequency of 60 Hz, with a prediction horizon of 48 actions (0.8 seconds). The VAE uses a temporal downsampling ratio of $f=4$.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Precision:** Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between predicted and ground-truth action chunks (deviation) for Cartesian position (xyz, mm) and orientation (rpy, degrees).
 - **Smoothness:** Acceleration and Jerk (third-order finite difference of trajectory).
 - **Execution Quality (Real-robot):** Task success rate, Jerk, and Exceed Count (number of actions exceeding a safe execution speed of 120 mm/s).
 - **Continuity (Asynchronous):** Overlap Diff (difference in overlapping regions of adjacent chunks) and Boundary Gap (discrepancy between last action of previous chunk and first non-overlapping action of subsequent chunk).
 - **Efficiency:** End-to-end execution latency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that learning high-frequency actions in a latent space, augmented with the Reuse-then-Refine strategy, improves precision, smoothness, and continuity in robotic manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Evaluation under Synchronous Inference

- **Dataset-based Benchmarking (Table 2):**
 - Latent-space training consistently improved action smoothness (lower acceleration and jerk) across all policies (DP, OFT, PI0.5) compared to their action-space counterparts.
 - Deviation (MAE) was generally reduced by latent-space training, with a notable improvement for OFT, where Cartesian position deviation decreased from 7.59mm to 1.47mm and orientation deviation from 8.52 degrees to 5.15 degrees. PI0.5 showed a slight increase in Cartesian position deviation but improved orientation deviation and smoothness.
- **Real-world Synchronous Execution (Table 1, Figure 6):**
 - Latent policies achieved lower jerk and reduced exceed counts across all three contact-rich tasks (Peel Cucumber, Wipe Vase, Write Board). This indicates smoother and more stable execution.
 - For OFT, latent-space training significantly improved success rates (e.g., from 28% to 74% for Peel Cucumber) and reduced jerk (e.g., from 5.238 to 0.558 for Write Board).
 - Qualitative results (Figure 6) illustrate that OFT-Latent produced cleaner written lines and faster end-to-end execution with fewer stalls compared to OFT trained directly in the action space.

#### 4.2. Evaluation under Asynchronous Inference

- **Chunk-to-Chunk Continuity (Table 4):**
 - The Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) strategy consistently improved chunk-level continuity. For PI0.5, RTR reduced the Cartesian position overlap difference from 1.575mm (original) to 0.331mm and the boundary gap from 5.636mm to 4.069mm. Similar improvements were observed for DP and OFT.
 - In contrast, directly applying RT-C (a prior chunk-level continuity method) in the latent space (Latent+RT-C for PI0.5) degraded continuity metrics compared to the latent policy alone, highlighting RTR's specific suitability for latent-space policies.
- **Real-world Asynchronous Execution (Table 3, Figure 7):**
 - RTR led to the lowest jerk and exceed counts across all policies and tasks, indicating smoother action generation and safer real-robot execution under asynchronous conditions.
 - For PI0.5, RTR reduced jerk in Write Board from 4.984 (original) to 1.754 and significantly lowered exceed counts.
 - Qualitative observations (Figure 7) showed that RTR eliminated visible rollback and resulted in smoother, more continuous execution compared to RT-C, which still exhibited noticeable gaps and rollbacks.
- **End-to-End Latency (Table 5):**
 - High-frequency policies generally achieved lower end-to-end latency compared to low-frequency policies, due to the elimination of repeated acceleration and deceleration.
 - Under asynchronous inference, the proposed Latent+RTR approach consistently achieved the lowest end-to-end latency across all tasks (e.g., 15.11s for Write Board compared to 17.93s for high-frequency original and 19.09s for latent without RTR). This reduction is attributed to RTR mitigating stalls and rollbacks caused by boundary discontinuities.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies and Supplementary Experiments

- **Comparison with RT-C:** RTR consistently outperformed RT-C in improving chunk-level continuity and reducing jerk in asynchronous real-robot execution, especially when combined with latent-space policies (Tables 3 and 4).
- **Effect of Latent Compression (Figures 8, 9, 10):** Moderate temporal downsampling ratios (e.g., f=2 to f=8 depending on the policy) improved action precision while largely preserving smoothness. Overly aggressive compression degraded both precision and smoothness. Continuous VAEs consistently achieved lower deviation than VQ-VAEs.
- **Comparison with Interpolation (Table 8):** Latent representations demonstrated lower jerk, fewer exceed counts, and reduced execution latency compared to policies trained at low frequencies and then interpolated to high frequencies, affirming the benefits of learning in latent space directly.
- **Generalizability of Latent Representation (Table 14):** Simulation experiments on the LIBERO benchmark showed that latent versions of ACT and PI0.5 achieved comparable or sometimes higher task success rates than their original counterparts, indicating that the latent representation does not compromise task-level generalization.
- **VAE Reconstruction Error:** The VAE introduced sub-millimeter reconstruction errors (e.g., 0.37mm, 0.11mm, 0.17mm for Peel Cucumber), indicating its ability to preserve fine-grained spatial structure while compressing.
- **OOD Generalization of RTR:** While RTR slightly increased open-loop prediction error, the error remained in the sub-millimeter range, demonstrating robustness for improving inter-chunk continuity in real-robot settings without significantly sacrificing accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an approach to enhance the learning and execution of high-frequency continuous actions in robotic systems, addressing practical challenges in real-world deployment. The significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Advancement in High-Frequency Robotic Control:** The work demonstrates a method for policies to learn fine-grained, high-frequency actions (e.g., 60 Hz) more effectively. This is significant because high-frequency control is critical for dexterous manipulation, contact-rich tasks, and achieving fluid, human-like robot motions, which are often hindered by the imprecision and jitter of current imitation learning methods.
2. **Improved Action Representation:** By shifting policy learning from the action space to a continuous latent space via VAEs, the research provides a more robust and efficient representation for complex, temporally dense action chunks. This latent representation simplifies the policy's learning task by focusing on coherent local motion patterns rather than granular command variations, leading to inherently smoother and more precise trajectories.
3. **Enhanced Real-time Execution Continuity:** The introduction of Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) is a practical contribution to addressing a critical issue in asynchronous policy deployment: chunk-level discontinuities. By enabling seamless transitions between successively inferred action chunks, RTR mitigates stalls, jerky movements, and rollbacks, which are common problems in real-world robot operation. This directly translates to more reliable and efficient task completion.
4. **Tangible Performance Gains:** The extensive real-world experiments on contact-rich tasks provide empirical evidence of the method's effectiveness. The consistent reductions in jerk, exceed counts, and end-to-end latency, alongside improved task success rates, demonstrate a practical pathway towards more capable and safer robotic systems.
5. **Co-design of Representation and Execution:** The findings highlight the importance of considering action representation alongside execution strategies. The combination of latent-space learning and RTR exemplifies a co-design approach that optimizes both the inherent quality of action generation and the continuity of its real-time deployment, suggesting a paradigm for future robotic policy development.

The potential impact of this work extends to various applications requiring high-precision and smooth robot control, such as industrial automation, surgical robotics, and assistive robotics, where continuous and reliable interaction with the environment is paramount. By enabling robots to execute complex tasks with reduced pauses and smoother motions, this research contributes to the development of more adaptable and dependable robotic agents in dynamic real-world settings.
