# "Just in Time" World Modeling Supports Human Planning and Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from MIT and UBC developed the "Just-in-Time" (JIT) framework, demonstrating how humans construct simplified environmental representations incrementally during planning and reasoning by integrating simulation, visual search, and dynamic representation modification. This model accurately predicts human memory and attention patterns across both planning and physical reasoning tasks, outperforming alternative models that rely on pre-computed optimal representations.

## Problem

- Humans fluently plan and reason in complex, detail-rich environments despite severe working memory and computational capacity limitations.
- Existing models often assume simplified mental representations are formed *before* simulation, leading to a "pre-computation paradox" where evaluating optimal representations can be more demanding than a full simulation.
- A lack of concrete algorithmic accounts explaining *how* these simplified representations are efficiently constructed online by the human mind.

## Method

- The =="Just-in-Time" (JIT) model== proposes an iterative process where simulation, local visual search, and dynamic representation modification are interleaved.
- The model uses a ==representational sketchpad==, a probabilistic simulator, and a ==perceptual lookahead module== to encode objects only when they become relevant along a simulated trajectory and probabilistically forget irrelevant ones.
- The JIT framework was implemented and tested in two distinct domains: a grid-world navigation planning task and a Plinko-like physical prediction task.

## Results

- The JIT model significantly outperformed the Value Guided Construal (VGC) model in explaining human memory for object locations (r=**0.95** vs. VGC r=**0.93**) and attention patterns (e.g., r=**0.88** vs. VGC r=**0.65** for Experiment 1D) during planning tasks.
- JIT accurately predicted human memory recall (r=**0.87**) and confidence (r=**0.96**) for object positions in physical reasoning tasks, explaining substantial unique variance beyond VGC (partial r_JIT|VGC = **0.49**).
- In specifically designed dissociation experiments (Experiment 2B), JIT aligned with human memory patterns (r=**0.84**), correctly predicting higher recall for frequently contacted "counterfactually irrelevant" objects, whereas VGC predicted the opposite (r=**-0.65**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Humans construct efficient, simplified representations of their environment *online* and *incrementally* during the simulation process itself, rather than through extensive pre-computation.
> - The "Just-in-Time" approach resolves the paradox of pre-computation by allowing representations to emerge organically as needed, deferring encoding until an object is immediately relevant for the current simulation.
> - This framework provides a unified algorithmic account for managing representational complexity across diverse cognitive functions, including planning and intuitive physics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a fundamental challenge in cognitive science and artificial intelligence: how humans can fluently plan, predict, and reason in visually complex, detail-rich environments, given severe limitations in working memory and computational capacity. The broader research landscape acknowledges that **probabilistic mental simulation** is a key component of human reasoning, allowing us to mentally explore potential future states or outcomes. However, the sheer complexity of real-world scenes makes a full, high-fidelity simulation computationally intractable for the human mind.

Prior work has converged on the idea that people overcome this challenge by constructing **simplified mental representations, or "construals,"** that abstract away irrelevant details and focus only on relevant features (e.g., Kinney & Lombrozo, 2024; Shin & Gerstenberg, 2023). These theories often frame human cognition as **resource-rational**, meaning that the mind makes a rational trade-off between the utility gained from representing an object (e.g., improved planning) and the cognitive cost of maintaining that representation (e.g., Arumugam et al., 2024; Gershman et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2022; Lieder & Griffiths, 2020).

However, a significant gap in this landscape, highlighted by the authors, is the **"how" of construal formation**. While there has been extensive research on *what* constitutes an optimal or useful simplified representation, less attention has been paid to the *algorithmic process* by which these representations are efficiently constructed. Many existing accounts implicitly assume that representation formation is a process that occurs *prior* to simulation or planning. This assumption leads to a "paradoxical conclusion" (Russell & Wefald, 1991): precisely evaluating the utility of a simplified construal often requires considering a simulation using the full environment, making the search for an optimal construal potentially *more* computationally demanding than simply simulating everything in the first place. Some proposals attempt to circumvent this by learning approximations from prior experience or optimizing within a time budget, but these often lack generality or efficiency.

This paper positions itself as a critical advancement by providing a **concrete algorithmic account** of how humans might construct reduced representations *online* and *incrementally*. By proposing a "Just-in-Time" (JIT) framework, the authors directly challenge the pre-computation paradigm. They bridge cognitive science (explaining human behavior) with AI (designing efficient agents) by offering a mechanism that is both psychologically plausible and computationally efficient, addressing a core problem shared by both fields.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to propose, formalize, and empirically validate a "Just-in-Time" (JIT) framework for simulation-based reasoning, demonstrating how humans can construct efficient, simplified representations of their environment **online with minimal added computation**. This framework aims to provide a mechanistic account of how people determine efficient simplifications as they simulate.

The motivations driving this research are multifaceted:

1. **Resolve the "Pre-computation Paradox":** A primary motivation is to overcome the theoretical and computational difficulties associated with models that assume representations are fully formed or optimized *before* mental simulation begins. The JIT model seeks to show that simplified representations can emerge organically from the simulation process itself, thus avoiding the paradox of requiring extensive pre-computation to achieve computational savings.
2. **Account for Human Cognitive Efficiency:** The authors are motivated to explain how humans manage the immense detail of real-world scenes despite limited working memory and computational capacity. The JIT model posits that people defer encoding objects until "just before that representation is relevant for the current simulation," aligning with resource-rational principles without explicit up-front optimization.
3. **Propose a General Algorithmic Framework:** The research aims to develop a general model that can explain representational choices across seemingly distinct cognitive domains, specifically planning and physical reasoning. Demonstrating this generality would suggest a fundamental cognitive strategy.
4. **Empirical Validation:** A key objective is to empirically test the JIT model against prominent alternative models, such as the Value Guided Construal (VGC) model, using human behavioral data from both planning and physical reasoning tasks. This includes not only predicting overall performance but also explaining fine-grained measures of memory and attention.
5. **Inform AI and Robotics:** Beyond cognitive science, the authors are motivated to provide insights for artificial intelligence and robotics, where efficient planning and interaction in cluttered environments remain significant challenges. An incremental approach to representation construction could reduce computational demands and improve the performance of artificial agents.

In essence, the research seeks to provide a compelling, empirically supported, and computationally viable explanation for how the human mind intelligently manages information overload to support effective planning and reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology is the development and testing of the "Just-in-Time" (JIT) model, which proposes an iterative, interleaved process of simulation, visual search, and representation modification.

**The Just-in-Time (JIT) Model:**
The JIT model is built upon three interacting components:
1. **Representational Sketchpad:** This serves as a working memory that holds object-centric information to support the current simulation. The collection of objects in the sketchpad at any given time forms the "construal" (C ⊆ O).
2. **Simulator:** Given a current state (s) and optionally an action (a), this module probabilistically forecasts possible future states, P(s'|s, a). JIT generates predictions by sampling many such trajectories.
3. **Perceptual Lookahead Module:** This module uses the current state of the simulation to guide a local visual search in the external scene. Its role is to quickly flag objects that are not yet represented but are relevant for imminent interactions along the simulated path.
This process is iterative:
- **Initialization:** The representation starts sparsely, including only the tracked object (e.g., avatar, ball), goal areas, and universally present features (e.g., a central cross obstacle).
- **Simulate:** The state is incremented by sampling from the simulator's probability distribution, generating a trajectory τ.
- **Lookahead:** After each simulation step, the model scans the visual scene for potential interactions near the simulated trajectory, drawing inspiration from how people's eyes follow moving objects. This identifies objects that *need* to be encoded.
- **Encode:** New objects flagged by the lookahead module are added to the construal. Simultaneously, the construal is culled: previously relevant objects are probabilistically forgotten based on a power law decay function (controlled by parameter γ), reflecting that objects no longer relevant are wasteful to retain.

**Test Domains:** The JIT model's generality is tested across two domains:
1. **Planning:** A grid-world navigation task, where an agent navigates a maze to a goal (re-using stimuli from Ho et al., 2022).
 - **Simulator:** Implemented as a stochastic variant of the A* search algorithm (Zhi-Xuan et al., 2020), using a Manhattan distance heuristic and a straight-line tiebreak bias.
 - **Lookahead:** A one-step lookahead, flagging objects that intersect the *next* proposed step of the plan.
2. **Physical Reasoning:** A Plinko-like task, where a ball falls through an array of bumpers, and participants predict its landing spot (similar to Gerstenberg et al., 2018).
 - **Simulator:** A probabilistic physics simulation engine (Pymunk, Blomqvist, 2007) incorporating noise in initial ball position, collision normal rotation, and collision restitution (Allen et al., 2020; Smith & Vul, 2013).
 - **Lookahead:** A simple circular spotlight centered on the simulated ball's current position.

**Comparison Model:** The JIT model is compared against the **Value Guided Construal (VGC) model** (Ho et al., 2022).
- **VGC premise:** People choose a construal that *jointly optimizes* the utility of planning/simulation (how well it matches a full simulation) and minimizes the number of represented objects, *prior to executing the simulation*.
- **Key contrast:** VGC relies on *pre-computation* of an optimal construal, whereas JIT *incrementally constructs* the construal during simulation.

**Empirical Evaluation:**
- **Re-analysis of Planning Data:** Behavioral data from 9 experiments in Ho et al. (2022) were re-analyzed, focusing on memory for object locations (Exp 1C) and attention measured by mouse hovering (Exp 1D, 1E).
- **Efficiency Analysis:** Simulation studies on 40 procedurally generated grid worlds compared JIT against VGC, a maximal representation baseline, and a random representation baseline. An algorithmic utility function (V(A) = E[U(A)] - α·C_computation(A) - β·C_representation(A)) was used to evaluate models across different cost regimes.
- **New Physical Reasoning Experiments:**
 - **Experiment 2A:** Participants predicted ball landing spots, then were probed for memory of a randomly selected object's position and confidence.
 - **Experiment 2B:** Designed to explicitly *dissociate* JIT and VGC predictions. It included "counterfactually relevant" objects (VGC predicts high recall, JIT moderate) and "counterfactually irrelevant" objects (JIT predicts high recall, VGC low).
- **Measures:** Model fit to human data was assessed using Pearson correlation (r), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and log-likelihood.
- **Parameter Fitting:** Physics simulator noise parameters were calibrated via a separate norming experiment (S3). The JIT memory decay parameter (γ) and VGC softmax temperature (α) were fit to human behavioral data, with parameters transferred directly between physics experiments (2A to 2B) to test generality.

This comprehensive approach combines computational modeling, re-analysis of existing datasets, and novel experimental designs to rigorously test the JIT framework against a strong alternative and demonstrate its explanatory power and generality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents compelling empirical evidence supporting the "Just-in-Time" (JIT) model over alternative accounts in both planning and physical reasoning domains.

#### 4.1. JIT Explains Human Planning and Efficiency

- **Human Data Fit (Ho et al., 2022 re-analysis):**
 - **Memory (Experiment 1C):** The JIT model demonstrated a superior fit to human memory for object locations (r=0.95, RMSE=0.08, LL=-1,763) compared to the VGC model (r=0.93, RMSE=0.10, LL=-1,809), despite having one fewer free parameter. While JIT and VGC predictions were highly correlated (r=0.95), JIT provided a better explanation.
 - **Attention/Information Seeking (Experiments 1D & 1E):** For tasks where objects were masked and revealed by mouse hovering, JIT significantly outperformed VGC in explaining human information seeking patterns. In Experiment 1D, JIT achieved r=0.88 (RMSE=0.18, LL=-8,093) versus VGC's r=0.65 (RMSE=0.40, LL=-11,864). Similarly, in Experiment 1E, JIT had r=0.95 (RMSE=0.14, LL=-5,892) versus VGC's r=0.74 (RMSE=0.29, LL=-7,607). This indicates JIT better captures the dynamic, online process of information acquisition during planning.
- **Efficiency of JIT Representations (Simulation Analysis):**
 - **Algorithmic Utility:** When evaluating models based on an algorithmic utility function that trades off planning utility, computational cost, and representational cost, JIT demonstrated superior performance in "most regimes" of these costs.
 - **Exceptions:** VGC was only more efficient when computational costs were virtually zero and representational costs were very low. The "maximal" model (representing all objects) was optimal when computation was expensive but representation was cheap.
 - **Representational Parsimony:** Crucially, JIT achieved its efficiency by consistently representing *fewer objects* on average than the VGC model. This is attributed to JIT's focus on the *currently simulated trajectory*, whereas VGC aims for an optimal construal for *any* potential plan, leading it to represent objects that might only block suboptimal paths that JIT wouldn't explore.

#### 4.2. JIT Explains Representations for Physical Prediction

- **Generalizability (Experiment 2A):**
 - JIT effectively predicted human memory recall (r=0.87, RMSE=0.18) and confidence (r=0.96) for object positions after a physical prediction task. The model's predictions align well with the idea that objects more deeply or frequently encoded during simulation are better remembered.
 - VGC showed a slightly weaker fit (recall r=0.82, RMSE=0.28; confidence r=0.90).
 - **Unique Variance:** Partial correlation analysis revealed that JIT explained substantial unique variance in human memory patterns beyond VGC (r_JIT|VGC = 0.49), while VGC offered only marginal improvement on top of JIT (r_VGC|JIT = 0.08). This indicates JIT captures aspects of human cognition not explained by VGC.
 - **Control Analyses:** Supplementary experiments demonstrated that human memory in these tasks was primarily driven by an object's relevance to the ball's simulated trajectory, rather than low-level visual processes (e.g., passive fixation, foreground/background distinction) or generic scene layout statistics.
- **Experimental Dissociation (Experiment 2B):**
 - This experiment was specifically designed to create scenarios where JIT and VGC made opposing predictions.
 - **Counterfactually Relevant Objects:** These objects significantly affected the final prediction outcome but were contacted only about half the time in noisy simulations. VGC predicted strong representation, while JIT predicted moderate representation (only when contacted).
 - **Counterfactually Irrelevant Objects:** These objects were frequently contacted but made little difference to the final outcome (landing bucket). VGC predicted low representation, while JIT predicted high representation (due to frequent contact).
 - **Result:** JIT's predictions strongly aligned with human memory (r=0.84), showing higher recall for counterfactually irrelevant objects than for relevant ones, as people tended to remember objects that frequently appeared in their mental simulations, regardless of their ultimate impact on the *final* outcome. In stark contrast, VGC predicted the *opposite pattern* to human behavior (r=-0.65), failing to account for memory in these critical dissociation trials.

Overall, the findings consistently demonstrate that the JIT model provides a more accurate and parsimonious account of how humans form representations for planning and physical reasoning, especially in dynamic, online scenarios where an incremental approach proves superior to pre-computing optimal construals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Just-in-Time" (JIT) World Modeling framework presented in this paper carries significant implications for both cognitive science and artificial intelligence, offering a novel perspective on how intelligent agents manage complexity.

#### 5.1. Significance for Cognitive Science

- **Algorithmic Account of Resource-Rational Cognition:** The work provides a concrete, algorithmic model for how humans achieve resource-rationality in complex environments. It moves beyond simply describing *what* people should represent to explaining *how* these efficient representations are dynamically constructed. This is a crucial step in understanding the computational mechanisms underlying human intelligence.
- **Resolution of the "Pre-computation Paradox":** By demonstrating the viability and empirical support for online, incremental representation formation, the JIT model elegantly resolves the long-standing paradox where evaluating optimal construals might require more computation than simply processing the full environment. This provides a compelling argument for the ecological validity of mental simulation as a core cognitive process, even in complex settings.
- **Unified Framework for Diverse Cognitive Domains:** The successful application of the JIT framework to both grid-world planning and intuitive physical reasoning tasks suggests a more general cognitive strategy. This offers a unifying perspective on seemingly disparate cognitive abilities, highlighting common underlying computational principles for managing environmental detail during simulation-based reasoning.
- **Implications for Memory and Attention:** The model directly links simulation processes to patterns of human memory and attention. The finding that people tend to remember objects that frequently appear in their simulated trajectories (even if "counterfactually irrelevant" to the final outcome) provides new insights into the "depth of processing" (Craik & Tulving, 1975) and how dynamic cognitive processes shape what we encode and recall.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact for Artificial Intelligence and Robotics

- **Efficient AI Planning in Complex Environments:** Modern robotics and AI planning algorithms often struggle with computational overhead when operating in cluttered, real-world environments with numerous irrelevant objects (e.g., Silver et al., 2021). The JIT framework offers a principled method for constructing simplified, high-utility construals online, thereby reducing the representational complexity and branching factor of planning. This could lead to more scalable and robust AI systems.
- **Generalizable Cognitive Architecture:** The principles of deferring computation until needed ("just-in-time" compilation in computer science, "just-in-time" logistics) are well-established for efficiency. This paper extends these principles to representational complexity in cognitive modeling, providing a blueprint for building AI agents that can adaptively manage their internal representations based on the immediate demands of a task.
- **Beyond Visually-Grounded Domains:** While tested in visually-grounded tasks, the authors propose that the JIT approach is not fundamentally limited to explicit perceptual input. Its principles of local goal-directed search and incremental representation building could be generalized to abstract cognitive domains, such as sub-goaling in logical reasoning (Cheyette et al., 2025; Olieslagers et al., 2024) or foraging-like search patterns in semantic memory (Hills et al., 2012). This broadens the scope of problems that can be tackled with JIT-inspired architectures.
- **Synergy with Learned Heuristics:** The paper opens avenues for future research exploring how the online, local lookahead of JIT could be integrated with pre-computed, learned representations (akin to VGC) from prior experience. This combination could yield hybrid systems that are both highly efficient in novel situations and leverage domain expertise in familiar contexts, leading to more human-like and versatile AI.

In summary, this research offers a powerful and empirically validated algorithmic account of a core human cognitive ability – efficient reasoning in complex environments. By providing a "Just-in-Time" mechanism, it not only deepens our understanding of the human mind but also offers actionable principles for designing the next generation of intelligent artificial agents capable of navigating the complexities of the real world.
