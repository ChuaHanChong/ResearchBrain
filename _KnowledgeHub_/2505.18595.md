# MisoDICE: Multi-Agent Imitation from Unlabeled Mixed-Quality Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> MisoDICE introduces a two-stage framework for offline multi-agent imitation learning, enabling policy learning from unlabeled, mixed-quality demonstrations by first using LLMs and preference learning to label trajectories, then applying a novel DICE-based algorithm. This approach achieved superior returns on SMACv1 and SMACv2 benchmarks, outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Traditional multi-agent imitation learning (MAIL) relies on costly, hand-labeled expert demonstrations, which are scarce in real-world scenarios.
- Real-world demonstration datasets often contain mixed-quality trajectories, making it difficult to distinguish expert from suboptimal behaviors without explicit reward functions.
- The combinatorial explosion of joint state-action spaces makes direct application of single-agent imitation learning methods intractable in multi-agent environments.

## Method

- A multi-step data labeling pipeline uses LLMs for initial ==preference generation==, refined by an offline preference-based MARL algorithm (O-MAPL) to rank and partition trajectories into expert and suboptimal sets.
- **MisoDICE**, a novel multi-agent imitation learning algorithm, extends the single-agent DICE framework to multi-agent settings through value factorization and linear mixing networks.
- A decentralized weighted Behavior Cloning approach is used for policy extraction, ensuring consistency with the global policy optimization and enabling decentralized execution.

## Results

- MisoDICE consistently achieved the highest average returns across most SMACv1 and SMACv2 maps, outperforming various Behavioral Cloning, OMAPL, and individual DICE baselines.
- Performance benefits from more capable LLMs (GPT-4o over GPT-4o-mini) in the preference labeling stage, indicating the importance of high-quality initial labels.
- Ablation studies showed that selecting a moderate number of top-K expert trajectories (e.g., around K=200 for SMACv2) and an optimal value for the suboptimal data influence hyperparameter (α=**0.05**) maximizes performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining LLMs with preference-based learning effectively infers trajectory quality from unlabeled, mixed-quality multi-agent demonstrations, providing a scalable labeling solution.
> - Employing linear mixing networks for global value and discriminator functions maintains the convexity of the multi-agent DICE objective, ensuring stable and efficient training.
> - Balancing the influence of explicitly identified expert data and the broader mixed-quality dataset through a hyperparameter (α) is critical for robust imitation learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the intersection of imitation learning (IL), multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), and offline learning. Imitation learning is a subfield of reinforcement learning (RL) where policies are learned from expert demonstrations, circumventing the need for explicit reward functions. Traditionally, IL algorithms, such as Behavior Cloning (BC) and occupancy-matching techniques like GAIL and AIRL, have primarily focused on single-agent settings and typically assume access to high-quality expert demonstrations. Recent efforts have extended single-agent IL to more challenging scenarios involving limited or suboptimal data.

The paper situates itself within the evolving landscape of multi-agent imitation learning (MAIL), which addresses the complexities arising from inter-agent dynamics. Existing MAIL algorithms often adapt single-agent approaches and incorporate game-theoretic concepts for competitive scenarios or policy decomposition for cooperative tasks. A significant limitation of these methods is their reliance on large volumes of high-quality expert data, which is often impractical to acquire in complex multi-agent environments due to the combinatorial explosion of joint state-action spaces.

The work also draws upon advancements in preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL), an emerging area where policies are trained using human preference data (e.g., pairwise trajectory comparisons) rather than direct rewards. Modern PbRL methods often involve learning a reward function from preferences or directly optimizing policies from them. The paper integrates PbRL, specifically the O-MAPL algorithm, and Large Language Models (LLMs) into its data labeling pipeline.

Furthermore, the paper operates within the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm, a common approach in MARL where global information is used during training, but agents act independently based on local observations during execution. This paradigm is crucial for managing the computational complexity of multi-agent systems and has been extended to various MARL subfields, including offline MARL and preference-based MARL.

This work distinguishes itself by tackling the problem of offline multi-agent IL from *unlabeled demonstrations of mixed quality* – a practical yet underexplored setting. By addressing the scarcity of labeled expert data and the presence of suboptimal trajectories, the proposed framework aims to make MAIL more applicable to real-world scenarios where data collection is imperfect.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and effective method for offline multi-agent imitation learning (IL) when the available demonstration data is unlabeled and of mixed quality, meaning it contains both expert and suboptimal trajectories. This setting presents several key challenges that motivate the proposed solution:

1. **Lack of Labeled Expert Data:** Traditional imitation learning algorithms typically require access to a dataset of demonstrations explicitly labeled as "expert." In many real-world multi-agent scenarios, acquiring such precisely labeled data is costly, time-consuming, and often impractical. The absence of explicit quality labels makes it difficult for algorithms to distinguish beneficial behaviors from inferior ones.
2. **Mixed-Quality Demonstrations:** Real-world datasets often contain a spectrum of demonstration qualities, ranging from highly proficient to significantly suboptimal. Learning directly from a mixed-quality dataset without discernment can lead to policies that inherit undesirable behaviors, diminishing performance. The challenge is to filter out or appropriately weigh suboptimal examples while effectively utilizing the valuable, high-quality portions of the data.
3. **Computational Complexity in Multi-Agent Settings:** Multi-agent environments inherently suffer from the curse of dimensionality, as the joint state-action space grows exponentially with the number of agents. This combinatorial explosion makes direct application of single-agent IL methods, or even naive extensions, computationally intractable. Effective solutions must incorporate mechanisms for scalability and decentralized control.
4. **Absence of Reward Functions:** As an imitation learning problem, the reward function is not available. This necessitates learning policies solely from observations and actions, without direct performance feedback from the environment. In the context of mixed-quality data, this further complicates the task of identifying "expert" behavior without an explicit reward signal to evaluate trajectories.

To address these motivations, the research proposes a two-stage framework:
- **Stage 1: Multi-Step Data Labeling:** This stage aims to overcome the unlabeled and mixed-quality nature of the demonstrations by creating a pipeline to infer trajectory quality. The goal is to progressively refine assessments of trajectory quality, ultimately partitioning the unlabeled dataset into expert and non-expert segments. This involves leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for initial preference generation, followed by refinement using a preference-based MARL algorithm (O-MAPL) to recover reward signals and rank trajectories more accurately.
- **Stage 2: Multi-Agent Imitation Learning (MisoDICE):** This stage focuses on developing a novel multi-agent IL algorithm capable of effectively learning from the *now-labeled* expert and suboptimal demonstrations. The objective is to learn robust policies that imitate expert behavior while addressing the computational challenges of large joint state-action spaces. This involves extending the single-agent DICE framework to multi-agent settings through a novel value decomposition and mixing architecture, aiming for a convex policy optimization objective and consistency between global and local policies.

The overarching objective is to enable effective and robust imitation learning in practical multi-agent scenarios where acquiring perfectly curated, labeled expert demonstrations is not feasible.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed solution, MisoDICE, is structured as a two-stage framework: a multi-step trajectory labeling pipeline followed by a novel multi-agent imitation learning algorithm.

#### 3.1. Multi-Step Data Labeling (Phase 1)

This phase aims to identify high-quality, expert-like behaviors within an unlabeled dataset of mixed quality (D_unlabeled). The process involves:

1. **LLM-Generated Preference Data:** A set of trajectory pairs (N_p) is randomly sampled from D_unlabeled. An LLM (e.g., GPT-4o) is prompted to provide preference labels for these pairs. The LLM's judgment is based on semantic features and contextual information from the multi-agent environment (e.g., unit health, positions, actions in SMAC). This generates an initial preference dataset (P). The authors acknowledge that LLM judgments can be error-prone in complex multi-agent dynamics.
2. **Preference-Based Reward Recovery (using O-MAPL):** The initial preference dataset P is used to train O-MAPL, an offline preference-based MARL algorithm. O-MAPL learns a soft Q-function and corresponding value function (V) directly from these preferences, providing a more grounded assessment of demonstration quality than raw LLM feedback.
3. **Trajectory Ranking and Partitioning:** After training O-MAPL, an underlying reward function R(s, a) is recovered for all state-action pairs in D_unlabeled, approximated as R ≈ Q - γV. The total return G(σ) = Σ_{(s,a)∈σ} R(s,a) is computed for each trajectory σ in D_unlabeled. Trajectories are then ranked by their returns. The top-K_expert trajectories are selected to form the expert dataset (D_E), and the remaining trajectories constitute the suboptimal dataset (D_Mix). These two datasets (D_E and D_Mix) are then used in the subsequent multi-agent IL phase.

#### 3.2. Multi-Agent Imitation Learning (MisoDICE - Phase 2)

This phase focuses on designing a novel multi-agent IL algorithm, MisoDICE, to leverage the labeled D_E and D_Mix (which together form D_U = D_E ∪ D_Mix) to learn robust policies.

1. **Distribution Matching Based Formulation:** MisoDICE follows an occupancy-matching principle, minimizing a combined KL divergence objective:
 min_{π_tot} D_KL(ρ_π_tot || ρ_E_tot) + α D_KL(ρ_π_tot || ρ_U_tot)
 Here, ρ_π_tot is the state-action stationary distribution of the learned policy, ρ_E_tot is from the expert dataset D_E, and ρ_U_tot is from the union dataset D_U. The hyperparameter α controls the influence of the suboptimal data. This objective is reformulated into a convex constrained optimization problem over the occupancy measure ρ_π_tot, incorporating Bellman flow constraints. Using Lagrangian duality, this is transformed into a saddle-point problem that simplifies to a non-adversarial minimization problem over a global value function ν_tot.

2. **Value Factorization:** To address the intractability of high-dimensional joint state-action spaces in multi-agent settings, MisoDICE adopts a value factorization technique within the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm.
 - The global value function ν_tot(s) is decomposed into local value functions {ν_i(s_i)} for each agent i using a mixing network M_ϕ, parameterized by ϕ: ν_tot(s) = M_ϕ[{ν_i(s_i)}].
 - The paper proposes a *linear mixing network* (e.g., a single-layer network) for M_ϕ. This design choice is critical as Proposition 5.1 proves that if M_ϕ is linear in both ν_i(s_i) and ϕ, the overall training objective L(ϕ, ν_ν_ν) remains convex in both ϕ and ν_ν_ν. This ensures stability and efficiency during training. Conversely, Proposition 5.2 states that non-linear mixing networks would result in a non-convex objective.

3. **Occupancy Ratio Estimation:** The training objective requires the occupancy ratio term log(ρ_E_tot(s, a)/ρ_U_tot(s, a)). This ratio is estimated by solving a discriminator-based classification problem:
 max_c J(c) = E_ρ_E_tot[log c(s, a)] + E_ρ_U_tot[log(1 − c(s, a))].
 The optimal solution c*(s, a) directly provides the ratio. In the multi-agent context, local discriminators {c_i(s_i, a_i)} are aggregated using another linear mixing network M_η (parameterized by η) to form the global discriminator c(s, a) = M_η[{c_i(s_i, a_i)}]. Proposition 5.3 shows that linearity of M_η preserves the concavity of the objective J(c, η), contributing to stable training.

4. **Policy Extraction:** After optimizing the value function ν_tot, the corresponding optimal occupancy ratio w_tot_ν_ν_ν_∗ (s, a) = ρ_π_tot_∗(s, a) / ρ_U_tot(s, a) is computed. The global optimal policy is then recovered by solving a weighted Behavior Cloning (BC) objective. For decentralized control, MisoDICE employs a *local weighted BC* approach for each agent i:
 max_{π_i} Σ_{(s_i,a_i)~ρ_U_tot} w_tot_ν_ν_ν_∗(s, a) log π_i(a_i | s_i).
 All agents share the same global weight w_tot_ν_ν_ν_∗(s, a), enabling globally-informed local policy optimization. Proposition 5.4 proves that this decentralized weighted BC is consistent with the global weighted BC. Furthermore, Proposition 5.5 shows that the optimal local policy can be explicitly expressed as a softmax function over local Q-functions and the local behavior policy, incorporating the mixing network parameters.

#### 3.3. Practical Algorithm

The implementation involves three types of local networks (discriminator, value, and policy networks) for each agent, with shared parameters across agents for efficiency. Two distinct linear mixing networks are used: one for discriminators (M_η) and one for value functions (M_ϕ). The overall training proceeds iteratively: discriminator training, value function training, and policy training, as outlined in Algorithm 1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MisoDICE was conducted on several multi-agent reinforcement learning benchmarks, specifically SMACv1, SMACv2, and MAMuJoCo. The core findings are summarized below:

1. **Superior Performance on SMACv1 and SMACv2:** MisoDICE consistently achieved the highest average returns across nearly all tested SMACv1 and SMACv2 maps (Table 1, Figure 1). This demonstrates its ability to learn effective policies from unlabeled, mixed-quality data in complex cooperative combat scenarios. The learning curves (Figure 1) indicated not only higher final performance but often faster and more stable learning compared to baselines. Win rates also showed MisoDICE outperforming baselines (Table 5, Figure 8).

2. **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - **Behavioral Cloning (BC) variants (β=0.0, 0.5, 1.0):** MisoDICE significantly outperformed all BC variants. This highlights that simply re-weighting or pooling mixed-quality data with BC is insufficient, and MisoDICE’s explicit quality assessment and distribution-matching approach provide a substantial advantage.
 - **OMAPL:** While OMAPL, which learns directly from preferences, showed competitive performance, MisoDICE generally surpassed it. This indicates the benefit of MisoDICE's second-stage (DICE-based) IL, which leverages the refined quality signals from the preference learning phase.
 - **Individual DemoDICE (INDD):** INDD consistently underperformed MisoDICE. This result supports the necessity of coordinated learning and value decomposition in multi-agent settings, rather than applying single-agent methods independently to each agent.
 - **MARL with Supervised Reward (MARL-SL):** This baseline, which learns a reward function via supervised learning from preferences before MARL policy optimization, was generally outperformed by MisoDICE. This suggests that MisoDICE's integrated DICE-based approach for reward recovery and policy learning offers advantages over a decoupled two-stage method.
 - **MisoDICE-VDN:** This ablated version, using a simpler VDN mixer, performed better than many baselines but still fell short of the full MisoDICE. This demonstrates the positive contribution of MisoDICE’s specific learnable mixing architecture, which enhances the value decomposition process.

3. **Ablation Study 1: Impact of Top-K Expert Trajectories:** Varying the number of top trajectories (K_expert) selected for D_E showed that performance tends to peak with a moderate number of expert trajectories (e.g., around K=200 for SMACv2 tasks, as shown in Figure 2). Using too few (K=50) or too many (K=800, 1200) expert trajectories led to lower median returns and potentially higher variance. This indicates that the multi-step data labeling successfully identifies high-quality data, but there's a trade-off in determining the optimal size of the expert dataset.

4. **Ablation Study 2: Impact of Suboptimal Data Influence Hyperparameter (α):** (Detailed in appendix) The study showed that an appropriate value of α (e.g., 0.05) is crucial for balancing imitation of expert-only data versus regularization from the broader mixed-quality data. Performance degraded if α was too low (over-reliance on possibly scarce D_E) or too high (over-regularization by potentially suboptimal D_U).

5. **Ablation Study 3: Comparison of LLM Capabilities:** (Detailed in appendix) Comparing GPT-4o with GPT-4o-mini for preference labeling indicated that the more capable GPT-4o generally led to better performance for MisoDICE. This suggests that the quality of LLM-generated feedback directly impacts the effectiveness of the subsequent preference-based reward recovery and expert data identification.

6. **Ablation Study 4: Performance with Rule-Based Preference Labeling:** (Detailed in appendix) MisoDICE demonstrated strong performance even when using a rule-based approach for initial preference generation (e.g., based on cumulative return). However, LLM-based labeling generally yielded superior results in complex SMAC environments, indicating that LLMs can capture more nuanced aspects of "expert" behavior that simple rules might miss. In environments like MaMuJoCo, where LLMs struggled with contextual understanding, a rule-based oracle was used, highlighting a limitation of LLMs in abstract domains.

7. **Ablation Study 5: Performance with Ground Truth Data Labels:** (Detailed in appendix) When MisoDICE was provided with ground truth labels for expert and suboptimal trajectories (bypassing the entire Phase 1 labeling pipeline), performance further improved. This establishes an upper bound and suggests that while the multi-step labeling pipeline is effective, there is still room for improvement in perfectly distinguishing expert data.

In summary, the results demonstrate that MisoDICE's integrated approach of leveraging LLMs for labeling, employing preference-based learning for reward recovery, and utilizing a value-decomposed DICE algorithm for policy learning, enables robust and high-performing imitation learning from challenging unlabeled, mixed-quality multi-agent demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a critical and practical challenge in the field of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL): learning effective policies from unlabeled, mixed-quality demonstration data in offline settings. The significance and potential impact of MisoDICE can be outlined as follows:

1. **Bridging the Gap in Practical Imitation Learning:** The reliance of traditional multi-agent imitation learning (MAIL) methods on perfectly curated, labeled expert demonstrations has limited their applicability in real-world scenarios. MisoDICE provides a framework that can operate with readily available, uncurated data, significantly expanding the practical utility of MAIL for complex multi-agent systems where expert data is costly or impossible to hand-label.

2. **Leveraging Advanced AI for Data Curation:** The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) and preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) for automated trajectory labeling is a notable contribution. This multi-step labeling pipeline offers a scalable and potentially more nuanced approach to identifying expert behaviors from raw data compared to purely rule-based or manual labeling. This could reduce the human effort and expertise traditionally required for preparing demonstration datasets.

3. **Theoretical Foundations for Multi-Agent DICE:** The extension of the single-agent DICE framework to multi-agent settings, incorporating novel value decomposition and mixing architectures, is a significant algorithmic contribution. The theoretical analysis establishing the convexity of the global learning objective and the consistency between global and local policies provides strong guarantees for the stability and efficiency of MisoDICE during training. This makes the approach more robust than adversarial imitation learning methods which often suffer from instability.

4. **Scalability in High-Dimensional Spaces:** By adopting the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm and developing a value factorization strategy with linear mixing networks, MisoDICE directly addresses the combinatorial explosion of joint state-action spaces in multi-agent environments. This allows for the learning of decentralized policies while leveraging global information during training, crucial for complex tasks.

5. **Robustness to Data Quality:** The explicit formulation to handle mixed-quality data, by balancing the influence of a small, identified expert dataset (D_E) with a larger, mixed union dataset (D_U), allows MisoDICE to extract maximum utility from imperfect demonstrations. This is particularly relevant in domains where generating purely expert demonstrations is challenging.

6. **Empirical Validation on Challenging Benchmarks:** The consistent superior performance of MisoDICE across a wide range of challenging SMACv1 and SMACv2 environments, and its competitive results on MaMuJoCo, demonstrates its effectiveness and generalization capabilities. The comprehensive ablation studies further elucidate the contributions of its various components, such as the LLM-based labeling and mixing network architecture.

**Potential Impacts:**
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** The framework can lower the barrier to entry for developing AI agents in multi-agent domains, as it reduces the need for perfectly curated expert data. This could accelerate AI development in areas like robotics, logistics, and resource management where multi-agent coordination is key.
- **Enhanced Autonomy:** By enabling learning from diverse, unannotated demonstrations, MisoDICE contributes to creating more adaptable and autonomous multi-agent systems that can learn from various sources of observational data.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for further research into improving LLM-based preference generation for abstract tasks, extending the framework to competitive or mixed cooperative-competitive settings, and exploring more sophisticated data filtering and weighting mechanisms.

**Limitations and Future Work (as stated by authors):**
- The current work is limited to cooperative settings, with extension to competitive environments remaining an open challenge.
- The reliance on LLMs for trajectory labeling may face limitations in domains with highly abstract task semantics, like MaMuJoCo, where LLMs struggle with contextual understanding. Addressing these challenges is highlighted as future research directions.

In conclusion, MisoDICE represents a significant step towards practical and robust multi-agent imitation learning by effectively handling unlabeled and mixed-quality demonstration data, leveraging contemporary AI tools, and providing a theoretically grounded, scalable algorithmic solution.
