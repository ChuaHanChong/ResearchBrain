# Transitive RL: Value Learning via Divide and Conquer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley introduce Transitive RL (TRL), a divide-and-conquer value learning algorithm for offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, which mitigates the "curse of horizon" by employing a transitive Bellman update with in-trajectory subgoals and expectile regression. This method achieves leading performance on long-horizon robotic tasks and competitive results across various standard benchmarks.

## Problem

- Traditional temporal difference (TD) learning in offline goal-conditioned RL suffers from severe bias accumulation, leading to the "curse of horizon" in long-horizon tasks.
- Monte Carlo (MC) methods for value learning exhibit high variance, particularly for long trajectories, limiting their practicality.
- Prior approaches leveraging the triangle inequality in goal-conditioned RL for value learning have not successfully scaled to complex, high-dimensional robotic environments without additional planning.

## Method

- Develops ==Transitive RL (TRL)==, an algorithm leveraging a divide-and-conquer approach to value learning, specifically designed for offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning.
- Employs a ==transitive Bellman update rule== that uses ==soft expectile regression== to approximate maximization, and critically restricts intermediate subgoals to those observed within the training trajectories.
- Utilizes ==distance-based re-weighting== to stabilize training by prioritizing the learning of values for shorter trajectory segments.

## Results

- TRL demonstrates state-of-the-art performance on demanding long-horizon robotic tasks like `humanoidmaze-giant` and `puzzle-4x6`, outperforming previous TD-based, MC-based, and other GCRL methods.
- The algorithm achieves the best average performance across **10** standard OGBench environments and **50** evaluation tasks, showing strong general competitive capability.
- Key components like expectile regression (with κ > **0.5**), restricting subgoals to in-trajectory states, and distance-based re-weighting were experimentally validated as crucial for TRL's stable and high performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A divide-and-conquer approach to value learning can fundamentally address the "curse of horizon" by reducing recursive dependencies from O(T) to O(log T) steps, combining benefits of TD and MC methods.
> - Preventing value overestimation is critical for scaling transitive value updates; this is effectively achieved by restricting intermediate subgoals to only those present within observed dataset trajectories.
> - The inherent triangle inequality property of goal-conditioned reinforcement learning can be practically leveraged for complex tasks through careful algorithmic design, specifically using expectile regression and in-trajectory subgoals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in off-policy reinforcement learning (RL) known as the "curse of horizon," which refers to the accumulation of biases in value functions over long horizons due to recursive temporal difference (TD) updates. Standard off-policy RL algorithms typically rely on TD learning with Bellman backups, where each update involves regression toward a potentially biased target value. This bias accumulates over the policy's effective horizon, hindering the application of off-policy RL to complex, long-horizon tasks.

Existing approaches to mitigate this issue include mixing Monte Carlo (MC) returns, either for short trajectory chunks (n-step returns) or full trajectories. While n-step returns can balance the bias-variance trade-off, they only reduce the effective horizon length by a constant factor and require careful tuning of the `n` parameter. Purely MC-based methods, conversely, suffer from high variance, particularly for long horizons.

This paper proposes a new paradigm, "divide-and-conquer," as a potential solution to this dilemma. The authors hypothesize that such an approach could logarithmically reduce the number of biased recursions, unlike TD methods (O(T) recursions), while avoiding the high variance of MC methods by performing dynamic programming.

The specific focus of this work is offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL), a subset of RL problems where the objective is to learn a policy that can reach any state from any other state in the minimum number of steps. GCRL problems exhibit a shortest-path-like structure that naturally satisfies a triangle inequality, making them amenable to a divide-and-conquer approach. Previous GCRL methods either extend general offline RL algorithms or leverage specific structures like quasimetric learning or hierarchical policies. Some prior works have utilized the triangle inequality in GCRL, categorized into "hard" approaches (imposing strict architectural constraints) or "soft" approaches (using it for value backups). However, soft approaches have generally not scaled beyond simple 2D toy environments without additional planning mechanisms, often due to challenges in mitigating value overestimation when applying the triangle inequality with function approximation. This work aims to develop a practical, scalable soft triangle inequality-based method for long-horizon robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an off-policy value learning algorithm that scales effectively to arbitrarily long-horizon tasks by mitigating the "curse of horizon," a significant challenge in off-policy RL. This challenge stems from the accumulation of biases in value function estimates during recursive temporal difference (TD) updates, where each Bellman update introduces a regression towards a biased target value. While n-step TD and Monte Carlo (MC) methods offer partial solutions by reducing bias or variance, respectively, they do not fundamentally resolve the issue: n-step TD only reduces the horizon by a constant factor and requires careful tuning, while MC methods suffer from high variance in long horizons.

The central hypothesis is that a "divide-and-conquer" approach could provide a more robust solution. Such an algorithm would incrementally learn values by combining two equal-sized trajectory segments into a larger one. Theoretically, this paradigm could reduce the number of biased recursions to O(log T) for a length-T trajectory, offering a significant advantage over TD-based methods (O(T) recursions). Simultaneously, by performing dynamic programming (aggregating intermediate values), it aims to avoid the high variance typically associated with MC-based methods.

As an initial step to validate this hypothesis, the authors focus on offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL). GCRL tasks, which involve finding shortest paths between states, naturally possess a triangle inequality structure for temporal distances. This structure, expressed as $d^*(s, g) \le d^*(s, w) + d^*(w, g)$, directly translates into a multiplicative relationship for optimal value functions: $V^*(s, g) \ge V^*(s, w)V^*(w, g)$. This property makes GCRL a suitable domain for developing a divide-and-conquer value learning algorithm.

The main motivation is to convert this theoretical triangle inequality structure into a practical, stable, and scalable value update rule for offline settings, specifically for long-horizon goal-reaching tasks. Prior attempts to leverage the soft triangle inequality for value backups have struggled with value overestimation under function approximation and typically failed to scale beyond simple toy domains. Therefore, a key motivation is to introduce novel techniques to address these scaling challenges, such as managing the maximization over subgoals in a high-dimensional state space and preventing detrimental value overestimation in the offline context.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Transitive Reinforcement Learning (TRL), is a divide-and-conquer algorithm designed for offline goal-conditioned RL (GCRL) in deterministic environments, aiming to learn a policy that minimizes the number of steps to reach any goal.

**Problem Setting:**
The work considers a controlled Markov process defined by states ($S$), actions ($A$), and deterministic transition dynamics ($p(s' | s, a)$). The input is an unlabeled dataset $D$ of reward-free trajectories $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, \dots, s_T)$. The objective is to learn a goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a | s, g)$ that maximizes the hitting-time-based value function $V^\pi(s, g)$, defined as $\gamma^{d^*(s,g)}$ in deterministic environments, where $d^*(s,g)$ is the minimum number of steps to reach $g$ from $s$.

**Theoretical Foundation (Triangle Inequality):**
The core of TRL is the exploitation of the triangle inequality inherent in goal-conditioned temporal distances: $d^*(s, g) \le d^*(s, w) + d^*(w, g)$ for any intermediate state $w$. This directly implies a multiplicative relationship for the optimal value function: $V^*(s, g) \ge V^*(s, w)V^*(w, g)$. This property motivates a transitive Bellman update rule for a value function $V(s, g)$:
$V(s, g) \leftarrow \max_{w \in S} V(s, w)V(w, g)$ (with base cases for $s=g$ and single-step transitions).
A similar update rule is derived for an action-value function $Q(s, a, g)$:
$Q(s, a, g) \leftarrow \max_{w \in S, a' \in A} Q(s, a, w)Q(w, a', g)$ (with base cases).
This recursive structure, akin to the Floyd-Warshall algorithm, can theoretically lead to O(log T) recursions, offering a potential advantage over O(T) recursions in TD learning and mitigating high variance issues of MC methods through dynamic programming.

**The Challenge of Practical Implementation:**
Directly implementing the $\max_{w \in S}$ operator in the transitive Bellman update is challenging with function approximation. Maximizing over a vast state space can lead to catastrophic value overestimation by exploiting positively biased estimation errors from individual subgoals, especially in the offline setting where out-of-distribution values are a concern. Previous attempts to address this, such as using target networks or separate generator networks for subgoals, have not scaled effectively to complex robotic tasks.

**TRL's Key Ideas to Address the Challenge:**
TRL introduces two modifications to make the transitive Bellman update practical:

1. **Soft Expectile Regression:** The strict $\max$ operator is replaced with soft expectile regression. Conceptually, the method minimizes $E[\ell^2_\kappa(V(s, g) - \bar{V}(s, w)\bar{V}(w, g))]$, where $\ell^2_\kappa$ is the expectile loss with parameter $\kappa \in [0.5, 1)$ and $\bar{V}$ is a target network. This allows approximating the maximum without explicit iteration over states, controlling for overestimation.

2. **In-Trajectory Subgoals (Behavioral Subgoals):** Instead of considering arbitrary states as subgoals, TRL restricts potential subgoals $w$ to be intermediate states $s_k$ within the same dataset trajectory $\tau=(s_i, \dots, s_j)$, such that $i \le k \le j$. This "in-sample maximization using only in-trajectory subgoals" is crucial for stability. Restricting to behavioral subgoals ensures that selected subgoals are relevant and "valid," preventing the value function from exploiting out-of-distribution or spurious subgoal values, which would require an excessively aggressive maximization otherwise.

**Practical Recipe (TRL Algorithm):**
TRL trains a goal-conditioned action-value function $Q(s, a, g)$ based on the Q-based transitive Bellman operator and extracts a goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a | s, g)$.

- **Value Learning:**
 - **Distance-based Re-weighting:** To prioritize accurate learning of shorter trajectory chunks, TRL weighs the loss for each sample $(s_i, s_j)$ by $w(s_i, s_j) := 1/(1 + \log_\gamma Q(s_i, a_i, s_j))^\lambda$. A positive $\lambda$ makes the algorithm focus more on shorter estimated distances, conceptually analogous to dynamic programming solving smaller subproblems first.
 - **Final Value Loss:** The value loss $L_{\text{TRL}}(Q)$ combines these elements. It is an expectile variant of a chosen loss function (e.g., binary cross-entropy, BCE), applied to the difference between $Q(s_i, a_i, s_j)$ and the target $\bar{Q}(s_i, a_i, s_k)\bar{Q}(s_k, a_k, s_j)$, with distance-based re-weighting. Base cases ($k-i \le 1$ or $j-k \le 1$) are handled by replacing $\bar{Q}$ terms with $\gamma^{length}$.
 - **Random Subgoal Sampling:** For a trajectory chunk from $s_i$ to $s_j$, an intermediate subgoal $s_k$ is sampled uniformly from $\{s_{i+1}, \dots, s_{j-1}\}$. It is theoretically proven that this random sampling variant still leads to logarithmic dependency on the horizon (O(log T) expected maximum Bellman recursions).

- **Policy Extraction:** TRL supports two standard policy extraction methods after value learning:
 - **Reparameterized gradients:** Maximizes a "DDPG+BC" objective $Q(s, a_\pi, g) + \alpha \log \pi(a | s, g)$, balancing value maximization with behavioral regularization.
 - **Rejection sampling:** Defines the policy as the $\arg\max$ of behavioral action samples that maximize the value function, typically using expressive generative models for the behavioral policy.

- **Oracle Distillation (for Oracle Representations):** For environments providing oracle goal representations (e.g., x-y coordinates in a maze), TRL employs "oracle distillation." An additional Q-function $Q_\phi(s, a, \phi(g))$ is trained by distilling from the original $Q(s, a, g)$ function, which leverages the triangle inequality on the full state space. The policy is then extracted from $Q_\phi$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TRL focused on its horizon scalability and performance on various offline GCRL benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Horizon Scalability on Long-Horizon Tasks

TRL's divide-and-conquer value learning was compared against standard TD, n-step TD, and Monte Carlo (MC) approaches on three highly complex, long-horizon environments: `humanoidmaze-giant`, `puzzle-4x5`, and `puzzle-4x6`. These tasks involved horizons up to 4000 steps and utilized 1B-sized datasets.

- **Superior Performance:** TRL achieved the highest success rates across all three environments, outperforming both TD and MC-based approaches. For instance, on `humanoidmaze-giant`, TRL achieved an overall success rate of 79%, matching the best TD-n baseline. On `puzzle-4x5`, TRL achieved 97% success, significantly higher than the 47% of MC and 19% of TD-n.
- **Insensitivity to `n`:** Unlike n-step TD, which required careful and task-dependent tuning of the `n` parameter for optimal performance, TRL achieved strong results without needing to set such a parameter. This highlights a practical advantage of the divide-and-conquer framework.
- **Outperformance of Other GCRL Methods:** TRL also significantly outperformed other existing offline GCRL algorithms, including FBC, IQL, CRL, SAC+BC, and other triangle inequality-based methods like QRL, TDP, and COE, on these challenging tasks. TRL was the only triangle inequality-based method to achieve non-trivial performance.

#### 4.2. Performance on Standard OGBench Tasks

TRL's performance was evaluated against a suite of 10 standard OGBench goal-reaching tasks, which include robotic maze navigation, ball control, object manipulation, and puzzle solving, using `oraclerep` variants and standard datasets.

- **Strong General Performance:** TRL demonstrated strong average performance across the OGBench tasks, achieving the best overall success rate. While the performance gap between TRL and the second-best method (IQL) was narrower compared to the long-horizon tasks, this was attributed to TRL's primary advantages being in long-horizon settings.
- **Scalability of Triangle Inequality:** TRL was the only algorithm leveraging the triangle inequality that achieved strong, competitive performance on these diverse robotic tasks, differentiating it from prior soft triangle inequality methods (TDP, COE) that generally struggled.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies investigated the impact of TRL's key components:

- **Expectile $\kappa$:** Increasing $\kappa$ (making the expectile regression more aggressive) was found to be crucial for strong performance on some highly complex tasks like `humanoidmaze-giant`. However, on simpler tasks like `humanoidmaze-medium` and `scene-play`, $\kappa=0.5$ (behavioral regression) also yielded good results, akin to one-step RL efficacy.
- **Subgoal Distributions:** Restricting subgoals to "in-trajectory" states was found to be critical. Using "random" subgoals (arbitrary states from the dataset) substantially degraded performance across tested environments (`humanoidmaze-medium`, `scene-play`, `puzzle-4x4`), underscoring the importance of behavioral subgoals for stable training and avoiding overestimation.
- **Distance-based Re-weighting Factor $\lambda$:** Enabling distance-based re-weighting (i.e., $\lambda > 0$, focusing on shorter trajectory chunks) generally led to better performance across tasks like `cube-double`, `scene-play`, and `puzzle-4x4`.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents Transitive RL (TRL) as a practical, divide-and-conquer value learning algorithm, demonstrating its efficacy in offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL). Its primary significance lies in providing a viable method to address the "curse of horizon," a long-standing challenge in off-policy RL that limits scalability to complex, long-horizon tasks.

The key contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Horizon Scalability:** TRL's ability to reduce the number of biased Bellman recursions to O(log T) (theoretically) provides a fundamental advantage over traditional TD-based methods (O(T)). The experimental results on highly challenging long-horizon robotic tasks confirm this, showing TRL consistently outperforms or matches the best-tuned TD and MC baselines without requiring task-specific parameter tuning like `n` in n-step TD. This positions TRL as a step towards more inherently scalable value learning algorithms.
- **Practical Implementation of Triangle Inequality:** While the triangle inequality has been recognized as a valuable structure in GCRL, prior "soft" approaches struggled with value overestimation under function approximation, limiting their real-world applicability. TRL's innovations—namely, the use of soft expectile regression and the restriction to in-trajectory (behavioral) subgoals—provide a robust mechanism to stabilize training and scale this paradigm to complex, continuous-control robotic domains with large datasets. This success opens the door for further exploration of metric-like structures in RL.
- **Reduced Bias Accumulation and Variance:** By combining intermediate values through dynamic programming and limiting the recursive depth, TRL offers a principled approach to mitigate both the bias accumulation of TD learning and the high variance of Monte Carlo methods. This favorable bias-variance profile is critical for learning in long-horizon scenarios.
- **Benchmarking and Future Research:** TRL's strong performance on both long-horizon and standard OGBench tasks establishes a new benchmark for offline GCRL, particularly for methods leveraging metric properties. The work also highlights important open questions for future research:
 - **Stochastic Environments:** Extending divide-and-conquer value learning to stochastic environments remains an open challenge, as TRL (like many other triangle inequality-based GCRL methods) primarily operates under a deterministic environment assumption. The paper suggests successor temporal distance frameworks could offer insights.
 - **General Reward-based RL:** The application of divide-and-conquer beyond goal-conditioned RL to general reward-based tasks is another significant area for future exploration.

Overall, TRL represents a notable advancement in off-policy RL by presenting a practical, divide-and-conquer value learning strategy that demonstrates superior horizon scalability. It provides affirmative evidence for the hypothesis that such algorithms can lead to value learning methods less susceptible to the "curse of horizon," thereby facilitating progress toward more scalable and robust reinforcement learning agents.
