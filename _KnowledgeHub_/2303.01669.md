# Learning Common Rationale to Improve Self-Supervised Representation for Fine-Grained Visual Recognition Problems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Adelaide devised a GradCAM Fitting Branch (GFB) to learn common discriminative patterns for self-supervised fine-grained visual recognition (FGVR). This mechanism guides feature extraction towards relevant object parts at inference, achieving up to 3.33% Top-1 classification accuracy improvement and 6.97% Rank-1 retrieval accuracy improvement over MoCo v2 on FGVR benchmarks.

## Problem

- Existing self-supervised learning (SSL) methods exhibit a 'coarse-grained bias,' learning features often influenced by background or non-discriminative object parts, which limits their effectiveness for fine-grained visual recognition (FGVR).
- FGVR tasks necessitate capturing subtle, localized discriminative patterns to distinguish visually similar subcategories, a requirement not inherently met by general SSL objectives.
- Previous approaches to guide SSL for FGVR typically rely on external mechanisms like pre-trained part detectors or saliency maps, introducing dependencies or assumptions that may not universally hold.

## Method

- The method introduces a **GradCAM Fitting Branch (GFB)** that operates in conjunction with a ==contrastive self-supervised learning== framework (e.g., ==MoCo v2==), learning common discriminative patterns from self-supervised ==GradCAM== signals.
- The **GFB**, a low-capacity convolutional branch, is trained using a ==KL divergence loss== to align its generated attention map with the ==GradCAM== map derived from the contrastive loss.
- At inference time, the **GFB**'s learned attention mask is used to perform ==weighted average pooling== on the backbone's feature map, thereby focusing the final representation on discriminative regions without altering the SSL training process.

## Results

- The proposed method consistently outperformed the MoCo v2 baseline, achieving up to **3.33%** higher Top-1 classification accuracy (e.g., **55.87%** on FGVC Aircraft) and up to **6.97%** higher Rank-1 retrieval accuracy (e.g., **49.69%** on CUB-200-2011).
- It demonstrated superior Top-1 classification performance across CUB-200-2011 (**71.31%**), Stanford Cars (**60.75%**), and FGVC Aircraft (**55.87%**) compared to a range of state-of-the-art SSL methods.
- GradCAM visualizations confirmed that the method effectively focused on foreground and potentially key object parts, unlike baseline SSL models that often activated on background context.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standard self-supervised learning methods exhibit a 'coarse-grained bias' that hinders performance on fine-grained visual recognition by failing to focus on subtle, discriminative object parts.
> - A dedicated, low-capacity branch can effectively learn 'common rationales' by fitting to self-supervised GradCAMs, which naturally highlight regions contributing to instance discrimination.
> - Applying the learned attention guidance only during inference (via weighted pooling) is crucial; introducing it during the self-supervised training phase can destabilize the feature learning process and lead to model collapse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a prominent paradigm for learning robust feature representations from unlabeled data, demonstrating considerable performance across various computer vision tasks. Methods like contrastive learning (e.g., MoCo, SimCLR) have achieved state-of-the-art results in general object recognition by learning to distinguish between different instances and bringing similar views of the same instance closer in the embedding space.

However, existing research, corroborated by the authors' preliminary investigations, suggests that these general-purpose SSL strategies may exhibit a "coarse-grained bias." This bias implies that features learned through standard SSL objectives might not be optimally suited for fine-grained visual recognition (FGVR) problems. FGVR tasks require distinguishing between visually similar subcategories of objects (e.g., different bird species or car models), often relying on subtle discriminative patterns located in specific, key parts of an object rather than broad object-level features or background cues. Standard SSL objectives, by contrast, tend to learn any visual pattern that helps minimize the pretext task loss, which can include background elements or less discriminative object regions.

Previous efforts to address this limitation typically involve external mechanisms such as pre-trained object part detectors or saliency detectors to guide SSL towards more relevant regions. However, these approaches introduce dependencies on external annotations or assumptions that salient regions always coincide with discriminative regions, which may not hold universally, especially for FGVR. This work situates itself within the landscape of improving SSL's applicability to FGVR by proposing an intrinsic mechanism that learns to identify common discriminative patterns directly from the target domain data, without external detectors or annotations. It builds upon ideas from attention mechanisms and interpretability tools like GradCAM, adapting them for an unsupervised FGVR context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of self-supervised learning for fine-grained visual recognition problems. This is motivated by the observed "coarse-grained bias" in existing SSL methods, which hinders their ability to learn representations that capture the subtle, discriminative patterns crucial for distinguishing visually similar subcategories in FGVR tasks.

The key motivations are:
- **Addressing the "Coarse-Grained Bias":** Standard SSL objectives, such as contrastive learning, aim to maximize agreement between augmented views of the same instance and push away features from different instances. While effective for general recognition, this can lead models to focus on easily discernible but non-discriminative features (e.g., background context) rather than the specific, fine-grained details (e.g., unique markings on a bird's head).
- **Improving Feature Discriminability for FGVR:** The discriminative patterns for FGVR are often subtle and localized, residing in key object parts. Current SSL methods often learn patterns from irrelevant regions, as illustrated by GradCAM visualizations that show activation on background patterns. The objective is to guide the SSL process to focus on these crucial fine-grained details.
- **Developing an Intrinsic, Annotation-Free Mechanism:** Prior approaches often rely on external supervision (e.g., human-annotated object parts) or pre-trained saliency detectors to guide feature learning to relevant regions. The authors are motivated to develop a method that can identify these discriminative clues directly from the unlabeled target domain data, thereby maintaining the core self-supervised principle and avoiding dependencies on potentially unreliable external information.
- **Learning "Common Rationales":** The central hypothesis is that discriminative patterns consistently observed across instances and classes—referred to as "common rationales"—are more likely to correspond to important object parts in FGVR. The motivation is to devise a mechanism that can learn and leverage these common rationales to screen out less relevant features.
- **Seamless Integration with Existing SSL:** The goal is to propose a mechanism that can be seamlessly integrated with existing SSL frameworks without significantly altering their core architecture or increasing computational complexity, thus making it broadly applicable.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to produce self-supervised representations that are inherently more suited for FGVR tasks, leading to improved performance in downstream applications like classification and image retrieval.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a "fitting and masking" procedure that operates in conjunction with an existing self-supervised contrastive learning framework, specifically MoCo v2. The core idea is to learn an additional screening mechanism, termed the GradCAM Fitting Branch (GFB), to identify common discriminative patterns.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Self-Supervised Contrastive Learning and GradCAM

- **Self-Supervised Contrastive Learning (MoCo v2):** The method is built upon MoCo v2, where two augmented views of an image, `x` and `x'`, are generated. An encoder `f_θ` and projector heads `g_θ` produce embeddings `z` and `z'`. The objective is to minimize a contrastive loss (`L_CL`) that maximizes agreement between positive pairs (from the same image) and minimizes agreement with negative pairs (from different images stored in a memory queue).
- **Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GradCAM):** GradCAM is typically used in supervised settings to visualize important regions for a specific class prediction by computing gradients of the loss with respect to feature maps of the last convolutional layer. The authors adapt GradCAM for SSL by replacing the cross-entropy loss with the contrastive loss (`L_CL`), calculating `[G]_i,j` as `ReLU( (∂L_CL / ∂[ϕ(I)]_i,j)ᵀ[ϕ(I)]_i,j )`, where `ϕ(I)` is the last convolutional layer's feature map. This `[G]_i,j` now indicates the contribution of each region to the instance discrimination task.

#### 3.2. Learning Common Rationale

The methodology revolves around an additional branch designed to capture common rationales:

- **Architecture of the GradCAM Fitting Branch (GFB):**
 - The GFB takes the last convolutional layer's feature map `ϕ(I)` as input.
 - It consists of a convolutional layer with `K` filters, each having a `1 × 1` kernel size.
 - This is followed by a channel-wise max-out operation: `A_i,j = max_k {w_kᵀ[ϕ(I)]_i,j }`, where `w_k` is the `k`-th filter.
 - This design is inspired by previous work and is chosen for its limited fitting capacity. The rationale is that a branch with constrained capacity will tend to learn only the most frequently occurring and robust discriminative patterns (the "common rationales") rather than overfitting to every detail of the GradCAM, including less relevant ones.

- **Training Loss:**
 - The overall training objective combines the standard contrastive loss (`L_CL`) and a new GradCAM fitting loss (`L_KL`).
 - The `L_KL` loss encourages the GFB's output attention map (`A`) to be similar to the GradCAM map (`G`) derived from `L_CL`.
 - Both `G` and `A` are normalized into probability distributions using a softmax function with a temperature parameter `τ`.
 - `L_KL` is calculated as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the normalized `A` and `G`: `L_KL = Σ_i,j [Ā]_i,j log( [Ā]_i,j / [Ḡ]_i,j )`.
 - The final loss is `L = λL_CL + νL_KL`, where `λ` and `ν` are hyperparameters controlling the weight of each loss term.

- **Inference:**
 - At inference time, the GFB generates an attention mask `A`.
 - This attention mask is normalized: `A'_i,j = (A_i,j - min(A)) / (1e-7 + max(A))`.
 - Instead of global average pooling, weighted average pooling is performed on the last convolutional feature map `ϕ(I)` using the normalized attention mask: `f = Σ_i,j [A']_i,j [ϕ(I)]_i,j`. This produces the final image representation `f` for downstream tasks.

#### 3.3. Discussion and Variants Explored

The authors highlight several key differences from a similar supervised method (SAM):
- **Application Context:** SAM was for supervised FGVC in low-data regimes; this work is for self-supervised learning.
- **Cross-branch Interaction:** Unlike SAM, this method intentionally avoids any interaction between the feature learning branch and the GFB *during training*. Using the GFB's attention mask to weight features during SSL training was found to hinder the general feature learning process. Interaction is only applied at test time for weighted average pooling.
- **Feature Dimensionality:** SAM-bilinear uses bilinear pooling, leading to higher feature dimensionality (C*K). This method uses max-out over K projections, resulting in a single attention mask and preserving the original feature dimensionality (C).

To validate their design choices, the authors also investigate several baseline and variant architectures:
- **SAM-SSL:** An extension of SAM to SSL, using contrastive loss, with a single projection GFB and auxiliary GradCAM fitting.
- **SAM-SSL-Bilinear:** An extension of SAM-bilinear to SSL, using contrastive loss, with K projections and bilinear pooling interaction.
- **Ours-MultiTask:** A variant where GFB training is only an auxiliary task; no weighted average pooling is performed at test time.
- **Ours-DualPooling:** A variant where weighted average pooling using the GFB's attention mask is applied during *both* training and testing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive experimental results across various fine-grained visual recognition (FGVR) datasets and evaluation protocols to assess the effectiveness of the proposed method.

#### 4.1. Datasets and Evaluation Protocols

- **FGVR Datasets:** CUB-200-2011, Stanford Cars, FGVC-Aircraft, and the large-scale iNaturalist2019.
- **Evaluation Protocols:**
 - **Linear Probing:** A linear classifier is trained on top of frozen features extracted by the self-supervised model. Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy are reported.
 - **Image Retrieval:** The learned features are used to find images of the same category as a query. Rank-1, Rank-5, and mean Average Precision (mAP) are reported. This task is considered to have higher feature quality requirements without external supervision.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of the Proposed Method (Ours vs. MoCo v2 Baseline)

- **Significant Performance Improvement:** The proposed method consistently outperforms its baseline, MoCo v2.
 - On CUB-200-2011, it achieved 71.31% Top-1 accuracy (3.01% higher than MoCo v2) and 92.03% Top-5 accuracy (1.18% higher).
 - Similar improvements were observed on Stanford Cars (60.75% Top-1, 2.32% improvement) and FGVC Aircraft (55.87% Top-1, 3.33% improvement).
 - The advantage became more pronounced with reduced label proportions (e.g., 20%), suggesting the method learns less noisy and more generalizable features.
- **Improved Retrieval Performance:** For image retrieval, the method showed substantial gains.
 - On CUB-200-2011, rank-1 increased by 6.97% (to 49.69%), rank-5 by 5.52% (to 75.23%), and mAP by 4.33% (to 24.01%).
 - Similar boosts were observed on Stanford Cars and FGVC Aircraft.
 - On the large-scale iNaturalist2019 dataset, it also demonstrated better rank-1 retrieval accuracy across various supercategories compared to MoCo v2.
- **Visualization:** GradCAM visualizations (Figure 1) illustrated that MoCo v2 and VICReg tend to be distracted by background patterns, whereas the proposed method effectively identified features from the foreground and potentially key object parts.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Other SSL Frameworks

- When compared against a range of state-of-the-art SSL methods (DINO, SimCLR, BYOL, SimSiam, BarlowTwins, VICReg) using ResNet-50 on FGVR datasets, the proposed method achieved the best Top-1 classification performance on CUB-200-2011 (71.31%), Stanford Cars (60.75%), and FGVC Aircraft (55.87%).
- It maintained competitive performance even with smaller batch sizes (e.g., 32), demonstrating robustness under computational constraints.
- In terms of training speed and GPU memory, it performed similarly to MoCo v2 and SimSiam, and more efficiently than SimCLR and BYOL, while outperforming methods like DINO, BarlowTwins, and VICReg in accuracy despite slightly higher resource usage.
- The method was also shown to be compatible with other SSL objectives, as "BYOL+Ours" consistently outperformed baseline BYOL.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Alternative Solutions and Ablation Studies

- **Comparison to SAM extensions (SAM-SSL, SAM-SSL-Bilinear):** The proposed method generally outperformed SAM-SSL and achieved comparable, sometimes superior, performance to SAM-SSL-Bilinear but with significantly lower feature dimensionality (C vs. C*K). This validated the proposed design choices regarding GFB interaction and feature aggregation.
- **Comparison to Localization-Aware SSL Methods (DiLo, CVSA, LEWEL, ContrastiveCrop):** The proposed method showed a clear advantage in classification and retrieval performance over recent localization-based SSL methods like DiLo, CVSA, and LEWEL. Combining "Ours+ContrastiveCrop" further improved performance (e.g., 72.84% Top-1 on CUB), highlighting its complementary nature.
- **Impact of Design Choices (Variants):**
 - **Ours-DualPooling:** Applying weighted average pooling during both training and testing led to complete model collapse, affirming the decision to only apply it at inference time.
 - **Ours-MultiTask:** Not performing weighted average pooling at test time (i.e., only using GFB as an auxiliary task) resulted in only minor improvements over the MoCo v2 baseline, indicating the importance of the weighted pooling step at inference.
- **Impact of Number of Projections (K):** Experiments on CUB-200-2011 showed that retrieval performance (rank-1) increased with K, peaking around K=32 (49.69%) and then slightly decreasing for larger K (e.g., 64). This suggests an optimal range for capturing common rationales without overfitting. The method was observed to be relatively insensitive to K within a reasonable range (4-64).
- **Alternative GFB Structure (MLP):** Replacing the max-out projections in the GFB with a two-layer MLP significantly reduced performance (e.g., rank-1 dropped from 34.56% to 25.57% on Stanford Cars), confirming the importance of the proposed GFB architecture with limited fitting capacity.
- **Impact of Loss Weights (λ, ν):** An ablation study on CUB-200-2011 indicated that setting `λ=1` and `ν=0.01` yielded the best retrieval rank-1 performance (49.69%).
- **Impact of Embedding Dimension:** Performance on CUB-200-2011 peaked at an embedding dimensionality of 256 for retrieval tasks, consistent with optimal choices for many SSL methods.
- **Understanding Projections:** Visualizations of high-variance projections in the GFB (Figure B1) showed them capturing meaningful object parts (e.g., bird heads, bodies), while low-variance projections focused on less relevant regions. Experiments selecting only high-variance projections ("Ours (high-variance K)") maintained strong performance, reinforcing their discriminative role.

#### 4.5. Performance on Non-fine-grained Dataset (CIFAR-100)

- The method also demonstrated an improvement on the non-fine-grained CIFAR-100 dataset, with a rank-1 retrieval accuracy of 27.14% compared to MoCo v2's 24.78%.
- However, the optimal K value was lower (K=8) for CIFAR-100 compared to FGVR datasets, and using an MLP as GFB showed less performance degradation compared to FGVR. This suggests potentially different characteristics for non-fine-grained data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of self-supervised learning, particularly in its application to fine-grained visual recognition.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Addressing a Critical Gap:** The work effectively addresses the identified limitation of existing SSL methods regarding their "coarse-grained bias" in FGVR tasks. By inherently guiding the model to focus on more discriminative, localized features, it makes SSL representations more suitable for challenging fine-grained distinctions.
- **Novelty of "Common Rationale" Concept:** The concept of "common rationales" and the proposed mechanism to learn them directly from SSL GradCAM signals is a significant contribution. It offers an elegant solution to the problem of attention guidance without relying on external supervision (like part annotations or pre-trained saliency detectors).
- **Simplicity and Efficiency:** The proposed GradCAM Fitting Branch is architecturally simple (a 1x1 conv layer with max-out) and integrates seamlessly into existing SSL frameworks (e.g., MoCo v2) with minimal overhead in terms of training time and memory footprint compared to the baseline.
- **Robustness and Generalizability:** The method demonstrates consistent and substantial performance gains across multiple diverse FGVR datasets and under various evaluation settings (classification, retrieval, varying label proportions). Its effectiveness even with reduced labeled data for linear probing suggests that it learns more robust and generalizable features.
- **Insights into Attention Guidance:** The ablation studies and visualizations provide valuable insights into how attention mechanisms can be intrinsically learned and leveraged in SSL to improve representation quality, particularly the importance of controlled fitting capacity and appropriate cross-branch interaction.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Advancement of SSL for Specialized Tasks:** This work can pave the way for more widespread and effective application of SSL in specialized domains beyond general object recognition, such as medical imaging, scientific image analysis, and quality control, where fine-grained distinctions are paramount.
- **Reduced Annotation Burden:** By enhancing the quality of self-supervised features for FGVR, the method could potentially reduce the need for extensive and costly human annotation in these complex domains, making the development of high-performance FGVR systems more accessible.
- **Improved Interpretability:** The use of a GradCAM-fitting mechanism and the visualization of "common rationales" could contribute to more interpretable self-supervised models, allowing researchers to better understand what features the models prioritize.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The findings, particularly regarding the optimal interaction between feature learning and attention guidance during training, and the different behaviors observed in non-fine-grained datasets, open new avenues for research into tailoring SSL for specific task characteristics. Future work could explore extending this approach to other SSL frameworks, different backbones, or investigating its applicability to other domains requiring localized feature extraction.

In conclusion, this research presents a practical and effective method to improve self-supervised learning for fine-grained visual recognition, offering a significant step towards more adaptable and robust self-supervised models.
