# Long-Tail Learning with Foundation Model: Heavy Fine-Tuning Hurts

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Nanjing University found that extensive fine-tuning of foundation models on long-tail datasets degrades performance on under-represented classes by distorting intra-class feature distributions. They propose LIFT (LIghtweight Fine-Tuning), a single-stage framework that uses structured lightweight fine-tuning, semantic-aware classifier initialization, and test-time ensembling to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy with significantly fewer parameters and faster convergence.

## Problem

- Machine learning models often struggle with long-tail datasets, where a small number of 'head' classes are well-represented, but many 'tail' classes have very few training examples, leading to poor generalization for rare classes.
- Existing fine-tuning methods for foundation models on long-tail tasks typically require extensive computational resources, long training times, multi-stage procedures, or reliance on external auxiliary data.
- The specific impact of different fine-tuning strategies on the performance of foundation models across head and tail classes in imbalanced datasets was not adequately understood.

## Method

- Developed **LIFT** (LIghtweight Fine-Tuning), a single-stage framework employing ==structured lightweight fine-tuning (SLF)== of a small, carefully selected subset of foundation model parameters (e.g., using ==AdaptFormer==) to reduce learnable parameters.
- Introduced ==Semantic-Aware Initialization (SAI)== for the classifier, leveraging the semantic knowledge from the foundation model's text encoder for robust initial weights.
- Combined an enhanced ==cosine classifier== with ==Logit-Adjusted (LA) loss== to mitigate biased predictions and incorporated ==Test-Time Ensembling (TTE)== to enhance generalization robustness.

## Results

- LIFT achieved state-of-the-art overall and tail-class accuracy on ImageNet-LT (**78.3%** overall, **73.4%** tail), Places-LT (**52.2%** overall, **50.9%** tail), and iNaturalist 2018 (**80.4%** overall, **82.2%** tail), surpassing previous methods, including those using external data.
- The framework operates with significantly fewer learnable parameters (e.g., **0.62M** for ImageNet-LT compared to **149.62M** for SOTA baselines) and converges rapidly, typically within **10-20 epochs**, leading to substantial computational efficiency.
- Ablation studies confirmed that each component of LIFT (Structured Lightweight Fine-tuning, Logit-Adjusted Loss, Semantic-Aware Initialization, and Test-Time Ensembling) contributes positively to its improved performance and efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - "Heavy fine-tuning hurts" tail-class performance in long-tail learning with foundation models, primarily by distorting intra-class feature distributions and creating inconsistent class conditions for tail classes.
> - Optimizing only a small, structured subset of foundation model parameters (lightweight fine-tuning) can effectively preserve consistent intra-class distributions while improving inter-class separability, leading to better tail-class performance.
> - Semantically informed initialization of the classifier using the foundation model's text modality provides a robust starting point that significantly improves generalization, particularly for data-scarce tail classes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of machine learning, especially in real-world applications, frequently encounters **long-tail learning** scenarios. This refers to datasets where a small number of "head" classes are abundantly represented, while a large number of "tail" classes have very few training samples. This imbalance severely hampers the generalization performance of standard machine learning models, leading to poor accuracy on the under-represented tail classes. Traditionally, approaches to mitigate this issue have fallen into three categories:
1. **Data Manipulation:** Techniques like re-sampling (oversampling minority classes, undersampling majority classes) or data augmentation (e.g., mixup, cutmix) to balance the effective number of samples per class.
2. **Representation Learning:** Methods that aim to learn more robust and discriminative features that are less biased towards head classes, often involving contrastive learning or knowledge distillation.
3. **Model Output Adjustment:** Modifying the loss functions (e.g., label-distribution-aware margin loss, focal loss) or post-hoc logit adjustments to recalibrate predictions and account for class imbalance.

With the advent of **foundation models** (FMs) such as Vision Transformers (ViT) and especially vision-language models like CLIP, a new paradigm for long-tail learning has emerged. These models, pre-trained on vast and diverse datasets, possess remarkable generalization capabilities and robust representations. Researchers have begun exploring fine-tuning FMs for long-tail tasks, demonstrating that this can significantly improve performance. However, existing fine-tuning strategies often involve extensive computational resources, long training times, multi-stage procedures, or reliance on external auxiliary data.

This paper critically intervenes in this emerging paradigm. While prior works acknowledge the potential of fine-tuning FMs for long-tail learning, they largely overlook *how* different fine-tuning strategies specifically impact performance across head and tail classes. This work explicitly identifies and quantifies a counter-intuitive phenomenon: **heavy fine-tuning, despite its benefits for overall performance or head classes, can actually harm the performance on tail classes.** This finding challenges the prevailing "more fine-tuning is better" assumption in the context of imbalanced data. By demonstrating that "heavy fine-tuning hurts" and that "lightweight fine-tuning is more effective," the paper shifts the focus towards more efficient and targeted adaptation strategies for FMs in long-tail scenarios. It provides a theoretical and empirical basis for rethinking fine-tuning practices in this crucial domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind this research are multi-fold:

1. **Quantify the Impact of Fine-Tuning on Long-Tail Learning:** The authors aimed to rigorously investigate and quantify how different levels of fine-tuning (especially "heavy" fine-tuning) affect model performance, particularly distinguishing between head and tail classes in long-tail datasets.
2. **Uncover the Mechanism of Performance Deterioration:** Beyond merely observing that heavy fine-tuning hurts tail classes, a key objective was to understand *why* this occurs. The paper hypothesizes and later demonstrates that this deterioration is attributable to **inconsistent class conditions caused by heavy fine-tuning**, specifically through the distortion of intra-class distance distributions.
3. **Develop an Efficient and Accurate Long-Tail Learning Algorithm:** Based on their observations and theoretical understanding, the authors sought to design a novel, low-complexity algorithm that leverages the strengths of foundation models while mitigating the identified drawbacks of heavy fine-tuning. This algorithm, named **LIFT (LIghtweight Fine-Tuning)**, aims to achieve superior predictive performance on tail classes, facilitate fast prediction, and result in compact models.
4. **Promote Resource-Efficient Foundation Model Utilization:** A significant practical motivation was to overcome the computational and data overheads associated with current fine-tuning practices (e.g., long training epochs, two-staged procedures, external training datasets). LIFT aims to be a single-staged framework that converges rapidly without requiring external data.

In essence, the paper is motivated by a critical gap in understanding how foundation models interact with long-tail data during fine-tuning. By exposing the negative consequences of heavy fine-tuning and proposing an efficient, principled alternative, the authors aim to make foundation models more practically deployable and robust for challenging real-world imbalanced datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this paper is characterized by a combination of empirical observation, theoretical analysis, and the development of a novel algorithm, LIFT, built upon these insights.

**Core Observation and Theoretical Analysis:**
The central discovery is that "heavy fine-tuning hurts" tail-class performance. The authors demonstrate this empirically (Figure 3a) and then delve into the underlying reasons:
- **Distortion of Intra-Class Distributions:** Full fine-tuning, while making features more discriminative by reducing inter-class similarities to near zero, simultaneously distorts the intra-class distributions. This creates a distribution shift between training and testing data for tail classes (Figure 3c).
- **Inconsistent Class Conditions:** This distortion leads to an inconsistency in class-conditional distributions between source (training) and target (test) domains.
- **Biased Predictions:** Proposition 3.1 and its proof (Appendix B) theoretically establish that such underestimated class-conditional probabilities lead to an underestimated loss for tail classes, consequently causing biased optimization towards other classes and performance deterioration.
- **Overfitting:** Full fine-tuning is also shown to cause severe overfitting on long-tail datasets, particularly for tail classes (Table 8 in Appendix C).

**The LIFT Algorithm (LIghtweight Fine-Tuning):**
Based on the insight that optimizing a small proportion of pre-trained weights can enhance discriminative capacity while preserving intra-class distributions, LIFT is developed with three key components:

1. **Lightweight Fine-Tuning (SLF - Structured Lightweight Fine-tuning):**
 - **Core Idea:** Instead of updating all parameters (heavy fine-tuning) or arbitrarily selected ones, LIFT advocates for fine-tuning only a *small, structured subset* of the foundation model's parameters. This drastically reduces the number of learnable parameters.
 - **Empirical Validation:** Initial experiments show that even *arbitrary* lightweight fine-tuning (e.g., 0.1%-2% of parameters) yields substantial performance improvements, preserving intra-class distributions while enhancing separability (Figure 4a, 4b, Table 6).
 - **Integration of PEFT Techniques:** LIFT is designed as a versatile framework that can integrate various existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, such as BitFit, Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT-shallow/deep), Adapter, LoRA, and AdaptFormer. The paper specifically highlights AdaptFormer as achieving the best empirical performance (Table 6). These methods introduce small, task-specific modules or modify only bias terms, keeping the vast majority of the pre-trained backbone frozen.
 - **Parameter Constraint:** LIFT carefully configures these modules (e.g., by setting bottleneck dimension `r = 2^floor(log2(K/2L))` for Adapter/AdaptFormer) to ensure that the total learnable parameters are often even fewer than those of the classifier itself.
 - **Classifier Design:** An **enhanced cosine classifier** ($z_j = \sigma \cdot \frac{w_j^\top f}{\|w_j\|_2 \|f\|_2}$) is used to mitigate biased predictions arising from imbalanced weight norms, leveraging the cosine distance optimization inherent in CLIP. This is combined with the **Logit-Adjusted (LA) loss** (Equation 5), which is theoretically optimal for long-tail learning under certain assumptions and proven effective in this context (Appendix B).

2. **Semantic-Aware Initialization (SAI):**
 - **Motivation:** Standard random initialization or class-mean feature initialization for the classifier can be problematic due to limited tail-class data or negative impacts on fine-tuning.
 - **Approach:** LIFT leverages the semantic knowledge embedded in CLIP's text modality. Hand-crafted textual prompts (e.g., "a photo of a [CLASS].") are fed through the CLIP text encoder to generate initial classifier weights. This provides a robust, semantically informed starting point with minimal computational overhead, as the text encoder is discarded thereafter.

3. **Test-Time Ensembling (TTE):**
 - **Motivation:** Transformer-based models, by dividing images into patches, can segment continuous patterns, impacting generalization robustness.
 - **Approach:** During inference, LIFT aggregates predictions from multiple perturbed versions of the input image (e.g., center crop, top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right crops from a slightly expanded image). This simple technique helps mitigate biases introduced by specific image cropping and further enhances generalization (Algorithm 1 in Appendix E).

**Overall Approach:** LIFT is designed as a **single-staged framework** that achieves rapid convergence (fewer than 20 training epochs) without requiring external training data, thereby offering significant practical advantages in terms of computational efficiency and resource requirements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical studies conducted on four widely used long-tail benchmark datasets (ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, iNaturalist 2018, and CIFAR-100-LT) robustly support the paper's claims and demonstrate the efficacy of LIFT.

#### 4.1. Heavy Fine-Tuning Hurts Tail Classes

- **Empirical Evidence:** Figure 3a clearly shows that full fine-tuning improves head-class accuracy but significantly *decreases* tail-class accuracy compared to classifier-only fine-tuning. This deterioration leads to a lower overall accuracy.
- **Underlying Cause:** Visualizations (Figure 3c) confirm that full fine-tuning distorts intra-class distributions for tail classes, creating a mismatch between training and test data. This inconsistency, coupled with severe overfitting on tail classes (Table 8), leads to biased predictions.

#### 4.2. Lightweight Fine-Tuning Helps

- **Superior Performance:** Figure 4a demonstrates that fine-tuning only a small proportion (e.g., 0.1%-2%) of all parameters yields superior performance compared to heavier fine-tuning, which often degrades performance.
- **Preserved Distributions:** Lightweight fine-tuning (both arbitrary and structured) effectively optimizes inter-class similarities while preserving consistent intra-class distributions between training and test data for tail classes (Figure 4b, 4c, and Appendix D, Figures 7 and 8).

#### 4.3. LIFT Outperforms State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Methods

- **ImageNet-LT (Table 1):** LIFT (with TTE) achieves 78.3% overall accuracy, surpassing the previous best non-external-data method (BALLAD at 75.7%) by 2.6%. It even outperforms methods using extensive auxiliary data like VL-LTR (77.2%) and GML (78.0%). Crucially, it does this with vastly fewer learnable parameters (0.62M vs. 149.62M for BALLAD/VL-LTR) and fewer training epochs (10 vs. 50+10 for BALLAD or 100 for VL-LTR). Tail-class accuracy shows a significant improvement (73.4% for LIFT vs. 69.8% for BALLAD).
- **Places-LT (Table 2):** LIFT (with TTE) achieves 52.2% overall accuracy, outperforming LPT (50.1%) and methods with external data like VL-LTR (50.1%) and RAC (47.2%) by significant margins. Again, it uses minimal parameters (0.18M) and epochs (10). Tail-class accuracy is notably boosted from 46.9% (LPT) to 50.9% (LIFT).
- **iNaturalist 2018 (Table 3):** On this challenging fine-grained dataset, LIFT (with TTE) achieves 80.4% overall accuracy, making it the best performing method, outperforming LPT (76.1%) and VL-LTR (76.8%). Its tail-class accuracy (82.2%) is particularly strong.
- **CIFAR-100-LT (Table 4):** LIFT also demonstrates superior performance across various imbalance ratios (100, 50, 10), consistently outperforming other fine-tuning and training-from-scratch methods. When pre-trained on ImageNet-21K (which has some class overlap with CIFAR-100), LIFT matches or slightly exceeds LPT with far fewer epochs and parameters.

#### 4.4. Efficiency and Convergence

- **Rapid Convergence:** LIFT exhibits remarkably fast convergence, typically within 10-20 training epochs (Figure 5). This is a significant improvement over many SOTA methods that require 90-400 epochs or two-stage procedures.
- **Compact Models:** The use of lightweight fine-tuning drastically reduces the number of learnable parameters, making LIFT highly memory-efficient.

#### 4.5. Contributions of Individual Components (Ablation Studies - Table 5)

- **Structured Lightweight Fine-tuning (SLF):** Significantly enhances performance on both head and tail classes.
- **Logit-Adjusted (LA) Loss:** Crucial for preventing predictions from being biased towards head classes.
- **Semantic-Aware Initialization (SAI):** Consistently improves generalization, especially for tail classes, by providing a robust starting point.
- **Test-Time Ensembling (TTE):** Provides an additional boost in performance across all class splits by enhancing generalization robustness.

#### 4.6. Generalizability and Robustness

- **Variety of PEFT Methods:** LIFT's framework is shown to be compatible with various structured lightweight fine-tuning methods, with AdaptFormer performing best (Table 6).
- **Classifier Initialization:** SAI outperforms random initialization and linear probing methods (Table 7).
- **Backbone Adaptability:** LIFT's effectiveness extends to larger backbones like ViT-L/14 (Tables 17-20) and even ResNet-50 by employing scaling and shifting or bias tuning (Tables 21-22).
- **Pre-training Models:** LIFT works effectively with different pre-training sources, including CLIP and ImageNet-21K (Tables 23-25).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes a highly significant contribution to the fields of long-tail learning and the application of foundation models, with several potential impacts:

1. **Re-evaluating Fine-Tuning Paradigms:** The core finding that "heavy fine-tuning hurts" tail-class performance fundamentally challenges the intuition that more fine-tuning is always better. This calls for a re-evaluation of current practices and encourages the adoption of more nuanced, resource-aware fine-tuning strategies, especially for imbalanced datasets. It shifts the focus from simply "fine-tuning foundation models" to "intelligently fine-tuning foundation models for specific challenges."

2. **Enabling Efficient and Accessible Foundation Model Deployment:** LIFT's ability to achieve SOTA performance with significantly fewer learnable parameters, faster convergence (fewer epochs), and without external data drastically lowers the computational barrier to deploying powerful foundation models in real-world long-tail scenarios. This is critical for organizations with limited computing resources and democratizes access to advanced AI capabilities.

3. **Improved Generalization for Scarce Data:** The method's demonstrated strength in improving tail-class accuracy is paramount. In many high-stakes applications (e.g., rare disease diagnosis in medical imaging, anomalous event detection in autonomous driving, minority class classification in industrial fault detection), the ability to accurately classify under-represented classes is far more important than overall accuracy. LIFT offers a robust solution to this critical challenge.

4. **A Principled Framework for Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning (PEFT) in Long-Tail Settings:** By integrating and validating various PEFT techniques within a unified framework, LIFT provides a strong baseline and inspiration for future research into designing even more effective and tailored lightweight fine-tuning modules for long-tail recognition. Its theoretical backing for preserving class-conditional distributions provides a deeper understanding of *why* PEFT methods are beneficial in this context.

5. **Inspiration for Future Research:** The paper opens several avenues for future work, including enhancing the efficiency of arbitrary lightweight fine-tuning, developing semantic-aware initialization for vision-only foundation models, and further exploring the interplay between different PEFT methods and long-tail challenges.

In conclusion, "Long-Tail Learning with Foundation Model: Heavy Fine-Tuning Hurts" provides not only a critical insight into the behavior of foundation models under fine-tuning for imbalanced data but also offers LIFT, a practical, efficient, and highly effective solution. This work represents a crucial step towards making foundation models truly robust and deployable in the diverse and often imbalanced data landscapes of real-world applications.
