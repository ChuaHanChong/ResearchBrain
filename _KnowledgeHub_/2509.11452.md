# Learning to Optimize Multi-Objective Alignment Through Dynamic Reward Weighting

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces dynamic reward weighting mechanisms for multi-objective alignment of large language models (LLMs) during online reinforcement learning. The methods, which include hypervolume-guided and gradient-based approaches, enable LLMs to achieve superior Pareto fronts across multiple objectives like accuracy, conciseness, and clarity, while also demonstrating faster convergence rates.

## Problem

- Fixed-weight linear reward scalarization in multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) is theoretically limited, unable to capture non-convex regions of the Pareto front in complex, stochastic online RL for LLMs.
- Different objectives in LLM alignment (e.g., accuracy, conciseness, clarity) often have varying learning difficulties and saturation points, leading to inefficient resource allocation with static weighting schemes.
- Heuristic rules for combining objectives lack flexibility and generalizability, requiring significant manual effort for new tasks or domains.

## Method

- Developed ==hypervolume-guided weight adaptation==, which uses a meta-reward based on hypervolume contribution to amplify scalarized rewards, proactively pushing the Pareto front in desired directions when human preferences are available.
- Introduced ==gradient-based weight optimization==, dynamically adjusting objective weights using an 'influence signal' derived from per-objective gradients, effectively reallocating learning effort towards objectives with greater potential.
- Integrated these ==dynamic weighting mechanisms== with standard online RL algorithms (GRPO, REINFORCE, RLOO) and validated them on mathematical reasoning tasks with various LLM architectures.

## Results

- Hypervolume-guided weight adaptation consistently achieved Pareto dominant solutions, often outperforming fixed-weight baselines in average scores across accuracy, conciseness, and clarity simultaneously.
- The gradient-based weight optimization consistently yielded superior Pareto fronts, largely dominating baselines across GRPO, REINFORCE, and RLOO training setups.
- Dynamic weighting methods demonstrated improved training efficiency, with the gradient-based method reducing the average number of training steps to reach Pareto fronts by **8.9** steps for GRPO and **8.2** steps for RLOO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic reward weighting methods consistently achieve Pareto dominant solutions that are unattainable by static, fixed-weight scalarization approaches in multi-objective LLM alignment.
> - The gradient-based approach dynamically adjusts weights based on individual objective learning contributions, revealing that objectives like conciseness saturate quickly, while accuracy requires sustained learning, and clarity is synergistic with accuracy.
> - Careful calibration of meta-rewards is necessary for stability, as overly aggressive exploration in hypervolume-guided methods can lead to suboptimal performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a limitation in the current paradigm of aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) through online reinforcement learning (RL). While online RL has demonstrated success in improving LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks (e.g., mathematical problem solving, code generation, logical reasoning), it predominantly focuses on optimizing single objectives, typically accuracy. This narrow focus often overlooks other crucial factors for practical LLM deployment, such as response length, output clarity, and computational efficiency.

Existing multi-objective alignment studies for LLMs typically employ one of two strategies: using fixed weights for linear reward scalarization or relying on heuristic rules for reward interpolation. The paper identifies several critical limitations inherent in these approaches:
1. **Varying Learning Difficulties**: Different objectives often exhibit distinct learning dynamics, with some objectives reaching saturation faster than others. Fixed-weight schemes continue to allocate equal gradient updates to saturated objectives, leading to inefficient resource utilization.
2. **Inability to Capture Non-Convex Pareto Fronts**: Theoretically, static linear scalarization is provably incapable of discovering solutions located on non-convex regions of the Pareto front. In online RL settings for LLMs, the mapping from policy parameters to objectives can be highly non-linear and non-convex, making fixed weighting schemes suboptimal for finding true trade-offs.
3. **Lack of Flexibility and Generalizability**: Heuristic rules for combining objectives are often task-specific and do not readily generalize to new objectives or different problem domains.

The presented research positions itself as an advancement in multi-objective RL by introducing dynamic reward weighting. This approach directly challenges the static nature of prior methods by adaptively adjusting objective weights throughout the online RL process. This enables more effective exploration of the objective space and the discovery of Pareto optimal solutions, including those in non-convex regions that static linear methods cannot reach.

The paper differentiates itself from "steerable multi-objective preference finetuning" methods, which offer inference-time flexibility but maintain static weights during training. It also extends beyond "general multi-objective preference finetuning" which often relies on static scalarization or human-defined rules. While multi-gradient descent algorithms (MGDA) exist in multi-objective optimization, they have primarily been applied to simpler supervised tasks. A concurrent work, PAMA (He and Maghsudi, 2025), applies MGDA principles to LLM alignment but has been evaluated on a limited scope (two objectives, specific algorithm). This paper offers a more comprehensive toolkit and broader empirical evaluation across diverse RL algorithms, datasets, and model families.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate novel methods for multi-objective online reinforcement learning for Large Language Models (LLMs) that can dynamically optimize multiple, potentially conflicting, objectives. The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current LLM alignment practices:

1. **Addressing the Single-Objective Bias in LLM Alignment**: Current LLM alignment often prioritizes a single performance metric, typically accuracy, especially in complex reasoning tasks. While crucial, accuracy alone does not define a universally "good" LLM response. Practical applications demand outputs that are also concise, clear, safe, or efficient. For instance, in mathematical problem-solving, a model should not only produce a correct answer but also a clear, step-by-step explanation (clarity) and avoid excessive verbosity (conciseness). Existing methods frequently neglect these auxiliary objectives during the reward process.

2. **Overcoming the Suboptimality of Static Reward Weighting**: The prevalent use of fixed-weight linear scalarization or heuristic reward interpolation rules in multi-objective LLM alignment presents significant challenges:
 - **Inefficient Resource Allocation**: The learning process for different objectives can vary in difficulty and convergence speed. If an objective quickly saturates (e.g., conciseness might be learned faster than complex mathematical accuracy), fixed weights lead to continued allocation of gradient updates to that already-optimized objective. This diverts learning effort from other objectives that still have substantial room for improvement, thereby impeding overall learning efficiency.
 - **Theoretical Limitations for Non-Convex Pareto Fronts**: A fundamental theoretical limitation of static linear scalarization is its inability to identify optimal solutions residing in non-convex regions of the Pareto front. In the context of online RL for LLMs, the stochastic nature of trajectory generation and the complex interplay of policy parameters with multiple objectives can create highly non-linear and non-convex mappings in the objective space. A single, static set of weights cannot effectively navigate or discover these non-convex trade-offs, leading to suboptimal policies.
 - **Lack of Generalizability for Heuristic Rules**: Reward interpolation based on heuristic rules, while sometimes effective for specific tasks, lacks the flexibility and robustness required to generalize across a wider range of objectives or different problem domains. Manually defining and tuning such rules becomes increasingly complex as the number and diversity of objectives grow.

3. **Facilitating Effective Exploration of the Objective Space**: To address these issues, the research is motivated by the principle of dynamically adjusting objective weights. This paradigm, termed "dynamic reward weighting," seeks to continuously rebalance and reprioritize objectives during training. The goal is to redirect learning efforts towards objectives that currently offer the greatest potential for improvement, thereby enabling a more thorough exploration of the multi-objective space. This allows the final policy to approximate Pareto optimal solutions more effectively, even in challenging non-convex regions, and to do so with greater training efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the introduction of dynamic reward weighting to overcome the limitations of static weighting schemes in multi-objective online reinforcement learning for LLMs. Two progressively sophisticated approaches are proposed: hypervolume-guided weight adaptation and gradient-based weight optimization.

**A. Hypervolume-Guided Weight Adaptation**
This method is designed for scenarios where human-specified preferences for objective weights (`w`) are provided. Instead of directly modifying these given weights, it introduces a meta-level reward signal that amplifies rewards when the policy discovers new, non-dominated solutions (i.e., new points on the Pareto front).

1. **Meta-Reward Calculation**: The central component is a meta-level reward, `r_pareto(r, B)`, which leverages the concept of hypervolume contribution. `B` represents a buffer storing the validation performance of the current set of Pareto-optimal solutions.
2. **Hypervolume Contribution (ΔHV)**: At each training step, after evaluating the current policy `π_θ_t` on a validation set to obtain a reward vector `r_θ_t`, the method calculates `ΔHV(r_θ_t, B)`. This value quantifies how much the new solution `r_θ_t` contributes to the overall hypervolume of the existing Pareto set `B`. A positive `ΔHV` indicates that `r_θ_t` is a new non-dominated solution or improves upon existing ones. The `ΔHV` is computed using the recursive dimension-sweep algorithm.
3. **Meta-Reward Function**: The meta-reward is defined as `r_pareto(r, B) = 0.5 + 1.5 * tanh(ΔHV(r, B))`. This function ensures that policies producing solutions with positive hypervolume contributions receive an amplified reward, proactively pushing the Pareto front. A minimum threshold of 0.5 for `r_pareto` is set to mitigate performance degradation in cases of zero reward, which could lead to increased response length and degraded performance.
4. **Reward Adaptation**: The scalarized reward for policy updates is then computed as `˜r_i = r_pareto * (w^⊤r_i)`. This effectively modulates the given human-specified weights `w` based on the current policy's progress toward the Pareto front.
5. **Policy Update**: The policy parameters `θ` are updated using the chosen RL objective function (e.g., GRPO, REINFORCE, RLOO) with these adapted scalar rewards. If the new solution `r_θ_t` yields a positive `ΔHV`, it is added to the Pareto set buffer `B`.
6. **Experiment Setup**: The method was evaluated using Qwen3-8B on Math500, and extended to MATH, SafeSQL, Deepseek-7B, Mistral-7B, and Llama3-8B. Objectives for math tasks included accuracy, conciseness (response length), and clarity (reasoning steps). Reward calculation used exact matching for accuracy, comparison to average length for conciseness, and heuristic rules for clarity. Baselines included fixed-weight linear scalarization with accuracy-focused, balanced, and efficiency-focused configurations, and a reimplementation of PAMA.

**B. Gradient-Based Weight Optimization**
This approach is designed for more flexible scenarios where user preferences for objective weights are not explicitly provided and need to be learned dynamically. It formulates online weight adaptation as an optimization problem, drawing inspiration from multi-gradient descent and domain adaptation.

1. **Linearity of Policy Gradients**: The method leverages the linearity property of the policy gradient for algorithms like REINFORCE: `∇J(θ) = Σ_k w_k ∇J_k(θ)`. This property implies that the overall objective function `J(θ)` is a linear combination of individual objective functions `J_k(θ)` (plus a constant).
2. **Weight Update Rule Derivation**: The objective is to minimize the sum of individual objective losses at the next training step. Using a first-order Taylor approximation for `J_k(θ^(t+1))` and incorporating a regularization term derived from Bregman divergence (with an entropy function), the following update rule for the weights is derived:
 `w^(t) = w^(t-1) ⊙ exp(η^(t) * I^(t) / μ)`.
 After this update, `w^(t)` is normalized: `w^(t) = w^(t) / Σ_k w^(t)_k`.
3. **Influence Signal (I^(t))**: The term `I^(t)_i = ⟨∇J_i(θ^(t)), Σ_k ∇J_k(θ^(t))⟩` measures the "influence" of learning objective `i` on the overall learning process. This includes its contribution to other objectives as well as its own gradient magnitude. Objectives with high influence and unlearned potential are upweighted.
4. **Convergence Analysis**: The paper provides a theoretical analysis (Lemma 5.1 and Theorem 5.2) demonstrating that under standard assumptions (Lipschitz continuity, bounded reward, converging learning rate, bounded policy gradient), the ratio of any two objective weights `w^(T)_i / w^(T)_j` remains uniformly bounded over time. This ensures numerical stability, prevents weight collapse or explosion, and guarantees convergence to a valid multi-objective equilibrium.
5. **Experiment Setup**: Weights are initialized uniformly (balanced setting). A polynomial learning rate scheduler (converging `η^(t)`) is used. To maintain the linearity assumption and differentiability, clipping mechanisms (clip range `ϵ` and dual clip constant `c`) in RL algorithms are practically disabled by setting them to high values (e.g., 100). For computational efficiency, gradients are computed only from middle layers of the LLM, as these layers are considered to encode more abstract knowledge.

Both methods are implemented within standard online RL algorithms (GRPO, REINFORCE, RLOO) and evaluated on various LLM architectures and datasets, with comprehensive comparisons against fixed-weight baselines and the PAMA approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the two dynamic reward weighting approaches demonstrates consistent performance improvements over fixed-weight linear scalarization baselines and a concurrent method, PAMA.

**A. Hypervolume-Guided Weight Adaptation:**
- **Performance Dominance**: In experiments conducted across various online RL algorithms (GRPO, REINFORCE, RLOO) and weight configurations (accuracy-focused, balanced, efficiency-focused) on the Qwen3-8B model with the Math500 dataset, the hypervolume-guided method frequently yielded Pareto fronts with superior average scores across the three objectives (accuracy, conciseness, clarity). In several instances, particularly with REINFORCE under accuracy- and efficiency-focused settings, the method achieved full dominance, showing improvements on all three objectives simultaneously compared to baselines.
- **Meta-Reward Dynamics**: Analysis of the meta-reward `r_pareto` during REINFORCE training indicated that a balanced weight setting led to higher meta-rewards, suggesting more aggressive exploration. However, this sometimes resulted in suboptimal performance compared to other configurations, implying that careful calibration of the meta-reward function may be beneficial to avoid over-aggressive updates that could hinder convergence.

**B. Gradient-Based Weight Optimization:**
- **Superior Pareto Fronts**: This approach consistently demonstrated superior Pareto fronts across GRPO and REINFORCE training setups when compared against all fixed-weight baselines and PAMA. Under RLOO training, it showed improved performance on conciseness and clarity, while maintaining competitive accuracy.
- **Dynamic Weight Evolution**: Analysis of the objective weights during training (e.g., for REINFORCE) revealed that the weight for conciseness rapidly converged to a lower value (approximately 0.2), with the corresponding weight reallocation primarily shifting to accuracy. This observation aligns with preliminary findings that conciseness improvements can be achieved quickly, requiring less sustained learning effort, whereas accuracy is a more challenging objective demanding continuous optimization.
- **Objective Interplay**: Further analysis showed that accuracy and clarity objectives exhibited synergistic effects, leading to consistently low KL-divergence between models trained for each individually. This suggests they induce similar parameter updates. In contrast, conciseness appeared to exert an orthogonal influence on model optimization, leading to significant divergence from accuracy- and clarity-focused models.

**C. Generalizability and Convergence Rate:**
- **Training Efficiency**: The gradient-based method exhibited a higher convergence rate, reducing the average number of training steps required to achieve the observed Pareto fronts by approximately 6.1 steps across different RL algorithms. The hypervolume-guided method showed more marginal efficiency gains.
- **Cross-Dataset Applicability**: Both methods were successfully applied and showed superior trade-offs on additional datasets, including mathematical algebra problems (MATH dataset) and code generation tasks (SafeSQL, with accuracy and security objectives).
- **Model Family Compatibility**: The approaches demonstrated effectiveness across diverse LLM architectures, including Qwen3, Deepseek-7B, Mistral-7B, and Llama3-8B.
- **Objective Saturation**: In some cases, particularly with models like Mistral-7B and Llama3-8B, objectives such as clarity rapidly reached a ceiling near their maximum performance. This resulted in Pareto fronts appearing to collapse to a single point in two-dimensional projections, indicating that meaningful trade-offs were predominantly occurring between other objective pairs (e.g., accuracy vs. response length). This highlights potential performance saturation or inherent capacity limitations in certain models for specific objectives.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research significantly advances the field of multi-objective reinforcement learning for Large Language Model (LLM) alignment by addressing fundamental limitations of existing methodologies. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Overcoming Suboptimality in Multi-Objective LLM Alignment**: The work provides a principled solution to the theoretical and practical challenges posed by static reward scalarization. By demonstrating that fixed-weight approaches provably fail to capture non-convex Pareto fronts and lead to inefficient learning, the paper underscores the necessity of dynamic reward weighting. The proposed methods enable LLMs to explore and discover a wider range of optimal trade-offs, leading to policies that are genuinely Pareto dominant across multiple objectives.

2. **Development of a Versatile Tool Kit**: The introduction of two distinct dynamic reward weighting approaches—hypervolume-guided adaptation and gradient-based optimization—offers a flexible toolkit for various alignment scenarios. The hypervolume-guided method is suitable when user preferences are explicitly known, allowing for directed exploration of the Pareto front. The gradient-based method, in contrast, provides a mechanism for automated weight optimization when preferences are not available or when a more data-driven approach is desired, making it highly adaptable and generalizable.

3. **Enhanced LLM Performance and Utility**: By enabling the simultaneous optimization of diverse objectives (e.g., accuracy, conciseness, clarity, security), the research contributes to the development of more capable and user-centric LLMs. Models trained with these dynamic weighting schemes can generate responses that are not only correct but also efficient, interpretable, and robust, thereby improving the overall user experience and expanding the practical deployment potential of LLMs in complex domains like mathematical reasoning and secure code generation.

4. **Improved Training Efficiency**: The gradient-based method, in particular, demonstrates a notable improvement in training efficiency, reducing the number of steps required to achieve superior Pareto fronts. This translates to faster model development cycles and potentially lower computational costs for LLM alignment, which is a significant factor given the resource-intensive nature of training large models.

5. **Insights into Objective Interactions and Learning Dynamics**: The analysis of objective weight evolution and pairwise KL-divergence trajectories provides valuable insights into how different objectives interact during training. Identifying synergistic (e.g., accuracy and clarity) and orthogonal (e.g., conciseness) relationships can inform future reward design, model architecture choices, and pretraining strategies, leading to more targeted and effective multi-objective learning.

6. **Broad Generalizability**: The extensive experimental validation across multiple online RL algorithms (GRPO, REINFORCE, RLOO), diverse datasets (Math500, MATH, SafeSQL), and different LLM families (Qwen3, Deepseek, Mistral, Llama3) demonstrates the robustness and wide applicability of the proposed dynamic weighting methods. This broad empirical evidence suggests that the methods are not specific to a particular setting but represent a general advancement in multi-objective LLM alignment.

**Future Work**: The research identifies crucial directions for future investigation, particularly concerning the inherent capacity of models to balance conflicting objectives. When irreducible conflicts arise (e.g., reducing response length degrades accuracy), the limitations of post-training alignment become apparent. This highlights the need for:
- **Pretraining Strategies**: Developing pretraining methods that explicitly equip LLMs with a foundational capacity to learn and balance diverse objectives more effectively from the outset.
- **Conflict Resolution in Post-Training**: Designing advanced post-training approaches that can intelligently determine which objectives to prioritize when faced with fundamental, irreducible conflicts, rather than attempting to optimize all objectives simultaneously beyond the model's inherent trade-off capabilities.
