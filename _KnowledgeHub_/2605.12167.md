# From Imagined Futures to Executable Actions: Mixture of Latent Actions for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The MoLA framework enhances robot manipulation by translating imagined future videos into executable action representations through a mixture of modality-aware inverse dynamics models. This approach achieved an average success rate of 92.7% on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, representing a 13.2 percentage point improvement, and a 73.0% average success rate on real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Imagination-based robot manipulation struggles with the disconnect between visually realistic video predictions and control-relevant action execution, leading to unstable control.
- Existing methods that condition policies on raw predicted frames or directly decode actions are prone to compounding errors due to a mismatch between video generation objectives and action relevance.
- Inverse dynamics models often lack scalability and robustness for use with imperfectly generated future frames, and single latent action representations may not capture all necessary manipulation cues.

## Method

- MoLA leverages Stable Video Diffusion to generate imagined future RGB rollouts conditioned on current observations and task instructions.
- It introduces a Mixture of Inverse Dynamics Models (MoIDM), pretrained to be modality-aware (semantic, depth, flow), which infers complementary latent actions from the imagined visual transitions.
- A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) action head, jointly fine-tuned with MoIDM, decodes these latent actions and visual features into executable robot control commands.

## Results

- MoLA achieved an average success rate of 92.7% on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, outperforming the strongest baseline by 13.2 percentage points, and reached 97.0% on the LIBERO benchmark.
- In real-world experiments with a UR5e robot, MoLA demonstrated an average success rate of 73.0% across five manipulation tasks, surpassing the VPP baseline by 11 percentage points.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the mixture of modality-aware inverse dynamics models is critical, with the flow-aware IDM providing the largest individual performance boost, and that pretraining and joint fine-tuning are essential for robust performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transforming imagined future videos into explicit action-centric latent representations via modality-aware inverse dynamics models effectively bridges the gap between visual imagination and robust policy execution.
> - Leveraging a mixture of specialized latent action representations (semantic, depth, flow) provides a more structured and physically grounded understanding of manipulation-relevant information.
> - Pretraining the inverse dynamics models on large datasets and subsequently jointly fine-tuning them with the action head is crucial for adapting to generated visual futures and achieving consistent control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot manipulation research has seen significant progress through two primary paradigms: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and imagination-based manipulation.

**VLA models** integrate visual perception, language understanding, and action generation, enabling robots to perform diverse tasks by learning directly from multimodal data. These systems, exemplified by models like RT-1 and Octo, demonstrate generalization across tasks but often rely on extensive, action-labeled robot datasets and can involve redundant visual reconstruction. This approach focuses on direct perception-to-action mapping.

**Imagination-based manipulation**, on the other hand, utilizes video generation models to predict long-horizon future observations. These predicted "imagined futures" are then used to inform action selection. Current approaches in this area typically either condition policies on these predicted frames or directly decode the generated videos into actions. Both strategies present challenges: conditioning on raw predicted frames places the burden of translating complex visual changes into control commands directly on the action head, while direct decoding makes the control highly susceptible to compounding errors in video prediction. A core limitation identified in the paper is that video generation models are primarily optimized for perceptual realism rather than for capturing action-relevant structures or the physical causes of state transitions. This mismatch can lead to indirect and unstable control.

**Latent action models** represent a parallel research direction aiming to learn compact, action-relevant representations that capture environmental dynamics, thereby reducing dependence on dense action annotations. These models infer abstract "latent actions" from observation transitions, which are then decoded into continuous controls. While effective in modeling the causal link from perception to action, existing latent action models often face limitations: they can be task-specific, lack scalability, struggle to operate robustly on imperfectly generated future frames, or rely on RGB reconstruction, which may capture task-irrelevant visual variations.

MoLA aims to bridge the gap between imagination-based methods and the need for action-centric representations. It integrates advanced video generation capabilities with novel latent action modeling. Specifically, MoLA proposes to transform imagined future videos into executable representations by inferring a *mixture* of latent actions. This approach seeks to overcome the challenges of existing imagination-based methods by providing structured, physically grounded action representations that are sensitive to manipulation-relevant information, and addresses the shortcomings of previous latent action models by pretraining on large-scale datasets and incorporating modality-aware cues.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance robot manipulation by effectively transforming imagined future observations, generated by video models, into executable robot actions. The authors identify a fundamental challenge in current imagination-based robot manipulation: the disconnect between the visual realism prioritized by video generation models and the control relevance required for robot action execution.

This motivation stems from several specific issues:
1. **Mismatch between Visual Realism and Control Relevance:** Video generation models are designed to produce perceptually convincing future observations. However, these predictions do not inherently emphasize the physical causes of state transitions or the manipulation-relevant structures. Consequently, directly using these "imagined futures" for action selection often leads to indirect and unstable control.
2. **Limitations of Existing Imagination-Based Approaches:**
 - Methods that condition policies on predicted frames require the policy's action head to implicitly learn to translate visual changes into effective control, which can be difficult and inefficient.
 - Methods that directly decode actions from generated videos are highly sensitive to even minor errors in video prediction, as these errors can compound and lead to control failures.
3. **Need for Action-Centric Representations:** To address the above, there is a motivation to convert visual imagination into a representation that is explicitly action-oriented and physically grounded. The concept of inverse dynamics, which models the action responsible for an observed state transition, is seen as a promising avenue.
4. **Enhancing Inverse Dynamics Models:** Existing inverse dynamics models often have limitations in scale, generalizability, and robustness, particularly when applied to generated (and thus potentially imperfect) visual futures. Furthermore, relying on a single latent action representation might be insufficient to capture the complementary information essential for diverse manipulation tasks (e.g., semantic intent, geometric structure, motion patterns).

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the MoLA framework are:
- To introduce a **control-oriented interface** that effectively transforms imagined future videos into executable representations, bridging the gap between video imagination and policy execution.
- To leverage a **mixture of pretrained inverse dynamics models (MoIDM)** to infer a rich set of latent actions from generated visual transitions, thereby distilling action-relevant information.
- To ensure these inverse dynamics models are **modality-aware**, specifically capturing complementary semantic, depth, and flow cues, which provide a more structured and physically grounded action representation.
- To demonstrate that this approach leads to **consistent gains** in task success, temporal consistency, and generalization across various simulated benchmarks and real-world robot manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MoLA (Mixture of Latent Actions) is an imagination-based robot manipulation framework designed to convert predicted future visual trajectories into structured, executable action representations. The approach comprises three main components: a video generation model, a mixture of modality-aware inverse dynamics models (MoIDM), and an action head, integrated through a three-stage training process.

#### 3.1. Overview of MoLA Architecture

Given a current observation ($o_t$) and a task instruction ($l$), MoLA operates in two main stages:
1. **Future Imagination:** A video generation model synthesizes a sequence of future visual rollouts ($ \hat{o}^{rgb}_{t:t+H}$) that represent the anticipated scene evolution for the task.
2. **Latent Action Inference and Policy Execution:** These imagined futures are processed by MoIDM to infer complementary latent actions implied by the visual transitions. The resulting mixture of latent actions, alongside visual features, conditions a diffusion-based action head to generate executable robot control commands.

#### 3.2. Video Generation Model

MoLA utilizes **Stable Video Diffusion (SVD)** as its backbone for visual imagination.
- **Input:** Current RGB observation ($o^{rgb}_t$) and a task instruction ($l$).
- **Output:** A sequence of predicted future RGB frames ($ \hat{o}^{rgb}_{t:t+H}$).
- **Mechanism:** SVD, based on the latent diffusion framework, encodes video clips into a compact latent space. It then performs conditional denoising, guided by the latent embedding of the current observation and the instruction representation, to synthesize task-consistent future rollouts.
- **Inference Efficiency:** For practical deployment, MoLA restricts the generation process to a single denoising step during inference. The paper states that even with this simplification, predicted latent futures retain sufficient visual and motion information for downstream action reasoning. For multi-view robot setups, predictions are generated independently for each camera stream.

#### 3.3. Mixture of Inverse Dynamics Models (MoIDM)

MoIDM is the core latent-action interface that transforms visually predicted futures into action-centric representations. It consists of multiple modality-aware inverse dynamics models.

##### 3.3.1. Inverse Dynamics Pretraining

Each inverse dynamics model (IDM) is trained independently to be modality-specific (semantic, depth, or flow-based motion).
- **Architecture:** Each IDM comprises a modality-specific spatiotemporal transformer and a modality-specific VQ (Vector Quantized) codebook for discrete latent actions.
- **Input Processing:** For a pair of current ($o^{rgb}_t$) and future ($o^{rgb}_{t+k}$) RGB frames, a ViT-based image encoder extracts RGB features ($h^{rgb}_t, h^{rgb}_{t+k}$).
- **Latent Action Inference:** Learnable latent action queries interact with the RGB features via the modality-specific spatiotemporal transformer to produce a representation ($\tilde{h}_{t \to t+k}^{(m)}$). This representation is then mapped to discrete latent actions ($z_{t \to t+k}^{(m)}$) using a modality-specific VQ codebook.
- **Modality Awareness through Dual Reconstruction Targets:** To specialize each IDM, it is trained with two types of reconstruction objectives:
 1. **Shared RGB Reconstruction:** A shared ViT-based RGB decoder reconstructs the future RGB frame ($\hat{o}^{rgb}_{t+k}$) from the current RGB feature ($h^{rgb}_t$) and the inferred latent action ($z_{t \to t+k}^{(m)}$). This ensures the latent action captures information relevant to general visual change.
 2. **Modality-Specific Reconstruction:** For each IDM, a dedicated ViT-based modality decoder reconstructs a future modality representation ($\hat{g}^{(m)}_{t+k}$) from its corresponding current modality feature ($h^{(m)}_t$) and the latent action. Frozen foundation models—Depth Anything v2 (for depth), SAM2 (for semantics), and CoTracker3 (for motion flow)—are used to extract features and ground-truth targets for this modality-specific supervision. This ensures the latent action encodes information crucial to that specific modality.

##### 3.3.2. Integration within MoLA (Joint Fine-tuning)

During the fine-tuning of the full MoLA model:
- The video generation model remains **frozen**. It provides imagined future RGB rollouts ($\hat{o}^{rgb}_{t:t+H}$).
- For each predicted future frame $\hat{o}^{rgb}_{t+k}$, MoIDM infers a **mixture of modality-specific discrete latent actions** ($Z_{t \to t+k} = \{z^{(sem)}, z^{(depth)}, z^{(flow)}\}$).
- These inferred latent actions, along with corresponding generated visual features, are passed as conditioning inputs to the action head.
- The parameters of MoIDM and the action head are **jointly optimized end-to-end**. This allows the latent actions to adapt to the specific characteristics of imagined futures and the control objective, while the imagination module remains stable.

#### 3.4. Action Head

MoLA employs a **Diffusion Transformer (DiT)** architecture as its action decoding module.
- **Training:** It is trained from scratch using a flow matching objective, which aims to learn a continuous transformation from noisy action samples to clean target actions, conditioned on the input features.
- **Input:** The mixture of latent action representations from MoIDM and visual features of the predicted future frames from the video model.
- **Output:** Executable robot action sequences. This architecture is designed to capture complex, multimodal action distributions and facilitate efficient and stable training.

#### 3.5. Model Training Stages

MoLA's training proceeds in three sequential stages:
1. **Stage I: Video Generation Model Fine-tuning:** The SVD model is fine-tuned on large-scale robot manipulation datasets to specialize its visual dynamics and viewpoints for robotic tasks.
2. **Stage II: MoIDM Pretraining:** The individual modality-aware inverse dynamics models are pretrained using large-scale robot datasets. This pretraining involves the dual reconstruction objectives to instill modality awareness and robustness.
3. **Stage III: End-to-end Fine-tuning:** The frozen video generation model is integrated with the pretrained MoIDM and the DiT-based action head. MoIDM and the action head are then jointly fine-tuned end-to-end on specific downstream manipulation demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MoLA was conducted on both simulated benchmarks and real-world robot manipulation tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness across various metrics.

#### 4.1. Simulation Benchmark Experiments

**Benchmarks Used:**
- **CALVIN:** A long-horizon, language-conditioned manipulation benchmark with multi-sensor demonstrations. Evaluation on an unseen environment (ABC-D split).
- **LIBERO:** Comprises four lifelong learning task suites: spatial reasoning, object generalization, goal adaptation, and long-horizon planning. Pretrained on LIBERO-90, fine-tuned and evaluated on each suite.
- **LIBERO-Plus:** An extension of LIBERO designed to assess robustness against controlled perturbations across seven dimensions, totaling 10,030 tasks.

**Results and Analysis:**
- **Overall Performance:** MoLA achieved the highest average success rate across all three benchmarks compared to existing approaches.
- **CALVIN Benchmark (Table 1):** MoLA demonstrated strong generalization, achieving a 98.5% success rate for single tasks and 82.6% for five tasks completed in a row, outperforming all other compared methods including DreamVLA and VPP.
- **LIBERO Benchmark (Table 2):** MoLA surpassed previous approaches across all four task suites, with an average score of 97.0%. This included 93.0% on Spatial, 99.5% on Object, 99.5% on Goal, and 96.0% on Long-horizon tasks, indicating strong performance across diverse manipulation challenges.
- **LIBERO-Plus Benchmark (Table 3):** On this large-scale robustness benchmark, MoLA achieved state-of-the-art performance with an average score of 92.7%. This represented a 13.2 percentage point improvement over the strongest baseline, OpenVLA-OFT+, signifying superior multi-task learning capabilities and generalization in complex environments.
- **Data Efficiency (Figure 4):** MoLA consistently outperformed VPP across various training data fractions (10%, 20%, 50%, 100%) on the CALVIN ABC-D benchmark. The advantage was more pronounced in low-data settings, suggesting a stronger ability to leverage pretrained representations under limited supervision.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

**Experiment Setup:**
- **Robot:** Universal Robots UR5e arm.
- **Cameras:** RealSense D455 (third-person) and RealSense D405 (egocentric).
- **Data:** 1,000 demonstration trajectories across five manipulation tasks (e.g., placing a bottle, grasping a bowl).
- **Evaluation:** 50 trials per task, with objects randomly initialized. Tasks considered successful if completed within 20 attempts.
- **Settings:** In-distribution (consistent with training) and out-of-distribution (distracting objects, lighting changes).
- **Baselines:** Diffusion Policy, OpenVLA, π0.5 (as a strong reference), and VPP.

**Results and Analysis (Table 4):**
- MoLA achieved an average success rate of 73.0% across all real-world tasks and settings.
- It significantly outperformed Diffusion Policy (41.5% average), OpenVLA (38.0% average), and the directly comparable world-model-based VPP (62.0% average).
- While π0.5 achieved a slightly higher average (77.5%), MoLA's performance highlights the effectiveness of its latent action interface in bridging video imagination and policy execution in real-world scenarios.
- Qualitative results (Figures 14-16) showed reliable performance in in-distribution settings and strong generalization to out-of-distribution conditions (novel object appearances, unseen configurations, varied lighting) without additional fine-tuning.
- On a complex high-precision continuous control task (pouring water, Figure 17), MoLA achieved a 45% success rate, indicating its efficacy for demanding fine manipulation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Contribution of Each IDM (Table 5):** Progressively adding flow-aware, depth-aware, and semantic-aware IDMs monotonically improved performance on CALVIN ABC-D. The flow-aware IDM provided the largest single improvement, emphasizing the importance of motion cues. The baseline (direct conditioning on predicted visual features) performed significantly worse, suggesting that the latent-action bottleneck is crucial.
- **Role of MoIDM Pretraining (Figure 5a):** Removing pretraining resulted in substantial performance degradation, indicating that pretraining on large-scale robot datasets is essential for robust action inference from generated frames.
- **MoIDM Freezing vs. Joint Fine-tuning (Figure 5b):** Joint fine-tuning of MoIDM during policy training consistently outperformed freezing its parameters, allowing better adaptation to the policy and control objective.
- **Action Head Comparison (Table 11):** The DiT-based action head trained with flow matching consistently outperformed an autoregressive Transformer over discretized action tokens, demonstrating its superiority in capturing long-horizon dependencies, multimodality, and stable control.
- **Stronger Video Generation Backbone (Table 12):** Replacing the SVD backbone with Wan2.2 5B led to further performance gains, confirming MoLA's compatibility with and benefit from more advanced video generators.
- **Multi-IDM Decomposition Necessity (Table 13):** The proposed three-IDM decomposition achieved better results than alternatives like direct multimodal inputs, a single RGB IDM, or a single multimodal IDM, indicating that specialized IDMs preserve complementary inductive biases.
- **Necessity of Explicit Imagined Futures (Table 14):** Replacing task-conditioned imagined futures with identical or noisy current frames caused performance degradation, demonstrating that MoLA benefits from explicit future cues provided by the video generation model at inference time.

#### 4.4. Failure Cases (Figure 11)

Qualitative analysis revealed limitations in complex scenarios involving unexpected state changes (e.g., a mug tipping over) or execution deviations (e.g., grasping an incorrect object). The model struggled with timely self-correction in such situations, suggesting room for improvement in robustness and error handling.

#### 4.5. Sensitivity to Video Generation Quality (Figure 12)

The paper showed that while video generation quality affects execution, MoLA exhibits relative robustness. Even with action prediction errors in generated videos, the policy could still succeed. This is attributed to MoIDM's ability to compress visual transitions into action-centric latent actions, allowing the action head to recover reasonable actions by exploiting preserved dynamical cues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MoLA framework represents a contribution to robot manipulation by addressing a fundamental challenge in imagination-based control: the disparity between the perceptual fidelity of generated future videos and the actual requirements for relevant action execution.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Bridging Imagination and Action:** MoLA establishes a control-oriented interface that transforms abstract imagined futures into concrete, executable action representations. This directly tackles the problem of converting visually rich but action-agnostic predictions into a format suitable for robot control.
- **Action-Centric Reasoning:** By leveraging inverse dynamics, MoLA enables reasoning over imagined trajectories directly in action space, moving beyond pixel-level manipulation. This reduces the burden on the policy to infer actions from raw visual changes and helps alleviate issues of indirect and unstable control.
- **Structured and Physically Grounded Representations:** The introduction of a "Mixture of Latent Actions" from modality-aware inverse dynamics models (semantic, depth, and flow) is significant. This multi-modal approach ensures that complementary cues—critical for understanding task intent, geometric structure, and interaction dynamics—are explicitly captured and integrated. This leads to more structured and physically grounded action representations compared to relying on single, less specialized latent action models.
- **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** The consistent improvements demonstrated across diverse and challenging simulated benchmarks (CALVIN, LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus) and real-world manipulation tasks validate MoLA's effectiveness. The superior performance in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution real-world settings, along with better data efficiency, indicates improved generalization capabilities and robustness in practical applications.
- **Modularity and Scalability:** MoLA's modular design, separating video generation, latent action inference, and action execution, allows for independent advancements in each component. Its compatibility with stronger video generation backbones (e.g., Wan2.2 5B) suggests that it can benefit from ongoing research in generative models. Pretraining MoIDM on large-scale datasets further contributes to its scalability.

**Potential Impact:**
- **More Robust Robot Policies:** By providing a clearer, action-focused interpretation of imagined futures, MoLA can contribute to the development of more robust and reliable robot manipulation policies, especially for long-horizon and complex tasks where compounding errors are a significant concern.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** The ability to effectively leverage pretrained representations and demonstrate strong performance in low-data regimes suggests a potential for reducing the need for extensive, explicitly action-labeled robot demonstration data, which is often costly and time-consuming to acquire. This could accelerate the deployment of robots in new tasks and environments.
- **Foundation for Future AI-Robot Integration:** MoLA's focus on action-centric imagination could serve as a valuable component in more sophisticated embodied AI systems. Its acknowledgment of limitations in high-level semantic reasoning and future plans for integration with large language models point towards a synergistic direction for developing truly generalist robots capable of understanding complex instructions and adapting to novel scenarios.
- **Advancement in World Models for Control:** The work underscores the potential of advanced world models for robotic control by demonstrating how visual predictions, when properly processed into action-relevant latent spaces, can effectively guide robot behavior.

While the paper identifies limitations in explicit high-level semantic reasoning and error correction compared to LLM-based approaches, MoLA’s foundational contributions to bridging visual imagination and actionable control provide a significant step towards more capable and autonomous robot manipulation.
