# Visual Whole-Body Control for Legged Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC San Diego developed a framework for visual whole-body control enabling autonomous legged robots to perform object pickup tasks in diverse environments. Their system leverages a hierarchical learning architecture to coordinate the robot's entire body, including its legs, to expand the manipulation workspace, achieving robust sim-to-real transfer with zero real-world data collection or fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Legged robots with arms have many degrees of freedom, making precise coordination and control for simultaneous locomotion and manipulation (loco-manipulation) challenging.
- Maintaining balance and stability while performing manipulation tasks on uneven terrain or with external disturbances is difficult for legged mobile manipulators, especially when compared to wheeled or static robots.
- Achieving autonomous legged loco-manipulation in diverse, unstructured environments with only egocentric visual observations, without relying on teleoperation, external tracking, or real-world data, remains a key challenge.

## Method

- The Visual Whole-Body Control (**VBC**) framework employs a two-level ==hierarchical architecture==: a low-level reinforcement learning policy for whole-body goal-reaching and a high-level policy for visual task planning.
- The low-level policy handles full body control, including leg movement and arm inverse kinematics, trained to track end-effector and body velocity commands in a height-invariant coordinate system to encourage whole-body posture adjustments.
- A privileged teacher policy, trained with full state information, guides a visuomotor student policy that uses segmented depth images from egocentric cameras, with the student policy being distilled via Dataset Aggregation (==DAgger==) for robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The UC San Diego system significantly outperformed teleoperated and fixed-height baselines in real-world object pickup tasks, achieving successful grasps across various heights (**0.0m**, **0.3m**, **0.5m**), where baselines failed completely at lower heights.
- The learned policy demonstrated robust sim-to-real transfer, performing effectively on a real Unitree B1 robot with a Unitree Z1 arm using only policies trained in simulation, requiring zero real-world data or fine-tuning.
- The robot exhibited emergent retrying behaviors, autonomously adjusting its body and attempting to re-grasp objects after initial failures, which substantially improved overall task success rates and robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A two-level hierarchical learning framework effectively decomposes the complex problem of visual whole-body control for legged loco-manipulation into manageable low-level goal-reaching and high-level task-planning policies.
> - Utilizing all robot degrees of freedom, including leg and torso adjustments, significantly expands the manipulation workspace, enabling a quadruped robot to pick up objects from diverse heights, including the ground.
> - Training policies entirely in simulation with extensive domain randomization, segmenting depth images, and distilling a privileged teacher to a visuomotor student policy enables robust sim-to-real transfer without real-world data collection or fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing towards intelligent, autonomous systems capable of operating in unstructured, dynamic environments. **Mobile manipulation**, the combination of mobility and manipulation capabilities, is a crucial step towards this goal. Historically, mobile manipulation has largely focused on wheeled robots due to their stability and easier control, especially in structured indoor environments (e.g., warehouses, homes). However, these wheeled platforms are severely limited in challenging, unstructured terrains like those found outdoors, which often require traversing obstacles, uneven ground, or slopes.

This paper addresses the critical need for **legged loco-manipulation**, enabling robots to leverage the agility and versatility of legs for both mobility and manipulation. Legged robots, such as quadrupedals, excel at navigating complex environments where wheeled robots struggle. However, integrating precise manipulation capabilities with dynamic locomotion presents significant challenges:
- **High Degrees of Freedom (DoF):** Legged robots with arms possess many DoFs (e.g., 19 DoF in this paper), making precise coordination and control complex.
- **Stability and Robustness:** Maintaining balance and stability while simultaneously moving and manipulating, especially on uneven terrain or with external disturbances, is far more difficult for legged robots than for static-base or wheeled manipulators.
- **Autonomous Operation:** Achieving these complex behaviors autonomously, using only egocentric visual observations, is a grand challenge, as it requires robust perception, planning, and control in diverse, real-world conditions.

Previous work in legged loco-manipulation has explored various approaches:
- **Model-based Control:** Some works utilize online motion planning and hierarchical optimization for complex tasks. While effective for specific tasks, these often require detailed environmental models, explicit task definitions, and significant human intervention (e.g., motion capture systems, dense scene descriptions, teleoperation). They often lack generalization to diverse, unknown environments or rely on simplified locomotion controllers (e.g., static-height).
- **Learning-based Control:** Recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) have enabled impressive locomotion skills for legged robots. Some research has also attempted end-to-end learning for whole-body control or combined model-based manipulation with learning-based locomotion. However, many learning-based approaches still require human teleoperation for task execution or lack the ability to adapt body posture (legs and torso) to reach objects at varying heights. Works like achieved autonomous mobile manipulation on quadruped robots but relied on default model-based locomotion controllers that do not leverage whole-body coordination for varying heights.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a **fully autonomous, vision-based, whole-body control system for legged loco-manipulation** that addresses the aforementioned limitations. It explicitly leverages the full DoF of the robot, including its legs, to expand the manipulation workspace (e.g., bending to pick up objects on the ground). Crucially, it achieves this with **zero real-world data collection or fine-tuning**, relying entirely on simulation-to-real (Sim2Real) transfer. The use of segmented depth images as visual input, rather than raw RGB, helps bridge the sim-to-real gap, making the system robust to real-world visual variations and enabling both indoor and outdoor deployment without external tracking systems. The hierarchical learning framework also simplifies the complex end-to-end learning problem, making it more tractable and robust.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and autonomous system for **legged loco-manipulation** that can perform precise manipulation tasks, specifically object pickup, in diverse environments using visual observations. The authors aim to overcome the limitations of traditional mobile manipulators (e.g., wheeled robots) which struggle in challenging, unstructured terrains, and address the complexities of controlling high-DoF legged platforms for manipulation.

The core motivations behind their approach are:
1. **Exploiting Whole-Body Control:** The authors emphasize the opportunity to utilize all available degrees of freedom (DoF) of a legged robot (e.g., 19 DoF for a quadruped with an arm) for "whole-body control." This means coordinating the robot's legs and torso with the arm to amplify manipulation capabilities and expand the workspace. This mimics human behavior, where we adjust our body posture to reach objects at different heights or in challenging positions (e.g., bending down, standing on tiptoes). This capability is crucial for tasks like picking trash from the ground or reaching items on a high shelf, which are unachievable with a static-height arm.
2. **Autonomous Operation with Visual Inputs:** The goal is for the robot to operate autonomously, given only observations from an egocentric camera. This eliminates the need for human teleoperation, external motion capture systems, or dense scene descriptions, enabling deployment in unknown and dynamic environments, including outdoors.
3. **Sim2Real Transfer without Real-World Data:** A major practical challenge in robot learning is the difficulty and cost of collecting real-world data. The authors aim to train all policies entirely in simulation and directly deploy them on a real robot without any real-world data collection or fine-tuning, leveraging the advancements in Sim2Real techniques.
4. **Robustness and Generalization:** The system should be robust to various object configurations (heights, locations, orientations) and environments, demonstrating generalization capabilities to unseen objects and scenarios. This includes emergent behaviors like retrying failed grasps.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide a practical, scalable solution for robust, autonomous mobile manipulation on legged robots, bridging the gap between advanced locomotion and precise manipulation in the wild.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **Visual Whole-Body Control (VBC)**, employs a two-level hierarchical architecture combining a low-level control policy with a high-level task-planning policy. All policies are trained in simulation and transferred to the real robot.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Architecture (VBC Framework)

The VBC framework consists of:
- **Low-Level Policy (Goal-Reaching):** This policy (π_low) handles the full whole-body control of the robot, tracking given body velocities (linear and yaw) and end-effector positions. It acts as a universal, robust foundation for different tasks.
- **High-Level Policy (Task-Planning):** This policy (π_high) proposes the necessary body velocities and end-effector pose commands for the low-level policy, based on visual observations. It is task-specific (e.g., pick-up).

#### 3.2. Low-Level Policy for Whole-Body Goal-Reaching

The low-level policy is trained using **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**.
- **Commands (b_t):** It receives commands for end-effector position (p_cmd), end-effector orientation (o_cmd), linear velocity (v_cmd_lin), and yaw velocity (ω_cmd_yaw). These are defined in a height-invariant robot frame.
- **Observations (o_t):** Includes proprioceptive states (base state, arm state, leg state), last action, environment extrinsic vector (encoding physics), and timing reference variables for stable trotting gait.
- **Actions:** The policy directly outputs target joint angles for the 12 leg joints, which are converted to torque commands by a PD controller. For the 6-DoF arm, it predicts the end-effector pose command, which is then converted to target joint angles using **Inverse Kinematics (IK)** with the pseudoinverse Jacobian method. This IK-based arm control is a deliberate design choice to ensure accurate end-effector positioning, contrasting with prior works that struggled by directly controlling arm joints via RL.
- **End-effector Goal Poses Sampling:** To train a robust low-level controller, a wide range of end-effector goals are sampled. A height-invariant coordinate system originating at the robot's base ensures that sampled goals expand the workspace by requiring the robot to adjust its body posture (e.g., bending, rising) to reach targets, fostering whole-body behavior. Goals are smoothly interpolated, and resampling occurs if self-collision or ground collision is detected.
- **Reward Functions:** A blend of command-following, energy penalty, alive reward, and phase rewards (for trotting gait) is used.
- **Domain Randomization & Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA):** Extensive domain randomization (terrains, friction, robot mass, center of mass) is applied to enhance robustness. ROA is used to learn an environment extrinsic latent vector from privileged information (available in sim) and then distill this into an adaptation module that infers it from history observations (available in real-world). This technique helps bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- **Policy Architecture:** A three-layer MLP for the policy, with smaller MLPs for the privileged encoder and adaptation module.

#### 3.3. High-Level Policy for Task Planning

This policy guides the low-level policy to accomplish specific tasks (e.g., pick-up). It's trained in two stages to handle visual observations effectively:

##### 3.3.1. Privileged Teacher Policy

- **Training:** Trained via RL with a fixed, pre-trained low-level policy.
- **Privileged Observations (o_t):** Includes latent object shape features (encoded from object point clouds using a pre-trained PointNet++), object pose relative to the arm base, and proprioceptive states (joint positions, velocities, end-effector pose).
- **High-Level Actions (a_t):** Outputs increments for gripper pose, quadruped linear and yaw velocity commands, and gripper status (open/close).
- **Domain Randomization:** Randomization of friction, robot mass, center of mass, high-level policy frequency, Kp/Kd for arm controller, table heights, and object/robot initial positions/poses. Randomly changing object positions encourages retrying behaviors.
- **Reward Functions:** Task-specific stage rewards (approaching, progress, completion) are combined with assistant rewards (e.g., penalizing action rates, encouraging slow velocity near objects, aiming arm/body at object).

##### 3.3.2. Visuomotor Student Policy

- **Motivation:** The privileged teacher policy uses information (object pose, shape features) not directly accessible in the real world. A visuomotor policy, relying on camera inputs, is needed for real-world deployment.
- **Observations (o_t):** Uses object segmentation masks and segmented depth images from two egocentric cameras (head and wrist), combined with proprioceptive states and last action. Color images are deliberately excluded to reduce sim-to-real gap challenges related to lighting and texture differences.
- **Training:** Distilled from the privileged teacher policy using **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)**, an online imitation learning method. DAgger warms up with teacher data, then iteratively samples with the student and requests corrections from the teacher, proving more efficient than direct RL for visuomotor policies.
- **Randomization and Augmentation:** Slight randomization of camera position/rotation and depth image clipping/normalization/augmentations (RandomErasing, GaussianBlur, GaussianNoise, RandomRotation) are applied to further bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- **Policy Architecture:** A CNN for image processing, followed by an MLP for high-level actions.

#### 3.4. Real-World Deployment

- **Robot Platform:** Unitree B1 quadruped robot equipped with a Unitree Z1 robotic arm, carrying two RealSense D435 RGBD cameras (head and wrist).
- **Computing:** Unitree B1's onboard computer handles low-level policy and arm control; an Nvidia Jetson Orin handles high-level vision-based planning. The system is untethered.
- **Software System Design:** A client-server multi-processing system allows asynchronous operation: low-level (quadruped at 50Hz, arm at 800Hz) and high-level (approx. 8Hz). The high-level policy sends commands to a goal receiver process, which is then picked up by the low-level controllers.
- **Vision Masks:** Uses **TrackingSAM** for real-time object segmentation. A user manually annotates an object once at the start of a trial (a few clicks with SAM), and TrackingSAM then continuously tracks and provides the mask (approx. 10 fps). Depth images are pre-processed (hole-filling, clipping). This minimal human intervention allows autonomous pickup once initiated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted both in simulation and on a real Unitree B1 robot, focusing on diverse object pick-up tasks across varying heights and environments.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Evaluation Setup:** Objects from the YCB dataset (33 objects, 7 categories) were used. Success was defined as picking up the object, failure if the object fell or the task timed out (150 high-level steps, ~18-24s).
- **Baselines:**
 - **VBC w.o. Shape Feature:** A visuomotor policy trained without access to pre-trained object shape features in the teacher stage.
 - **Floating Base Policy:** A policy with perfect low-level navigation but no arm-leg coordination, allowing robot body height to vary only within a limited range (0.4m to 0.55m).
 - **Non-Hierarchical:** A unified, end-to-end policy attempting to jointly learn low-level and high-level control from visual inputs.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Effectiveness of Hierarchy:** The non-hierarchical baseline consistently failed (0% success rate), underscoring the necessity and effectiveness of VBC's two-level hierarchical training pipeline.
 - **Benefits of Whole-Body Control (vs. Floating Base):** VBC showed significant advantages over the Floating Base policy across almost all tested heights (Figure 6). This highlights VBC's ability to achieve flexible whole-body behaviors, allowing the robot to bend or rise to reach objects at various heights (e.g., 0.0m on the ground), which fixed-height controllers cannot.
 - **Impact of 3D Shape Features:** VBC (with shape features) achieved the best performance on 4/7 object categories (Table 1). While slightly worse on regular shapes (where a policy targeting the object's center suffices), it was significantly better on irregular, complex objects (long box, cup, bowl, drill). This indicates that explicit 3D shape understanding aids in grasping irregularly shaped items.
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap (Teacher vs. Student):** The visuomotor student policy's performance was notably lower than the privileged teacher policy in simulation. This is attributed to the increased difficulty for the vision-based student to re-grasp a randomly re-oriented object after an initial touch-and-fail, especially within the limited 150 steps and with only visual inputs.
 - **Challenge with Long Boxes:** The student policy performed worst on long boxes, likely due to the difficulty of inferring precise object pose from visual inputs alone for such slender objects, and the gripper's limitations for certain initial orientations.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

- **Evaluation Setup:** Deployed the trained visuomotor student policy directly. Tested with 14 diverse, *unseen* real-world objects (including irregular shapes) across three height configurations (on the ground: 0.0m, on a box: ~0.3m, on a table: ~0.5m). Each object was tested 5 times for each height, allowing up to 5 continuous attempts (1 initial + 4 retries) without human reset.
- **Baseline:** Teleoperated default Unitree base controller (limited height variation 0.47m-0.55m) combined with a stationary-leg high-level policy. The robot was teleoperated to be close before grasping.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Superiority of VBC:** VBC significantly outperformed the teleoperated baseline across all height settings (Figure 7). Crucially, the baseline failed completely at 0.0m and 0.3m heights because its fixed-height locomotion couldn't accommodate reaching the ground or low boxes. Even at 0.5m (where a static arm is more viable), VBC was generally better.
 - **Emergent Retrying Behaviors:** The robot autonomously learned to retry failed grasps, greatly improving robustness and overall success rates. This behavior emerged naturally from simulation training and proved highly beneficial in the real world, as initial failures could result in changes in object pose or robot body movement that facilitated subsequent attempts.
 - **Generalization to Unseen Objects:** VBC demonstrated strong generalization capabilities to completely unseen real-world objects, validating the effectiveness of the training strategy and visual input design.
 - **Robust Sim2Real Transfer:** The policy, trained entirely in simulation, transferred well to the real robot without any real-world data collection or fine-tuning, a significant achievement. Qualitative analysis (Figure 8) showed that high-level commands translated into clear, reasonable robot behaviors (approaching, grasping, picking up) with distinct phases.
 - **Tolerance to Visual Tracking Loss:** The policy showed capability to recover from short-term visual tracking loss, a common issue in dynamic robot operations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Visual Whole-Body Control for Legged Loco-Manipulation" marks a significant advancement in the field of mobile robotics, particularly for systems operating in unstructured and challenging environments.

### 6. Significance

- **Pioneering Autonomous Legged Loco-Manipulation:** VBC provides a comprehensive and demonstrably effective framework for achieving fully autonomous, vision-based whole-body control on legged robots for complex manipulation tasks. This moves beyond teleoperated or limited-mobility systems, enabling true independence.
- **Effective Whole-Body Coordination:** The framework effectively coordinates the entire robot's degrees of freedom, including the legs, to extend the manipulation workspace and reach objects at diverse heights. This is a crucial capability for real-world applications where objects are rarely at a convenient "table height."
- **Robust Sim2Real Transfer:** A core strength is the ability to train complex, high-DoF policies entirely in simulation and deploy them on a real robot with zero real-world data collection or fine-tuning. This drastically reduces the cost and time associated with robot learning development. The clever use of segmented depth images and comprehensive domain randomization plays a key role in bridging this gap.
- **Hierarchical Learning for Complexity:** The two-level hierarchical design (low-level goal-reaching, high-level task-planning) successfully decomposes a very challenging end-to-end learning problem into more tractable sub-problems, leading to more stable and robust policy learning.
- **Emergent Behaviors:** The observation of emergent retrying behaviors in the real robot is a powerful indicator of the robustness and adaptability of the learned policy, suggesting a move beyond brittle, single-attempt task execution.
- **Practicality with Minimal Human Intervention:** Once an object is initially annotated, the system operates fully autonomously, requiring minimal human intervention during operation.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Expanded Application Domains:** This research opens doors for legged mobile manipulators in entirely new application areas. Robots could autonomously perform tasks in disaster relief (e.g., clearing debris), environmental cleanup (e.g., picking up trash outdoors), construction sites, or even agriculture, where challenging terrains are ubiquitous and human access might be difficult or dangerous.
- **Foundation for More Complex Tasks:** The robust whole-body control and manipulation capabilities established by VBC provide a strong foundation for future research into more complex, long-horizon tasks, multi-object manipulation, or even dexterous manipulation, where dynamic body adjustments are critical.
- **Advancement in Sim2Real Techniques:** The success of VBC further validates and pushes the boundaries of Sim2Real transfer methodologies, motivating continued investment in high-fidelity simulators and robust domain randomization techniques.
- **Inspiration for Future Robot Design:** The demonstration of flexible whole-body control could influence future robot hardware design, emphasizing integrated body-arm systems rather than viewing them as separate modules.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by authors):**
The authors are transparent about the limitations of the current system, which also point to exciting avenues for future research:
1. **Compounding Error:** The hierarchical pipeline is susceptible to compounding errors, where inaccuracies in one module can negatively impact subsequent ones. Future work could explore more tightly integrated or end-to-end approaches that retain robustness.
2. **Inaccurate Depth Estimation:** RGBD cameras do not provide perfect depth estimations, especially for reflective objects, leading to precision issues. Upgrading hardware with better depth sensors or integrating sensor fusion techniques could mitigate this.
3. **Inadequate Gripper Design:** The Unitree Z1 arm's beak-like gripper is not ideal for all manipulation tasks, sometimes pushing objects away. Using a parallel gripper or a more dexterous end-effector could improve grasping success.
4. **Unstable Visual Tracking:** The TrackingSAM tool, while effective, can lose track due to camera deviation, occlusion, or confusing object colors. Improving the robustness of the visual tracking module or incorporating object re-identification strategies would be beneficial.
5. **Manual Annotation & Limited Visual Information:** The current system still requires manual initial object annotation. Future work aims to achieve fully automated annotation and integrate more comprehensive visual information (e.g., RGB colors) to enable tasks requiring color differentiation or category-based sorting.
6. **Fixed Object Sizes:** The current training uses fixed object sizes. Randomizing object sizes during training could further enhance generalization to novel objects.
7. **Simplification of Pipeline:** Future efforts could focus on simplifying the overall system pipeline, potentially reducing complexity and increasing efficiency.

Despite these limitations, VBC represents a significant leap towards robust, autonomous, and versatile legged mobile manipulation, demonstrating capabilities that were previously challenging or unachievable, particularly for real-world deployment without extensive real-data reliance.
