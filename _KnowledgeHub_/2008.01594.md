# An Imitation from Observation Approach to Transfer Learning with Dynamics Mismatch

> [!summary] Summary
> GARAT (generative adversarial reinforced action transformation) grounds a black-box source simulator by learning an action-transformation policy with adversarial imitation from observation. The paper proves grounded action transformation is an IfO problem, derives a minimax objective matching grounded and target marginal transition distributions, and shows policies transfer with a few thousand target transitions, beating GAT, RARL, and noise-envelope baselines.

## Problem

- Most simulator-grounding and system-identification methods require a parameterized, modifiable simulator, which is unavailable when the source environment is a black-box physics engine.
- Grounded action transformation composes separately trained forward and inverse dynamics models via supervised learning, so per-step model errors compound and grounding quality saturates despite more real data.
- Policy-robustness approaches such as domain randomization, robust adversarial RL, and action-noise envelopes ignore target data entirely, trading task performance for conservatism and failing in higher-dimensional domains.

## Method

- ==Reduction of action-transformation learning to imitation from observation==: the joint state-action pair becomes the state of a new MDP, so the transformer is a policy imitating state-only target trajectories.
- ==Action-transformation inverse RL (ATIRL)== plus Theorem 1, proving the induced policy matches directly minimizing a ==convex-conjugate divergence== between grounded and target marginal transition distributions, bypassing the costly IRL step.
- **GARAT** algorithm: a ==discriminator== over (s, a, s') triples trained against target rollouts, ==PPO== updates of the transformer with reward -log D, then ==TRPO== retraining of the agent in the grounded simulator.

## Results

- In InvertedPendulum with a heavier target pendulum, GARAT-grounded environments show lower average L2 per-step transition error than GAT at every target-data budget, with pendulum-angle trajectories closely tracking the target.
- On the high-fidelity Minitaur simulator, GARAT reaches over **80%** of optimal target return from **1000** target transitions, while the best baseline GAT reaches at most **50%** using ten times more data.
- Across MuJoCo and PyBullet domains, a few thousand target transitions let GARAT rival policies trained directly on **1 million** target transitions, beating GAT, black-box RARL, and ANE over **5** seeds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recasting a transfer problem as an instance of imitation from observation imports an entire algorithmic literature at once; the reduction, not the specific algorithm, is the reusable contribution.
> - Distribution matching over transitions beats supervised model composition for grounding, since adversarial objectives avoid the compounding per-step errors of forward-plus-inverse dynamics pipelines.
> - Grounding only corrects dynamics where real data was gathered, so a grounded simulator stays unreliable off-distribution and can breed false confidence in the transferred policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the field of transfer learning, specifically addressing the challenge of dynamics mismatch between a source and a target environment. Transfer learning aims to leverage experience gained in one environment to expedite learning in another. The focus on dynamics mismatch implies that the fundamental rules governing how actions translate into state changes differ between the source and target.

A key application area for this problem is sim-to-real transfer, where a policy is trained in a simulated environment (source) and then deployed in the real world (target). Simulators inevitably model real-world dynamics imperfectly, leading to discrepancies, and gathering data in the real world is often expensive, time-consuming, or even hazardous. This makes efficient transfer with limited real-world interaction a critical requirement.

The paper focuses on a sub-paradigm of sim-to-real known as **simulator grounding**. This approach uses a limited amount of target environment data to modify the source environment’s dynamics, making them more closely align with the target. This work specifically builds upon the "grounded action transformation (GAT)" technique, which modifies the agent's actions before they are executed in the source environment, rather than altering the simulator itself, which may be a black-box.

Concurrently, the machine learning community has developed **imitation learning**, where an agent learns to mimic demonstrated behaviors. A specialized variant is **imitation from observation (IfO)**, where the imitator observes only the states resulting from an expert's actions, without explicit knowledge of those actions. Recent progress in IfO has demonstrated the feasibility of learning policies from such state-only trajectories.

The present work establishes a theoretical link between grounded action transformation and imitation from observation. By demonstrating that the problem of learning an action transformation can be framed as an IfO problem, the authors propose that advanced IfO techniques could be repurposed to improve simulator grounding. This positions the work at the intersection of transfer learning, sim-to-real, and adversarial imitation learning.

Other related sim-to-real methods include:
- **Policy robustness methods**: These train policies to be robust to environmental variations (e.g., Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (RARL), Additive Noise Envelope (ANE)).
- **Domain adaptation/System identification**: These approaches, often requiring a parameterized simulator, adapt the simulator itself using real-world data.

This paper distinguishes itself by offering a black-box simulator approach that enhances dynamic fidelity through action transformations, leveraging the theoretical and algorithmic advancements in the IfO domain.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective method for transfer learning in scenarios characterized by dynamics mismatch between a source and a target environment, with a particular emphasis on minimizing interaction with the target environment during the learning process. This problem is of significant importance for sim-to-real transfer, where simulators inherently possess dynamics that do not perfectly align with real-world systems.

The motivation for this work stems from two observations:
1. **Challenge of Dynamics Mismatch in Sim-to-Real**: In sim-to-real transfer, the physical parameters (e.g., friction coefficients, gravity, mass) of a simulator often differ from those of a real robot. This mismatch can cause policies learned in simulation to perform poorly when transferred to the real world. Collecting extensive data from the real world to bridge this "reality gap" is typically expensive or impractical. Therefore, methods that enable efficient transfer with limited real-world data are crucial.
2. **Relationship between Grounded Action Transformation and Imitation from Observation**: The paper identifies a close, previously unarticulated relationship between an existing grounded transfer technique, Grounded Action Transformation (GAT), and Imitation from Observation (IfO). GAT aims to modify an agent’s actions in the source environment such that the resulting dynamics in the source environment more closely mimic those of the target environment. The authors hypothesize that this process, where the objective is to match the observed state transitions of the target environment without knowing the specific "grounding actions" that would produce them in the source, aligns with the definition of an IfO problem.

Based on this hypothesized relationship, the core motivation is to investigate whether advanced, state-of-the-art techniques from the IfO literature, which have demonstrated success in mimicking observed behaviors, can be effectively repurposed to improve grounded transfer learning. The specific objectives are:
- To formally establish the connection between learning a grounded action transformation and the IfO problem.
- To derive and develop a novel algorithm, Generative Adversarial Reinforced Action Transformation (GARAT), by adapting adversarial imitation from observation techniques. This algorithm is designed to learn an action transformation policy that grounds the source environment by minimizing the distributional mismatch between the grounded source and the target environment dynamics.
- To empirically validate whether GARAT leads to more effective grounding of the source environment and subsequently to better transfer of learned policies to the target environment, compared to existing black-box transfer methods.

In essence, the research is driven by the desire to leverage theoretical insights from imitation learning to address a practical and critical challenge in robot learning: making policies learned in simulation more robust and performant in real-world applications with minimal real-world data.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology adopted in this paper proceeds in three main stages: first, establishing a theoretical link between grounded action transformation and Imitation from Observation (IfO); second, deriving a novel algorithm, GARAT, based on this insight; and third, experimentally validating its performance.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work considers sequential decision processes modeled as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) defined by a tuple $\langle S, A, R, P, \gamma, \rho_0 \rangle$. The core problem is transfer learning with dynamics mismatch, where a source environment ($M_s$) and a target environment ($M_t$) are identical except for their transition functions, $P_s$ and $P_t$, respectively. The objective is to efficiently learn a control policy for $M_t$ using limited data from $M_t$ and abundant data from $M_s$.

The paper focuses on **simulator grounding**, specifically through **grounded action transformation (GAT)**. In this approach, an action transformation function $\pi_g: S \times A \to \Delta(A)$ is learned. This function takes the agent's intended action and state, and outputs a modified action ($\tilde{a}$) to be executed in the source environment. This effectively changes the source environment's transition function from the agent's perspective, creating a "grounded environment" with transition function $P_g(s'|s,a) = \sum_{\tilde{a} \in A} P_s(s'|s,\tilde{a})\pi_g(\tilde{a}|s,a)$. The goal is to learn $\pi_g$ such that $P_g$ is as close as possible to $P_t$.

#### 3.2. GAT as Imitation from Observation

The central theoretical contribution is demonstrating that the problem of learning the action transformation function $\pi_g$ can be framed as an IfO problem. An IfO problem requires learning a policy to imitate state-only trajectories generated by an expert, without knowledge of the expert's actions.
1. **Sequential Decision-Making**: The authors define a new MDP where the state space $X$ combines the original state $S$ and the agent's action $A$. The action transformation function $\pi_g$ then acts as a policy within this new MDP, mapping combined states $x=(s,a)$ to transformed actions $\tilde{a}$. This confirms it as a sequential decision-making problem.
2. **Imitating State-Only Trajectories**: When learning $\pi_g$, the target environment provides trajectories of states and actions $\{(s_0, a_0), (s_1, a_1), \dots\}$ generated by the fixed agent policy $\pi$ in $M_t$. If an optimal $\pi_g^*$ exists such that $P_g = P_t$, then these target trajectories can be viewed as being produced by a Markov process where the "expert" is the combination of the original source dynamics $P_s$ and this unknown $\pi_g^*$. The learning agent, attempting to find $\pi_g$, only observes the state-action-next-state transitions from the target environment, but not the specific $\tilde{a}$ that would make $P_s$ mimic $P_t$. This satisfies the IfO criteria.

#### 3.3. Generative Adversarial Reinforced Action Transformation (GARAT)

Leveraging the IfO connection, the authors derive GARAT, inspired by generative adversarial approaches to imitation learning (GAIL) and IfO (GAIfO).
1. **Action Transformation Inverse Reinforcement Learning (ATIRL)**: The derivation begins by formulating an ATIRL problem, aiming to find a cost function $c$ under which the observed target environment transitions would be optimal, and then learning $\pi_g$ to minimize this cost in the grounded source environment.
2. **Characterizing the Induced Policy**: A key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that the combined ATIRL and subsequent RL procedure is equivalent to directly minimizing a divergence metric $\psi^*(\rho_g - \rho_t)$, where $\rho_g$ and $\rho_t$ are the marginal transition distributions of the grounded source and target environments, respectively, and $\psi^*$ is the convex conjugate of a regularizer $\psi$. This means that instead of explicitly solving an IRL problem, one can directly optimize $\pi_g$ to match the marginal transition distributions.
3. **Adversarial Objective**: By selecting a specific form for the regularizer $\psi$, the problem of minimizing $\psi^*(\rho_g - \rho_t)$ is transformed into a minimax objective:
 $\min_{\pi_g} \max_{D} E_{P_g}[\log(D(s,a,s'))] + E_{P_t}[\log(1-D(s,a,s'))]$.
 Here, $D$ is a discriminator network that attempts to distinguish between transitions from the grounded source environment ($P_g$) and the target environment ($P_t$). The action transformation policy $\pi_g$ acts as a generator, attempting to "fool" the discriminator by making its generated transitions indistinguishable from target transitions. The reward for $\pi_g$ is derived from the discriminator's output, specifically $-\log(D(s,a,s'))$.

#### 3.4. GARAT Algorithm Overview (Algorithm 1)

The GARAT algorithm operates iteratively:
1. An agent policy $\pi$ is pretrained in the source environment.
2. The policy $\pi$ is deployed in the target environment to collect expert trajectories.
3. An iterative adversarial process then unfolds:
 - Trajectories are collected by deploying $\pi$ in the *grounded* source environment (where $\pi_g$ transforms actions).
 - A discriminator network $D_{\phi}$ is updated to minimize its ability to distinguish between transitions from the grounded source and the actual target environment.
 - The action transformation policy $\pi_g$ is updated using a policy gradient method, with a reward signal derived from the discriminator, aiming to maximize the discriminator's error (i.e., make its generated transitions look more like target transitions).
4. After several updates to $\pi_g$, the main agent policy $\pi$ is re-optimized within the newly grounded source environment. This entire process repeats until satisfactory performance in the target environment is achieved.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- The agent policy $\pi$ is trained using Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO).
- The action transformation policy $\pi_g$ is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- The discriminator $D_{\phi}$ uses L2 regularization and a gradient penalty term for stable GAN training.
- Experiments also explored compatibility with other divergence measures (e.g., those from AIRL, WAIL).

### 4. Main findings and results

The experiments conducted aimed to validate two primary hypotheses: that GARAT leads to improved grounding of the source environment, and that this enhanced grounding translates to better policy transfer in the presence of dynamics mismatch.

#### 4.1. Source Environment Grounding

1. **Quantitative Evaluation (InvertedPendulum domain, Figure 1a)**: GARAT demonstrates superior performance in grounding the source environment. The average error in per-step transitions, measured by the L2 norm between the grounded source and target environments, was significantly lower for GARAT compared to GAT. Notably, GARAT achieved a similar average transition error with just a single trajectory of target environment data as GAT did with 100 trajectories. This indicates higher data efficiency in achieving dynamics alignment.
2. **Qualitative Evaluation (InvertedPendulum domain, Figure 1b)**: When the same agent policy was deployed from an identical start state across different environments (ungrounded source, target, GAT-grounded source, GARAT-grounded source), the trajectory generated in the GARAT-grounded source environment qualitatively resembled the target environment's trajectory most closely. The GAT-grounded source environment showed initial similarity but diverged more substantially over time, attributed to compounding errors, leading to premature episode termination.

#### 4.2. Transfer Experiments

The effectiveness of GARAT in policy transfer was evaluated across several MuJoCo and PyBullet simulated environments, including InvertedPendulum, Hopper, HalfCheetah, Walker, Ant, and the Minitaur domain. The performance of GARAT was compared against GAT, Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (RARL) adapted for a black-box simulator, and Action Noise Envelope (ANE). Policies trained directly in the target environment ($\pi_t$) served as an upper bound, and policies trained solely in the source environment ($\pi_s$) served as a baseline (performance scaled from 0 to 1, where $\pi_t$ is 1 and $\pi_s$ is 0).

1. **Consistent Outperformance (Figure 3)**: GARAT consistently achieved higher returns in the target environment across most evaluated domains compared to GAT, RARL, and ANE. The only exception was HopperHighFriction, where most methods performed comparably well.
2. **Data Efficiency and Near-Optimal Performance**: In several domains, GARAT enabled policy transfer that resulted in performance on par with policies trained directly in the target environment with 1 million transitions. This was achieved using only a few thousand transitions from the target environment for grounding, demonstrating significant data efficiency.
3. **Performance in Challenging Domains**:
 - **Ant (PyBullet)**: In this relatively high-dimensional domain with modified gravity (twice that of the source), GARAT successfully transferred a competent policy, whereas related methods largely failed, with source-trained policies performing ineffectually.
 - **Minitaur Domain**: Using a high-fidelity simulator as the target environment (realistic sim-to-real setting), GARAT enabled a policy to achieve over 80% of the optimal target environment performance with only 1000 target environment transitions. The next best baseline, GAT, attained at most 50% performance, requiring ten times more target environment data. This indicates GARAT's effectiveness in realistic and complex scenarios.
4. **Robustness to Divergence Measures**: The paper also noted in its appendix that GARAT remained effective with alternative divergence measures (e.g., those inspired by AIRL and WAIL), suggesting a robustness to the specific adversarial objective formulation.

In summary, the results indicate that GARAT effectively grounds source environments to match target dynamics with high data efficiency and consistently facilitates the transfer of higher-performing policies across a variety of dynamics-mismatched environments compared to existing black-box transfer approaches.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning and robotics by offering a novel theoretical framework and an effective algorithmic solution for transfer learning with dynamics mismatch.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

The primary theoretical contribution is the formal establishment of a connection between learning a grounded action transformation—a practical technique for sim-to-real transfer—and the Imitation from Observation (IfO) problem. This insight repositions grounded transfer learning within the broader context of adversarial imitation learning, opening avenues for applying advanced IfO techniques to this challenge. By demonstrating that the problem can be characterized as minimizing a divergence between marginal transition distributions, the work provides a solid theoretical foundation for developing algorithms like GARAT.

#### 5.2. Algorithmic Significance

The development of Generative Adversarial Reinforced Action Transformation (GARAT) represents an algorithmic advancement in addressing dynamics mismatch. By leveraging adversarial training principles, GARAT effectively learns an action transformation policy that grounds the source environment. The experimental results indicate that GARAT outperforms existing black-box transfer methods such as GAT, RARL (adapted for black-box simulators), and ANE across a range of simulated environments.

#### 5.3. Practical Significance and Potential Impact

1. **Reduced Real-World Interaction for Robot Learning**: One of the most significant potential impacts is the substantial reduction in the need for real-world data collection during robot policy training. Real-world interactions can be expensive due to wear and tear on hardware, time-consuming, or dangerous in sensitive applications (e.g., medical robotics). GARAT's ability to achieve high transfer performance with only a few thousand target environment transitions (compared to millions required for direct training) directly addresses this bottleneck, potentially making robot learning more accessible and scalable.
2. **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The enhanced grounding capabilities of GARAT can lead to policies that are more reliable and performant when transferred from simulation to the real world. This can accelerate the deployment of autonomous agents in various domains, from manufacturing and logistics to exploration and service robotics.
3. **Relaxed Requirements for Simulator Fidelity**: By effectively adapting a source environment's dynamics, GARAT can potentially alleviate the intensive engineering effort typically required to develop high-fidelity simulators. This could allow for the use of less precise, more easily modifiable simulators, which can then adapt to specific tasks based on limited real-world feedback.
4. **Broader Applications Beyond Robotics**: While the paper emphasizes sim-to-real, the underlying principles of efficiently adapting environment dynamics with limited target data could find applications in other domains where precise modeling is challenging and real-world data is scarce, such as complex system control or biological modeling.

#### 5.4. Considerations and Limitations (Broader Impact)

The authors also discuss important broader impact considerations:
- **False Confidence**: Improved simulator grounding might inadvertently foster a false sense of confidence in policies trained in such grounded environments. If the real-world data used for grounding does not cover all relevant operating conditions, the policy might still perform poorly in out-of-distribution situations, leading to unexpected and potentially hazardous consequences. Careful validation of the grounding's coverage is therefore essential.
- **Computational Cost**: While reducing real-world data, generative adversarial approaches can be computationally intensive. An increased reliance on such methods might shift the burden from data collection to computational resources, necessitating continued research into sample-efficient learning methods in terms of both real-world data and computational power.

Overall, this research represents a step towards more efficient and robust transfer of policies, particularly in sim-to-real contexts, by drawing a novel theoretical link and developing a practical algorithmic solution.
