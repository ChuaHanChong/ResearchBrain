# ACSAC: Adaptive Chunk Size Actor-Critic with Causal Transformer Q-Network

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Tongji University introduced Adaptive Chunk Size Actor-Critic (ACSAC), a reinforcement learning framework that leverages a causal Transformer Q-network and a flow-based policy to dynamically select action chunk lengths. This method outperformed all baselines across 25 OGBench robotic manipulation tasks by adaptively balancing reactivity and temporal consistency in long-horizon, sparse-reward environments.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces challenges in long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks due to bootstrapping error accumulation and slow value propagation in single-step Temporal Difference (TD) learning.
- Existing action chunking methods use a fixed chunk size, forcing a single trade-off between reactivity and temporal consistency that is suboptimal for varying task phases.
- The manual tuning of fixed chunk sizes for each task imposes a practical burden, hindering generalizability and deployability of RL approaches.

## Method

- ACSAC proposes an Adaptive Chunk Size Actor-Critic framework that allows the agent to dynamically select the length of its action chunks at each replanning state.
- It employs a causal Transformer critic to learn and output prefix-conditioned Q-values for all potential chunk lengths up to a maximum, ensuring cross-horizon comparability.
- The approach integrates a flow-based behavioral cloning policy for generating diverse action chunks and uses a joint rejection sampling mechanism to select the optimal action prefix and its duration.

## Results

- ACSAC achieved the best overall performance across 25 OGBench robotic manipulation tasks in both offline and offline-to-online settings, surpassing single-step, multi-step, and fixed chunk size baselines.
- Qualitative analysis on a `cube-double` task showed that ACSAC adaptively selected longer chunks (mean ≈ 4) for initial coarse approaches and shorter chunks (mean ≈ 1) for precise placements.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the full ACSAC architecture, including the multi-horizon TD objective and joint argmax policy extraction, is crucial for its performance advantage, outperforming baselines that only incorporated the Transformer critic.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adapting action chunk sizes based on the current state significantly enhances performance in long-horizon tasks by balancing temporal consistency for coarse movements and high reactivity for precise manipulations.
> - A causal Transformer critic, trained with a multi-step TD objective across all prefix lengths, can effectively learn prefix-conditioned Q-values that are mutually comparable, enabling principled adaptive chunk selection.
> - The ACSAC Bellman operator, which incorporates expected-prefix-max bootstrapping, is a contraction whose unique fixed point is the action-value function of the adaptive policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces a significant challenge in long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks, primarily due to the issue of bootstrapping error accumulation in single-step Temporal Difference (TD) learning. Each Bellman update, which regresses towards the Q-network’s own next-state estimate, can compound small errors over many recursive steps, thereby hindering efficient learning and value propagation.

A common approach to mitigate slow value propagation is the use of multi-step returns. While these methods accelerate learning by shifting the regression target further into the future, they can introduce an off-policy bias, as intermediate rewards might be collected under a behavior policy different from the current policy being learned.

Action chunking has emerged as a strategy to address these limitations. By training critics and policies on sequences of actions rather than individual actions, action chunking allows for multi-step value backups without incurring off-policy bias. This is because the critic conditions on the entire action sequence, ensuring consistency with the rewards observed. Beyond value propagation, action chunking also contributes to temporally coherent behaviors, which can capture non-Markovian patterns in data and improve exploration, especially in sparse-reward environments.

However, existing action-chunking actor-critic methods typically employ a fixed chunk size, which is manually tuned for each task and applied uniformly across all states. This fixed size necessitates a single trade-off between reactivity (the ability to respond quickly to new observations) and temporal consistency (executing a coherent sequence of actions). A large fixed chunk size can reduce responsiveness and lead to over-commitment in situations requiring fine-grained control, while a small fixed chunk size may result in incoherent motions and slow value backups. The optimal balance between these factors is often state-dependent, a limitation that current fixed-chunking methods do not address.

This research introduces Adaptive Chunk Size Actor-Critic (ACSAC) to address this specific limitation by enabling state-dependent and adaptive selection of action chunk sizes. It builds upon recent advancements in flow matching for generative policies, which can model complex, multi-modal action distributions, and causal Transformer networks for Q-function approximation, known for their ability to process sequential data while respecting temporal dependencies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance and adaptability of reinforcement learning agents in long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks by addressing a fundamental limitation of existing action chunking methods: their reliance on a fixed, manually tuned chunk size.

The motivation for developing ACSAC stems from the observed deficiencies of fixed chunk sizes in dynamic environments:
- **Trade-off Imbalance:** A fixed chunk size imposes a single trade-off between reactivity and temporal consistency across an entire task. For instance, in a robotic manipulation task, initial movements may benefit from long, temporally consistent action chunks for coarse movements, while fine-grained manipulation (e.g., grasping or precise placement) requires frequent replanning and shorter, more reactive action chunks. A single, fixed chunk size cannot optimally accommodate these varying requirements.
- **Reduced Reactivity with Large Chunks:** When a large fixed chunk size is used, the agent commits to an extended sequence of actions in an open-loop manner. In sensitive states, such as approaching an object for grasping or navigating a tight turn, this over-commitment can prevent the agent from reacting to new observations or correcting errors promptly. This can lead to suboptimal trajectories, driving the agent off the goal-reaching path and reducing overall returns.
- **Incoherent Motion and Slow Value Backups with Small Chunks:** Conversely, if a small fixed chunk size is chosen to maximize reactivity, the agent might execute many short, disconnected actions. This can result in incoherent and inefficient movements that fail to leverage the benefits of temporally extended actions. Moreover, very small chunks effectively revert towards single-step TD learning, hindering the accelerated value propagation that action chunking is designed to provide.
- **Task-Specific Tuning Burden:** The necessity to manually tune the optimal fixed chunk size for each new task is a practical burden, requiring extensive experimentation and domain expertise. An adaptive mechanism would remove this requirement, making the RL approach more generalizable and easier to deploy.

To overcome these limitations, the key objective is to enable an RL agent to adaptively select an appropriate action chunk size based on the current state. This allows for longer, more coherent action sequences in stable or exploratory phases and shorter, more reactive sequences in critical or sensitive phases, thereby balancing reactivity and temporal consistency in a state-dependent manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ACSAC proposes an Adaptive Chunk Size Actor-Critic framework that allows the agent to dynamically select the length of its action chunks at each replanning state. The approach integrates a causal Transformer critic, a flow-based behavioral cloning (BC) policy, and a joint rejection sampling mechanism.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Chunk-based RL

The work considers an infinite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) and assumes access to an offline dataset of transitions. It highlights that standard single-step TD learning suffers from slow value propagation in long-horizon tasks. Multi-step returns can accelerate this, but introduce off-policy bias. Action chunking addresses this by learning a chunked critic $Q_\phi(s_t, a_{t:t+H})$ that evaluates a sequence of $H$ actions, allowing for $H$-step value propagation without off-policy bias, as the critic conditions on the exact action sequence used for rewards.

#### 3.2. Flow Policy for Action Chunk Generation

ACSAC utilizes a flow matching technique to train a state-conditioned velocity field $v_\theta(u, s, a_z)$. This velocity field transforms a noise distribution into the target data distribution of action chunks. The policy $\pi_\theta(s, z)$ is a deterministic function that maps a noise vector $z \sim N(0, I_{Hd})$ to an action chunk $a = \pi_\theta(s, z)$ of length $H$. This flow policy is trained using a behavior cloning objective (Equation 7), fitting full-length action chunks from the offline dataset. This allows $\pi_\theta$ to capture complex, multi-modal, and non-Markovian behavior patterns present in the data, which is beneficial for generating temporally coherent exploration sequences.

#### 3.3. Causal Transformer Critic Architecture

A central component of ACSAC is its causal Transformer critic $Q_\phi$. Unlike traditional MLP critics that consume fixed-length inputs, this Transformer is designed to evaluate action chunks of varying lengths up to a maximum $H$.
- **Prefix-conditioned Q-values:** The Transformer ingests the current state $s_t$ and a maximum-length action chunk $a_{t:t+H}$ as a token sequence. It then outputs $H$ prefix-conditioned Q-values, $\{Q_\phi(s_t, a_{t:t+h})\}_{h \in [H]}$, simultaneously.
- **Causal Attention Mask:** A causal attention mask is applied to ensure that the Q-value for any given prefix length $h$ depends only on the state $s_t$ and the actions within that prefix $a_{t:t+h}$, not on subsequent actions in the full chunk $a_{t+h:t+H}$. This prevents "causal leakage" and ensures that each output represents a valid estimate for executing precisely $h$ actions.
- **Cross-Horizon Comparability:** Because all $H$ Q-values share a common Transformer backbone and are trained jointly against specific targets for each horizon, they are designed to reside on a common return scale. This property is crucial for the joint argmax selection process, allowing for direct comparison of expected returns across different chunk lengths. Theoretical arguments (Appendix G.3) formally establish this cross-horizon comparability.

#### 3.4. Multi-Step TD Objective for Critic Training

The critic $Q_\phi$ is trained using a multi-step TD loss (Equation 6) that considers every prefix length $h \in [H]$. For each $h$, an $h$-step return target $G_h(s_t, a_{t:t+h})$ is defined as the sum of $h$ on-policy rewards collected from the dataset, plus a bootstrapped value from the target critic $Q_{\bar{\phi}}$ at the subsequent state $s_{t+h}$.
- **Off-policy Bias Elimination:** Similar to other action-chunking methods, this multi-step target avoids off-policy bias because the critic conditions on the exact action sequence used to obtain the $h$-step rewards.
- **Bootstrapping Adaptive Policy:** The bootstrap value $Q_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{t+h}, \pi_*(s_{t+h}))$ is obtained by sampling $N$ candidate chunks from $\pi_\theta$ at $s_{t+h}$ and applying the adaptive policy extraction rule (joint argmax) using the target critic.
- **Gradient Averaging:** The loss function averages the squared error across all prefix lengths $h \in [H]$. This averaging is demonstrated to reduce gradient variance while preserving sparse reward signals (Appendix G.5).
- **Contraction Property:** The paper provides a proof (Appendix G.4) that the ACSAC Bellman operator, which incorporates this expected-prefix-max bootstrapping, is a $\gamma$-contraction whose unique fixed point is the action-value function of the adaptive policy $\pi_*$.

#### 3.5. Adaptive Policy Extraction

At each replanning state $s_t$, ACSAC's policy $\pi_*$ is extracted via a joint rejection sampling process:
1. **Candidate Generation:** $N$ full-length action chunks $\{a^{(n)}_{t:t+H}\}_{n \in [N]}$ are sampled from the flow BC policy $\pi_\theta(s_t, z^{(n)})$.
2. **Q-value Evaluation:** The causal Transformer critic $Q_\phi$ evaluates the Q-value for every prefix $a^{(n)}_{t:t+h}$ for all $n \in [N]$ and $h \in [H]$. This yields $N \times H$ candidate prefix Q-values.
3. **Optimal Prefix Selection:** The policy selects the candidate prefix $(n^*, h^*)$ that maximizes the Q-value: $(n^*, h^*) = \text{arg max}_{n \in [N], h \in [H]} Q_\phi(s_t, a^{(n)}_{t:t+h})$.
4. **Execution and Replanning:** The selected prefix $a^{(n^*)}_{t:t+h^*}$ is then executed. The agent replans at the end of this executed chunk.

This adaptive selection mechanism allows the execution horizon $h^*$ to vary per state. In stable phases, Q-values for longer prefixes may remain high, leading to larger $h^*$. In sensitive states where reactivity is critical, Q-values for shorter prefixes may be higher relative to longer ones, resulting in smaller $h^*$. The rejection sampling acts as an implicit behavior constraint, aligning the extracted policy with the data distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

ACSAC was evaluated on 25 long-horizon, sparse-reward robotic manipulation tasks from OGBench, across five domains: `scene-sparse`, `puzzle-3x3-sparse`, `cube-double`, `cube-triple`, and `cube-quadruple`. These environments require both long-range value propagation and precise manipulation at specific task phases. Experiments were conducted in both offline RL (1M training steps) and offline-to-online RL (1M offline + 1M online steps) settings.

Baselines included:
- **Single-step methods:** IQL, ReBRAC, FQL, BFN.
- **Multi-step methods (without chunked policy/critic):** FQL-n, BFN-n.
- **Fixed chunk size methods:** QC, QC-FQL, DEAS.

#### 4.2. Q1: Offline-to-Online RL Performance

The main results (Table 1) indicate that ACSAC achieved the best overall performance across the OGBench suite in both the offline and offline-to-online phases.
- **Overall Superiority:** ACSAC outperformed all single-step, multi-step, and fixed chunk size action-chunking baselines.
- **Impact on Long-Horizon Tasks:** The performance improvement was particularly evident on tasks requiring extended planning and adaptive control, such as `cube-triple` and `scene-sparse`. In these domains, the ability to switch between coarse transport and precise manipulation through adaptive chunk sizing proved beneficial.
- **Action Chunking Benefits:** Fixed chunk size methods (QC, QC-FQL, DEAS) already showed substantial improvements over single-step and multi-step flow baselines on the challenging `cube` domains, highlighting the general effectiveness of action chunking. ACSAC further extended these gains by introducing state-dependent adaptivity.
- **`cube-quadruple` Performance:** On `cube-quadruple`, fixed chunk size methods remained competitive online. The authors suggest this might be due to the default maximum chunk size (H=5) potentially being too short relative to the complex temporal structure of a four-object manipulation task, limiting the full potential of adaptivity.

#### 4.3. Q2: Qualitative and Quantitative Analyses of Adaptive Chunk Size and Q-values

- **Distribution of Chunk Size Decisions:** A qualitative analysis on a `cube-double` pick-and-place task (Figure 3) demonstrated that ACSAC's mean executed chunk size dynamically adapted to semantic task phases. Longer chunks (mean ≈ 4) were selected during the initial coarse approach to the cube. Intermediate chunks (mean ≈ 3) were used during grasping and transport. Progressively smaller chunks, reaching a mean of approximately 1, were chosen for precise placement, aligning the cube at the target. This behavior suggests that chunk size selection emerges directly from the learned prefix-conditioned Q-values, rather than being driven by explicit mechanisms like action entropy.

- **Prefix-Q Calibration and Cross-Horizon Comparability:** To assess the reliability of the Q-values, calibration plots were generated (Figure 4). These plots compared predicted Q-values ($\hat{Q}$) against realized Monte-Carlo returns ($\hat{G}$) for both the deployed adaptive policy and five fixed-h control policies. The results showed that all six curves closely tracked the diagonal line ($\hat{Q} = \hat{G}$) and largely coincided across the return range. This indicates that ACSAC's prefix Q-values are individually well-calibrated and mutually comparable across different prefix lengths $h$. This empirical validation supports the principled nature of the joint argmax rule used for policy deployment.

#### 4.4. Q3: Ablation Study

Ablation studies on `cube-double` and `cube-triple` investigated the impact of key design choices (Figure 5).
- **Maximum Chunk Size (H):**
 - $H=1$ (single-step execution) failed on both domains, confirming the need for action chunking in sparse-reward tasks.
 - $H=3$ and $H=5$ performed comparably, with $H=3$ slightly better on `cube-triple`.
 - $H=7$ showed performance degradation on `cube-triple`, consistent with the hypothesis that overly long chunks can make the behavior policy and critic harder to learn. $H=5$ was selected as a suitable default for broader execution horizon range.
- **Rejection Sampling Size (N):**
 - $N=2$ (fewer candidate chunks) weakened performance, particularly during online fine-tuning on `cube-triple`.
 - Increasing $N$ to 6 or 8 did not yield consistent performance gains, suggesting that $N=4$ provided a robust trade-off between computational cost and performance.
- **Causal Transformer Critic Contribution:**
 - Two control baselines, QT-QC and QT-BFN, were created by replacing the MLP critics of QC and BFN with ACSAC's causal Transformer critic, while keeping other components identical.
 - These controls improved over their original MLP-based baselines but did not fully close the performance gap with ACSAC, especially on `cube-triple`. This indicates that ACSAC's performance advantage is not solely attributable to the Transformer architecture, but crucially relies on the multi-horizon TD objective (Equation 6) and the joint argmax over candidate index and prefix length (Equation 8).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel contribution to reinforcement learning by proposing ACSAC, an Adaptive Chunk Size Actor-Critic framework. The primary significance lies in its ability to enable state-dependent and adaptive selection of action chunk sizes, moving beyond the limitations of fixed-chunking methods.

The key aspects contributing to its significance are:
- **Adaptive Control for Long-Horizon Tasks:** ACSAC demonstrates that dynamically adjusting the execution horizon based on the current state can significantly improve performance on challenging long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks. This adaptability allows the agent to maintain temporal consistency for efficient, coarse movements while also enabling high reactivity for precise manipulations, balancing these often conflicting requirements.
- **Cross-Horizon Calibrated Value Learning:** The use of a causal Transformer critic, trained with a multi-step TD objective across all prefix lengths, is central to ACSAC. This architecture ensures that prefix-conditioned Q-values are not only prefix-consistent (i.e., depend only on the prefix itself) but also cross-horizon comparable, meaning they reside on a common discounted-return scale. This calibration is fundamental for the joint argmax decision rule to reliably compare and select optimal chunk sizes.
- **Principled Policy Extraction:** The adaptive policy extraction mechanism, which combines a flow-based behavioral cloning policy with joint rejection sampling over candidate action chunks and prefix lengths, provides a robust method for action selection. This approach implicitly enforces behavior constraints and leverages the critic's value estimates to select the most promising action sequence and its appropriate duration.
- **Empirical Performance Improvements:** Experiments on OGBench manipulation tasks show that ACSAC achieves superior performance compared to single-step, multi-step, and fixed chunk size action-chunking baselines in both offline and offline-to-online RL settings. This empirical validation supports the effectiveness of the proposed adaptive chunk selection strategy.
- **Reduced Manual Tuning:** By automatically determining the appropriate chunk size per state, ACSAC eliminates the need for extensive, task-specific manual tuning of a fixed chunk size, potentially making RL methods more accessible and generalizable across different problems.

Potential impacts and future directions include:
- **Broader Applicability:** The concept of state-dependent chunking could be beneficial across a wider range of sequential decision-making problems where the optimal planning horizon varies, such as in hierarchical planning or adaptive control systems.
- **Scaling and Efficiency:** Future work could explore scaling the maximum chunk size (H) and rejection sampling size (N) in conjunction with larger Transformer critic capacities. This could lead to more nuanced and effective long-term planning capabilities.
- **Robustness to Stochasticity:** The current theoretical analysis assumes deterministic transitions. Extending ACSAC to stochastic environments would broaden its applicability to real-world scenarios, which often involve inherent uncertainty.
- **Integration with Advanced Models:** The paper suggests integrating state-dependent chunk sizes with large vision-language-action (VLA) models. Given that chunked value learning has shown promise in scaling to such complex models, combining adaptive chunking with VLAs could lead to more robust and intelligent robotic systems capable of complex, long-horizon tasks from high-dimensional observations and language instructions.
- **Performance on Diverse Domains:** Further evaluation on navigation tasks (e.g., `antmaze`, `humanoidmaze`) where action-chunked methods have historically shown limitations could provide insights into its generalizability across different types of control problems.
