# Improving and Accelerating Offline RL in Large Discrete Action Spaces with Structured Policy Initialization

> [!summary] Summary
> Structured Policy Initialization (SPIN), a two-stage framework for offline reinforcement learning in large discrete combinatorial action spaces, achieves higher asymptotic performance and significantly faster training times. It pre-trains an Action Structure Model to capture action dependencies before optimizing the control policy, leading to more robust and efficient learning.

## Problem

- Offline reinforcement learning in large discrete combinatorial action spaces faces intractable computational complexity due to the exponential growth in joint actions.
- Existing methods often struggle to capture coherent and valid inter-sub-action dependencies, leading to suboptimal or nonsensical actions.
- The simultaneous learning of action representation and control policy in complex spaces often results in slow convergence and unstable learning.

## Method

- **SPIN** decouples ==action structure== learning from policy optimization by introducing a two-stage framework.
- An Action Structure Model (ASM) is pre-trained in a self-supervised manner using a masked conditional modeling objective to capture the manifold of plausible actions from static datasets.
- A policy network is then trained with the ASM's learned representation frozen, leveraging lightweight components for efficient policy optimization, compatible with actor-critic offline RL algorithms.

## Results

- SPIN achieved the highest overall average return of 594.1 across benchmarks, surpassing the next-best baseline (SAINT at 572.1) and showing over **13%** improvement on challenging `random-medium-expert` datasets.
- The framework demonstrated superior training efficiency, reaching **95%** of asymptotic performance up to 3.8 times faster than baselines, with the ASM pre-training contributing a minor portion of total wall-clock time.
- A linear probe showed the pre-trained ASM improved exact-match accuracy for full action tuples by **45x** (to **4.52%**) compared to a random initialization, quantifying its ability to capture cross-dimensional dependencies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the learning of action structure from control significantly improves both the asymptotic performance and training efficiency of offline RL agents in large combinatorial action spaces.
> - The pre-trained Action Structure Model effectively captures cross-joint action coordination, providing a strong inductive bias that simplifies the subsequent control policy learning task.
> - The quality of the pre-trained action representation is critical for downstream policy performance, enabling rapid adaptation and high returns even with minimal policy training steps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) in discrete combinatorial action spaces addresses decision-making problems where an agent must select multiple interdependent sub-actions simultaneously, forming a coherent joint action. Such action spaces are prevalent in real-world applications including healthcare, robotic assembly, recommender systems, and ride-sharing. The inherent challenge arises from the exponential growth in the number of possible joint actions with increasing sub-action dimensions and cardinalities, making traditional RL methods that maximize a Q-function or parameterize a policy over the full action set intractable.

Offline reinforcement learning (RL) is particularly relevant in these domains, as online exploration can be prohibitively costly or unsafe. However, standard offline RL methods are not designed to handle the scale and complexity of large discrete combinatorial action spaces. Existing approaches to address these spaces often fall into several categories, each with limitations:
- **Factorized policies:** These methods simplify policy learning by assuming conditional independence between sub-actions (e.g., Tang et al., 2022; Beeson et al., 2024). While this factorization maintains tractability, it limits representational capacity, often leading to incoherent or invalid actions by failing to capture critical cross-dimensional dependencies.
- **Joint learning of action representation and control:** Some approaches attempt to simultaneously learn the underlying action structure and optimize a policy (e.g., Zhang et al., 2018; Landers et al., 2024; Landers et al., 2025). This conflation of objectives can render the learning process slow and unstable, as the model must address both tasks concurrently.
- **Task-specific methods:** Certain RL methods for combinatorial action spaces rely on domain-specific knowledge, limiting their generalizability (e.g., Nazari et al., 2018; Delarue et al., 2020).
- **Online learning methods:** General-purpose methods exist for large action spaces in online settings (e.g., Dulac-Arnold et al., 2015; Tavakoli et al., 2018), but they are not easily adapted to the constraints of static offline datasets.
- **Representation learning for flat action spaces:** Methods like MERLION (Gu et al., 2022) learn action representations, but they treat actions as atomic entities and typically require computationally expensive nearest-neighbor searches over the full, enumerated action set, which is infeasible for combinatorial settings.

This work, Structured Policy Initialization (SPIN), is positioned to address the limitations of these prior approaches by decoupling the problem of learning action structure from that of learning control. It aims to develop a general-purpose framework that can handle combinatorial action spaces in offline RL more efficiently and effectively by explicitly modeling action composition and coherence. This contrasts with common self-supervised pre-training in RL, which is often state- or trajectory-centric and frequently presumes online interaction or multi-task fine-tuning. SPIN's action-centric pre-training objective aims to provide a structured initialization for policies in these complex action spaces, without requiring online interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to improve the performance and accelerate the training of offline reinforcement learning agents operating in large discrete combinatorial action spaces. The motivation stems from several critical challenges inherent in these environments:

1. **Exponentially Large Action Spaces:** In combinatorial action spaces, a joint action is composed of multiple sub-actions, each chosen from a discrete set. The total number of possible joint actions grows exponentially with the number of sub-action dimensions and the cardinality of each dimension. This exponential scale makes direct maximization over actions (e.g., in Q-learning) or explicit policy parameterization over the entire action space computationally intractable for most standard offline RL methods.
2. **Action Coherence and Validity:** Beyond the sheer size, the problem requires that the selected sub-actions form *coherent* and *valid* combinations. Randomly sampling sub-actions or assuming independence often leads to nonsensical or physically impossible joint actions, which are ineffective for control. Capturing these underlying structural dependencies between sub-actions is crucial for effective decision-making.
3. **Limitations of Existing Approaches:**
 - **Representational Capacity vs. Tractability Trade-off:** Current methods designed for these spaces often simplify the problem. Approaches that assume conditional independence across sub-actions (factorized policies) gain tractability but sacrifice the capacity to model complex interactions, leading to suboptimal policies.
 - **Learning Stability and Speed:** Methods that attempt to learn the action representation and optimize the control policy simultaneously frequently suffer from slow convergence and instability. This is because the agent must concurrently discover the underlying geometry of valid actions and learn how to act optimally within that geometry.
 - **Offline Constraints:** The offline RL setting, where online exploration is not permitted, exacerbates these issues, as agents cannot interact with the environment to correct errors or explore the action space more effectively. Overestimation errors, where actions outside the data support are assigned high values, can propagate and destabilize learning.

The key motivation for developing SPIN is to decouple these two intertwined problems: discovering the structure of valid actions and learning a control policy. By first training a dedicated Action Structure Model (ASM) to capture the manifold of plausible actions from the dataset, and then freezing this learned representation during policy optimization, the authors hypothesize that the control problem becomes significantly simpler and more tractable. This separation is intended to lead to faster training times, more stable learning, and ultimately, higher-performing policies by allowing agents to exploit the inherent action geometry rather than attempting to learn it from scratch alongside control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Structured Policy Initialization (SPIN) is a two-stage framework designed to address offline reinforcement learning in discrete combinatorial action spaces. It explicitly decouples the learning of action structure from the learning of control.

#### 3.1. Action Structure Modeling (ASM)

The first stage of SPIN focuses on pre-training an Action Structure Model (ASM) to capture the structure of plausible actions, conditioned on the environment state. This pre-training is conducted in a self-supervised manner without reward supervision, making it fundamentally an offline procedure that utilizes the static dataset.

- **ASM Architecture:** The core of the ASM is a Transformer encoder, denoted as $f_{\text{ASM}}(s, a; \psi)$, parameterized by $\psi$. This encoder processes an input sequence $X \in \mathbb{R}^{(M+N) \times d}$, which is formed by concatenating $M$ learned state embeddings $(x_{s_1}, \ldots, x_{s_M})$ with $N$ sub-action embeddings $(x_{a_1}, \ldots, x_{a_N})$. Positional encodings are omitted across sub-actions to preserve permutation-equivariance.
- **Training Objective: Masked Conditional Modeling:** The ASM is trained using a masked conditional modeling objective, analogous to masked language modeling in BERT. For each state-action pair $(s, a)$ sampled from the dataset, a subset $M \subseteq \{1, \ldots, N\}$ of sub-action indices is randomly selected for perturbation. For each chosen sub-action $a_i$ where $i \in M$, it is either:
 1. Replaced by a special mask token (80% probability).
 2. Replaced by an element drawn uniformly at random from its full sub-action space $A_i$ (10% probability).
 3. Left unchanged (10% probability).
 This perturbed action $a_{\text{mask}}$ is then fed into $f_{\text{ASM}}$, which produces contextualized per-slot embeddings $h_{a_i}$ for each sub-action slot $i$. Finally, each $h_{a_i}$ is passed through a slot-specific linear head $f_i: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{|A_i|}$ to output logits over the corresponding sub-action space $A_i$. A cross-entropy loss $L_{\text{ASM}}$ is computed only for the *masked* sub-actions, aiming to reconstruct their original values. This objective enables the ASM to learn the manifold of valid actions by predicting missing or corrupted parts of a joint action based on the state and the other sub-actions.

#### 3.2. Policy Learning with a Frozen Representation

In the second stage, SPIN performs policy learning by leveraging the frozen representation learned by the ASM. This means the parameters $\psi$ of the ASM are fixed, and only lightweight components of the policy network are updated.

- **Policy Architecture:** SPIN implements its policy $\pi_{\theta}$ using a variation of the SAINT architecture (Landers et al., 2025). During a policy forward pass, $M$ state embeddings and $N$ learnable action queries are passed through the *frozen*, permutation-equivariant Transformer from the ASM stage. This process yields contextualized embeddings $z_1, \ldots, z_N$. Each embedding $z_i$ implicitly encodes information about the state, its corresponding action query, and its relationships to other sub-actions through shared attention, which was learned during ASM pre-training. These contextualized embeddings $z_i$ are then fed into sub-action-specific MLP heads $f_i$, which output logits $\ell_i$ over their respective sub-actions. These logits parameterize categorical distributions $\pi_{\theta}(\cdot | s, z_i) = \text{softmax}(\ell_i)$, from which individual sub-actions $a_i$ are sampled. The joint policy is factorized over these contextualized distributions: $\pi_{\theta}(a | s) = \prod_{i=1}^N \pi_{\theta}(a_i | s, z_i)$.
- **Policy Training:** The policy network $\pi_{\theta}$ updates only the parameters of the learnable action queries and the output MLP heads, while the core ASM encoder remains frozen. This separation ensures that the previously learned action structure is preserved, making the policy optimization tractable and stable.
- **Compatibility with Offline RL Methods:** This design makes SPIN broadly compatible with actor-critic offline RL algorithms where the actor update is based on weighted log-likelihood maximization over actions in the dataset, such as IQL (Kostrikov et al., 2021), AWAC (Nair et al., 2020), and BCQ (Fujimoto et al., 2019). The framework is not directly compatible with value-regularization methods like CQL (Kumar et al., 2020) that require global operations over the joint action space without factorization, as this conflicts with SPIN's objective of modeling cross-dimensional action coherence through contextualized distributions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of SPIN was conducted on discretized variants of the DeepMind Control Suite and a Maze navigation task, encompassing a range of action dimensionalities (6 to 38 sub-action dimensions) and cardinalities (3 to 30 bins per dimension), resulting in joint action spaces spanning from hundreds to over $10^{56}$ possible actions. Datasets varied in quality (medium, expert, medium-expert, random-medium-expert) and size ($2 \times 10^5$ to $2 \times 10^6$ transitions). SPIN was compared against Factored IQL (F-IQL), Autoregressive IQL (AR-IQL), and SAINT (a Transformer-based policy that jointly learns structure and control). The primary offline RL objective used was IQL, with additional evaluations using AWAC and BCQ.

#### 4.1. Asymptotic Performance and Training Efficiency

- **Overall Performance:** SPIN achieved the highest overall average return of 594.1 across all benchmarks, surpassing the next-best baseline, SAINT, at 572.1. This improvement was consistent across environments and particularly pronounced in the more heterogeneous and challenging `medium-expert` and `random-medium-expert` datasets. On `random-medium-expert` datasets, SPIN's average return of 499.2 represented a >13% improvement over SAINT (438.9).
- **Training Efficiency:** SPIN demonstrated superior computational efficiency, reaching 95% of F-IQL's asymptotic performance in an overall average of 223.3 wall-clock minutes. This was approximately 2.5 times faster than F-IQL (558.1 minutes) and 3.8 times faster than SAINT (845.0 minutes). The acceleration was most notable in `medium-expert` datasets, where SPIN required 62 minutes compared to over 250 minutes for other methods. The ASM pre-training stage constituted a minor portion of the total wall-clock time, ranging from 1.4% to 5.6% across different tasks and datasets.

#### 4.2. Robustness to Action Cardinality

- **Scalability:** When tested on the `dog-trot` environment with 38 sub-action dimensions and varying cardinalities from 3 to 30 bins (resulting in action spaces up to $\approx 1.35 \times 10^{56}$ actions), SPIN consistently achieved the highest average return at every cardinality. The performance gap between SPIN and baselines increased with action space size; at 30 bins, SPIN achieved 703.9 average return compared to 562.5 for SAINT, an improvement of over 25%.
- **Efficiency under Scale:** SPIN's training efficiency remained stable as action cardinality increased, while baselines exhibited stagnation or deterioration in performance and efficiency.

#### 4.3. Mechanisms Underlying SPIN’s Effectiveness

- **Effect of Representation Quality on Policy Performance:** Experiments varied the duration of ASM pre-training (10-100 epochs). Downstream policy returns generally improved with more pre-training, with significant gains observed within the first 20 epochs. Policies initialized from ASMs trained for just 20 epochs surpassed the fully trained F-IQL baseline in all tasks. Untrained ASMs (Epoch 0) resulted in poor policy performance. This indicates that the quality of the pre-trained action representation is a primary determinant of final policy performance.
- **Quantifying Representation Quality:** A linear probe was used to assess how well the ASM captured joint action dependencies. In the `dog-trot` environment (38 sub-actions, 30 bins):
 - A randomly initialized ASM achieved 76.6% per-slot accuracy but only 0.10% exact-match accuracy for the full action tuple.
 - A pre-trained ASM achieved 90.0% per-slot accuracy and 4.52% exact-match accuracy, a 45x improvement in tuple accuracy. Crucially, this observed tuple accuracy (4.52%) was more than double the accuracy expected under an assumption of independence (0.90^38 ≈ 1.83%), providing quantitative evidence that the pre-trained representation captures substantial cross-joint coordination.
- **Isolating the Contribution of the Learned Representation (SPIN-Distill):** A variant called SPIN-Distill replaced the Transformer-based policy in Stage 2 with a lightweight, attention-free MLP, which was distilled from the frozen ASM. SPIN-Distill achieved asymptotic performance comparable to the full SPIN model, while substantially outperforming all other baselines and training nearly 8 times faster than SAINT. This suggests that SPIN’s performance gains are primarily attributable to the quality of the pre-trained representation rather than the complexity of the policy network's architecture.
- **Emergent Rapid Adaptation:** After only 10,000 gradient steps (1% of the total training budget), SPIN's policies often achieved at least 90% of the target performance. In contrast, baselines improved much more gradually. For instance, on the `humanoid` task with `medium-expert` data, SPIN reached 93.4% of target performance, while SAINT achieved only 9.3%. This rapid learning confirms that the ASM provides a strong structural prior, simplifying downstream control learning.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Offline RL Training Objective (Appendix D)

- SPIN’s performance and efficiency benefits extended to other offline RL objectives, AWAC and BCQ, consistently achieving higher average returns and faster training times compared to the respective baselines.

#### 4.5. Performance on Maze (Appendix E)

- On the Maze navigation task, all methods achieved near-expert performance, but SPIN maintained a consistent efficiency advantage, being approximately 1.5 times faster than F-IQL.

#### 4.6. Comparison to Trajectory-Centric Pre-training (Appendix F)

- Compared to REPREM, a trajectory-centric pre-training method, SPIN achieved higher returns (499.2 vs. 372.2) and was significantly faster (2.5 minutes vs. 587.9 minutes for pre-training, over 200x faster). This highlights the benefit of an action-centric masked modeling objective for combinatorial action spaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Structured Policy Initialization (SPIN) presents a framework that addresses a central challenge in offline reinforcement learning: effective and efficient control in large discrete combinatorial action spaces. By explicitly decoupling the learning of action structure from policy optimization, SPIN demonstrates several significant advancements.

Firstly, SPIN provides a principled method for managing the combinatorial complexity of action spaces. Instead of either ignoring critical inter-sub-action dependencies (as in factored policies) or attempting to learn them concurrently with control (as in joint-learning approaches), SPIN’s two-stage process allows for a dedicated, self-supervised discovery of the underlying manifold of valid and coherent actions. This representation-first approach means the subsequent control task operates on a more tractable and meaningful feature space.

Secondly, the empirical results consistently demonstrate that SPIN achieves both higher asymptotic performance and significantly faster convergence rates compared to existing state-of-the-art baselines. Improvements in average return of up to 39% and reductions in time to reach target performance by up to 12.8x indicate a substantial leap in efficiency and effectiveness. These gains are particularly pronounced in challenging, heterogeneous datasets and as the cardinality of the action space increases, highlighting SPIN's robustness and scalability. The rapid adaptation capabilities observed, where policies achieve high performance within a small fraction of the total training budget, suggest that the pre-trained action structure acts as a strong inductive bias, simplifying the downstream RL problem.

The quantitative analysis using linear probes, demonstrating a 45x improvement in exact-match accuracy for joint actions by the pre-trained ASM compared to a random baseline, directly supports the claim that SPIN's effectiveness is rooted in its ability to capture cross-joint coordination. Furthermore, the strong performance of SPIN-Distill, a variant with a simpler policy architecture, reinforces that the quality of the learned representation, rather than the complexity of the control policy itself, is a primary driver of success.

The potential impact of SPIN extends to various real-world applications where decision-making involves complex, interdependent actions and online exploration is constrained or unsafe. Industries such as healthcare (e.g., treatment planning with multiple simultaneous interventions), robotics (e.g., complex assembly tasks), intelligent transportation (e.g., ride-sharing fleet management), and recommender systems could benefit from more robust and efficient offline RL agents.

Future research directions suggested by this work include extending compatibility to value-regularization methods (like CQL) through tailored objectives that respect the learned action structure, and adapting SPIN to action spaces with different structural assumptions (e.g., ordered or sequence-based sub-actions). Ultimately, SPIN offers a foundational framework that could enable broader and more practical deployment of reinforcement learning in high-dimensional, structured domains by making complex action space problems more tractable.
