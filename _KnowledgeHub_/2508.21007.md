# Rapid Mismatch Estimation via Neural Network Informed Variational Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> The GRASP Lab at the University of Pennsylvania developed Rapid Mismatch Estimation (RME), a framework combining a neural network and variational inference to estimate robot end-effector mass and center-of-mass mismatches in approximately 400 milliseconds, using only proprioceptive feedback. This method allows compliant controllers to maintain passivity and stability during interactions with unknown loads, achieving robust real-time adaptation without external force-torque sensors.

## Problem

- Compliant robotic control strategies struggle with model mismatch, leading to instability, poor performance, and safety issues when interacting with uncertain environments or objects.
- Existing online learning and estimation techniques for model mismatch are often too slow for real-time adaptation, sensitive to noise, or rely on external force-torque sensors, limiting practicality.
- Maintaining passivity and stability in passive impedance controllers is undermined by inaccurate dynamics models, especially during interactions with unknown or dynamically changing loads.

## Method

- A probabilistic, controller-agnostic framework called **Rapid Mismatch Estimation (RME)** is introduced, combining a neural network (NN) with a ==variational inference (VI)== solver.
- The NN provides an informed prior for the VI solver, enabling rapid estimation of end-effector mass and center-of-mass (CoM) parameters from collected pseudo-wrenches.
- The framework operates solely on proprioceptive feedback (joint torque estimates), eliminating the need for external force-torque sensors for mismatch detection and estimation.

## Results

- The framework estimates end-effector mass and center-of-mass within approximately **400 milliseconds**, with an average of **226 ms** in static scenarios.
- Neural network guidance reduced the Mean Squared Error for CoM estimation (e.g., r_z by **60%**), demonstrating its effectiveness in achieving rapid and accurate parameter convergence.
- In real-world experiments, RME enabled a **7-DoF** robot to adapt to unknown, dynamic payloads during pick-and-place and human-robot interaction tasks, maintaining passivity and stability where traditional methods failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a neural network to inform the prior distribution for variational inference significantly accelerates convergence and improves accuracy, particularly for under-determined CoM estimation.
> - A short data collection window (e.g., 200 ms) is sufficient to achieve rapid and robust parameter estimation, balancing speed with accuracy.
> - The proposed compensation mechanism, by augmenting the control torque externally, ensures the preservation of the underlying controller's passivity and stability guarantees, even with imperfect estimates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic systems capable of safe and adaptive physical interaction with uncertain environments, unknown objects, and humans is a prominent area of research in robotics. Compliant control strategies are fundamental to achieving this objective. While some commercial robots incorporate basic impedance or admittance control, many struggle to balance passivity to physical perturbations with precision in task execution, a balance that humans achieve through neuro-musculoskeletal compliance and redundant degrees of freedom.

Passivity-based impedance controllers have been extensively developed and demonstrated across various applications, including grasping, manipulation, and robotic surgery. A key limitation of these model-based control techniques is their reliance on accurate dynamics models of the robot and the objects it interacts with. Discrepancies between the actual system dynamics and the robot's internal model, known as model mismatch, can lead to instability, poor tracking performance, and unsafe behaviors. Traditional approaches to mitigate model mismatch, such as ad-hoc gain tuning or adding PID layers, often compromise the inherent passivity, compliance, and safety guarantees of the control system.

Prior research has addressed the problem of model mismatch through various online learning and estimation techniques. These include online learning of residual dynamics based on predictive errors, episodic learning of inverse dynamics (ID) to account for multiple mismatch factors, and the use of Gaussian Processes to improve ID learning. Other approaches involve compensating for mismatch via reinforcement learning policies or improving control policies through quantification of predictive error. However, many of these methods face challenges such as sensitivity to feedback measurement noise, requiring extended estimation times that preclude real-time performance, or making conservative assumptions about dynamic changes. For instance, some require initial conditions of zero mismatch or assume smooth dynamic transitions.

More directly related to online parameter estimation, existing system identification frameworks may require significant calibration time (e.g., 10 seconds before trajectory execution) or rely on external force/torque (FT) sensors for center-of-mass (CoM) estimation. The use of external FT sensors can decrease robot dexterity, reduce allowable payload, and increase assembly costs.

This work contributes to the landscape by addressing the gap for a real-time estimation framework that can rapidly adapt to abrupt changes in robot end-effector dynamics, arising from interactions with unknown environments, without necessitating external force-torque sensors. It seeks to provide robots with a human-like ability to estimate and adapt to dynamic model mismatches instantaneously, thereby maintaining passivity and task execution while interacting physically.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robots to estimate and adapt to dynamic model mismatches in real-time. This capability is intended to allow robots to maintain passivity and effectively perform tasks, particularly in scenarios involving physical interaction with unknown or dynamically changing objects, mirroring human adaptive abilities.

The primary motivations for pursuing this objective stem from several challenges in current robotic control:

1. **Ensuring Safety and Compliance in Human-Centric Environments**: As robots are increasingly deployed in environments shared with humans, ensuring soft, safe, and compliant physical interactions is paramount. Model mismatch directly undermines the safety and compliance of impedance-controlled robots, leading to unpredictable and potentially unsafe reactions during contact.
2. **Maintaining Passivity and Stability with Unknown Loads**: Passive impedance controllers are designed to guarantee stability during free motion and physical contact. However, these guarantees are predicated on accurate dynamic models. When robots interact with objects of unknown or varying mass and inertia, the model accuracy degrades, jeopardizing the passivity and stability of the closed-loop system. The goal is to correct for these mismatches to preserve the fundamental properties of passive control.
3. **Real-time Adaptation to Abrupt Dynamic Changes**: Unlike many existing methods that require lengthy calibration or are slow to converge, there is a need for rapid adaptation to instantaneous changes in end-effector dynamics. This is crucial in dynamic interaction scenarios, such as a human attaching or altering the contents of an object held by a robot. The motivation is to enable estimation and compensation within hundreds of milliseconds to prevent task failures or unsafe states.
4. **Eliminating Reliance on External Force-Torque Sensors**: Many payload estimation methods rely on external force-torque (FT) sensors. While effective, these sensors add complexity, cost, reduce the robot's effective payload, and can decrease dexterity. A key motivation is to perform mismatch estimation using only the robot's proprioceptive feedback (e.g., joint torque estimates), thereby enhancing the practicality and versatility of the robotic system.
5. **Controller Agnosticism**: The framework aims to be compatible with various passive impedance control strategies or even passivity-preserving reinforcement learning policies. This broad applicability increases the utility of the proposed solution across diverse robotic platforms and control paradigms.
6. **Quantifying Uncertainty**: Moving beyond single point estimates, the objective includes providing a probabilistic estimate of mismatch parameters. Quantifying uncertainty can offer valuable information for subsequent robust decision-making and motion planning.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for robust, real-time, and sensor-efficient adaptation mechanisms in compliant robot control, to overcome the limitations imposed by model mismatch and facilitate safer, more versatile human-robot and robot-environment interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce Rapid Mismatch Estimation (RME), a probabilistic, controller-agnostic framework designed for real-time quantification of dynamic model mismatch. The methodology integrates a neural network (NN) with a variational inference (VI) solver.

**Problem Formulation:**
The rigid-body dynamics of an n-DoF manipulator arm are defined by `M(q)¨q + C(q, ˙q)˙q + G(q) = τ_c + τ_ext`. Model mismatch, `τ_mm`, is assumed to arise from unknown mass `m` and center-of-mass `r_CoM` at the end-effector. The objective is to estimate these parameters, `θ = {m, r_x, r_y, r_z}`, online and compensate for `τ_mm` by augmenting the control torque `τ_c` to `ˆτ_c = τ_c - J(q)^T * [ˆr_CoM(θ) x ˆF_m(θ)]`. This estimation relies solely on proprioceptive feedback (joint torque estimates) without external FT sensors.

**Control Paradigm (Preliminaries):**
While RME is designed to be controller-agnostic, the experiments utilize an extension of a dynamical systems (DS) based passive interaction controller, specifically the Constrained Passive Interaction Controller (CPIC). This controller drives the end-effector's desired motion via a globally asymptotically stable DS, `˙x = f(x)`, and enforces passivity via a velocity-based impedance control law `F_c(x) = G_x(q) - D(x)(˙x - f(x))`. To handle kinematic constraints (e.g., joint limits, self-collision), `F_c(x)` is optimized through a Quadratic Programming (QP) based safety filter. The QP minimizes `||J(q)^(-T)τ_c - F_c(x)||_2^2` subject to robot dynamics and exponential control barrier functions (CBFs). The passivity guarantees of this controller depend on accurate dynamics models, thus motivating RME.

**RME Framework:**
The RME framework operates in several stages:

1. **Mismatch Detection**: A detection algorithm continuously monitors the `L2`-norm of external joint torques, `||τ_ext||_2^2`. It distinguishes true dynamic mismatch from transient human perturbations by identifying rapid increases or decreases in `||τ_ext||_2^2` that exceed an activation threshold (0.7-1.1 Nm), followed by stabilization within a specified band (0.2 Nm) over a 230 ms period. Additionally, it checks that the principal applied force at the end-effector is along the global z-direction, with minimal forces in x and y, to confirm a load-induced mismatch.
2. **Data Collection**: Upon detection, a dataset `D` of `τ_ext` measurements is collected over a 200 ms time window. These joint torques are converted into a sequence of `M` (typically `M=20`) pseudo-wrenches `ˆW_ext ∈ R^6` at the end-effector using the damped pseudo-inverse of the Jacobian: `ˆW_ext = (J(q)J(q)^T + λI)^(-1) J(q)τ_ext`.
3. **Neural Network Model Mismatch Estimator (f_NN)**:
 - **Architecture**: The NN processes the `M`-dimensional sequence of pseudo-wrenches. It employs a 1D convolution layer (kernel size 5, output dimension 64) for local pattern capture, followed by positional embedding and a multi-head attention mechanism (8 heads) to capture global dependencies within the sequence. Mean pooling of attention outputs then feeds into a sequence of three Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) blocks (linear (64->256), ReLU, linear (256->64)), culminating in a final linear layer (64->4) that regresses to the mean mismatch parameters `θ`.
 - **Training**: The `f_NN` is trained in simulation using a dataset generated from 350 simulations of manipulator dynamics with varying mismatch parameters. Mean-Squared Error (MSE) is used as the loss objective, with dropout (rate 0.1) applied after each MLP block to prevent overfitting. Data augmentation is also used to enhance robustness to noise and facilitate sim-to-real transfer.
4. **Variational Inference (VI) Solver**:
 - **Bayesian Inference Problem**: The goal is to estimate the posterior distribution `p(θ | D)`. This is formulated as a Bayesian inference problem, where the likelihood `p(D | θ)` is defined as a Gaussian distribution based on the manipulator's Inverse Dynamics (ID) model `f_ID(q, θ)` and a likelihood noise `σ_likelihood`.
 - **NN-Informed Prior**: A key aspect is the construction of the prior distribution `p(θ)`. This is a Gaussian distribution whose mean (`μ_prior`) is provided by the `f_NN(D)` prediction, with a fixed diagonal covariance `σ_prior = [0.5, 0.02, 0.02, 0.05]`. This NN-informed prior guides the VI toward plausible solutions, addressing the under-determination of the problem in short time windows.
 - **Approximation**: Since the true posterior `p(θ | D)` is intractable due to the non-linear dynamics, VI is used to construct a mean-field approximation `q_φ(θ | D)`. This approximation is parameterized by variational parameters `φ = (μ_m, μ_x, μ_y, μ_z, σ^2_m, σ^2_x, σ^2_y, σ^2_z)`.
 - **Optimization**: The optimal variational parameters `φ*` are found by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between `q_φ(θ | D)` and `p(θ | D)`, which is equivalent to maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). This optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate `η = 0.025` and gradient clipping, via stochastic gradient descent and the reparameterization trick. The optimization converges when variational parameters stabilize.
5. **Compensation**: The estimated mismatch parameters `ˆθ` from the VI solver are used to augment the chosen passive impedance controller, `τ_c`. The compensation term `J(q)^T * [ˆr_CoM(θ) x ˆF_m(θ)]` is applied as an additional torque, `ˆτ_c`, effectively canceling the unmodeled dynamics. This approach keeps the RME framework control-agnostic, as the compensation is provided externally to the nominal controller, allowing it to maintain its original passivity and stability proofs.

**Computational Setup**:
The QP optimization for CPIC uses CVXGEN. The RME components (NN inference and VI optimization) are executed on a workstation CPU (11th Gen Intel Core i7-11700K @ 3.60 GHz), with no GPU acceleration required at runtime.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the performance and characteristics of the Rapid Mismatch Estimation (RME) framework, evaluated through a combination of ablation studies, static experiments, and dynamic interaction scenarios on a real 7-DoF Franka Emika manipulator.

**Estimation Speed:**
A primary finding is the speed of adaptation. RME demonstrates the ability to estimate and adapt to sudden changes in end-effector mass and center of mass (CoM) in approximately 400 milliseconds. In static experiments, the average estimation time was measured at 226 ms.

**Impact of Neural Network Guidance:**
An ablation study quantitatively assessed the contribution of the Neural Network (NN) model mismatch estimator (`f_NN`) to the variational inference (VI) process. By comparing VI performance when its Gaussian prior's mean (`μ_prior`) was set by `f_NN(·)` versus being set to zero, it was observed that NN guidance significantly reduced the Mean Squared Error (MSE) in CoM estimation. For instance, the MSE for `r_z` was reduced from `6.513 × 10^-3 m^2` to `2.560 × 10^-3 m^2`, and similar improvements were noted for `r_x` and `r_y`. This indicates that the NN effectively provides a robust initial estimate, which is crucial for the rapid convergence of VI, particularly for CoM parameters whose estimation can be sensitive to non-linear dynamics.

**Effect of Data Collection Interval:**
An analysis of different data collection interval lengths showed that a 200 ms window achieved a balance between estimation accuracy and speed. While longer intervals (e.g., 300 ms) sometimes resulted in slightly lower MSE for certain parameters, the 200 ms interval was deemed optimal for fast and robust estimation in the context of abrupt dynamic changes.

**Static Experiments:**
In static settings, where unknown masses were suddenly added to the end-effector while the manipulator aimed to maintain an equilibrium position using a constrained passive impedance controller (CPIC), RME provided accurate and rapid estimations of mass and CoM parameters. Parity plots for mass predictions over 60 independent experiments demonstrated consistent and stable mass estimation. The robot, with RME adaptation, rapidly converged back to its original equilibrium position, whereas without RME, it deviated significantly due to the uncompensated load. A limitation was observed when the CoM of the added mass was close to the end-effector's z-axis, leading to reduced observability of CoM offset due to non-linear inverse dynamics dependencies. However, the system still converged safely, and subsequent predictions could correct CoM if the robot's orientation changed.

**Dynamic Experiments:**

1. **Tracking a Stable Limit Cycle**: When the manipulator was commanded to track a stable limit cycle in the y-z plane, RME successfully estimated mismatch parameters, allowing the robot to converge to the desired trajectory. Without RME, the robot failed to follow the trajectory, instead converging to a spurious attractor where the impedance controller's task force was balanced by the gravitational force from the uncompensated mass.
2. **Sequential Adaptation with Human-Robot Interactions (pHRI)**: In a collaborative scenario, a human attached an unknown basket to the robot, dynamically added and removed items, and perturbed the robot. RME consistently provided accurate sequential estimations of mismatch parameters. The robot maintained its target position and orientation while remaining passive to human-generated forces. Notably, even when the model initially confused human perturbations with model mismatch, it allowed the perturbation and then rapidly corrected its estimate once the perturbation ceased. Further experiments involving pick-and-place tasks with unknown, heavy objects demonstrated that RME enabled tasks that were impossible for the robot without adaptation, maintaining stability and passivity throughout the interaction.

**Preservation of Passivity and Stability:**
A theoretical proof was provided, demonstrating that when the augmented control law with RME compensation is applied, the closed-loop system behavior *remains passive* with respect to the input-output port `(F_ext + ∆F_mm, ˙x)`, even with imperfect mismatch parameter estimation (where `∆F_mm` represents the remaining estimation error). While imperfect estimates could potentially lead to locally asymptotically stable unwanted equilibria, the fundamental passivity property is preserved. This is a crucial finding, supporting the safety guarantees of the RME framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Rapid Mismatch Estimation (RME) framework presents a notable contribution to the field of robotics, particularly in the domain of human-robot interaction and adaptive control. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Robustness in Human-Robot Interaction**: By providing real-time, online adaptation to sudden changes in end-effector dynamics, RME directly addresses a critical challenge in collaborative robotics. The ability to rapidly compensate for unknown payloads ensures that torque-controlled robots maintain passivity and stability, even during abrupt physical interactions with humans or dynamic environments. This reduces the risk of unsafe behaviors or task failures, fostering safer and more reliable human-robot collaboration.
2. **Real-time Adaptation to Unknown Dynamic Changes**: Current methods often struggle with the speed required for true real-time adaptation to abrupt changes. RME's demonstrated estimation time of approximately 400 ms allows for instantaneous compensation, which is essential for tasks where payloads are dynamically altered (e.g., a human adding objects to a robot-held basket). This capability extends the operational envelope of robots into more unpredictable and unstructured environments.
3. **Reduced Hardware Dependency**: A key impact is the elimination of reliance on external force-torque (FT) sensors at the end-effector for mismatch estimation. By leveraging only the robot's proprioceptive feedback, RME potentially reduces the complexity and cost of robotic systems, while simultaneously preserving dexterity and maximizing allowable payload. This makes advanced adaptive capabilities more accessible and practical for a wider range of robotic platforms and applications.
4. **Controller Agnosticism**: The design of RME as a controller-agnostic framework means it can be integrated with various passive impedance controllers, and potentially other passivity-preserving control policies. This versatility broadens its applicability across different control architectures and robotic systems, offering a general solution to the model mismatch problem.
5. **Probabilistic Estimation and Uncertainty Quantification**: The use of variational inference allows RME to provide probabilistic estimates of mismatch parameters, including quantification of uncertainty. This uncertainty information can be valuable for future developments in robust motion planning and decision-making, enabling robots to make more informed choices about their actions based on their confidence in the estimated dynamics.
6. **Foundation for Future Research**: RME establishes a foundation for several promising future research directions. These include developing more sophisticated mismatch detection conditions, designing exploration strategies that utilize uncertainty quantification for improved CoM observability, extending the framework to model more complex dynamic interactions (beyond point mass assumptions), and explicitly accounting for unmodeled actuator dynamics like friction.

In essence, RME represents a step towards robots that can adapt to their physical world with greater autonomy and safety, paving the way for more fluid, robust, and intuitive physical interactions in dynamic and human-centric environments.
