# Addressing the Orchestration Gap in Generalist Robots via Physical Agency

> [!summary] Summary
> Pigey (Physical Agency) is an inference-time orchestrator: a frozen frontier VLM plans short subgoals, routes each to one of two frozen motor backends (a TAMP grasp planner or a π0.5 VLA), verifies outcomes from gripper sensors and wrist images, and recovers on failure. With no new data collection or post-training, it lifts LIBERO-PRO from 12.8% to 53.3% and real-robot success from 16.7% to 97.3%.

## Problem

- VLA scaling asks a single network to perceive, reason, plan, verify, recover and act in one forward pass, so failures cannot be attributed to any missing component.
- Code-as-policies and task planners add structure but depend on symbolic APIs, hand-built primitives, privileged simulator state, or code-level action spaces rather than pixel-conditioned learned skills.
- Reasoning-VLAs and bolt-on memory or verifier modules each require extra training and supply one capability, leaving success detection and recovery outside the execution loop.

## Method

- **Pigey** ==closed-loop orchestrator==: a frontier VLM iterates ==observe-reason-act-verify== over five tools (Perceive, Pick, DropAbove, VLARollout, Done) under a fixed tool-call budget, emitting no motor commands itself.
- Two frozen backends routed by object type: a TiPToP-based ==TAMP== planner for rigid pick-and-place, and a π0.5 ==VLA== for deformable, contact-rich, and recovery actions, each given only a short subgoal.
- ==Conservative dual-signal verification== plus ==retry-escalate recovery==: the ==is_grasped== sensor flag overrides an optimistic backend, wrist images confirm the grasp, and a twice-failed Pick escalates to the VLA.

## Results

- On LIBERO-PRO the frozen π0.5-LIBERO policy rises from **12.8%** to **53.3%** mean success, versus **18.2%** for CaP-Agent0 and **0%** for π0, with no fine-tuning.
- Across **30** real Franka tasks overall success is **97.3%** versus **16.7%** for direct π0.5 and **48.7%** for open-loop TiPToP; reasoning-limited probes go **4.6%** to **96.9%**.
- All nine reasoners beat both non-orchestrated baselines, spanning **44.3-53.3%** mean; failure analysis over **150** trials shows **4** Pigey failures versus **125** for π0.5 and **77** for TiPToP.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Measure the orchestration gap before collecting more robot data: much apparent motor incompetence is unused capability that an inference-time loop already recovers.
> - Splitting an open-loop primitive into separately verified steps, with a hardware sensor able to override an optimistic module, converts guaranteed failures into recoverable ones.
> - Gains come from loop structure, not reasoner identity, but the ceiling is the backend skill set, and verifier false-successes plus API latency are the new failure modes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robots capable of performing a wide array of manipulation tasks in unstructured environments is a central challenge in robotics. Such robots require a complex interplay of capabilities, including perception, world knowledge, intent reasoning, planning, success detection, recovery from failures, and low-level motor control.

Historically, the dominant approach to achieving generalization in robotics has involved scaling up robot data and training large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. Works such as $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, OpenVLA, GR00T N1, and DROID have demonstrated that large-scale pre-training can lead to increasingly capable visuomotor skills, particularly for grounded execution, grasping, and embodiment-specific control. However, the paper identifies a limitation in this approach: robot data is expensive, and it often provides an indirect means of teaching higher-level task capabilities like negation, multi-step decomposition, progress tracking, and autonomous recovery. When a VLA policy, trained as a single network, fails on a complex instruction, it becomes challenging to determine if the failure stems from a lack of low-level motor skill or a deficiency in reasoning, planning, or verification.

A second line of research integrates task-level structure above control. Efforts like SayCan, Code-as-Policies, ProgPrompt, Inner Monologue, VoxPoser, and embodied coding agents introduce planning and feedback mechanisms. While these approaches demonstrate the value of structured reasoning, they frequently rely on symbolic APIs, hand-designed primitives, privileged simulator states, or code-level action spaces, which can limit their applicability in diverse, unconstrained real-world scenarios or require specialized training.

A third category focuses on wiring reasoning directly into learned policies or augmenting them with specific capability modules. Examples include Hi Robot (task decomposition), GR00T N1 and Gemini Robotics (dual VLM-action architectures), Steerable Policies (richer command structure), MemER (memory), ECoT (embodied chain-of-thought), RoboMonkey (verification), and RoboMeter (learned reward models). These systems often require additional training—either more robot data, fine-tuning, or the development of specialized reasoning modules—and typically address only one or a few aspects of the full closed loop.

The current work distinguishes itself by proposing to bridge the "orchestration gap"—the discrepancy between what frozen motor skills can achieve alone and what they can achieve within an agentic loop—primarily at inference time. Instead of training policies to embed all reasoning capabilities or building bespoke modules requiring new data, this research suggests that a general VLM can act as an orchestrator, managing existing, frozen low-level skills to achieve complex goals. This approach aims to complete the closed loop of "deciding what to do, checking whether it worked, and repairing when it did not," which the authors argue is often missing in prior work.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address what the authors term the "orchestration gap" in generalist robots. This gap refers to the substantial difference in performance between low-level motor skills when executed in isolation (or with direct, single-pass prompting) and when they are managed by a higher-level, closed-loop reasoning agent.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations regarding the current state of general-purpose robot manipulation:
1. **Complexity of General-Purpose Manipulation:** Real-world robotic tasks are multifaceted, demanding capabilities beyond mere motor control. A capable robot needs to integrate perception, world knowledge, intent reasoning, hierarchical planning, success detection, error recovery, and low-level control into a cohesive system. The authors emphasize that "the hard part is rarely the motion alone."
2. **Limitations of Data Scaling for Reasoning:** While large-scale robot datasets are crucial for learning robust visuomotor skills (e.g., for contact, grasping), they are less direct and often inefficient for teaching high-level, symbolic, or logical task-level capabilities. For instance, negation ("put everything except the cup"), conditionals ("if the cup is empty, put the doll in it"), progress tracking, or recognizing a failed grasp and retrying are difficult to encode solely through more demonstrations. Current VLA policies, when prompted directly with abstract instructions, are forced to integrate all these capabilities into a single forward pass, which can lead to failures even when the underlying motor skills are present.
3. **Need for a Closed-Loop Process:** Prior work often addresses individual components of the robotic stack (e.g., better control, specific planning modules, or verification components) but rarely provides a complete, inference-time closed loop. This closed loop is essential for dynamic task execution: it involves continuously observing the environment, making decisions, executing actions, verifying outcomes, and adapting the plan or recovering from failures. A system lacking this continuous feedback and adaptation cannot robustly handle unexpected events or complex, multi-step goals.
4. **Leveraging Existing Capabilities:** The authors posit that current low-level control policies and planning systems already possess significant capabilities. The challenge lies in effectively orchestrating these capabilities. By decoupling high-level reasoning from low-level control, the goal is to unlock the full potential of existing "frozen" motor skills without requiring additional, expensive data collection or retraining of the base policies. This approach aims to isolate where generalization is truly lacking—whether in motor control or in the orchestrating logic.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives are:
- To design and implement Pigey (Physical Agency), a closed-loop inference-time orchestrator built around a frontier Vision-Language Model (VLM).
- To demonstrate that Pigey can effectively manage and combine *existing*, *frozen* low-level motor skills (specifically, a TAMP grasp planner and a $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA policy) to solve complex reasoning tasks in the real world and in simulation.
- To quantify the performance improvements achieved by Pigey over direct prompting of the same frozen skills, thereby explicitly measuring the "orchestration gap."
- To show that these performance gains are concentrated on tasks that demand high-level reasoning, planning, memory, and recovery, rather than on tasks that primarily test low-level motor competence.
- To achieve these objectives without requiring any additional data collection, policy fine-tuning, or post-training of the low-level motor skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is Pigey (Physical Agency), a closed-loop inference-time orchestrator designed to manage and enhance the capabilities of generalist robots without requiring additional data collection or training of underlying motor policies. The approach fundamentally decomposes the robotic control problem into a high-level agent manager (the orchestrator) and a set of low-level, language-conditioned control layers (motor backends).

#### 3.1. System Overview

Pigey operates as a closed-loop agent driven by a frontier Vision-Language Model ($\phi$). Given a high-level instruction ($I$), the VLM iteratively performs the following steps:
1. **Perceive:** Reads the current visual observation and integrates it with the interaction history.
2. **Reason:** Plans the next concrete subgoal based on the instruction, observations, and memory.
3. **Act:** Selects and calls one of the frozen motor backends to execute the subgoal.
4. **Verify:** Checks the outcome of the executed action using sensory and visual feedback.
5. **Recover/Replans:** If verification fails, it initiates a recovery strategy or replans the next step.
This cycle continues until the instruction is deemed satisfied or a maximum number of tool calls ($T_{max}$) is reached. The orchestrator itself does not emit motor commands; all physical motion is delegated to the backends.

#### 3.2. Components of Pigey

- **Frontier VLM Orchestrator ($\phi$):** A pre-trained, general-purpose VLM (e.g., Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.5) serves as the brain of Pigey. It grounds its reasoning in live camera observations and is responsible for all high-level cognitive functions.
- **Tool Interface:** The orchestrator interacts with the environment through a set of defined tools:
 - `Perceive`: Captures camera views (wrist and third-person), robot state, and detects objects with labels. This provides the agent's current understanding of the scene.
 - `Pick(ℓ)`: Grasps a detected object (identified by label `ℓ`) using the TAMP backend.
 - `DropAbove(ℓ)`: Places the currently held object onto or near a target (identified by label `ℓ` or absolute coordinates).
 - `VLARollout(s)`: Executes a short, concrete natural-language subgoal (`s`) using the VLA backend.
 - `Done`: Signals task completion, requiring a preceding `Perceive` call for final verification.
 - Additional tools like `Release` (open gripper unconditionally), `LookAway` (rotate arm to hide scene), and `LookBack` (return to capture pose or check if scene is uncovered during `LookAway`) are used for specific tasks (e.g., memory, recovery).
- **Observation and Grounding:** After every tool call, the orchestrator receives a wrist image, end-effector pose, gripper aperture, an `is_grasped` binary flag from the gripper sensor, and a set of object labels from an open-vocabulary detector. Crucially, backends also provide *typed failure annotations* (e.g., "no grasp found," "motion-planning failure"), allowing the orchestrator to understand *why* a step failed, not just that it did. This rich feedback is critical for informed recovery.
- **Memory:** Pigey maintains an explicit internal memory of the episode's history. This includes attempted subgoals, their outcomes, what is currently held, and which objects have been successfully placed. This memory enables long-horizon tasks and supports coherent multi-step planning.

#### 3.3. Grounding Pigey in Actions: Frozen Backends

Pigey orchestrates two pre-existing and frozen (not trained or fine-tuned for this work) motor backends, each accessed via short subgoals:
1. **TAMP Backend (`Pick`/`DropAbove`):** This uses TiPToP for open-vocabulary grounding, grasp prediction, and collision-free motion planning. Unlike TiPToP's original open-loop pick-and-place, Pigey decomposes this into separate `Pick` and `DropAbove` tools. This allows the orchestrator to insert an explicit verification step between grasping and placement, ensuring the object is securely held before attempting to place it.
2. **VLA Backend (`VLARollout`):** A frozen $\pi_{0.5}$ policy is used for closed-loop visuomotor control. This backend is particularly suited for deformable objects, contact-rich interactions, cluttered environments, and serves as a robust recovery path when TAMP fails.

These backends are complementary: TAMP provides geometric precision and a verifiable grasp signal for rigid objects, while the VLA offers robustness for more complex or uncertain scenarios where geometric planning might break down.

#### 3.4. Verification

Verification is a key differentiator of Pigey from open-loop prompting. It combines two types of signals:
- **Deterministic:** The `is_grasped` flag from the gripper sensor provides a binary success/failure for `Pick` actions. Explicit failure flags from backends (e.g., motion-planning failure) are also used.
- **Visual:** The post-action wrist image is returned to the VLM, which visually confirms the intended object is grasped and removed from its initial position.
A conservative combination is used: if a backend reports success but the gripper sensor indicates no grasp, the step is overridden to failure. Placement (`DropAbove`) is only attempted after a preceding grasp is verified.

#### 3.5. Planning and Recovery Strategies

The VLM orchestrator applies a set of rules (articulated in the system prompt) for planning and recovery:
- **Backend Routing:** Rigid, table-resting objects are typically routed to TAMP. Deformable objects, items inside containers, or stacked objects go directly to the VLA.
- **Retry and Escalation:** An unverified `Pick` is retried once after re-perceiving the scene. A second failure escalates to `VLARollout`. Recovery is bidirectional: a non-progressing VLA rollout can trigger a fallback to TAMP.
- **Advanced Recovery:** Pigey implements strategies such as: returning a wrongly grasped object, treating visible objects as occluders to uncover hidden targets, and clearing occupied destinations before placing new items.
- **Dynamic Re-perception:** Re-perceiving the scene before each grasp makes the system robust to environmental changes (e.g., an object being nudged).

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Simulation:** Evaluated on LIBERO-PRO, a benchmark designed to test robustness to object, spatial, and goal perturbations, using $\pi_{0.5}$-LIBERO as the base policy.
- **Real World:** Evaluated on the DROID tabletop manipulation platform with a Franka FR3 arm. A suite of 30 "capability probe" tasks was designed to specifically test world knowledge, conditional logic, multi-step reasoning, spatial reasoning, obstacle handling, error recovery, and long-horizon memory.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Real Robot:** Direct $\pi_{0.5}$ prompting (VLA alone) and TiPToP (open-loop pick-and-place).
 - **Simulation:** Raw $\pi_0$, raw $\pi_{0.5}$, and CaP-Agent0.
- **Metrics:** Binary success/failure rate (5 trials for real robot tasks, 10 for simulation tasks per reasoner).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that Pigey significantly enhances the performance of frozen low-level motor skills by providing inference-time orchestration, leading to substantial improvements across both simulation and real-world benchmarks without additional data collection or policy training.

#### 4.1. LIBERO-PRO Simulation Benchmark

- Pigey achieved a mean success rate of 53.3% across six LIBERO-PRO perturbation suites. This represents a more than 4-fold improvement over the frozen $\pi_{0.5}$-LIBERO baseline, which scored 12.8%. CaP-Agent0, another baseline, achieved 18.2%.
- The improvements were observed even in tasks with minimal explicit reasoning, suggesting that Pigey's error recovery mechanisms and strategic tool usage contribute to robustness against common perturbations.
- A sweep across nine different frontier VLMs showed that the orchestration gain was consistent across models, with mean success ranging from 44.3% to 53.3%, indicating that the closed-loop structure is the primary driver of the improvement, with the VLM's capability influencing the magnitude.

#### 4.2. Pigey vs. Vanilla VLA on DROID: The Orchestration Gap

- On a suite of 30 real-world robotic manipulation tasks, Pigey achieved an overall success rate of 97.3%. This is a notable increase compared to direct $\pi_{0.5}$ prompting, which achieved 16.7% success.
- The performance gains were most pronounced in "reasoning-limited" tasks (e.g., world knowledge, conditional logic, multi-step reasoning, spatial reasoning, obstacle/safety reasoning, error recovery, and long-horizon memory). In these categories, the raw $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA averaged 4.6% success, whereas Pigey achieved 96.9%.
- For "simple pick-and-place" tasks, Pigey achieved 100% success, a slight improvement over the VLA's 95%. This indicates that orchestration did not hinder performance on straightforward tasks and even provided marginal benefits through robustness.
- These results suggest that for many complex tasks, the primary bottleneck is not the low-level motor control capability of the VLA, but rather its inability to interpret instructions, decompose goals, track progress, manage memory, and recover from failures in a single forward pass.

#### 4.3. Pigey vs. TAMP on DROID: Orchestrating a Visuomotor Policy

- Pigey also significantly outperformed TiPToP, a system that uses similar open-vocabulary grounding and motion planning but operates in an open-loop fashion. TiPToP achieved an overall success rate of 48.7%.
- Even on simple pick-and-place tasks, TiPToP achieved only 80% success, compared to Pigey's 100%. This difference is attributed to Pigey's closed-loop verification (e.g., detecting an empty gripper via `is_grasped` sensor) and retry mechanisms, which TiPToP's open-loop plan lacks.
- The performance gap widened in tasks involving adversarial perturbations or complex scenarios (multi-step, obstacle, recovery probes). Pigey's ability to re-perceive the scene before each grasp (adapting to moved targets) and to uncover hidden objects before attempting to grasp them proved critical, turning guaranteed failures for open-loop planning into successes.

#### 4.4. First-Failure Mode Distribution

- Analysis of failure modes revealed distinct patterns: direct $\pi_{0.5}$ mostly failed due to grounding issues (selecting the wrong object), while TiPToP primarily failed due to limitations in reasoning or planning (e.g., unable to adapt to dynamic environments).
- Pigey exhibited a very low number of failures (4 out of 150 trials), demonstrating its robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Pigey, that significantly advances the capabilities of generalist robots by addressing the "orchestration gap." Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Task-Level Generalization without Retraining:** The most impactful contribution is the demonstration that a substantial portion of task-level generalization—including complex reasoning, multi-step planning, error recovery, and memory—can be supplied at inference time by a high-level orchestrator over *frozen*, pre-existing motor skills. This bypasses the expensive and time-consuming process of collecting new robot data or fine-tuning large VLA policies for every new task or reasoning capability. This offers a more resource-efficient pathway to deploy general-purpose robots.

2. **Highlighting the "Orchestration Gap":** The concept and empirical measurement of the "orchestration gap" provide a crucial insight into robot learning. It suggests that what often appears as a deficiency in low-level motor competence might, in fact, be a deficit in the higher-level logic that decides *when*, *why*, and *how* to invoke those motor skills. This re-framing guides future research to focus on inference-time architectural improvements and VLM capabilities rather than solely on larger datasets for motor skill acquisition.

3. **Modular and Interpretable Robotic Systems:** Pigey's decomposition into a VLM orchestrator and distinct motor backends creates a more modular and interpretable system. Decision-making is made explicit through tool calls and reasoning traces, allowing for clearer attribution of successes and failures (e.g., whether a task failed due to poor grounding, inadequate planning, or a low-level motor error). This auditability is valuable for debugging, system improvement, and understanding robotic behavior.

4. **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The closed-loop nature of Pigey, with its continuous observation, verification, and recovery mechanisms, makes robotic systems significantly more robust to unexpected environmental changes, execution errors, and task perturbations. Features like re-perceiving before each grasp, explicit grasp verification, and escalating recovery strategies enable dynamic adaptation, which is critical for real-world deployment.

5. **A Sequencing Rule for Robot Learning:** The paper proposes a practical "sequencing rule" for robot learning: before investing substantial resources in teaching a policy to reason through extensive data collection, researchers should first evaluate how much of that capability an inference-time agent can recover around existing frozen policies. This can help prioritize research and development efforts more effectively.

6. **Foundation for Future Work:** The structured inference traces generated by Pigey (observations, subgoals, backend choices, verification signals, recovery decisions) offer a rich dataset for future research. These traces could be used as supervision signals to distill orchestration behaviors into smaller, more efficient learned orchestrators or task-level policy adapters, potentially leading to systems that retain the benefits of orchestration with reduced latency and computational cost.

**Limitations acknowledged by the authors include:**
- **Skill Ceiling:** Pigey's performance is ultimately bounded by the capabilities and "support" of the underlying low-level motor tools it orchestrates.
- **Imperfect Verification:** Partial observability (e.g., from occlusions) can lead to verification errors or false successes.
- **Latency and Cost:** Reliance on API calls to frontier VLM models for orchestration introduces per-step latency and computational costs, which might limit its applicability in high-speed or low-latency scenarios.

Despite these limitations, Pigey represents a significant step towards more intelligent and adaptable general-purpose robots, emphasizing the power of inference-time reasoning and orchestration over purely data-driven, end-to-end learning for complex task execution.
