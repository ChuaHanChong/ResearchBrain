# One Policy, Many Embodiments: Unified Camera-Centric Action Geometry Pre-training for Heterogeneous Embodied Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> UCAG-P is a generalist vision-language-action policy that unifies heterogeneous robot and human data by predicting a camera-frame semantic anchor pair (wrist/end-effector and grasp center) as a shared action target, then converting it via a geometry-conditioned translator using camera-to-base transforms and Jacobians into embodiment-specific commands. Trained on 6,373 hours across nine embodiments, one checkpoint reaches 98.3% LIBERO and 82.0% zero-shot LIBERO-Plus.

## Problem

- Embodied datasets differ in morphology, camera geometry, control frequency and native action space, so one low-level command parameterization cannot serve as a shared prediction target.
- Embodiment-specific heads, embodiment prompts and cross-embodiment conditioning still predict in robot-dependent control spaces, separating action learning by dataset or robot body.
- Human demonstrations need retargeting, trajectory reconstruction or robot-video synthesis before use, imposing conversion assumptions about morphology and action parameterization.

## Method

- ==Camera-centric action space==: a semantic anchor pair (wrist/end-effector, grasp center) in 3D camera-frame coordinates plus in-plane rotation, gripper openness and optional camera motion, shared by robots and human hands.
- ==Geometry-conditioned action translator== maps predicted motion, robot state, camera-to-base transform, local Jacobians and geometry tokens into an 80-dimensional sparse command layout with per-embodiment slot masks.
- Three-stage training on a Qwen3-VL-4B backbone: shared motion pretraining, translator training on ground-truth trajectories, then joint robot-human alignment, all with ==masked L1 losses== over available targets, together forming **UCAG-P**.

## Results

- A single checkpoint without benchmark-specific fine-tuning reaches **98.3%** LIBERO, **88.7%** RoboTwin Easy, **89.2%** RoboTwin Hard and **62.0%** RoboCasa GR-1, beating the generalist Qwen-VLA baseline everywhere.
- Zero-shot LIBERO-Plus averages **82.0%** over seven perturbations, best overall, with **92.8%** on robot-state and **98.9%** on lighting shifts, but only **51.2%** under camera changes.
- Ablating pooled VLM features in the action head drops RoboCasa GR-1 from **62.0%** to **58.3%**; real Piper bread pickup via human-hand transfer reaches **60%** versus **20%** for the pi-0.5 baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Camera-observable geometry is a more stable cross-embodiment invariant than low-level controllers, letting unpaired human video supervise robot policies without retargeting or video synthesis.
> - Decoupling shared geometric prediction from a kinematics-conditioned translator preserves precise per-platform controllability while a single generalist checkpoint rivals benchmark-specific specialists.
> - Geometric interfaces inherit calibration dependence: depth, extrinsics or hand-keypoint errors propagate, and direct arm swaps still collapse performance to 35%.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies represents a significant direction in robotics research, aiming to enable robots to understand language instructions and execute tasks across diverse environments. Current VLA systems leverage large robot datasets, advanced vision-language backbones, and cross-embodiment conditioning to scale their capabilities. However, a primary challenge in scaling these systems is the inherent heterogeneity of embodied data. This data spans various robot morphologies (e.g., single-arm, bimanual, humanoids), camera configurations, proprioceptive states, control frequencies, and low-level action spaces. Consequently, identical manipulation behaviors can be represented differently, such as end-effector deltas, joint commands, gripper states, or human hand motion.

Existing approaches to address this data heterogeneity include embodiment-specific policies, action retargeting, human-to-robot video synthesis, or dataset-specific adaptation branches. While these methods mitigate some discrepancies—for instance, embodiment prompts and cross-embodiment conditioning provide context, and unified camera-frame end-effector action spaces improve robot compatibility—they often maintain robot-specific control targets or require computationally intensive conversion steps. These limitations hinder the joint learning of a truly unified policy capable of absorbing diverse data sources directly.

This work addresses the challenge of action representation by proposing a shared, camera-centric geometric action space that abstracts over embodiment differences. This approach aims to unify heterogeneous robot and human demonstrations, allowing a single policy to learn transferable manipulation geometry while retaining embodiment-specific controllability. It positions itself within the VLA paradigm by focusing on a more abstract and generalized action interface compared to prior methods that often remain tethered to specific robot command structures or rely on intermediate data transformations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to overcome the severe bottleneck imposed by the inherent heterogeneity of embodied data on the scalability of generalist VLA policies. The paper identifies that current paradigms, which rely on explicit action retargeting, human-to-robot video synthesis, or dataset-specific adaptation, fundamentally impede the joint learning of a unified policy that can effectively utilize diverse robot morphologies, camera configurations, and low-level action spaces.

The key motivations and specific objectives are:

- **Develop a Unified Action Formulation:** The primary motivation is to introduce a novel action formulation that structurally aligns heterogeneous embodied datasets into a shared geometric action space. This formulation aims to represent manipulation through camera-observable anchor motion in camera-frame coordinates, treating different robot arms, humanoids, and human hands as varying embodiments of a common action schema.
- **Decouple Policy Learning from Embodiment-Specific Control:** The research aims to create a decoupled architecture where a shared VLA policy learns transferable manipulation geometry (e.g., motion of a wrist or grasp center) separately from the embodiment-specific mechanisms required to convert this geometry into executable controls. This decoupling is intended to allow the base policy to learn general manipulation skills across diverse data without being constrained by the native action spaces of individual robots.
- **Directly Integrate Human Demonstrations:** A significant motivation is to enable the direct integration of human-hand videos into policy training without requiring explicit human-to-robot video synthesis, inpainting, editing, or action retargeting as a prerequisite. By treating human hands as a distinct embodiment within the shared camera-centric action space, the policy can directly learn from large volumes of human data.
- **Achieve Cross-Embodiment Generalization without Fine-tuning:** The overarching goal is to pre-train a single, unified policy checkpoint that can achieve competitive performance across a wide range of tasks and robot embodiments (single-arm, bimanual, humanoid) and generalize to out-of-distribution and new-embodiment settings, all without benchmark-specific fine-tuning. This aims to demonstrate the transferability and reusability of the learned policy.

In essence, the work is motivated by the need for a more scalable and generalizable approach to robot learning that can leverage the full spectrum of available embodied data, from simulated robot trajectories to real-world human demonstrations, by identifying a stable common structure in manipulation that is observable and independent of specific hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, UCAG-P (Unified Camera-Centric Action Geometry Pre-training), addresses data heterogeneity by defining a shared, camera-centric action space and a decoupled architecture for policy learning and action execution.

**Problem Formulation:**
The framework models a collection of embodied manipulation datasets, each associated with a unique embodiment, sensor configuration, and control interface. Native action spaces across these datasets are generally incompatible. UCAG-P separates the learning objective into predicting a shared geometric action and translating this prediction into an embodiment-specific executable command. The core idea is that camera-observable manipulation motion serves as the universal policy target.

**Camera-Centric Action Space:**
The central innovation is the definition of a shared action space, *G*, that describes manipulation through camera-observable geometry rather than low-level, embodiment-specific controls. This space is based on a semantic anchor pair (p0, p1):
- **p0:** Denotes the wrist or end-effector point.
- **p1:** Denotes the gripper center for robot data or the grasp center for human-hand data.
Both anchors are represented by their 3D camera-frame coordinates (p_cam_i,t). The action chunk g_t:t+H for a horizon H consists of individual steps g_t+h, each a 30-dimensional vector:
g_t+h = [m_L_t+h, m_R_t+h, ξ_cam_t+h] ∈ R^30.
- **m_a_t+h (for manipulator a ∈ {L, R}):** A 10-dimensional vector representing anchor motion, including displacements of p0 and p1 (∆p_cam_0,t+h, ∆p_cam_1,t+h), in-plane rotation (∆ψ_cam_t+h), gripper state or hand openness (γ_gripper_t+h), and padding. Displacements are relative to the chunk's first frame.
- **ξ_cam_t+h:** A 10-dimensional camera motion term (translation and 6D continuous rotation) used only for moving cameras, otherwise zero.
This two-anchor representation maintains consistent semantic roles while allowing physical instantiation to vary. For human demonstrations, MediaPipe keypoints for the wrist (p0) and midpoint of thumb/index fingertips (p1) are used to derive camera-centric pseudo-actions, with metric depth or calibration information masked if unavailable.

**UCAG-P Architecture:**
The architecture consists of three main components:
1. **Vision-Language Backbone:** Instantiated with Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, this component encodes multi-view RGB observations, language instructions, and optional proprioceptive context into hidden states. Learnable action-query tokens are appended to the input sequence, whose final-layer hidden states are used for action prediction.
2. **Shared Camera-Centric Motion Head:** This head maps the action-query features from the VLM backbone to the shared geometric action chunk (ˆg_t:t+H). It is shared across all data sources and supervised when camera-centric labels are available.
3. **Geometry-Conditioned Action Translator:** This module converts the predicted shared motion (ˆg_t:t+H) into executable commands (ˆu_t:t+H) for a target embodiment. It conditions on:
 - The predicted camera-centric chunk (ˆg_t:t+H).
 - Current embodiment state (s^(k)_t).
 - Camera-to-base transform (T^(k)_base←cam).
 - Local Jacobians (J^(k)_t) for current state.
 - Geometry tokens (z^(k)_geo) encoding embodiment structure.
 The translator outputs an 80-dimensional sparse command chunk (ˆu^(k)_t:t+H), where only active slots for the target embodiment are used for downstream control. This sparse layout allows common batching while masking inactive dimensions.

**Three-Stage Training:**
UCAG-P employs a progressive three-stage training regimen:
1. **Stage 1: Camera-centric specialization:** The VLM backbone and shared motion head are trained on all samples with available camera-centric supervision. This stage focuses on establishing accurate anchor-motion prediction in camera-frame coordinates.
2. **Stage 2: Geometry-conditioned action translation:** The geometry-conditioned translator is trained. It receives ground-truth camera-frame trajectories, target-embodiment state, camera-to-base calibration, local Jacobians, and executable command labels. This isolates the mapping from ideal geometry to executable commands.
3. **Stage 3: Joint robot-human training:** Both the shared motion head and the translator are jointly optimized on the mixed robot-human corpus. In this stage, the translator receives trajectories predicted by the shared policy, mirroring inference conditions. Learnable query tokens are used to attention-pool VLM hidden states, passing global visual-language context to the action head. Auxiliary VLM data is excluded to focus on embodied control.

**Training Objectives and Inference:**
The training uses masked L1 losses:
- **L_geo:** A masked L1 loss for the shared camera-centric branch, applied only to available dimensions of the geometric target.
- **L_cmd:** A masked L1 loss for the translator, applied to available and active dimensions of the executable command.
The total objective is L = L_geo + λ_cmd * L_cmd, where λ_cmd balances the two supervisions.
At inference, the base policy predicts ˆg_t:t+H, and for robot control, the translator uses this prediction, along with target embodiment state and geometry, to produce ˆu^(k)_t:t+H. The downstream controller executes only the active command slots.

**Pre-training Data:**
The model is pre-trained on an embodied corpus of over 6,300 hours (1,020,672 episodes) from 11 dataset subsets, covering single-arm, dual-arm, humanoid, and human-hand embodiments. This includes 266 hours of real-robot data, 3,767 hours of simulated robot data (e.g., RoboCasa GR-1, LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, InternData), and 2,339 hours of egocentric human-hand videos (e.g., VITRA, EgoDex, EgoVerse). A separate vision-language mixture provides instruction following, spatial grounding, and affordance understanding. Data curation involves indexing, visual/language standardization, geometric and action alignment (including deriving camera-centric geometry for human data), and temporal windowing with normalization and validity masks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

UCAG-P, trained with a single unified checkpoint without benchmark-specific fine-tuning, demonstrates performance across heterogeneous simulation and real-world manipulation tasks.

**Unified Manipulation Across Embodiments:**
- **LIBERO (Single-arm):** UCAG-P achieves a 98.3% success rate, which is competitive with specialist models. Detailed breakdown shows 98.8% on LIBERO-Spatial, 98.6% on LIBERO-Object, 99.2% on LIBERO-Goal, and 96.4% on LIBERO-Long.
- **RoboTwin (Bimanual):** Success rates are 88.7% on RoboTwin Easy and 89.2% on RoboTwin Hard, positioning it competitively among recent specialist and generalist policies. The model exhibits strong robustness to randomized scene conditions.
- **RoboCasa GR-1 (Humanoid):** A 62.0% success rate is achieved, demonstrating its capability in generating executable humanoid commands. While slightly below the top specialist models (e.g., ZR-0 at 69.3%), it surpasses earlier baselines and outperforms the generalist Qwen-VLA baseline across all evaluated datasets.

**Robustness Under Distribution Shift (LIBERO-Plus):**
UCAG-P obtains an 82.0% zero-shot total score on LIBERO-Plus, a benchmark designed to evaluate robustness under various perturbation categories. This score indicates particular robustness to robot-state, lighting, and background perturbations. Challenges remain with camera, sensor-noise, and object-layout shifts. The framework requires no LIBERO dataset for post-training on these shifts.

**New Embodiment Transfer and Adaptation:**
- **Real-World Transfer (Piper robots):** In real-world evaluations, UCAG-P achieves 60% success on bread grasping (a human-to-robot transfer task), 90% on drawer opening, and 75% on bimanual bowl stacking. This performance compares favorably to baselines like π0.5 (20%, 85%, and 65% respectively), suggesting improved real-robot adaptation under the same demonstration budget and setup.
- **Simulation Cross-Embodiment Transfer (ALOHA-to-ARX):** When directly replacing the ALOHA robot in RoboTwin with ARX (a different morphology), UCAG-P achieves 35.0% success. This indicates that while camera-centric motion provides a transferable intermediate target, significant morphological and kinematic mismatches remain a challenge for zero-shot transfer without further adaptation.

**Qualitative Observations:**
Visualizations demonstrate that the shared camera-centric representation preserves task-relevant geometry across both real-world and simulated domains. Successful transitions from initial to completed states, especially in bimanual and humanoid tasks, suggest that the geometry-conditioned translator effectively converts shared motion predictions into coordinated embodiment-specific commands, despite variations in scene appearance, robot morphology, and control interface.

**Ablation Study (from Appendix):**
An ablation study on RoboCasa GR-1 demonstrated that conditioning the action head on pooled VLM hidden features (i.e., visual-language context) improved the success rate from 58.3% to 62.0%. This indicates that the VLM context provides complementary visual and language information beneficial for downstream action prediction and execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of UCAG-P carries several significant implications for the field of embodied AI and generalist robot learning:

- **Unified Policy Learning:** UCAG-P provides a framework for learning a single, unified manipulation policy from highly heterogeneous embodied data. By structurally aligning diverse datasets (simulated robots, real robots, human hands) into a shared camera-centric geometric action space, it facilitates joint learning across previously incompatible data sources. This could significantly streamline the development of generalist VLA models by allowing them to leverage the full breadth of available data.
- **Direct Integration of Human Demonstrations:** A notable impact is the ability to directly incorporate human-hand demonstrations as a distinct embodiment within the shared action space. This bypasses the need for complex and computationally expensive intermediate steps such as explicit human-to-robot video synthesis or action retargeting, potentially unlocking massive volumes of human video data for robot policy learning. This direct integration could accelerate the acquisition of diverse manipulation skills.
- **Decoupled Architecture for Transferability:** The decoupled design, separating the learning of transferable manipulation geometry from embodiment-specific action translation, enhances the reusability and generalizability of the base policy. The policy learns "what to do" in a camera-observable, embodiment-agnostic manner, while the translator handles "how to do it" for a specific robot. This modularity is crucial for scaling reusable robot policies across different hardware platforms.
- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization and Robustness:** The demonstrated ability of a single UCAG-P checkpoint to achieve competitive performance across single-arm, bimanual, and humanoid tasks, as well as showing robustness to distribution shifts and zero-shot transfer capabilities (e.g., to new real-world tasks), indicates progress toward more robust and versatile robot agents. This suggests that the camera-centric action geometry provides a practical interface for developing generalist robots that can adapt to new scenarios and hardware with reduced retraining.

**Limitations:**
Despite these advancements, the paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Reliance on Geometric Information Fidelity:** The system's performance is dependent on the reliable estimation or calibration of geometric information, including camera calibration, depth estimation, embodiment kinematics, and hand-keypoint localization (e.g., MediaPipe). Errors in these underlying estimations can propagate and degrade the effectiveness of the camera-centric target and the action translator.
- **Challenges in Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** While UCAG-P demonstrates non-zero transfer in cross-embodiment settings (e.g., ALOHA-to-ARX), a performance gap from the source embodiment indicates that camera-centric motion alone does not fully resolve the challenges posed by significant morphological and kinematic mismatches between different robot platforms. Further research may be required to bridge this gap.
- **Controlled Real-World Evaluation Scope:** The real-world evaluation, while supportive of human-to-robot transfer and data-efficient fine-tuning adaptation, is intentionally controlled and limited in scale. Achieving broader real-world robustness would necessitate more extensive evaluation across a wider variety of robots, camera setups, object categories, and longer-horizon, more complex tasks.
