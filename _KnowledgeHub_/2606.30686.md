# Position: Vision-Language-Action Models Cannot Be Verified to Perform Physical Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper argues that current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model benchmarks are insufficient to verify claims of physical reasoning, as they cannot differentiate between semantic generalization and genuine physical execution. It proposes a re-evaluation of VLA progress, suggesting evaluation reforms and architectural reorientation.

## Problem

- Traditional robot manipulation requires extensive, task-specific training data, hindering generalization across diverse tasks.
- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems implicitly assume semantic representations from large pre-trained VLMs are sufficient for guiding physical action decisions.
- Current VLA evaluation protocols, using task success rates, fail to distinguish between improvements stemming from semantic generalization and those from physical execution capabilities.

## Method

- The authors theoretically decompose ==VLA== policies into distinct ==semantic mapping== and ==physical action decision== components to analyze their independent contributions.
- They analyze the information boundaries of ==VLM pre-training==, arguing that static image-text data fundamentally lacks signals about physical properties and action consequences.
- The paper proposes a "forward path" involving evaluation reform with controlled variations and a reorientation towards explicitly separated semantic and physical modules in VLA architectures.

## Results

- Current VLA benchmarks are found unable to reliably verify claims of physical reasoning, preventing accurate attribution of performance improvements.
- This **identifiability gap** leads to potential misallocation of research resources and unwarranted confidence in system robustness for real-world deployment.
- The paper recommends evaluation reform through controlled experiments that independently manipulate semantic content and physical conditions, alongside designing architectures with explicitly separated semantic interfaces and physical modules.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The implicit "semantic sufficiency assumption" in VLM-backbone VLA research is structurally unjustified due to the absence of physical interaction data in VLM pre-training signals.
> - Current VLA evaluation protocols exhibit "non-identifiability," meaning task success cannot causally attribute performance gains to either semantic interpretation or physical execution capabilities.
> - A "narrative drift" has occurred in the field, with increasingly strong capabilities attributed to VLM backbones without explicit causal verification, often misdiagnosing physical failures as engineering factors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Robot manipulation has traditionally required extensive, task-specific training data and significant engineering effort, often involving curated demonstrations or hand-engineered reward functions for each new task. The advent of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs), pre-trained on internet-scale visual and linguistic data, introduced an alternative paradigm. Researchers posited that the broad semantic knowledge encoded in these models could provide a generalizable foundation for robot control.

This led to the rapid development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems, beginning notably with RT-2. These systems typically employ end-to-end architectures that map visual observations and linguistic instructions directly to action commands. While various architectural lineages have emerged—including discrete token policies, diffusion-based policies, and dual-system designs—a common structural core persists: a pre-trained VLM backbone responsible for mediating perception, language grounding, and action generation. A prevalent, implicit assumption underlying these developments is that semantic representations acquired through large-scale vision-language pre-training are sufficient to guide physical action decisions. Consequently, performance improvements in VLA systems are often interpreted as evidence of enhanced semantic generalization translating into more robust physical execution. This has motivated further extensions, such as increased model scale, diffusion-based action modeling, and the integration of additional modalities like proprioception, as seen in systems like OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π0, and GR00T N1. These systems generally report continuous benchmark improvements, often interpreted as progress in capabilities like cross-task generalization, cross-embodiment generalization, semantic reasoning, and physical adaptation.

This paper positions itself as a critical analysis of this prevalent interpretation. It argues that the observed performance gains in VLA benchmarks, while real, cannot be causally attributed to physical reasoning due to fundamental limitations in current evaluation protocols. The work challenges the unverified assumption of semantic sufficiency and highlights a "narrative drift" in the field, where escalating capability claims are made without isolating the underlying causal mechanisms. It aims not to dismiss the role of VLMs in robotics but to establish clearer conditions for evaluating claims of physical generalization.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The central objective of this paper is to argue that existing VLA systems, despite reporting improved performance on robot manipulation benchmarks, cannot be reliably verified to perform physical reasoning under current evaluation protocols. The authors contend that the predominant metric of task success rate fails to differentiate between semantic generalization and genuine physical execution capabilities, thus obscuring the true source of performance improvements.

This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Challenging an Unverified Assumption:** The paper identifies an implicit but unverified assumption foundational to VLM-backbone VLA research: that semantic representations learned from internet-scale data are sufficient to guide physical action decisions. It argues that this assumption has not been independently tested and lacks support from the properties of VLM pretraining, which primarily relies on static image-text data without information about interaction outcomes or physical consequences.

2. **Decomposition of VLA Policies:** To support its argument, the paper motivates a decomposition of a VLA policy into two distinct components:
 - **Semantic Mapping:** Transforms observations and instructions into task-relevant abstractions (e.g., object categories, spatial relations, goals).
 - **Physical Action Decision:** Selects actions considering constraints of dynamics, contact, embodiment, and temporal interaction.
 The motivation for this decomposition is to highlight that improvements in task success could stem from either component or their interaction, and that current evaluations do not distinguish between these sources. Physical capabilities, by definition, require different actions under varying physical contexts even if semantic interpretation remains constant.

3. **Addressing "Narrative Drift":** The authors observe a phenomenon termed "narrative drift," where successive VLA systems inherit and reinforce prior interpretations of performance gains, progressively attributing stronger capabilities (e.g., generalizability, reasoning, physical adaptation) to the VLM backbone, often without explicit causal verification. The motivation is to critically examine this trend and call for a more rigorous attribution of capabilities.

4. **Identifying Evaluation Gaps:** A core motivation is to expose fundamental limitations in current VLA evaluation protocols. The paper asserts that these protocols lack the necessary signals to isolate and measure semantic generalization separately from physical execution generalization. This absence prevents causal attribution of performance improvements, meaning that observed gains could plausibly result from semantic matching, distributional overlap between training and test data, or genuine physical generalization, but the current benchmarks cannot differentiate.

5. **Preventing Systemic Consequences:** The authors are motivated by the potential systemic consequences of this identifiability gap, including misallocation of research resources (e.g., investing in larger VLMs when the bottleneck might be physical interaction data), false confidence in deployment readiness (if benchmark success relies on distributional overlap rather than robust physical execution), and persistent misdiagnosis of architectural failures. They aim to prevent the field from addressing symptoms (e.g., "more data," "larger models") when the root cause might be an untested architectural assumption.

Ultimately, the paper aims to clarify the conditions under which claims of physical generalization in VLA systems can be meaningfully evaluated, thereby guiding future research towards more robust and verifiable advancements in embodied AI.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The paper primarily employs a theoretical and analytical methodology rather than conducting new empirical experiments. Its approach involves a critical examination of the conceptual foundations, architectural designs, and evaluation practices prevalent in contemporary VLM-backbone VLA research.

1. **Conceptual Decomposition and Formalization:**
 - The authors begin by conceptually decomposing a VLA policy, which maps observations and instructions to actions, into two distinct components: **semantic mapping** (f_vlm, yielding a semantic representation z_sem from observations o_t and language l) and **physical action decision** (which selects actions a_t conditioned on z_sem and physical context z_phys).
 - They formalize the implicit assumption underlying VLA architectures as the "semantic sufficiency assumption": π(a_t | z_sem, z_phys) ≈ π(a_t | z_sem). This implies that physical properties (z_phys) are either negligible or sufficiently encoded within the semantic representation.

2. **Analysis of Information Boundaries:**
 - The paper then analyzes the information boundary of z_sem. It argues that z_sem, derived from large-scale static image-text data through objectives like image-text contrastive training or captioning, fundamentally lacks information about physical properties (e.g., mass distribution, friction, contact geometry). These properties are revealed only through interaction and action consequences, which are absent from VLM pre-training signals.
 - This analysis establishes that the semantic sufficiency assumption is "structurally unjustified" because the VLM backbone's training signal explicitly excludes the kind of data needed to encode physical properties.

3. **Review of Architectural Lineages:**
 - The authors examine various VLA architectural lineages (discrete token, diffusion-based, dual-system) that have emerged since RT-2. They observe that despite variations in output representations and control interfaces, all these systems maintain a core reliance on a pre-trained VLM backbone. They argue that modifications primarily address execution limitations without fundamentally isolating or testing the semantic sufficiency assumption.

4. **Tracing "Narrative Drift":**
 - The paper analyzes the historical progression of capability claims made for VLM-backbone VLA systems, from early assertions of "web-scale knowledge transfer" (RT-2) to later claims of "semantic reasoning" (π0) and "physical adaptation" (GR00T N1). This process, termed "narrative drift," highlights how progressively stronger capabilities are attributed to a core architectural component that has not fundamentally changed in its underlying assumptions or verification methodology.

5. **Structural Argument for Non-Identifiability in Evaluation:**
 - The core of the paper's analytical approach lies in detailing three levels at which current evaluation protocols fail to provide identifiable signals for causal attribution:
 - **Attribution Non-Identifiability:** Task success rate, the dominant metric, collapses semantic and physical contributions. It cannot distinguish whether success (or failure) stems from correct semantic interpretation or robust physical execution.
 - **Source Non-Identifiability:** Even if attribution could be resolved, the source of generalization (e.g., VLM pre-training priors vs. robot interaction data) remains ambiguous because evaluation protocols do not control for differential exposure to these data sources. Out-of-distribution (OOD) definitions are typically insufficient, as objects novel to robot data might be familiar from VLM pre-training.
 - **Representation-Level Non-Identifiability:** Current benchmarks, by focusing on feasible tasks, do not assess whether underlying VLM capabilities like constraint-aware reasoning or feasibility judgment are preserved or degraded during fine-tuning into action policies. Changes in these deeper representations remain unobservable.

6. **Empirical Signature Analysis:**
 - The authors review published observations and attributed limitations from representative VLA systems (e.g., RT-2, OpenVLA-OFT, π0). They identify a consistent "empirical signature": observed physical execution failures or limitations are almost uniformly attributed to "engineering factors" such as data coverage, model scale, or training procedures, rather than questioning the semantic sufficiency assumption itself. This pattern is presented as evidence that the evaluation framework structurally prevents alternative explanations from being formulated or tested.

7. **Proposal for Evaluation Reform and Architectural Reorientation:**
 - Based on their critique, the authors propose a "forward path." This includes:
 - **Evaluation Reform:** Designing experiments with controlled variation that independently manipulate semantic content (holding physical conditions constant) and physical configuration (holding semantic content constant). They suggest using object properties that are linguistically expressible but not visually recoverable (e.g., mass, friction) to create separate, measurable outcomes for semantic correctness and physical success. They also advocate for defining OOD relative to *all* training data sources.
 - **Architectural Reorientation:** Shifting from assuming physical capability emerges from VLMs to designing systems with explicitly separated "semantic interfaces" and "physical modules" to allow independent assessment and improvement of each.

Through this rigorous analytical process, the paper constructs a comprehensive argument against the interpretability of current VLA benchmark results and offers concrete directions for methodological and architectural reform.

### 4. Main findings and results

The paper presents several core findings and conclusions derived from its theoretical analysis and review of existing literature, rather than empirical results from new experiments.

1. **Unverified Semantic Sufficiency Assumption:** The primary finding is that a foundational assumption in VLM-backbone VLA research—that semantic representations learned from internet-scale data are sufficient to guide physical action decisions—remains implicit and unverified. The authors demonstrate that the training signal for VLM backbones, derived from static image-text data, fundamentally lacks information about physical properties and action consequences, rendering the assumption structurally unjustified.

2. **Non-Identifiability in Current Evaluation Protocols:** The paper identifies a critical identifiability gap in current VLA evaluation. It shows that task success rate, the dominant metric, cannot distinguish between contributions from semantic generalization and physical execution generalization. This leads to non-identifiability at three levels:
 - **Attribution Non-Identifiability:** Observed performance gains or failures cannot be causally attributed to either semantic interpretation capabilities or physical execution capabilities separately.
 - **Source Non-Identifiability:** The origin of generalization (e.g., semantic priors from VLM pre-training versus physical understanding from robot interaction data) remains ambiguous, as current benchmarks do not control for VLM pre-training exposure.
 - **Representation-Level Non-Identifiability:** Changes or degradation of pre-existing VLM capabilities (e.g., constraint-aware reasoning, feasibility judgment) during fine-tuning into action policies are unobservable because current benchmarks do not test for these.

3. **"Narrative Drift" and Escalating Claims:** The analysis reveals a pattern, termed "narrative drift," where successive VLA systems (e.g., RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, GR00T N1) progressively attribute stronger and more general capabilities to their VLM backbones. This occurs despite the architectural core and fundamental verification methodologies remaining largely unchanged, and without explicit causal mechanisms being isolated. This trend indicates that interpretations of performance gains are often reinforced without rigorous empirical substantiation.

4. **Systemic Misdiagnosis of Failures:** The paper observes an "empirical signature" in the literature: when VLA systems exhibit physical execution limitations, these are almost consistently attributed to engineering factors such as insufficient data coverage, model scale, or training procedures. The alternative explanation—that the semantic sufficiency assumption itself might be failing—is rarely considered or evaluated. This pattern suggests a systemic misdiagnosis of architectural failures, driven by the limitations of the current evaluation framework.

5. **Consequences for Research and Deployment:** These limitations have significant systemic consequences:
 - **Misallocation of Research Resources:** Without clear diagnostic signals, research investments may be misdirected (e.g., scaling VLMs) along avenues that do not address the true bottlenecks in physical generalization.
 - **False Confidence in Deployment Readiness:** Benchmark successes, if driven by distributional overlap rather than genuine physical generalization, can lead to unwarranted confidence in a system's robustness for real-world deployment where physical conditions are less controlled.
 - **Persistent Architectural Misdiagnosis:** The inability to distinguish between semantic and physical failure modes hinders the identification and resolution of underlying architectural issues, potentially leading to a divergence between perceived and actual progress.

6. **Proposed Path for Verifiable Progress:** The paper's constructive "result" is the proposal for a "forward path" involving:
 - **Evaluation Reform:** Designing experimental protocols that introduce controlled variation to independently measure semantic and physical generalization. This includes varying semantic content while holding physical conditions constant, and vice versa, and utilizing object properties that are linguistically expressible but not visually recoverable.
 - **Architectural Reorientation:** Advocating for a shift in architectural framing from expecting physical capabilities to emerge implicitly from VLMs, to explicitly separating semantic grounding interfaces and physical modeling modules.

In summary, the paper's main finding is a fundamental methodological flaw in how VLA systems are currently evaluated, which prevents verifiable claims of physical reasoning and can lead to misleading interpretations of progress.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The significance and potential impact of this paper span both methodological and architectural aspects of embodied AI research, particularly within the VLA paradigm.

1. **Re-evaluation of VLA Progress and Claims:** The paper's most immediate impact is its critical re-evaluation of the progress claimed by VLM-backbone VLA systems. By demonstrating that current benchmarks cannot reliably verify physical reasoning, it challenges the interpretation of many reported performance gains. This could lead to a more nuanced and cautious assessment of VLA capabilities, distinguishing genuine advances in physical understanding from improvements in semantic matching or distributional alignment.

2. **Guidance for Robust Evaluation Design:** The proposed "forward path" for evaluation reform offers concrete, actionable guidance for the research community. By advocating for controlled experimental designs that disentangle semantic and physical capabilities, it provides a blueprint for creating more diagnostic and informative benchmarks. This could lead to the development of new evaluation protocols that can causally attribute performance, thereby accelerating progress in areas that truly contribute to robust physical interaction.

3. **Improved Resource Allocation and Research Focus:** By highlighting the potential for misallocation of research resources due to non-identifiable evaluation signals, the paper encourages a more strategic and evidence-based approach to research investment. Researchers might be prompted to critically assess whether scaling VLMs or collecting more robot data addresses the core bottleneck, leading to more efficient and impactful research directions.

4. **Enhanced Interpretability and Trustworthiness:** Enabling separate measurement of semantic and physical competence could significantly improve the interpretability of VLA systems. Understanding *why* a system succeeds or fails—whether due to semantic misunderstanding or physical execution error—is crucial for debugging, safety, and developing more trustworthy autonomous agents. This clarity is vital for real-world deployment where robust physical interaction is paramount.

5. **Shifting Architectural Paradigms:** The paper's call for reorienting the role of VLM backbones and explicitly separating semantic interfaces from physical modules could influence future architectural designs. This shift encourages the development of hybrid systems where components are engineered to fulfill distinct functions, potentially leading to more modular, robust, and understandable robotic intelligence. It implies that physical reasoning might need to be explicitly learned through interaction, rather than implicitly emerging from large-scale static data.

6. **Addressing "Narrative Drift" and Promoting Scientific Rigor:** By naming and analyzing "narrative drift," the paper encourages greater scientific rigor in attributing capabilities. It challenges the tendency to make progressively stronger claims without sufficient causal verification, fostering a culture of more precise language and substantiated claims within the field.

7. **Broader Implications for Embodied AI:** The analysis extends beyond VLA models to a general principle for embodied AI: the necessity of identifiable signals in evaluation. It underscores that implicit assumptions can become entrenched if not empirically challenged, potentially hindering the development of truly general embodied intelligence. It also highlights the importance of measuring the preservation or degradation of pre-existing capabilities (like feasibility judgment) during fine-tuning, which are often overlooked by task completion metrics.

In summary, this paper provides a timely and critical examination of fundamental methodological issues in VLA research. Its significance lies in its potential to redirect research efforts, improve evaluation practices, foster more robust and interpretable robotic systems, and ultimately contribute to a more scientifically grounded understanding of intelligence in embodied agents.
