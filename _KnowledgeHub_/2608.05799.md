# XEWorld: Can Action-Conditioned World Models Generalize to Unseen Robot Embodiments?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NLPR and the Chinese Academy of Sciences developed XEWorld, a diagnostic testbed, to investigate if action-conditioned world models can generalize to robots they were not trained on. Their findings reveal that current models primarily function as 2D visual pattern matchers, struggling to simulate unseen robot kinematics and suffering from catastrophic forgetting during adaptation.

## Problem

- Existing action-conditioned world models are typically evaluated only on robots they were trained on, making it difficult to assess their true generalization capabilities to unseen embodiments.
- A lack of systematic diagnostic testbeds that can rigorously decouple and evaluate a world model's understanding of physical dynamics versus memorization of visual patterns.
- Assumptions that generalization extends to new kinematics or that static visual references resolve discrepancies for unseen robots might overlook critical architectural limitations.

## Method

- Developed **XEWorld**, a controlled cross-embodiment testbed utilizing five bimanual robot setups and a ==held-out-embodiment protocol== to isolate the robot body as the sole variable.
- Implemented a ==decoupled metric suite== to diagnose performance across visual quality, robot morphology, kinematics, and object dynamics, alongside quantifying kinematic and appearance distances.
- Conducted extensive interventions on action representations and robot descriptions, and evaluated ==few-shot adaptation== and cross-architecture generalization using state-of-the-art world models.

## Results

- Pixel-space action inputs (optical flow) cut LPIPS up to **57%**, boost IoU up to **116%** vs. joint pose; aligned per-frame robot renders cut LPIPS up to **50%**, IoU up to **22%** (static descriptions: marginal).
- Few-shot adaptation to an unseen robot led to catastrophic forgetting of learned representations for known embodiments (e.g., **69%** increase in LPIPS error on a previously seen robot).
- Evaluations of other state-of-the-art world models confirmed a universal generalization failure, with LPIPS error increasing between **38% and 221%** for unseen robots, indicating architectural limitations beyond individual models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current action-conditioned world models primarily behave as 2D visual pattern matchers, with generalization ability strongly correlating (r=0.812) with visual appearance distance rather than physical kinematic distance.
> - Pixel-space action representations (e.g., optical flow) are crucial for cross-embodiment transfer, outperforming abstract numeric joint poses by sharing the geometric coordinate space of visual output.
> - Static or unaligned dynamic visual descriptions of unseen robots provide only marginal generalization benefits; explicit temporal and spatial alignment of appearance information is critical for effective usage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the broader field of robot learning, specifically focusing on action-conditioned world models and their generalization capabilities. Action-conditioned world models are learned simulators designed to predict the visual outcomes of a robot's actions over time, holding significant potential for downstream tasks such as offline policy evaluation, planning, and data generation in robotics.

Existing research in this area typically evaluates world models exclusively on robots they were trained on (in-distribution evaluations). This approach, while useful for assessing rendering quality on familiar platforms, cannot ascertain whether a model genuinely captures underlying, generalizable physical dynamics or merely memorizes embodiment-specific visual patterns. This paper addresses this limitation by proposing a novel testbed and evaluation framework designed to probe cross-embodiment generalization.

The research also relates to benchmarks for video world models, which primarily focus on perceptual quality or functional utility on *seen* target embodiments. These existing benchmarks often lack kinematics-grounded metrics or controlled counterfactual isolation necessary to verify if generated videos adhere to the geometric and mechanical constraints of a robot, especially an unseen one. XEWorld is introduced as a diagnostic testbed to fill this gap.

Furthermore, this work intersects with the long-standing goal of cross-embodiment robot learning, which aims to enable generalization across different physical robot structures. While much of this literature investigates whether control policies can generalize across various robot bodies, this paper shifts the focus to the generalization capabilities of action-conditioned world models themselves. The authors suggest these two directions are complementary, as a generalizable world model could provide essential counterfactual rollouts for evaluating cross-embodiment policies. By investigating the ability of world models to simulate unseen robots, this research contributes to the development of more robust and transferable learned simulators for robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate a fundamental question: "Can an action-conditioned world model faithfully render a robot it has never seen?" The motivation behind this question stems from the recognition that for world models to serve as reliable simulators in robotics, they must capture invariant physical dynamics rather than memorizing visual patterns specific to particular robot embodiments. Current evaluation practices, which assess models solely on training robots, do not provide sufficient insight into this distinction. A model that perfectly reproduces observed visual patterns might simply be memorizing, rather than developing a deeper understanding of physical interactions.

To address this, the authors aim to introduce a systematic testbed, XEWorld, that enables controlled empirical studies of cross-embodiment generalization. By isolating the robot embodiment as the sole varying factor while keeping the surrounding scene and object dynamics constant, the testbed allows for a rigorous examination of a world model's capacity to decouple the physical environment from specific robot appearances.

The research is further motivated by the need to re-examine two prevalent assumptions within the field of cross-embodiment transfer:
1. The belief that generalization naturally extends to new embodiments possessing similar kinematics.
2. The expectation that visual discrepancies when encountering unseen robots can be resolved by merely supplying static visual references of the target embodiment.

The authors postulate that these assumptions might overlook a critical disconnect between the task of static visual pattern matching and the more complex challenge of simulating dynamic physical motion. Through their investigation, they seek to identify architectural bottlenecks in current world models that prevent them from achieving true cross-embodiment generalization, thereby providing concrete design guidelines for future model development. The ultimate goal is to understand if current architectures genuinely capture physical dynamics or if their success on seen robots is largely attributable to memorization of visual characteristics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the development and application of XEWorld, a controlled cross-embodiment testbed, and a systematic empirical study using an adapted reference world model, along with evaluations of other state-of-the-art models.

#### 3.1. XEWorld Testbed Design

XEWorld is designed to isolate the robot's physical body as the primary experimental variable.

- **Embodiments:** The testbed utilizes five bimanual robot setups—Aloha-Agilex, Arx-X5, UR5, Franka Panda, and Piper—re-rendered in the RoboTwin simulator.
- **Data Protocol:** A "held-out-embodiment protocol" is employed. For a given task and seed, the scene layout, object poses, lighting, and camera parameters are kept byte-identical across all five robots. This ensures that any performance variation on an unseen robot is attributable solely to its embodiment.
 - **Data Splits:** For intervention studies, a fixed 3-train / 2-held-out split is used (Aloha-Agilex, Arx-X5, UR5 for training; Franka Panda and Piper for held-out). Franka and Piper were selected due to their similar kinematic distances to the training fleet but distinct visual appearance distances. For distance analysis, a leave-one-embodiment-out (LOEO) split is used.
 - **Dataset:** 25 manipulation tasks x 5 robots, with 50 paired training seeds and 20 disjoint held-out test seeds per task. Each episode includes RGB images, joint actions, rigid and articulated object poses, contact states, and camera parameters.
- **Evaluation Protocols:**
 - **Zero-shot setting:** Models generalize to a new embodiment given only descriptive specifications (e.g., a reference image or URDF geometry), without training data.
 - **Few-shot setting:** Models adapt by being provided with a limited number of real demonstrations of the held-out robot.
- **Decoupled Metric Suite:** Predicted rollouts are graded across four distinct dimensions to diagnose successes and failures precisely:
 - **Visual Quality:** Standard image-level fidelity metrics (SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS).
 - **Robot Morphology:** Evaluation of correct physical shape and appearance (Symmetric IoU, boundary F1, region-LPIPS between predicted and ground-truth robot masks, segmented via SAM2).
 - **Robot Kinematics:** Measurement of adherence to correct joint constraints (Keypoint reprojection accuracy (PCK), keypoint pixel error, normalized DTW comparing URDF-derived forward kinematics against CoTracker3-tracked predictions).
 - **Object Dynamics:** Assessment of correct object interaction (object-trajectory pixel error against simulator-recorded object poses, tracked with CoTracker3).
- **Embodiment Distance Quantification:** To understand generalization difficulty, each robot's distance to the training fleet is quantified:
 - **Kinematic distance:** Computed as the workspace Chamfer distance between point clouds sampled from each robot's forward-kinematics reachable set, capturing structural movement differences.
 - **Appearance distance:** Measured as the cosine distance between visual features extracted from standardized reference renders, capturing differences in color distribution and silhouette.

#### 3.2. Reference World Model and Interventions

The core empirical study utilizes an adapted version of FlowWAM (Chen et al. 2026a), a video diffusion model, fine-tuned on the three training robots. This model processes three input streams: scene RGB, a pixel-space action signal (robot-masked optical flow from a forward-kinematics render), and an embodiment specification stream. The decoupled architecture allows varying the richness of the embodiment specification.

- **Action Representation Interventions:** Comparison of four action representations: abstract numeric joint poses, projected ray maps, per-frame robot masks, and robot-masked optical flow. All are derived from commanded actions, URDF, and camera parameters, without privileged access to the target rollout.
- **Robot Description Interventions:** Progressively richer descriptions of the unseen robot provided through the specification stream:
 - Flow only (baseline, empty specification)
 - +Reference image (single front-view render)
 - +Multi-view (nine renders from different angles)
 - +Articulation clip (short video of joints sweeping)
 - +Per-frame robot render (target rendered with forward kinematics at every time step, perfectly aligned).
- **Few-Shot Adaptation:** Fine-tuning the reference model directly on M ∈ {1, 2, 3} target demonstrations per task. Performance on held-out robots measures recovery, while performance on previously seen robots (UR5) measures catastrophic forgetting.

#### 3.3. Cross-Architecture Evaluation

To confirm the generalizability of findings, three other recent world models—IRASim (Zhu et al. 2024), Ctrl-World (Guo et al. 2026), and EnerVerse-AC (Jiang et al. 2025)—were fine-tuned and evaluated on the XEWorld dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the generalization capabilities of action-conditioned world models to unseen robot embodiments:

#### 4.1. Impact of Action Representation

The choice of action representation significantly influences cross-embodiment transfer. Pixel-space action inputs demonstrated a substantial advantage over abstract numeric joint poses. Specifically, using optical flow as an input reduced LPIPS error by 29% on the Franka robot and 57% on the Piper robot, while improving symmetric IoU by 25% and 116%, respectively, compared to joint pose inputs. This suggests a representational bottleneck where current visual backbones struggle to translate abstract numeric kinematics into coherent spatial transformations for novel robot morphologies. Pixel-space actions, by sharing the geometric coordinate space of the visual output, effectively bypass this translation gap. The study also observed a trade-off: binary masks yielded superior morphological preservation (higher IoU), while optical flow led to better overall perceptual quality (lower LPIPS) by allowing more flexibility in synthesizing textures and lighting.

#### 4.2. Efficacy of Richer Robot Descriptions

The hypothesis that rich visual descriptions of an unseen robot would be sufficient for generalization was largely not supported. Providing progressively richer static descriptions—from a single reference image to nine static views and even a dynamic articulation video clip—yielded only marginal improvements. A single reference image offered some benefit (reducing robot-region LPIPS by 8% for Franka and 7% for Piper), but further static or unaligned dynamic cues provided negligible additional gains. Instead, the critical factor was **temporal and spatial alignment**. When a perfectly aligned per-frame robot render was provided at every time step, the improvements were substantial, cutting robot-region LPIPS by 50% on Franka and 45% on Piper, and boosting shape IoU by 22% and 8% respectively. This indicates that world models can effectively use robot appearance information only when it is explicitly registered to the target frames over time.

#### 4.3. Limitations of Few-Shot Adaptation

Directly fine-tuning the model on a few target demonstrations showed rapid improvements in rendering the unseen robot. With just one demonstration per task (25 episodes), LPIPS error was reduced by 30% on Franka and 32% on Piper, with diminishing returns for more data. However, this adaptation exhibited two significant limitations:
1. **Incomplete Appearance Recovery:** While global LPIPS scores improved substantially, the region-specific LPIPS for the robot itself only closed 51% (Franka) to 57% (Piper) of the gap, suggesting the model still struggled with precise mechanical details and textures.
2. **Catastrophic Forgetting:** This adaptation came at a severe functional cost. Fine-tuning to adapt to an unseen robot (e.g., Franka) caused a substantial increase in LPIPS error (e.g., 69% on the previously seen UR5 robot), indicating catastrophic forgetting of learned representations for known embodiments. This implies that current architectures overwrite existing knowledge to accommodate new visual patterns, acting as a localized patch rather than a fundamental solution.

#### 4.4. Correlation between Embodiment Distance and Generalization Difficulty

A strong empirical correlation (r = 0.812) was found between **visual appearance distance** (how visually different the unseen robot is from the training fleet) and the resulting global LPIPS error. This correlation was consistent and stable across different LOEO folds. In contrast, the **physical reachable workspace distance** (structural differences in how bodies move) showed only a weak and unstable relationship (r = 0.549) with generalization error. This finding suggests that current world models behave primarily as 2D visual pattern matchers, and their generalization capacity is dictated more by visual familiarity than by an understanding of underlying physical kinematics.

#### 4.5. Generalization Gap Across World Models

The observed generalization failure was not an artifact of the reference model but a shared limitation across diverse state-of-the-art architectures. Evaluations of IRASim, Ctrl-World, and EnerVerse-AC on the XEWorld dataset showed that every model experienced a notable performance drop (LPIPS error increases between 38% and 221% for Franka, and shape IoU drops by nearly 20%) when required to generate unseen robots. Models incorporating pixel-space action conditioning generally achieved stronger absolute performance on held-out robots, corroborating the earlier findings regarding action representations. This universal degradation underscores that scaling existing 2D generative priors is insufficient; fundamental architectural changes are needed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research significantly impacts the understanding and development of action-conditioned world models for robotics by challenging prevailing assumptions and highlighting critical architectural limitations.

**Re-evaluation of World Model Capabilities:** The study provides empirical evidence that current action-conditioned world models, despite their performance on training data, primarily function as "2D visual pattern matchers" rather than genuine simulators of physical dynamics. This re-evaluation necessitates a shift in how these models are assessed, moving beyond in-distribution rendering quality to focus on out-of-distribution generalization and a deeper understanding of learned physics.

**Diagnostic Testbed for Future Research:** The introduction of XEWorld provides a controlled, systematic testbed with decoupled metrics. This resource enables researchers to rigorously diagnose generalization failures, isolate contributing factors (e.g., morphology vs. kinematics), and test targeted interventions. It establishes a new standard for evaluating the cross-embodiment capabilities of world models.

**Identification of Key Bottlenecks:** The research identifies fundamental architectural bottlenecks:
- The struggle of visual backbones to translate abstract numeric joint actions into coherent spatial transformations for novel morphologies.
- The critical need for explicit temporal and spatial alignment of appearance information when rendering unseen robots, as static descriptions are largely ineffective.
- The catastrophic forgetting observed during few-shot adaptation, indicating that current models cannot dynamically bind new appearances to motion without overwriting existing knowledge.

**Concrete Design Guidelines:** The findings offer actionable guidelines for the design of future world models:
- Prioritizing **pixel-space action representations** (e.g., optical flow, projected ray maps) that intrinsically align with the visual output space.
- Developing mechanisms that establish **persistent, explicit geometric or structural conditioning** to the target morphology throughout a video rollout, rather than relying on unaligned static visual cues.
- The overarching call for **architectural innovations that explicitly decouple high-level visual appearance generation from underlying physical dynamics and kinematic constraints**. This suggests a move beyond merely scaling existing 2D generative priors.

**Impact on Robot Learning and Deployment:** If world models are to realize their full potential as reliable learned simulators for offline policy evaluation, planning, and data generation across diverse robot fleets, these generalization barriers must be overcome. The insights from XEWorld steer the field toward developing more physically grounded, robust, and generalizable robot learning systems. Addressing catastrophic forgetting in few-shot adaptation is also crucial for practical deployment in real-world scenarios where robots might encounter novel embodiments requiring rapid, incremental learning without degrading performance on previously mastered tasks. This research provides a foundational step towards building truly adaptable and intelligent robotic systems.
