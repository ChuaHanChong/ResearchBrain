# Decentralized Real-Time Planning for Multi-UAV Cooperative Manipulation via Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Delft University of Technology developed a decentralized, communication-free real-time planning method for multiple UAVs cooperatively manipulating cable-suspended loads, enabling full-pose control under partial observability. Their system, trained via imitation learning with physics-informed neural networks, achieved an orientation RMSE of 9.335 degrees in real-world tests and demonstrated robustness to communication delays.

## Problem

- Payload capacity limitations restrict the utility of single UAVs in tasks involving heavy or bulky object transportation.
- Existing centralized multi-UAV control methods for cooperative manipulation require high-bandwidth, low-latency communication and suffer from exponential scalability issues with increasing numbers of agents.
- Prior decentralized approaches often require continuous inter-agent communication or struggle to achieve agile and precise full-pose manipulation of cable-suspended payloads, frequently neglecting load orientation control.

## Method

- A machine learning-based framework utilizes imitation learning to train a strongly homogeneous decentralized student policy for each UAV, mimicking a state-of-the-art centralized kinodynamic motion planner.
- Individual UAVs generate their own receding-horizon reference trajectories using only local observations (ego UAV pose and twist, load pose) and a global load reference trajectory, without explicit inter-agent communication.
- Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) are integrated into the student policy's decoder to ensure kinematically consistent and smooth trajectory generation by explicitly enforcing derivative relationships (velocity as derivative of position, acceleration as derivative of velocity).

## Results

- In real-world experiments, the student policy demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, achieving an orientation RMSE of **9.335** degrees and position RMSE of **0.377** m for a figure-eight trajectory, comparable to or exceeding the teacher policy in orientation control.
- PINNs significantly improved kinematic consistency, with a Mean Absolute Error of **0.015** m/s for velocity estimation, outperforming a standard MLP decoder (**0.081** m/s) and closely matching the teacher policy (**0.016** m/s).
- The system showed robustness to communication delays and sloshing loads but exhibited performance degradation under significant model mismatches, such as doubling the payload mass (orientation RMSE increased from **13.107** to **44.469** degrees).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Imitation learning from a privileged centralized teacher policy effectively transfers complex cooperative coordination strategies to decentralized agents, improving sample efficiency by using full trajectories during training.
> - The use of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) as the trajectory decoder guarantees kinematic consistency, resulting in inherently feasible and smooth trajectories crucial for low-level controller tracking.
> - Implicit coordination among UAVs emerges through physical interactions with the shared cable-suspended load, allowing for full-pose manipulation without direct inter-agent communication.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are employed across various sectors due to their agility and versatility. However, their payload capacity limitations restrict their utility in tasks involving the transportation of heavy or bulky objects, which are common in industries such as construction and large-scale logistics. Cooperative aerial manipulation, where multiple UAVs collectively transport a cable-suspended payload, presents a scalable and cost-effective approach to overcome this limitation, enabling full pose manipulation of objects whose weight may exceed the capacity of a single UAV.

Existing control strategies for cooperative transportation of suspended payloads using UAV teams can be broadly categorized into three frameworks based on their communication and control architecture:

**Centralized Approaches:** These methods typically gather full system state information at a central coordinator to solve for force equilibrium or optimize control inputs. Examples include formation-based geometric control, which often neglects payload attitude and has limited validation in simulation, and Model Predictive Control (MPC). While linear MPC offers computational efficiency, it models the payload as a point mass, restricting its ability to control payload orientation and often resulting in suboptimal performance on dynamic trajectories. Nonlinear MPC (NMPC) techniques have advanced to allow full-pose manipulation along agile trajectories, including approaches that consider load-cable-UAV dynamics without time-scale separation assumptions. Despite their potential for agile and accurate manipulation, centralized controllers are constrained by their reliance on high-bandwidth, low-latency communication networks and their computational cost, which scales exponentially with the number of agents, thereby limiting scalability and practical deployment in environments with unreliable communication.

**Decentralized Approaches with Communication:** These frameworks distribute the computational burden across agents while maintaining performance comparable to centralized methods. For instance, distributed MPC approaches require a fully-connected communication graph, where each UAV shares its control inputs with neighbors and independently optimizes its own input sequence. While these methods exhibit scalability and coordination capabilities in simulation, they still necessitate continuous inter-agent communication, which may not be feasible in all real-world scenarios.

**Decentralized Approaches without Communication:** These strategies aim to eliminate the need for explicit inter-agent communication, offering benefits such as increased scalability and robustness to communication failures. Examples include distance-based formation control combined with incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion (INDI), where UAVs compute local acceleration commands based on relative position measurements. However, such methods often do not regulate payload orientation. Master-slave architectures, where a master UAV generates a reference trajectory for slave UAVs, are typically limited to two-UAV systems and lack full-pose manipulation capabilities. Generally, decentralized approaches lacking inter-agent communication have demonstrated challenges in achieving agile and precise full-pose manipulation of payloads.

The current research aims to address the identified gap by developing a decentralized, communication-free online kinodynamic motion planner that enables agile and accurate full-pose control of a rigid object using multiple autonomous UAVs, thus offering a solution that is both scalable and robust to communication limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a decentralized, real-time planning method for multi-UAV cooperative manipulation of cable-suspended loads that operates effectively under partial observability and without inter-agent communication. The method seeks to enable multiple autonomous UAVs to achieve agile and accurate full-pose control of a rigid object.

The key specific objectives include:
- To train a decentralized student policy for each UAV using imitation learning, mimicking a centralized kinodynamic motion planner (teacher policy) that has access to privileged global observations.
- To design the student policy to generate smooth trajectories by employing physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) that explicitly enforce derivative relationships for motion, ensuring kinematic consistency.
- To enhance sample efficiency during training by utilizing the full trajectory generated by the teacher policy.
- To validate the proposed method through both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating performance comparable to centralized approaches while adhering to decentralized constraints.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several limitations observed in existing cooperative aerial manipulation systems:
- **Reliance on Centralized Control:** Many high-performance control strategies for multi-UAV cooperative manipulation rely on a centralized control architecture. This requires high-bandwidth, low-latency communication networks for a central coordinator to process global state information and issue commands. Such reliance is often impractical in real-world environments where communication can be unreliable or unavailable.
- **Scalability Issues:** The computational cost of centralized control approaches typically increases exponentially with the number of agents, which limits their scalability to large UAV teams.
- **Limitations of Existing Decentralized Methods:** While some decentralized control strategies exist, many either require inter-agent communication or struggle to achieve agile and precise full-pose manipulation of a payload, often neglecting payload orientation control.
- **Need for Robustness:** Real-world deployments necessitate systems that are robust to communication failures and capable of operating with partial observability. A decentralized approach, where each UAV operates independently, enhances overall system robustness.
- **Efficiency in Training and Deployment:** Developing a machine learning-based approach that is efficient to train and can be deployed in real-time on onboard hardware is a significant motivation for practical application.

By addressing these issues, the research aims to provide a more practical, scalable, and robust solution for complex aerial manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a machine learning-based approach for decentralized kinodynamic planning in multi-UAV cooperative manipulation. It leverages imitation learning to enable individual UAVs to generate their own receding-horizon trajectories without explicit inter-agent communication.

**System Definition:** The system consists of `n` UAVs cooperatively transporting a single rigid load via cables. Each UAV is modeled as a unidirectional thrust platform, with cables attached below its center of mass and at arbitrary points on the load using universal joints. The primary objective is for the UAVs to collaboratively control the full pose (position and orientation) of the load to track a desired trajectory. In this fully decentralized setting, each UAV operates autonomously with its onboard control policy, relying solely on local observations (its own pose and twist, and the load's pose) and without direct communication with other UAVs. Coordination emerges implicitly through physical interactions.

**Teacher Policy:** The planning algorithm from Sun et al. serves as the teacher policy. This is a state-of-the-art centralized kinodynamic motion planner designed for multi-lifting systems that achieves agile and robust full-pose control of cable-suspended loads. It solves an optimization-based problem to generate receding-horizon reference trajectories for each UAV at 10 Hz, given the load's reference trajectory. The teacher policy relies on access to the full state information of all UAVs and the load.

**Student Policy:** The student policy is designed to learn from the teacher and generate trajectories for the ego UAV in a decentralized manner.
- **Homogeneous Architecture:** All UAVs run identical copies of the student policy, sharing the same architecture and weights.
- **Control Frequency:** The policy operates at 10 Hz, consistent with the teacher policy.
- **Architecture (Figure 2):** The student policy consists of an Encoder and a Decoder.
 - **Observation Space (Encoder Input):** The student policy for UAV `i` receives only local observations:
 - `o_i = [p_L^L, q_L^L, p_i^L, v_i^L, q_i^L, ω_i^L, p_{C1,i}^L, p_{C2,i}^L]`
 - `p_L^L`, `q_L^L`: Position and orientation of the load (expressed in load frame `L`).
 - `p_i^L`, `v_i^L`, `q_i^L`, `ω_i^L`: Position, linear velocity, orientation, and angular velocity of the ego UAV (expressed in load frame `L`).
 - `p_{C1,i}^L`, `p_{C2,i}^L`: Positions of the cable attachment points on the load and on the ego UAV, respectively (expressed in load frame `L`). These convey crucial information about applied forces and moments, acting as a form of implicit communication.
 - **Rotation Representation:** Rotations are represented using a 6D continuous formulation to avoid gimbal lock and discontinuities, improving differentiability and learning stability.
 - **History and Reference Trajectory:** To reason under partial observability, the policy uses a history of past observations (`O_i = [o_{i,t}, o_{i,t−dt}..., o_{i,t−Ndt}]`). Additionally, it receives the reference trajectory for the load for the next 2 seconds (`Y = [y_0..., y_N]`), where `y_j` includes load position, velocity, acceleration, orientation, and angular velocity.
 - **Encoder Network:** Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) are used to process the sequential observation histories and reference trajectories. The outputs of separate TCNs for different information streams are concatenated into a latent vector `x_i` that encodes the current system state and desired future references.
 - **Action Space (Decoder Output):** The student policy is an online planner that directly generates reference trajectories for the ego UAV. The action `U = [u_0..., u_N]` for UAV `i` consists of `u_j = [p, ω, v, a]_j`, representing desired position, body rates, velocity, and acceleration for each node `j` in the horizon.
 - **Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs):** A key aspect is the use of PINNs as the decoder to ensure kinematic consistency. Instead of directly predicting `p, v, a`, the PINN predicts `p_j` and `ω_j` as a function of the latent vector `x_i` and time `t_j`. The velocity `v_j` and acceleration `a_j` are then computed as the first and second time derivatives of the predicted position: `v_j = ∂F_mlp(x_i, t_j)/∂t` and `a_j = ∂^2F_mlp(x_i, t_j)/∂t^2`. This architectural constraint guarantees that the generated trajectories are inherently feasible and smooth.

**Training Environment and Procedure:**
- **Simulator:** The student policy is exclusively trained within the Gazebo (RotorS) simulation environment.
- **Learning Algorithm:** Privileged learning in conjunction with the Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) algorithm is employed. DAgger is an iterative imitation learning algorithm where data collection alternates between the teacher and student policies, and the student is trained to imitate the teacher's actions. The probability of using the student policy for data collection gradually increases over iterations to generate a more diverse dataset and enhance robustness.
- **Robustness Enhancement:** Randomized external force-torque disturbances are applied to the load during data collection to promote exploration of a broader range of system dynamics and improve the generalization capability of the policy.
- **Low-Level Control:** Both teacher and student policies utilize the same low-level flatness-based controllers with INDI to track the generated reference trajectories and compensate for cable forces/torques.
- **Training Time:** Each student policy can be trained in under two hours on a standard laptop.

This integrated approach, combining imitation learning, a homogeneous policy, and PINNs for kinematically consistent trajectory generation, aims to deliver a decentralized solution capable of complex cooperative aerial manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides several findings from validation in both simulation and real-world environments.

**Real-World Validation and Trajectory Tracking:**
- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted using three 0.6 kg quadrotors (Agilicious platform) cooperatively manipulating a 1.4 kg rigid payload suspended by three 1m cables. Onboard computation for low-level controllers was handled by Raspberry Pi 5 mini PCs, while the student policy inference ran on a laptop, transmitting trajectories via Wi-Fi. A motion capture system provided high-precision state estimation.
- **Performance Comparison (Figure 3, Table I):** The student policy, trained on a figure-eight trajectory, demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. In real-world flights, it achieved an orientation RMSE of 9.335 degrees, comparable to the teacher policy's 10.447 degrees. However, its position RMSE was 0.377 m, which was higher than the teacher policy's 0.171 m.
- **Inter-Agent Distance (Figure 4, 6, 10):** Despite operating without inter-agent communication, the quadrotors generally maintained safe inter-agent distances. While occasional breaches of the 1 m separation threshold were observed in real-world experiments, the UAVs typically recovered. The paper notes that using motion capture for load state estimation (Student Policy MoCap) significantly improved safe distance maintenance compared to an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) based estimation (Student Policy EKF), which suffered from noise in real-world deployment.

**Generalizability to Unseen Trajectories:**
- **Similar Trajectories (Table II, V, Figure 7):** When trained on two distinct figure-eight trajectories and evaluated on multiple unseen figure-eight trajectories of varying sizes in simulation, the student policies showed limited ability to generalize. Performance degraded for trajectories significantly different in size or curvature, indicating potential overfitting to the training data's specific motion patterns.
- **Dissimilar Trajectories (Table IV, VI, Figure 9, 11, 12):** The student policy was also trained on a diverse set of trajectories (Eight, Circle, Square) with two orientation modes (Constant orientation and Zero Side-slip).
 - **Constant Orientation:** The policy performed well, achieving low errors (e.g., 0.049m position RMSE, 3.518 deg orientation RMSE for a 2m radius circle).
 - **Zero Side-slip (ZSS) Orientation:** For ZSS, which requires more complex UAV maneuvers, the policy exhibited higher errors (e.g., 0.134m position RMSE, 10.403 deg orientation RMSE for a 2m radius circle).
 - **Zandvoort F1 Track Evaluation:** On the novel Zandvoort F1 track (dissimilar to training data), the student policy (trained on both orientation modes) showed good position tracking for constant orientation (0.092m RMSE) and acceptable orientation tracking (3.157 deg RMSE). However, for ZSS orientation, position RMSE increased to 0.378m and orientation RMSE significantly deteriorated to 93.575 degrees, indicating challenges in generalizing complex orientation patterns to entirely novel paths.

**Advantage of PINN over MLP as Decoder (Table III):**
- Comparing the PINN-based decoder with a standard Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) decoder, PINNs demonstrated a significantly lower Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between the numerical derivative of predicted position and the predicted velocity. For instance, the overall MAE for PINN was 0.015 m/s, comparable to the teacher's 0.016 m/s, while the MLP exhibited a higher error of 0.081 m/s. This confirms that PINNs effectively enforce kinematic consistency, generating smoother and physically feasible trajectories.

**Full Pose Control and Sequential Commands (Table VIII, IX, Figure 13):**
- The student policy successfully manipulated the payload to desired goal poses. It achieved smoother payload manipulation compared to the teacher policy, which exhibited oscillations attributed to noisy EKF measurements. However, the centralized teacher policy generally achieved superior accuracy in the final measured pose (e.g., 0.015m position RMSE for teacher vs. 0.097m for student). The policy also demonstrated generalization to sequential pose commands, with average position and orientation errors of 0.058m and 3.590 degrees respectively across multiple trials.

**Robustness (Table X, Figure 14):**
- The method was tested against various disturbances in simulation:
 - **Sloshing Load:** Robustness was maintained with minor degradation (position RMSE 0.271m, orientation RMSE 17.795 deg vs. baseline 0.181m, 13.107 deg).
 - **Communication Delay:** The receding horizon trajectory generation made the system robust to communication delays (20% dropped packets, 30ms random delay), with minimal performance impact.
 - **Mass Variation (double load mass) and Attachment Point/Rope Length Variations:** These significant model mismatches led to notable performance degradation, particularly in orientation tracking (mass variation: position RMSE 0.334m, orientation RMSE 44.469 deg; attachment/rope mismatch: position RMSE 0.289m, orientation RMSE 47.562 deg).

**Computational Time (Figure 15):**
- Student policy inference on a laptop GPU (NVIDIA RTX 3060) averaged 27 milliseconds. On a Raspberry Pi 5 CPU, it took 97 milliseconds. The teacher policy (for 3 UAVs) required an average of 101 milliseconds on an Intel Core i7-13700H CPU. The decentralized nature of the student policy allows for potential horizontal scaling, where computational time per UAV does not increase with the number of UAVs, unlike the teacher policy whose complexity grows exponentially.

These results indicate the method's effectiveness in achieving decentralized, communication-free cooperative manipulation, with comparable performance to centralized methods in certain aspects, particularly regarding orientation control and real-time execution. Challenges remain in precise position tracking and generalization to highly novel trajectories, especially under significant model perturbations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a decentralized real-time planning method for multi-UAV cooperative manipulation, addressing key limitations of existing approaches. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several points:

**Addressing Core Challenges in Multi-Robot Systems:**
- **Overcoming Communication Dependency:** The proposed method eliminates the need for explicit inter-agent communication, which is a significant advancement for real-world deployment where communication links can be unreliable, delayed, or unavailable. This enhances the robustness and autonomy of multi-UAV systems.
- **Scalability:** By employing a strongly homogeneous decentralized student policy, the computational burden on individual UAVs remains constant regardless of the team size. This offers a pathway to scalable cooperative manipulation with a large number of UAVs, a challenge for centralized approaches whose computational cost typically grows exponentially.
- **Real-time Operation under Partial Observability:** The system demonstrates real-time planning capabilities while each UAV operates with only local observations, a more realistic scenario for autonomous agents than requiring privileged global information.

**Technological Contributions:**
- **Imitation Learning from a Privileged Expert:** The use of imitation learning from a centralized, high-performance teacher policy effectively transfers complex coordination strategies to decentralized agents. This paradigm efficiently trains robust policies by leveraging the teacher's full trajectory during training, improving sample efficiency compared to reinforcement learning.
- **Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) for Kinematic Consistency:** Integrating PINNs into the decoder ensures that the generated trajectories are kinematically consistent, meaning velocity is the derivative of position and acceleration is the derivative of velocity. This architectural constraint guarantees physically feasible and smooth trajectories, which are crucial for reliable control and tracking by low-level onboard controllers.
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The ability to train the policy entirely in simulation and deploy it in the real world without further fine-tuning demonstrates robustness and transferability, which is a desirable characteristic for robotic systems.

**Practical Implications:**
- **Enhanced Autonomy and Robustness:** The decentralized nature makes the system more resilient to individual agent failures or communication outages, as each UAV can continue to operate based on its local observations and learned behaviors.
- **Broader Application Scope:** This method has potential applications in various domains requiring cooperative aerial manipulation, such as construction (lifting beams, modules), logistics (transporting large packages in warehouses or outdoor environments), and disaster response (moving debris). The ability to manipulate the full pose of objects increases the versatility of such systems.
- **Computational Efficiency:** While the inference time on a Raspberry Pi 5 CPU is noted, the overall approach's computational efficiency per agent and its potential for horizontal scaling suggest a viable path for practical onboard deployment without excessive hardware requirements.

**Acknowledged Limitations and Future Research:**
- **Inter-Agent Collision Avoidance:** A notable limitation is the absence of hard guarantees for inter-agent collision avoidance in the decentralized student policies. While the policies learn safe behaviors by imitating the teacher, explicit collision constraints are not enforced. This suggests a future research direction for incorporating onboard sensing for inter-UAV perception and obstacle detection to enhance safety.
- **Generalization to Novel Trajectories and Robustness to Model Mismatches:** The policy showed limited generalization to entirely novel trajectory patterns, particularly for complex orientation modes (zero-sideslip), and exhibited sensitivity to significant model mismatches (e.g., changes in payload mass or cable attachment points). Future work could focus on training with more diverse and randomized trajectory sets or employing adaptive mechanisms to improve robustness to unmodeled dynamics.

In summary, this research contributes a novel, machine learning-based framework that advances the state of decentralized cooperative aerial manipulation. By addressing critical issues of communication dependency, scalability, and trajectory consistency, it paves the way for more autonomous, robust, and widely applicable multi-UAV systems in complex real-world environments.
