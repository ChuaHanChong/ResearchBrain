# SPADE: Self-Play in Adaptive Synthetic Executable Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> SPADE is a self-play RL framework where one LLM plays Environment Designer (writes executable Gym-style reset/step environments as Python code) and Reasoning Agent (solves them), with the Designer trained via hint-based regret so the curriculum tracks the Agent's frontier; at 30B scale it beats the strongest fixed-environment baseline by +5.3 points across eight held-out benchmarks and +5.7 to +13.9 on tool-use evals.

## Problem

- Hand-curated, statically synthesized, or frozen-verifier environment pools stay fixed as the learner scales, so agents stop improving once they exhaust them.
- Ungrounded self-play proposers are bounded by information symmetry, collapse toward their own knowledge, and amplify errors without an external novelty source.
- Prior task-generation self-play methods produce single problems with sparse terminal rewards, not full multi-turn MDPs with state transitions and verification code.

## Method

- ==Dual-role self-play== where a single shared-weight LLM alternates Environment Designer and Reasoning Agent roles, representing any environment as an executable Gym-style Python program.
- **Hint-based regret reward**: Designer reward is the Reasoning Agent's return gap with versus without a privileged hint, targeting environments at the solvable-but-hard learning frontier.
- ==Corpus-grounded, memory-augmented design pipeline==: fresh pretraining-corpus passages seed novelty while an environment memory of past regret-scored tasks steers difficulty away from mastered or intractable cases.

## Results

- At 30B-A3B, **SPADE** reaches suite average **58.3**, **+8.1** over base and **+5.3** over the strongest fixed-environment baseline (Fixed-env RLVE), with the margin growing with model scale.
- Tool-use setting lifts BFCL v4 multi-turn by **+10.3** at 4B and **+5.7** at 30B-A3B, and ACEBench-Agent by **+13.9** at 30B-A3B, surpassing dedicated data-synthesis systems like AgentScaler and EnvScaler.
- Ablations show freezing the Designer and dropping memory drops the average to **40.5** (9.7 points below untrained base), and hint-based regret outperforms an EMA learning-potential signal (**+8.1** versus **+5.7**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Making environment generation itself a trained, co-evolving component (not a frozen generator) is what sustains a curriculum as the learner improves.
> - External corpus grounding is the mechanism that prevents self-play mode collapse, an 'invisible leash' problem where ungrounded generators narrow toward their own outputs.
> - Representing environments as executable code rather than parameterized task spaces lets one interface unify single-turn reasoning and multi-turn agentic tool-use training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the advancement of language agents and reinforcement learning (RL) through self-improvement mechanisms. The current landscape for training advanced AI agents, particularly large language models (LLMs), faces a significant bottleneck: the supply of diverse, high-quality interactive training environments. Existing approaches to provide these environments have limitations:
- **Harness engineering** improves inference-time performance but does not update model weights, limiting general capability improvements.
- **Human-curated scaling** relies on manual environment creation, which is slow and expensive.
- **Synthetic generation** uses fixed generators, leading to agents exhausting the environment space as their capabilities grow.
- **Self-play methods** for LLMs exist, but often rely on curated seed data, generate only "tasks" (problems with sparse terminal rewards) rather than complete multi-turn "environments," or can exhibit mode collapse and error amplification due to information symmetry or ungrounded generation.

This research addresses these limitations by introducing a framework where the environment design itself is a learnable component, evolving alongside the agent. It integrates concepts from:
- **Self-play in RL**, as seen in game-playing AIs like AlphaZero, but adapted for the open-ended nature of language models.
- **Unsupervised Environment Design (UED)**, which aims to automatically generate increasingly complex and diverse training environments, particularly building on minimax regret objectives. However, traditional UED often operates within parameterized, fixed design spaces.
- **Environment synthesis and scaling**, which leverages LLMs to programmatically create large-scale training environments, but typically with a frozen or separately trained generator.

SPADE aims to bridge these areas by creating a system capable of open-ended, continual self-improvement by dynamically generating its own training environments as executable code, adapting their difficulty and diversity in response to the agent's evolving capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable continuous self-improvement for language agents by overcoming the limitations of static or fixed-distribution training environments. The core motivations are:

- **Addressing the Environment Bottleneck:** High-quality human text data for pretraining is a finite resource. As LLM agents become more capable through learning from experience, the supply of interactive tasks with verifiable rewards becomes a critical bottleneck for further progress. Manual creation of such environments is costly and slow, while existing synthetic generators are static. The work aims to automate and adapt this environment generation process.
- **Enabling Open-Ended Self-Improvement:** Current training paradigms often lead to agents plateauing once they have mastered a fixed pool of environments. The goal is to move beyond this by creating a system where the training environment distribution continually expands and adapts in complexity as the agent improves, fostering open-ended learning.
- **Developing an Adaptive Curriculum:** A key motivation is to design a mechanism that generates environments at the "learning frontier" – tasks that are challenging enough to drive improvement but still solvable. This contrasts with purely adversarial generation, which might produce unsolvable tasks, or cooperative generation, which might produce trivial tasks.
- **Unifying Task Types:** The research aims to develop a single, coherent framework that can handle both single-turn reasoning problems and multi-turn agentic tasks (like tool use) under a unified interface. This simplifies the training pipeline and suggests a more generalizable approach to agent training.
- **Grounding and Stabilizing Self-Play:** Unbounded self-play in LLMs can suffer from mode collapse and amplification of errors due to a lack of external information or proper reward signals. A motivation is to integrate mechanisms like corpus grounding and environment memory to stabilize the self-play process and ensure the generated environments are diverse, relevant, and challenging.
- **Demonstrating Scalability:** The work seeks to validate that such a self-play framework for adaptive environment generation can be effectively implemented and scaled to large language models (e.g., 30B parameters) with a practical training recipe.

In summary, the objective is to make environment design a dynamic, learnable component of the training process, allowing an LLM to both create and learn from its own executable environments, thereby advancing towards more autonomous and continuous self-improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPADE (Self-Play in Adaptive Synthetic Executable Environments) is a reinforcement learning framework that employs a single Large Language Model (LLM) to fulfill two distinct roles: an Environment Designer and a Reasoning Agent. The methodology centers on a co-evolutionary process where both roles improve over time by interacting within a shared training loop.

### 4. Dual-Role Self-Play and Code-as-Environment

- **Single LLM, Dual Roles:** A single LLM, parameterized by $\pi_\theta$, alternates between two roles dictated by role-specific system prompts:
 - **Environment Designer ($\pi_D$):** Generates complete training environments as executable Python code.
 - **Reasoning Agent ($\pi_A$):** Learns to act and solve problems within the generated environments.
- **Code-as-Environment Representation:** Each generated environment is a Python program implementing the standard OpenAI Gym-style `reset()`/`step()` API. This representation models the environment as a full Markov Decision Process (MDP), defining state transitions and reward functions within the `step()` method. This design allows the framework to unify single-turn reasoning tasks (e.g., one interaction to a terminal reward) and multi-turn agentic tasks (sequential interactions with dynamic state changes) under a consistent interface. The programmability of environments allows for an unbounded task space.
- **Parameter Sharing:** Both roles share the same LLM parameters $\theta$. This means improvements in one role (e.g., the Reasoning Agent becoming better at problem-solving) directly influence the other (e.g., the Environment Designer becoming more adept at crafting challenging tasks).

### 5. Hint-Based Regret Reward for Environment Designer

- **Regret Signal:** The Environment Designer's reward for generating an environment $e$ is defined as the hint-based regret: $r_D(e) = \bar{r}_A(e | h) - \bar{r}_A(e)$.
 - $\bar{r}_A(e | h)$: The Reasoning Agent's average return on environment $e$ when provided with a privileged hint $h$ (task-relevant information generated by the Environment Designer itself).
 - $\bar{r}_A(e)$: The Reasoning Agent's average return on environment $e$ without the hint.
- **Frontier Targeting:** This regret signal incentivizes the Environment Designer to create environments that are at the edge of the Reasoning Agent's capabilities.
 - High regret indicates environments solvable with hints but not without, representing a learnable frontier.
 - Low regret when the agent succeeds both with and without hints indicates mastery.
 - Low regret with low returns even with hints indicates an intractable environment.
- **Minimax Regret Connection:** This approach is conceptually aligned with the minimax regret objective in Unsupervised Environment Design (UED), where the hint-equipped Reasoning Agent serves as an upper-bound policy, effectively constraining the competitive dynamics to produce solvable challenges.
- **Hint Generation:** For each generated environment, the Environment Designer (using a specific prompt) also produces a hint—a few sentences providing key insight or strategy without revealing the direct solution.

### 6. Environment Design Pipeline

- **Corpus Grounding:** To prevent mode collapse and ensure diversity, the Environment Designer conditions its generation on documents sampled from a large pretraining corpus (e.g., mathematics and science documents for games, code documents for tool use). This supplies external novelty.
- **Environment Memory:** A buffer stores previously generated environments, annotated with their regret scores and skill tags. This memory guides the Environment Designer to generate variations of high-regret environments and avoid those already mastered or found intractable, helping to maintain difficulty at the Reasoning Agent's evolving frontier.
- **Environment Validation:** Generated environments undergo syntactic and executability checks. For tool-use environments, additional semantic checks verify solvability and criterion correctness (e.g., a criterion is not pre-satisfied or impossible to meet).
- **Curriculum Design:** Environments are categorized by skill (e.g., Mathematical Reasoning, Logical Deduction). The Environment Designer rotates through these skills, ensuring broad training.
- **Reward Blending:** The Environment Designer's final reward combines the floored hint-based regret (0.4 weight) with a flat-top difficulty anchor (0.6 weight) that rewards environments where the Reasoning Agent's win rate falls within a target band (e.g., [0.4, 0.6]).

### 7. Training Mechanism

- **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization):** The LLM policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained using GRPO, a reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) algorithm. GRPO samples a group of responses, computes group-normalized advantages, and updates the policy via a clipped policy gradient with KL regularization.
- **Stabilization Techniques:** Several techniques are implemented for stable dual-role training: independent advantage normalization for each role, mean-centering Environment Designer rewards per skill, upweighting less frequent Environment Designer trajectories, and delaying the Environment Designer update to allow full evaluation of Reasoning Agent performance.

This comprehensive methodology aims to create an autonomously evolving training system where the agent and its learning environment co-adapt and improve.

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across two primary settings: synthetic game environments and synthetic tool-use environments, evaluated on Qwen3 backbones (4B, 8B, 30B-A3B parameters).

### 9. Quantitative Analysis

- **Games Setting Performance (Table 1, Figure 5):**
 - SPADE demonstrates performance improvements over fixed-environment baselines across held-out benchmarks in mathematics, science, code generation, and procedural reasoning.
 - At the 30B-A3B scale, SPADE achieves an average score of 58.3, representing an +8.1 percentage point (pp) gain over the untrained base model and a +5.3 pp gain over the strongest fixed-environment baseline (Fixed-env RLVE).
 - The gains are observed to grow with model scale (e.g., +5.2 pp at 4B, +5.7 pp at 8B, +8.1 pp at 30B-A3B), while fixed-environment baselines show limited improvements (around +1.2 pp) across scales. This suggests that larger models benefit more from the adaptive curriculum.
 - Improvements are particularly pronounced in procedural reasoning categories (Reasoning-Gym), with gains of +18.3 on RG-Math, +14.1 on RG-Algorithmic, and +14.7 on RG-Cognition at 30B-A3B. Performance on competition math is preserved, while science reasoning (GPQA-Diamond) and code generation (LiveCodeBench-v6) also show notable increases.
 - Evaluation trajectories indicate that SPADE sustains improvements throughout the 400 training steps, whereas fixed-environment baselines tend to saturate earlier.

- **Tool-Use Setting Performance (Table 2):**
 - SPADE improves performance on multi-step tool-use benchmarks, with gains correlating with the structural similarity between generated environments and evaluation tasks.
 - At 30B-A3B, SPADE lifts ACEBench-Agent by +13.9 pp and BFCL v4 multi-turn by +5.7 pp (and +10.3 pp at 4B). Gains on $\tau^2$-bench are +3.6 pp.
 - SPADE outperforms several dedicated synthetic-environment systems on BFCL v4 multi-turn and ACEBench-Agent at the 30B-A3B scale, suggesting effective transfer of training signals from its self-generated, structurally similar environments.

### 10. Qualitative Analysis

- **Adaptive Curriculum Evolution (Figure 2, Figure 6):**
 - The Environment Designer continuously provides "learnable" environments (where the Reasoning Agent's win rate is between 20% and 80%). The fraction of learnable environments in a rollout increases over training, reaching approximately one-third by the end of the 400-step run for full SPADE, indicating successful difficulty targeting.
 - The generated environments shift from simpler single-skill tasks early in training to more complex, state-gated, multi-turn interactions later.
- **Sustained Diversity (Figure 7, Table 8):**
 - Corpus grounding is observed to be crucial for maintaining environment diversity. Full SPADE maintains high semantic diversity (Vendi/n score of 0.68) throughout training, contrasting with the "no corpus grounding" ablation, which quickly collapses to a limited set of environment types (Vendi/n score of 0.04).
 - The diversity of generated environments stems from the corpus, while Environment Designer training and memory contribute to increased difficulty and learning frontier targeting within that diverse space.
- **Increased Environment Difficulty and Granularity (Figure 8, Figure 16):**
 - Over training, the Environment Designer learns to create harder environments in measurable ways. For example, in physics tasks, the rate at which the opening observation reveals the governing formula decreases from 25% to 5%.
 - Reward functions become more finely graded, with the average number of distinct reward levels per environment increasing from 3.7 to 5.8 (with strictly partial rewards increasing from 2.2 to 4.0), enabling more nuanced learning signals.
- **Reasoning Agent Behavioral Shift (Figure 9):**
 - The Reasoning Agent's interaction style evolves: from front-loaded derivation that struggles with format errors early on, to testing short hypotheses and revising based on feedback (mid-training), and eventually to evidence-first gathering and derivation only once sufficient information is acquired (late-training). This suggests the agent learns to adapt its reasoning strategy to interactive environments.

### 11. Ablation Studies (Table 3, Figure 10, Figure 11)

- **Importance of Environment Designer Adaptation and Memory:**
 - Removing both Environment Designer training and memory results in performance below the untrained base model (-9.7 pp), highlighting the importance of the co-adaptive setup.
 - Freezing the Environment Designer (even using a strong frontier model like GPT-5.5) yields smaller gains compared to the fully adaptive SPADE (+2.8 pp vs. +8.1 pp), indicating that dynamic adaptation is more effective than static generation.
 - Removing either corpus grounding or environment memory individually diminishes gains and can lead to earlier performance plateaus or declines.
- **Environment Designer Reward Comparison:**
 - Hint-based regret as the Environment Designer's reward consistently outperforms an EMA-based learning potential signal, achieving a larger suite average gain (+8.1 pp vs. +5.7 pp). This suggests hint-based regret is more effective at targeting the learning frontier.

### 12. Scaling Results (Figure 12, Figure 13)

- **Model Size:** The average performance gain over the base model increases with LLM scale (from +5.2 pp at 4B to +8.1 pp at 30B-A3B). This indicates that larger models are better equipped to leverage the adaptive curriculum generated by SPADE.
- **Curriculum Diversity:** A broader curriculum (6 cognitive skills vs. 2 skills) leads to greater performance gains, particularly in the Reasoning-Gym categories, suggesting that diversity in generated environments is beneficial for generalization.

Overall, the findings indicate that making environment design a learnable, RL-trained component within a self-play framework, particularly when grounded in external data and augmented with memory, enables continuous improvement and robust generalization for LLM agents across diverse reasoning and tool-use tasks.

### 13. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPADE framework represents an advancement toward open-ended, continual self-improvement for large language models, addressing a significant bottleneck in current AI agent development. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Autonomous AI Development:** By making environment design a learnable component, SPADE takes a step towards systems that can autonomously generate their own training curricula and improve indefinitely. This reduces reliance on human-curated datasets and fixed synthetic generators, which often limit the scalability and diversity of training experiences.
- **Generalizable Agent Capabilities:** The framework demonstrates that training on self-generated, adaptive environments can lead to improved generalization across diverse, held-out benchmarks, including complex math, science, code, and procedural reasoning tasks. This suggests that the skills learned within SPADE's flexible environment space are transferable.
- **Unified Approach to Reasoning and Agentic Tasks:** The "code-as-environment" paradigm, which uses a standard Gym-style interface, effectively unifies single-turn reasoning problems and multi-turn agentic tool-use tasks. This provides a versatile foundation for training generalist AI agents capable of handling a broad spectrum of interactions.
- **Adaptive Curriculum Generation:** The hint-based regret reward mechanism is a key innovation for curriculum learning. It allows the Environment Designer to dynamically target environments at the precise frontier of the Reasoning Agent's capabilities, ensuring that training remains challenging but feasible, thus sustaining learning. This contrasts with fixed curricula that an agent might quickly exhaust.
- **Scalability to Large Models:** The research provides a practical recipe for implementing such a self-play framework at scales up to 30B parameters. The observation that performance gains increase with model size suggests that this approach could unlock further capabilities in even larger, future LLMs.
- **Mitigation of Self-Play Limitations:** The integration of corpus grounding and environment memory effectively addresses common issues in ungrounded self-play, such as mode collapse and the amplification of errors. Corpus grounding provides necessary external novelty, while memory ensures the curriculum remains relevant and avoids redundancy.
- **Foundational for Future Research:** SPADE lays groundwork for exploring how AI systems can not only learn but also actively shape their own learning experiences. This opens avenues for research into meta-learning of learning rules, the interplay between learned weights and in-context evolution for environment design, and integrating adaptive environment generation with other stages of autonomous training pipelines.

Despite certain limitations (e.g., complexity bounded by base model scale, human-designed optimizers, fixed-task evaluation), SPADE contributes a concrete method for fostering open-ended learning, potentially accelerating the development of more generally intelligent and continually improving AI agents.
