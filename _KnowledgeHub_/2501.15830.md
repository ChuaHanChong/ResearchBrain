# SpatialVLA: Exploring Spatial Representations for Visual-Language-Action Model

> [!summary] Summary
> Shanghai AI Laboratory's SpatialVLA introduces novel spatial representations for Vision-Language-Action models, equipping them with profound 3D spatial understanding via Ego3D Position Encoding for observations and unifying actions via Adaptive Action Grids. The model achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on diverse real-world robot tasks and demonstrates efficient adaptation to new robot setups, surpassing larger models like RT-2-X with a smaller parameter count.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models primarily rely on 2D observation inputs, lacking precise perception and comprehension of the 3D physical world essential for robot manipulation.
- Observations from diverse robot embodiments are heterogeneous due to varying camera placements and calibrations, leading to non-aligned 3D observation spaces.
- Learning generalizable spatial actions is challenging across heterogeneous robots due to differing kinematics, degrees of freedom, and motion controllers.

## Method

- **SpatialVLA** introduces ==Ego3D Position Encoding==, which integrates 3D spatial context into 2D visual features by estimating depth and encoding egocentric 3D pixel positions, making observations 3D-aware and robot-agnostic.
- Proposes ==Adaptive Action Grids==, which discretize continuous robot actions into discrete ==spatial tokens== using ==parameterized Gaussian distributions== for non-uniform, probability-equal binning, enabling generalizable action learning with fewer tokens for faster inference.
- Employs a ==two-stage training== scheme: pre-training on 1.1 million real robot demonstrations (Open X-Embodiment and RH20T) followed by efficient post-training using a novel ==Spatial Embedding Adaption== technique for new robot setups.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on SimplerEnv (Google Robot **71.9%** Visual Matching, **68.8%** Variant Aggregation) and real-world WidowX (highest average success), outperforming larger models like RT-2-X with significantly fewer parameters.
- Demonstrated superior adaptation by achieving the highest average success rate (**78.1%**) on the LIBERO simulation benchmark after fine-tuning and significantly outperforming OpenVLA and Diffusion Policy in Franka instruction-following tasks (**+12%** improvement).
- Consistently showed superior spatial understanding, achieving **73%** accuracy in a Franka task requiring spatial prompt understanding and improving manipulation in out-of-distribution tasks involving explicit height changes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating 3D spatial representations via Ego3D Position Encoding is crucial for Vision-Language-Action models to achieve robust and generalized robot manipulation across varied environments and camera poses.
> - Discretizing continuous robot actions into 'adaptive' grids based on their statistical distribution significantly improves generalization and convergence compared to uniform discretization.
> - Freezing pre-trained language embeddings during training preserves general world knowledge, while a novel spatial embedding adaptation method enables efficient transfer of learned action knowledge to new robot embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields in artificial intelligence and robotics: **generalist robot policies, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, and 3D foundation models for robotics.**

Traditionally, robot policies were trained for specific tasks and embodiments, lacking the generality required for real-world deployment across diverse scenarios. The emergence of large-scale datasets and powerful pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has paved the way for "generalist robot policies." These policies aim to interact with physical environments, adapt to various robot embodiments, and perform complex tasks, often leveraging the semantic understanding capabilities of VLMs. Pioneering efforts like RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA have demonstrated significant progress by fine-tuning VLMs on large, diverse robot demonstration datasets (e.g., Open X-Embodiment - OXE).

However, the authors identify a critical gap in existing VLA models: **the lack of precise perception and comprehension of the 3D physical world.** While VLMs excel at 2D vision-language understanding, robot manipulation inherently requires a deep understanding of 3D space, object positions, and spatial relationships. Humans instinctively construct rich 3D mental representations for manipulation, a capability largely missing in current VLA models that primarily rely on 2D observation inputs.

The second challenge addressed is the heterogeneity of robot observations and actions. Different robots have varying camera placements and extrinsic calibrations, leading to non-aligned 3D observation spaces. Furthermore, diverse robot kinematics, degrees of freedom, and motion controllers result in vastly different action characteristics, making it difficult to learn generalizable spatial actions across embodiments.

SpatialVLA directly addresses these limitations by introducing novel spatial representations for both robot observations and actions. It positions itself as a "spatial-enhanced vision-language-action model" that aims to equip VLA models with "profound spatial understanding of the 3D physical world," moving beyond the primary 2D focus of many existing generalist robot policies. It also contributes to the emerging field of "3D Foundation Models for Robotics," which seeks to extend the reasoning and understanding capabilities of LLMs and VLMs into the 3D domain for embodied agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of SpatialVLA is to **explore and implement effective spatial representations for robot foundation models, claiming that spatial understanding is the keypoint in robot manipulation.** The authors are motivated by the fundamental limitation of existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which, despite their success in leveraging pre-trained VLMs, largely remain confined to 2D observation inputs and lack precise comprehension of the 3D physical world essential for robot manipulation.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

- **Enhance 3D Spatial Understanding:** To equip VLA models with a profound understanding of the 3D physical world, akin to human spatial cognition, enabling them to align objects within a canonical workspace for manipulation.
- **Overcome Observation Heterogeneity:** To address the challenge of non-3D-aligned observations arising from diverse camera sensors and placements across different robot embodiments. The goal is to provide a universal, robot-agnostic way to integrate 3D spatial context.
- **Learn Generalizable Spatial Actions:** To tackle the difficulty of learning generalizable spatial actions across heterogeneous robots, which have different degrees of freedom, motion controllers, and workspace configurations. The aim is to unify the action space in a way that facilitates learning transferable spatial action knowledge.
- **Achieve Generalist Robot Policy:** To develop a generalist robot policy capable of performing a wide array of manipulation tasks across various robot environments (simulated and real-world) in a zero-shot manner, and efficiently adapting to new robot setups with minimal fine-tuning.
- **Improve Inference Efficiency:** To enhance the practical applicability of VLA models by optimizing for faster inference speeds, potentially by reducing the number of tokens required to represent robot actions.

In essence, SpatialVLA is motivated by the need to bridge the gap between powerful language-vision capabilities and the inherent 3D nature of real-world robotic interaction, thereby creating more robust, adaptable, and generalizable robot policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SpatialVLA proposes a novel architecture built upon a vision-language model backbone, specifically **PaliGemma 2**, augmented with two key components designed to inject 3D spatial intelligence: **Ego3D Position Encoding** for observations and **Adaptive Action Grids** for actions. The training process involves a two-stage scheme: pre-training on a large-scale dataset followed by efficient post-training for adaptation.

#### 3.1. The SpatialVLA Model Architecture

SpatialVLA processes image observations and natural language task instructions to generate robot actions autoregressively.

- **Ego3D Position Encoding:** This component is designed to integrate 3D spatial context with 2D semantic features, making observations 3D-aware and robot-agnostic.
 - It uses **ZoeDepth** to estimate a depth map from the input RGB image.
 - Pixel-wise 3D positions in an egocentric camera frame are derived by back-projection using camera intrinsic parameters. This egocentric approach is crucial as it eliminates the need for specific robot-camera extrinsic calibration, making it universally applicable.
 - A SigLIP visual encoder extracts 2D semantic features.
 - The 3D positions are then encoded into 3D position embeddings using a sinusoidal function followed by a learnable MLP.
 - Finally, these 3D position embeddings are *added* to the 2D visual tokens from the SigLIP encoder to form the Ego3D spatial representations, infusing 3D context into the visual features.

- **Adaptive Action Grids:** This mechanism translates continuous robot actions into discrete spatial tokens, making them suitable for autoregressive generation by the VLM backbone and facilitating generalizable action learning.
 - For a single-arm robot, 7D actions ({x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw, grip}) are separated into translation (∆T), rotation (∆R), and gripper (G) components.
 - Translation movements (x, y, z) are further transformed into polar coordinates (ϕ, θ, r) to disentangle direction and distance.
 - Instead of uniform discretization, continuous translation and rotation movements are normalized and then discretized into adaptive spatial grids. This is done by fitting a **parameterized Gaussian distribution** to the statistical distribution of actions on the whole dataset. Grids are then split into intervals with *equal probability* (not equal size) on each action variable, allowing for finer-grained representation in high-frequency action spaces.
 - For example, if there are Mϕ, Mθ, Mr bins for translation and Mroll, Mpitch, Myaw bins for rotation, the total discrete action space is V = Mtrans + Mrot + 2 (for gripper).
 - Learnable spatial action token embeddings are associated with these discrete grids. This approach allows the model to predict only 3 tokens per action (one for translation, one for rotation, one for gripper) instead of 7, contributing to faster inference.

#### 3.2. The Pre-training and Post-training Scheme

The training procedure is divided into two stages to achieve a generalist robot policy with effective adaptation capabilities.

- **Pre-training Procedure:**
 - SpatialVLA is pre-trained on a massive cross-robot dataset mixture of **1.1 Million real robot demonstrations**, combining subsets of the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset and the RH20T dataset. The authors specifically modified the mixture weights based on real-world evaluation performance of individual datasets.
 - The model is initialized from the PaliGemma 2 backbone. Spatial action token embeddings and Ego3D position encoding MLP parameters are randomly initialized and optimized during training, alongside the vision encoder and LLM backbone parameters.
 - Training uses a standard autoregressive next-token prediction objective.
 - Crucially, **text token embeddings are frozen** to preserve the general world knowledge inherent in the pre-trained VLM, which the authors found beneficial for instruction following.
 - The DROID dataset is removed from the mixture for the final third of pre-training, indicating a strategic data curriculum to improve quality.

- **Post-training Designs (Spatial Embedding Adaption):**
 - This stage focuses on efficiently adapting the pre-trained model to new robot setups or tasks with limited robot-specific demonstrations.
 - A novel technique called **Spatial Embedding Adaption** is proposed, leveraging the Adaptive Action Grids.
 - For a new robot setup, a new Gaussian distribution is fitted to its specific action data.
 - New discrete spatial action grids are created based on this new distribution.
 - The embeddings for these new spatial action tokens are **initialized by trilinear interpolation** from the pre-trained action tokens. This allows the model to effectively transfer the pre-learned spatial action knowledge to the new embodiment.
 - These new action token embeddings and relevant model parameters are then optimized using the next-token prediction objective. This method offers a flexible and effective approach for robot-specific fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SpatialVLA was extensively evaluated on diverse robot manipulation tasks and different robot embodiments in both simulation and real-world settings, including 24 real-robot tasks and 3 simulation environments.

#### 4.1. Performing Zero-shot Robot Control

- **SimplerEnv Evaluation (Google Robot and WidowX):**
 - SpatialVLA achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on the Google Robot tasks, surpassing RoboVLM and even the much larger RT-2-X (55B parameters vs. SpatialVLA's 3.5B) in both Visual Matching (71.9% vs. 60.7%) and Variant Aggregation (68.8% vs. 64.3%) scores. It also matched or outperformed the latest SOTA model π0.
 - On WidowX setup, SpatialVLA outperformed RoboVLM in zero-shot success rates (34.4% vs. 13.5%).
 - Qualitatively, SpatialVLA showed greater generalizability and robustness to varying visual appearances and environmental conditions.
- **Real-world WidowX Evaluation:**
 - In real-world "out-of-the-box" evaluation on 7 task suites, SpatialVLA achieved the highest average success rate, showcasing robust and generalizable operation.
 - It demonstrated superior robustness to motion disturbances (human-induced dynamic object movement) and strong instruction-following ability (e.g., distinguishing cup colors for placement).

#### 4.2. Adapting to New Robot Setups

- **LIBERO Simulation Benchmark:**
 - After fine-tuning, SpatialVLA achieved the highest average success rate (78.1%) across four LIBERO task suites, outperforming Diffusion Policy, Octo, OpenVLA, and TraceVLA.
 - Notably, it achieved 88.2% success on LIBERO-Spatial, demonstrating strong understanding of spatial relationships even with different object layouts.
- **Franka Robot Fine-tuning:**
 - In single-task tests, SpatialVLA was competitive with Diffusion Policy (82% vs. 81%).
 - In instruction-following tasks, SpatialVLA significantly outperformed OpenVLA (+12% improvement) and Diffusion Policy (struggled with 26% success rate).
 - For multi-tasks, SpatialVLA achieved a 57% accuracy rate, surpassing other generalist policies, leveraging its pre-training and 3D perception.

#### 4.3. Evaluating Spatial Understanding Capability

- SpatialVLA consistently showed superior spatial understanding across Franka fine-tuning, WidowX zero-shot, and LIBERO-Spatial tasks.
- It achieved 73% accuracy in a Franka task requiring spatial prompt understanding ("place plush toy closest to robot on car").
- It significantly improved manipulation capabilities for complex positional changes in out-of-distribution WidowX zero-shot tasks involving explicit height changes.
- Policies lacking integrated depth information struggled significantly (e.g., Octo, Diffusion Policy, OpenVLA often below 50% success rate) in tasks requiring spatial layout adaptation, highlighting the benefit of Ego3D Position Encoding.

#### 4.4. Ablations on Design Decisions

- **Pre-training Ablations (Google Fractal & BridgeData V2 mixture):**
 - **Adaptive Spatial Action Grids vs. Linear Discretization:** Adaptive grids showed significant advantages, promoting variant aggregation and visual matching success rates by +36.5% and +42.1% respectively, and converging faster. Initializing grids based on dataset distribution (rather than uniform) further improved generalization.
 - **Action Grid Resolution:** Higher resolutions (e.g., 8194) generally improved performance, especially in tasks requiring precise movements, compared to lower resolutions (e.g., 1026).
 - **Ego3D Position Encoding:** Models *without* Ego3D PE suffered significant performance drops in variant aggregation (from 81.6% to 68.9%), confirming its crucial role in handling varied lighting, colors, textures, and camera poses.
 - **Freezing LLM Embedding:** Freezing language embedding and sharing a trainable spatial embedding improved manipulation capabilities and instruction following while speeding up training.
- **Post-training Ablations (Google Fractal, Bridge V2, LIBERO):**
 - **Spatial Embedding Adaption:** This technique yielded marginal gains on large datasets (where distributions were similar to pre-training) but provided substantial boosts (e.g., +4.6% on LIBERO-Spatial) on small-scale datasets with different distributions. This confirms its value for quick and effective transfer by aligning pre-trained spatial features with the target fine-tuned model.
 - **LoRA vs. Full Fine-tuning:** LoRA fine-tuning generally outperformed full-parameter fine-tuning on small datasets, making it the preferred method for efficient adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SpatialVLA represents a significant step forward in the development of generalist robot policies, primarily by robustly addressing the crucial aspect of **3D spatial understanding**, which has been a bottleneck for many existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**

- **Enhanced Spatial Intelligence:** The introduction of Ego3D Position Encoding and Adaptive Action Grids directly equips VLA models with 3D awareness. This is a fundamental advancement, moving beyond 2D-centric perception to enable robots to operate more intelligently in the inherently 3D physical world.
- **Universal and Robot-Agnostic Design:** The egocentric nature of Ego3D PE eliminates the need for complex robot-camera calibrations, making the observation representation universally applicable. Similarly, adaptive action grids offer a unified, flexible action space that can represent diverse robot movements, crucial for cross-embodiment generalization.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** SpatialVLA's superior performance in zero-shot generalization across various simulation and real-world tasks (e.g., outperforming RT-2-X with significantly fewer parameters, achieving SOTA on SimplerEnv and real-world WidowX) demonstrates its effectiveness as a highly capable generalist policy.
- **Efficient Adaptation:** The novel Spatial Embedding Adaption technique, leveraging action grid re-discretization and intelligent initialization, provides a new and highly effective paradigm for post-training. This dramatically reduces the effort and data required to adapt the pre-trained model to new robot setups and tasks, fostering practical deployment.
- **Improved Inference Speed:** By representing actions with fewer tokens (3 tokens per action instead of 7), SpatialVLA achieves faster inference speeds (approximately 20Hz), which is critical for real-time robot control.

**Potential Impact:**

The advancements presented in SpatialVLA have the potential to accelerate the deployment of intelligent robots in diverse real-world scenarios. By improving spatial understanding and generalization, robots can become more adaptable to unseen environments, objects, and tasks, reducing the need for extensive task-specific data collection and training. This could lead to:

- **Broader Robot Applications:** Enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks in unstructured environments, from industrial settings to domestic assistance, with greater robustness and less human intervention.
- **Faster Development Cycles:** The efficient adaptation capabilities mean new robotic applications or variations can be developed and deployed much more quickly, lowering the barrier to entry for robotics research and commercialization.
- **Foundation for Future Generalist Robots:** SpatialVLA contributes a critical piece to the puzzle of building truly generalist robots. Its architectural innovations, particularly in spatial representation, could serve as a foundational element for future embodied AI systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations, which also suggest avenues for future research:

- **Gaussian Distribution Optimality:** The current approach fits action signals with Gaussian distributions. The authors question if this is truly optimal, noting potential issues like grid clustering for single-axis motions or sensitivity to dataset noise. Future work could explore more generalizable implicit data distribution modeling (e.g., VAE-based) combined with explicit grid partitioning.
- **Flexible VLA Architectures:** While fast (21Hz inference), the autoregressive decoding paradigm is slower than diffusion decoding methods (which predict multiple actions simultaneously). Future work will explore integrating diffusion decoding with spatial grid action presentation and investigating dynamic token numbers for action mapping.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Like many generalist policies, SpatialVLA struggles with long-horizon tasks due to relying solely on current frame observations and history tokens. Enhancing the model's ability to perceive and model historical information efficiently is crucial for complex, multi-step tasks.
- **Data Quality and Composition:** The variable quality of large-scale datasets like OXE can hinder training. Future efforts should focus on optimizing data composition and distilling high-quality subsets to further boost model efficiency and generalizability.

In conclusion, SpatialVLA pushes the boundaries of VLA models by prioritizing and effectively implementing 3D spatial understanding. Its robust performance and efficient adaptation mechanisms make it a significant contribution towards realizing generalist, deployable robot intelligence.
