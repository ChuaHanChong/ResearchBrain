# Agility Meets Stability: Versatile Humanoid Control with Heterogeneous Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed the Agility Meets Stability (AMS) framework for humanoid robots, enabling a single control policy to achieve both dynamic motion tracking and robust balance by integrating human motion capture with synthetic balance data and employing adaptive learning strategies. The policy demonstrated superior tracking accuracy and stability compared to baselines in simulation and successfully executed diverse behaviors on a Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid robot controllers are specialized, optimized either for dynamic agility (e.g., motion tracking) or robust balance (e.g., challenging poses), but not both simultaneously within a single policy.
- Training data, primarily human motion capture (MoCap), lacks extreme balance scenarios and is biased, limiting the robot's ability to learn robust stability or exploit its unique physical capabilities.
- Combining dynamic and balance-focused motions in a single reinforcement learning framework often leads to conflicting optimization objectives, degrading performance for one or both capabilities.

## Method

- ==The AMS== framework integrates heterogeneous data sources, combining human MoCap data for agile behaviors with physically plausible, robot-specific synthetic balance motions.
- A hybrid reward scheme is introduced, applying general motion-tracking rewards universally but augmenting supervision with balance-specific prior rewards exclusively for synthetic balance-critical motions.
- An adaptive learning strategy is employed, featuring performance-driven sampling to focus on challenging motions and motion-specific adaptive reward shaping to optimize learning efficiency across diverse tasks.

## Results

- The AMS policy significantly outperformed baseline methods (OmniH2O, HuB) in simulation, achieving superior tracking accuracy (e.g., global MPJPE of 48.60mm for MoCap data) and robust stability across both dynamic and balance tasks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that synthetic balance data, the hybrid reward scheme, and both adaptive sampling and adaptive reward shaping components are critical for the policy's combined agility and stability.
- The unified AMS policy was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating zero-shot execution of challenging balance motions and robust real-time teleoperation for diverse behaviors, showcasing strong generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single control policy for humanoid robots can effectively combine dynamic agility and robust stability by leveraging a combination of human motion capture data and specially generated synthetic balance data.
> - Selectively applying balance-specific prior rewards to synthetic balance motions, rather than uniformly, is crucial for resolving conflicting optimization objectives and preventing performance degradation on dynamic tasks.
> - Adaptive learning strategies, including dynamically adjusting sampling probabilities and motion-specific reward shaping, are essential for efficient training and generalization across diverse and heterogeneous motion distributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of operating in human-centered environments necessitates controllers that can execute a broad spectrum of movements, seamlessly integrating dynamic agility with robust balance. The existing landscape of humanoid control research has seen significant progress in distinct areas:
- **Learning-based Whole-Body Tracking (WBT):** This area has focused on training policies to imitate human motion capture (MoCap) data, enabling robots to perform agile, expressive, and complex dynamic skills such as dancing, martial arts, and various athletic maneuvers. Examples include frameworks like DeepMimic and approaches that scale to large motion libraries for broad coverage of human-like movements. These methods typically emphasize fidelity to reference kinematics and dynamic execution.
- **Robust Balance Control:** A separate thrust in research has concentrated on achieving extreme stability, often focusing on quasi-static balance and maintaining equilibrium in challenging poses. Methods such as HuB, for instance, are designed to enable robots to hold difficult balancing stances that are often underrepresented in standard human datasets. These approaches prioritize stability and robust contact over dynamic motion.

The current challenge within this broader landscape is the integration of these two capabilities—dynamic agility and robust balance—into a single, unified control policy. Prior research has largely pursued these objectives in isolation. Controllers optimized for agile movements may compromise stability, particularly in quasi-static scenarios or during transitions to challenging poses. Conversely, policies designed for extreme balance often impose constraints that restrict the natural momentum transfer and fluidity required for dynamic, agile motions.

This work, Agility Meets Stability (AMS), positions itself at the intersection of these two research directions. It aims to bridge the identified gap by developing a framework that trains a single policy capable of both high-fidelity dynamic motion tracking and resilient balance maintenance. By addressing the limitations of specialized controllers, AMS seeks to advance the versatility and applicability of humanoid robots in real-world, human-centric environments, where tasks often demand a combination of both agile movement and precise, robust balance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a single, unified control policy for humanoid robots that can simultaneously achieve dynamic motion tracking and robust balance maintenance across a diverse range of tasks. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified challenges in current humanoid control paradigms:

1. **Specialization of Existing Controllers:** Current reinforcement learning (RL) based whole-body tracking (WBT) policies tend to be specialized. They are typically optimized either for agile dynamic behaviors (e.g., fast locomotion, expressive movements) or for stability-critical behaviors (e.g., maintaining difficult balance poses). This specialization often means that a policy excelling in one domain compromises performance in the other. Humans, in contrast, naturally integrate these capabilities, such as maintaining a stable single-leg stance while reaching, or performing precise object placement after dynamic movement. Endowing humanoids with similar integrated versatility remains a fundamental challenge.

2. **Limitations of Training Data:** Existing WBT approaches predominantly rely on human motion capture (MoCap) data. While these datasets provide rich kinematic information for dynamic behaviors, they suffer from several limitations:
 - **Underrepresentation of Extreme Balance Scenarios:** MoCap datasets often exhibit long-tailed distributions, with extreme balance situations being scarce. This scarcity makes it difficult for RL policies to learn robust balance from human data alone.
 - **Human-Centric Bias:** MoCap data inherently restricts the robot to motions that humans can perform. This prevents the exploitation of a robot's unique mechanical capabilities (e.g., different joint limits, actuator strengths, mass distributions) that might enable balance configurations beyond human physical constraints.
 - **Noise and Retargeting Errors:** Human MoCap data can contain sensor noise and introduces errors during the kinematic retargeting process from human to robot morphology. Such inaccuracies can degrade the quality of training references for robots.

3. **Divergent Optimization Objectives:** In an RL framework, dynamic and balance-focused motions typically exhibit distinct distributions and require different optimization goals. Reward functions designed to promote agility (e.g., encouraging momentum transfer) can inadvertently penalize stability in quasi-static poses. Conversely, strict balance rewards (e.g., keeping the center of mass precisely over the support foot) can be overly restrictive for dynamic motions that rely on transient instability and coordinated whole-body movements. Combining these conflicting objectives within a unified learning framework often leads to degradation in performance for one or both capabilities.

To address these motivations, AMS proposes a framework that aims to:
- Integrate heterogeneous data sources: human MoCap data for agile behaviors and physically constrained synthetic balance motions for stability.
- Design a hybrid reward scheme that applies general tracking objectives universally but injects balance-specific priors only into synthetic motions.
- Implement an adaptive learning strategy with performance-driven sampling and motion-specific reward shaping for efficient training across diverse motion distributions.

By tackling these challenges, the research seeks to enable a single policy to learn both dynamic agility and balance robustness without compromising either objective, thereby enhancing the versatility and operational capabilities of humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The AMS framework addresses the challenges of unifying dynamic motion tracking and extreme balance maintenance through a combination of a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (RL) problem setup, heterogeneous data generation, a hybrid reward scheme, and an adaptive learning strategy.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The problem is formulated as a goal-conditioned RL task where a policy $\pi$ is trained to track a reference motion sequence in real-time. The system state $s_t$ at timestep $t$ comprises the robot's proprioceptive observations $o_t$, while $g_t$ represents the target motion state from reference motions. The policy generates desired joint positions $a_t$ which are applied via a PD controller. A teacher policy is initially trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with privileged information, and subsequently distilled into a student policy that relies only on deployable sensory inputs for real-world application. Human MoCap data is retargeted to the humanoid robot's kinematics and filtered for feasibility.

#### 3.2. Synthetic Balance Motion Generation

To overcome the limitations of human MoCap data, AMS generates synthetic balance motions that are physically plausible and robot-specific.
- **Rationale:** Human MoCap data often lacks extreme balance scenarios, is biased towards human kinematics, and contains noise. Synthetic data directly samples the humanoid's motion space, ensuring physical realism and expanding the range of feasible behaviors.
- **Motion Generation Process:**
 1. **Sampling:** Given a robot model, a support foot, and a time horizon $N$, the method samples a target pose for the swinging foot, a target pelvis height, and an initial joint configuration. Initial joint configurations are biased towards natural lower-limb postures, with randomized upper-body joints to introduce diversity.
 2. **Reference Trajectory Construction:** Reference trajectories for the support foot, swinging foot, and pelvis are constructed using SE(3) interpolation.
 3. **Two-Stage Batch Trajectory Optimization:** This process ensures kinematic feasibility, smoothness, and balance.
 - **Stage 1:** Minimizes a composite cost $J_1$ comprising terms for pose tracking, soft joint limits, rest-pose regularization, and temporal smoothness. This stage yields a kinematically consistent and smooth trajectory.
 - **Stage 2:** Augments $J_1$ with a balance-enforcing term. This term penalizes deviations where the 2D projection of the Center of Mass (CoM) falls outside a predefined support rectangle around the support foot. The cost is formulated as $J_2 = J_1 + \lambda_{bal} \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} \max(0, \lVert p_t - c_t - s \rVert^2 - \epsilon)$, where $p_t$ is the CoM projection, $c_t$ is the support foot center projection, $s$ defines the support rectangle, and $\epsilon$ is a tolerance.
 4. **Solver and Validation:** The optimization is solved using a Levenberg–Marquardt solver. Only trajectories that satisfy the balance constraint (max CoM deviation within $\epsilon$) are accepted, ensuring physical feasibility. This hierarchical two-stage approach separates kinematic generation from balance refinement.

#### 3.3. Hybrid Rewards

To reconcile the conflicting optimization objectives for dynamic agility and balance stability, a hybrid reward scheme is introduced:
- **General Motion-Tracking Rewards:** For human MoCap data, the policy is guided by general motion-tracking reward terms that encourage alignment with natural, human-like movements.
- **Balance-Specific Prior Rewards:** For synthetic balance-critical motions, the supervision is augmented with balance-specific priors. These include rewards for precise Center of Mass (CoM) alignment over the support polygon and consistency in foot contact states. These priors provide precise, physically grounded guidance for stability without imposing undue restrictions on dynamic motions.
By selectively applying balance priors only to synthetic data, the framework allows the policy to learn agile behaviors from human data while maintaining robust stability in challenging postures from synthetic data.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Learning

An adaptive learning strategy, comprising adaptive sampling and adaptive reward shaping, is employed to enhance training efficiency and generalization across heterogeneous data.
- **Adaptive Sampling:** This performance-driven strategy dynamically adjusts the sampling probability of motion sequences based on their tracking performance.
 1. **Performance Assessment:** Performance is evaluated based on execution failure, mean per-joint position error (MPJPE), and maximum joint position error for each motion.
 2. **Probability Update:** For failed motions, the sampling probability is significantly increased by a failure boost factor. For successful motions, probabilities are adjusted based on how well they perform relative to performance thresholds ($\tau_{poor}$ and $\tau_{good}$), which are defined using percentiles of the error distribution. Poorly performing motions (errors above $\tau_{poor}$) get increased sampling probability, while well-performing motions (errors below $\tau_{good}$) get reduced probability.
 3. **Normalization and Minimum Threshold:** All probabilities are normalized and then clamped to a minimum threshold to ensure continued exploration and prevent any motion from being completely ignored. This mechanism enables the policy to focus on challenging or underrepresented motion patterns.
- **Adaptive Reward Shaping:** This component addresses the limitation of uniform and fixed reward shaping coefficients in traditional WBT methods.
 1. **Motion-Specific Shaping Coefficients:** Instead of a single, fixed shaping coefficient $\sigma$ (which controls error tolerance in the reward function, e.g., $r = \exp(-\text{err}/\sigma)$), AMS maintains motion-specific $\sigma$ parameters. These are further compartmentalized for different body parts to provide fine-grained control.
 2. **Exponential Moving Average (EMA) Update:** The $\sigma$ parameters are updated dynamically using an EMA based on the current tracking error for each specific motion sequence: $\sigma_{new} = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \sigma_{current} + \alpha \cdot \text{err}_{current}$, where $\alpha$ is the update rate. This allows the error tolerance to adapt to the progress of learning for each motion.
This adaptive reward shaping allows for simultaneous adaptation to training progress and the inherent diversity of motion types, improving learning efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of AMS was evaluated through extensive experiments in simulation and on a physical humanoid robot, with comparisons against baseline methods and detailed ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Simulator:** IsaacGym was used for physics-based simulation.
- **Robot:** Experiments were conducted on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot (23 DoFs, 1.3 meters tall, 35 kg).
- **Training Data:** A filtered subset of AMASS and LAFAN1 human MoCap datasets (over 8,000 sequences) was combined with 10,000 synthetic balance motion sequences generated by AMS.
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed using:
 - **Success rate (Succ.):** Percentage of motions completed without the average deviation exceeding 0.5m.
 - **Global MPJPE (E_g-mpjpe):** Global position tracking accuracy (mm).
 - **Root-relative MPJPE (E_mpjpe):** Local joint position tracking performance (mm).
 - **Contact mismatch (Cont.):** Percentage of frames where foot contact states differed from reference.
 - **Slippage (Slip.):** Ground-relative velocity of the support foot (m/s), indicating unstable contact.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Existing Methods (Q1)

AMS was compared against two baseline methods:
- **OmniH2O:** A general humanoid whole-body motion tracking framework using a teacher-student paradigm, adapted for the G1 robot.
- **HuB:** A stability-focused method re-implemented within the OmniH2O framework, emphasizing balance motions and contact-aware tracking.

The results (Table I(a)) demonstrated that AMS significantly outperformed both baselines across MoCap (dynamic) and synthetic (balance) datasets, as well as the combined "All" data category.
- **Tracking Performance:** AMS achieved lower E_g-mpjpe and E_mpjpe for both MoCap and synthetic data, indicating superior tracking accuracy. For example, on MoCap data, AMS had E_g-mpjpe of 48.60mm compared to OmniH2O's 68.31mm and HuB's 158.80mm.
- **Stability:** AMS maintained comparable or better stability metrics. Its Contact mismatch and Slippage values were generally low, on par with or better than HuB (which is specialized for stability) and significantly better than OmniH2O on MoCap data's stability metrics.
- **Overall Versatility:** While HuB showed good stability on balance motions (low Cont. and Slip.), its performance on general MoCap data was poor (high tracking errors, low success rate). OmniH2O was more general but less stable than AMS. AMS demonstrated the ability to combine high agility with robust stability in a single policy.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study (Q2)

- **Ablation on Synthetic Balance Data (Table I(b)):**
 - Training "w/o Synthetic Balance Data" (i.e., on MoCap data only) resulted in poor performance on balance motions (E_g-mpjpe of 112.20mm, Succ. 94.54%), highlighting the necessity of balance-specific data.
 - The inclusion of synthetic balance data significantly improved tracking and stability on balance-critical motions while maintaining comparable performance on MoCap data.
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Performance (Table II):** Synthetic balance data also improved generalization to unseen motions. AMS (Ours) achieved an E_g-mpjpe of 63.48mm and a success rate of 99.7% on OOD data, outperforming both OmniH2O w/ All Data (76.26mm, 99.1%) and AMS w/o Synthetic Balance Data (86.61mm, 96.0%). This indicates that the generated data broadens the policy's robustness.

- **Ablation on Hybrid Rewards (Table I(c)):**
 - "w/ General Rewards Only" (removing balance prior rewards) led to degraded performance on balance motions (higher tracking errors, more contact mismatches), confirming the importance of balance-specific guidance.
 - "w/ All Rewards for All Data" (applying balance priors uniformly) improved balance tasks but caused significant performance degradation on MoCap data (E_g-mpjpe 54.09mm vs. 48.60mm for AMS), demonstrating the conflict between objectives when priors are not selectively applied.
 - The hybrid reward scheme effectively provided strong balance guidance without compromising dynamic motion agility, leading to improved overall performance.

- **Ablation on Adaptive Learning (Table I(d)):**
 - Removing both Adaptive Sampling (AS) and Adaptive Reward Shaping (ARS) ("w/o Adaptive Learning (AS+ARS)") resulted in the most pronounced performance degradation across all metrics, with significantly higher tracking errors and lower success rates.
 - Removing only Adaptive Sampling ("w/o Adaptive Sampling (AS)") led to a decrease in overall performance, particularly for balance motions, indicating that prioritizing difficult samples is crucial.
 - Removing only Adaptive Reward Shaping ("w/o Adaptive Reward Shaping (ARS)") also showed substantial degradation in tracking errors across both data types, underscoring the benefit of motion-specific, adaptive error tolerances.
 - These ablations confirm that both components of the adaptive learning strategy are critical for effectively managing data diversity and improving learning efficiency.

#### 4.4. Real-World Deployment (Q3)

The unified AMS policy was successfully deployed on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot.
- **Diverse Motion Execution:** The robot demonstrated the ability to execute a wide range of behaviors, including:
 - Challenging zero-shot balance motions like Ip Man's Squat and single-leg balance stances, which were unseen during training.
 - High-mobility and expressive motions such as running and dancing.
- **Real-time Teleoperation:** The policy also enabled real-time teleoperation using an off-the-shelf RGB camera-based human pose estimation model. Despite the inherent noise in such a simple teleoperation setup, the robot exhibited robust adaptation to diverse motions, showcasing the policy's generalizability in an interactive context.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AMS framework represents a notable contribution to the field of humanoid robot control by addressing the fundamental challenge of integrating dynamic agility and robust balance into a single policy. The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

1. **Unified Control Paradigm:** AMS introduces the first framework that successfully unifies dynamic motion tracking and extreme balance maintenance within a single policy. This moves beyond specialized controllers that optimize for either agility or stability, paving the way for more versatile and broadly capable humanoid robots.
2. **Enhanced Versatility and Task Scope:** By enabling a humanoid robot to seamlessly transition between agile, dynamic movements and challenging, stable poses, AMS significantly expands the range of tasks that humanoids can perform in complex, human-centric environments. This includes applications in household assistance, industrial tasks, and interactive scenarios where both fluid motion and precise equilibrium are required.
3. **Effective Heterogeneous Data Integration:** The work demonstrates an effective approach to leveraging diverse data sources—human motion capture datasets for natural dynamics and physically constrained synthetic motions for extreme balance. This strategy overcomes limitations of purely human-centric data, particularly the underrepresentation of critical balance scenarios and the exploitation of robot-specific kinematics.
4. **Robustness and Generalization:** The ability of the AMS policy to execute challenging zero-shot balance motions (e.g., Ip Man's Squat) and adapt robustly to real-time teleoperation from noisy human pose estimation highlights its generalization capabilities. This suggests a policy that is less prone to brittleness and can operate reliably in varied and unscripted environments.
5. **Advanced Learning Strategies:** The proposed hybrid reward scheme and adaptive learning strategy (adaptive sampling and adaptive reward shaping) provide a methodological advancement in training complex multi-skill policies. These components efficiently address conflicting optimization objectives and data distribution challenges, offering insights for future reinforcement learning applications in robotics.
6. **Foundational Control Model:** The development of a single policy capable of such versatility positions AMS as a foundational control model for future autonomous humanoid applications. It could serve as a robust base for developing more complex behaviors, including loco-manipulation, interaction with dynamic environments, and more sophisticated human-robot collaboration.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge two primary limitations. Firstly, the current approach lacks precise end-effector control, which restricts its applicability to manipulation and contact-rich tasks. Future work could integrate capabilities for fine-grained interaction. Secondly, the RGB camera-based pose estimation used for teleoperation introduces significant noise, hindering agile locomotion operations. Addressing this would involve exploring more precise teleoperation systems and incorporating online retargeting algorithms to improve the quality of reference poses. These planned future directions indicate potential avenues for further enhancing the capabilities of the AMS framework.
