# Statistically Assuring Safety of Control Systems using Ensembles of Safety Filters and Conformal Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Washington University in St. Louis introduces a two-phase framework that integrates conformal prediction with Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis to provide statistical safety guarantees for learning-enabled control systems. The framework enables principled switching between controllers by calibrating learned safety functions and offers trajectory-level probabilistic safety assurances, demonstrating robust performance with ensemble models in a 10-dimensional highway overtaking scenario.

## Problem

- Scalability issues in Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis for high-dimensional systems make formal safety verification computationally intensive.
- Learning-based approximations of HJ value functions lack guarantees on accuracy, creating uncertainty in their safety predictions.
- Current methods for switching between nominal and learned safe controllers often rely on unprincipled thresholds, lacking statistical assurance for the combined policy.

## Method

- A two-phase framework using ==one-sided conformal prediction== to calibrate learned ==Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) value functions==, establishing a statistically principled ==switching condition== between a nominal and a learned safe controller.
- The framework introduces an ==ensemble of independently trained HJ value functions==, employing distinct strategies (single vs. multiple switching) to aggregate their safety predictions and enhance robustness.
- ==Trajectory-level statistical safety guarantees== are provided for the deployed switched policy, quantifying the probability of avoiding failure states using a ==Beta distribution== based on certification runs.

## Results

- In a 10-dimensional highway takeover environment, individual Hamilton-Jacobi safety filters reduced safety violations from **46%** (nominal controller) to as low as **2%** and **8%** for the best-performing models.
- The ensemble approach achieved a **96%** success rate and **4%** violation rate, matching the best individual model's performance and demonstrating robustness against poorly performing ensemble members.
- The multiple switching strategy within the ensemble consistently yielded higher success rates and lower violation rates compared to the single switching strategy, and the statistical safety guarantees were empirically validated, showing increased precision with more certification trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conformal prediction effectively quantifies the uncertainty in learned Hamilton-Jacobi value functions, providing a statistically sound basis for switching between nominal and safe control policies.
> - An ensemble of independently trained HJ value functions significantly improves robustness, consistently matching the performance of its best individual member even when some members perform poorly.
> - Dynamically selecting the safest policy from an ensemble at each timestep (multiple switching strategy) yields superior safety performance compared to committing to a single safe policy for an entire unsafe episode.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of learning-enabled autonomous systems necessitates rigorous safety assurance. Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis is a established method for formally verifying safety and synthesizing safe controllers. This approach traditionally involves computing an HJ value function, which characterizes the backward reachable set (BRS) of undesirable failure states. However, computing this value function scales exponentially with the system's state dimension, presenting a computational challenge for high-dimensional systems.

To address this "curse of dimensionality," researchers have increasingly adopted deep reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to approximate HJ value functions and their corresponding safe policies. While this improves scalability, it introduces a reliability concern: learned value functions and policies are not guaranteed to be accurate. The value estimated by a learned function at a given state may not precisely reflect the actual safety return achieved by following the learned policy. This discrepancy creates uncertainty regarding the safety of systems relying on such approximations.

This research integrates Conformal Prediction (CP), a statistical framework designed for uncertainty quantification, into the safety assurance process. CP offers distribution-free and finite-sample guarantees for predictions made by arbitrary black-box models, including machine learning algorithms. Its application in robotics and autonomous systems for providing formal guarantees, particularly for safety filters with learned components, has seen increased interest. Previous applications include enhancing formal guarantees for control barrier value functions (CBVFs) and verifying neural reachable tubes.

This work contributes to this landscape by focusing specifically on HJ value functions. It differentiates itself from existing literature by proposing a principled method for switching between a nominal controller and a learning-based HJ safety policy, calibrated using CP, and subsequently providing statistical guarantees for the entire switched policy. Unlike some prior works that focus solely on assuring safety or employ unguided thresholds for switching, this research considers both a primary reward-driven objective (from the nominal controller) and safety assurance, establishing a statistically rigorous framework for their integration. Furthermore, the paper introduces a safety filter framework that incorporates an ensemble of independently trained HJ value functions, which is presented as a novel approach within this context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the safety assurance of learning-enabled autonomous systems by addressing the inherent inaccuracies of learned Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) value functions and their associated safe policies. Specifically, the paper aims to provide probabilistic safety guarantees when these learned components are employed within safety filters to prevent systems from entering failure states.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in the current landscape of autonomous system control:

1. **Inaccuracy of Learned HJ Value Functions:** While reinforcement learning (RL) enables the approximation of HJ value functions for high-dimensional systems, these approximations are not guaranteed to be exact. A learned value function might overestimate or underestimate the true safety return that would be achieved by executing its corresponding safe policy. This discrepancy compromises the formal safety guarantees that HJ reachability analysis traditionally provides. The research is motivated by the need to quantify and account for this uncertainty.

2. **Unprincipled Switching Conditions:** Safety filters often operate by switching from a nominal (potentially unsafe) controller to a safe control policy when the system approaches an unsafe region. In the context of learned HJ value functions, this switching decision is typically based on a threshold applied to the learned value (e.g., `V_θ(f(x, π_nom(x))) ≤ 0`). Such thresholds lack formal guidance or statistical guarantees, meaning the switched policy itself may not be reliably safe. A key motivation is to introduce a principled, statistically rigorous method for determining this switching condition.

3. **Lack of End-to-End Safety Guarantees for Switched Policies:** Even if individual components (nominal and safe policies) have some level of assurance, the overall behavior of a system that dynamically switches between these policies requires comprehensive safety verification. The distribution of states encountered during deployment with a switched policy can differ from the data used to train or calibrate the individual learned components, leading to potential out-of-distribution failures. This motivates the development of a framework that can provide statistical safety guarantees for the entire trajectory under the deployed switched policy.

4. **Robustness and Reliability of Learned Safety Components:** Relying on a single learned HJ value function introduces a single point of failure and sensitivity to the training process. The research is motivated to investigate whether an ensemble of independently trained HJ value functions can offer improved robustness and more consistent safety performance compared to individual models, particularly when individual models may exhibit varying degrees of accuracy or susceptibility to training noise.

In summary, the core objectives are to:
- Introduce a conformal prediction (CP)-based framework to bound the uncertainty associated with learned HJ value functions.
- Leverage CP to calibrate the switching logic between a nominal controller and a learned HJ-based safe policy, providing probabilistic guarantees for this decision.
- Develop a method to derive trajectory-level probabilistic safety guarantees for the overall switched policy, ensuring the system avoids failure states with high probability under deployment conditions.
- Explore the utility of an ensemble of independently trained HJ value functions as a safety filter, evaluating methods for aggregating their policies and comparing ensemble performance to individual models.

These objectives collectively aim to bridge the gap between the computational scalability offered by learning-based control and the stringent reliability requirements of safety-critical autonomous systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a two-phase framework to provide statistical safety guarantees for control systems utilizing learning-based Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability safety filters.

**Phase 1: Conformal Prediction-based Switching**

This phase focuses on calibrating the learned HJ value function and establishing a principled switching criterion between a nominal controller and a learned safe policy.

1. **Calibration Dataset Construction:**
 - A preliminary dataset `D` of trajectories is generated by executing the system using a basic switching policy: the system follows the nominal controller `π_nom` unless the learned HJ value function `V_θ` predicts that the next state under `π_nom` is unsafe (i.e., `V_θ(f(x_t, π_nom(x_t))) ≤ 0`), in which case it switches to the learned safe policy `π_safe_θ`.
 - A calibration set `D_cal` is then formed by sampling one state uniformly at random from each trajectory in `D`.
 - For each sampled state `x_i` in `D_cal`, the "actual" safety value `V*_θ(x_i)` is computed. This `V*_θ(x_i)` represents the true safety return achieved by unrolling the learned safe policy `π_safe_θ` starting from `x_i`. This actual value is calculated recursively, simulating the system forward and discounting future safety returns until either a failure state is reached or a predefined horizon `H` is met. The value `h(x_i)` (distance to failure set) is incorporated into this calculation.

2. **One-Sided Conformal Calibration:**
 - A one-sided nonconformity score `s(x_i) = max(0, V_θ(x_i) - V*_θ(x_i))` is defined. This score quantifies how much the learned value function `V_θ` *overestimates* safety compared to the actual safety `V*_θ`. A score of 0 indicates either accurate prediction or underestimation.
 - Given a desired miscoverage rate `α`, the `(1-α)`-quantile `q̂(α)` of these nonconformity scores from `D_cal` is computed.
 - A conformal lower bound for the learned value function is then defined as `V_lower(x, α) = V_θ(x) - q̂(α)`. This lower bound is guaranteed, with probability at least `1-α` (under an exchangeability assumption), to contain the actual safety value `V*_θ(x)`.

3. **Calibrated Switched Policy (Algorithm 1: `π_sw`):**
 - During deployment, the system continuously evaluates its next state `x_next` if it were to follow the nominal controller `π_nom`.
 - The conformal lower bound `V_lower(x_next, α)` is computed.
 - If `V_lower(x_next, α) > 0`, implying the nominal control is deemed safe with high probability, the system continues to use `π_nom`.
 - Otherwise (if `V_lower(x_next, α) ≤ 0`), the system switches to the learned safe policy `π_safe_θ` to avoid potential unsafe conditions.
 - The paper acknowledges that the exchangeability assumption, crucial for CP guarantees, may be partially violated due to distribution shift between calibration and deployment, as the switching behavior during calibration differs from that induced by the fully calibrated policy.

4. **Ensemble of HJ Value Functions (Algorithm 2: `π_sw_ensemble`):**
 - To improve robustness, an ensemble of `M` independently trained HJ value functions `{V_θ_j}` and their corresponding safe policies `{π_safe_θ_j}` is created (e.g., by using different random initializations for neural network parameters during training).
 - Each member model `j` is individually calibrated using conformal prediction to obtain its own quantile `q̂_j(α)`.
 - Two strategies for aggregating and switching among ensemble members are proposed:
 - **Single switching strategy:** When the nominal controller is deemed unsafe by all ensemble members (i.e., `V_lower_j(x_next, α) ≤ 0` for all `j`), the ensemble identifies the "safest" member `j*` (the one with the maximum `V_lower_j(x_next, α)`). The system then commits to using `π_safe_θ_j*` until the nominal controller is again deemed safe by `j*`.
 - **Multiple switching strategy:** In contrast, if the nominal controller is unsafe, this strategy dynamically re-evaluates all ensemble members at *each* time step and selects the current safest member `j*` to execute its policy `π_safe_θ_j*`. This allows for continuous re-optimization within an unsafe episode.
 - The ensemble policy (`π_sw_ensemble`) uses `π_nom` if at least one member `j` predicts `V_lower_j(x_next, α) > 0`. Otherwise, it activates the safe policy based on the chosen ensemble switching strategy.

**Phase 2: Safety Verification using Conformal Prediction**

This phase addresses the potential distribution shift issue by providing trajectory-level statistical safety guarantees for the deployed switched policies (`π_sw` or `π_sw_ensemble`).

1. **Trajectory Safety Margin:** For a given policy `π` (which can be `π_sw` or `π_sw_ensemble`) and an initial state `x_0`, a trajectory safety margin `J_π(x_0)` is defined as the minimum value of `h(x)` (the function defining the failure set `F`) encountered along the entire trajectory. If `J_π(x_0) > 0`, the trajectory is safe; if `J_π(x_0) ≤ 0`, it indicates a safety violation.

2. **Statistical Safety Guarantee (Theorem 1):** `N_cert` initial states are sampled independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) from the deployment distribution `P_0`. The system's behavior under the chosen policy `π` is simulated for each initial state. Let `k` be the number of these `N_cert` trajectories that result in a safety violation (i.e., `J_π(x_0) ≤ 0`). Theorem 1 states that the probability of a *new* trajectory (sampled from `P_0`) being safe (`J_π(x_0) > 0`) follows a Beta distribution: `P_{x_0~P_0}(J_π(x_0) > 0) ~ Beta(N_cert - k, k + 1)`. This provides a quantifiable statistical confidence interval for the safety of the deployed policy.

Together, these two phases form a comprehensive framework for statistically assuring the safety of control systems that utilize learned HJ value functions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The framework was evaluated using a 10-dimensional triple-vehicle highway takeover environment. In this scenario, an ego vehicle, modeled with nonlinear unicycle dynamics, is tasked with safely overtaking a leading vehicle while avoiding a lateral vehicle and staying within road boundaries. Five independent HJ value functions were trained using DDPG, each with different random initializations. The calibration dataset size was 10,000, and a simulation horizon of 200 timesteps was used.

**Analysis of Single-Model Safety Filters:**
- Most individual HJ reachability-based safety filters (member models) demonstrated a reduction in violation rates compared to using only the nominal controller (`π_nom`), which exhibited a 46% violation rate. For instance, Member Model 3 achieved a 2% violation rate, and Model 1 achieved 8% (at `α = 0.06`).
- The study observed that smaller values of `α`, intended to produce more conservative switching behavior and theoretically lead to monotonically decreasing violation rates, did not consistently result in this trend. This non-monotonicity was attributed to two factors: the limited sample size (50 trials) and potential distribution shift.
- Distribution shift arises because `π_safe_θ` is trained on states where `π_nom` is near the unsafe boundary. When deployed with a very small `α`, `π_safe_θ` might be activated much earlier, encountering out-of-distribution states for which its behavior could be unpredictable and potentially unsafe. Member Model 2, for example, had the highest `q̂(α)` (2.37) at `α = 0.06`, indicating excessive conservatism, which led to a 22% violation rate—higher than most other individual models.

**Analysis of the Ensemble of HJ Value Functions:**
- A key finding was that the ensemble approach consistently achieved performance comparable to its *best-performing* individual member model, even when the ensemble included members that performed significantly worse. For example, with `α = 0.06`, the ensemble of all five models achieved a 96% success rate and 4% violation rate. This matched the performance of Member Model 3 (the best individual) and was superior to the mean individual model performance (92% success, 8% violation). This robustness was maintained despite the inclusion of Member Model 2, which had a 78% success rate individually.
- This suggests that the ensemble's aggregation mechanism effectively identifies and leverages the most reliable model at a given state, thereby mitigating the negative impact of poorly performing or overly conservative individual members. However, it was also noted that the ensemble did not necessarily *outperform* its best individual member.
- An analysis of ensemble size and member quality showed that even when combining a good and a poor performer (e.g., ensemble 1-2), the ensemble performance aligned with the better-performing member. Adding more members generally maintained performance close to the best individual model.

**Evaluation of Ensemble Switching Strategies:**
- Comparing the "multiple switching strategy" (dynamic re-evaluation and selection of the safest model at each timestep) against the "single switching strategy" (committing to one safe model for an entire unsafe episode), the multiple strategy generally resulted in higher success rates and lower violation rates across different `α` values.
- This performance advantage of the multiple strategy was attributed to its ability to dynamically select the most appropriate safe policy at each moment, potentially enabling the agent to exit unsafe states more quickly.

**Statistical Guarantees on Safety (Phase 2):**
- The research empirically validated Theorem 1, demonstrating that the statistical safety guarantee provided by Phase 2 becomes more reliable with an increased number of certification trajectories (`N_cert`).
- As `N_cert` increased (e.g., from 500 to 7500), the Beta distribution representing the probability of a new trajectory being safe (`P(J_π(x_0) > 0)`) concentrated more tightly around its mean. This confirms that higher `N_cert` values lead to more precise and confident statistical statements about the overall safety of the deployed switched policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous systems by addressing a fundamental challenge in deploying learning-enabled controllers: the lack of guaranteed reliability for learned safety functions. The proposed two-stage conformal prediction framework offers a method for assuring the safety of control systems that integrate learning-based Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis.

The significance of this work can be highlighted through several points:

1. **Bridging Scalability and Reliability:** The paper provides a framework that helps bridge the gap between the computational scalability offered by deep reinforcement learning for high-dimensional HJ problems and the stringent reliability requirements of safety-critical applications. By bounding the uncertainty of learned HJ value functions, it enables their more trustworthy deployment.

2. **Principled Safety-Critical Switching:** The introduction of a conformal prediction-based switching condition represents a advancement over traditional methods that rely on arbitrary or heuristically chosen thresholds. This statistically principled approach quantifies the uncertainty in learned safety predictions and uses it to calibrate when to intervene with a safe controller. This can lead to more robust and less conservative safety filters.

3. **Statistical Assurance for Switched Policies:** By providing trajectory-level, distribution-free, and finite-sample statistical safety guarantees for the *overall* switched policy, the framework addresses the critical issue of verifying system behavior under actual deployment conditions. This is particularly relevant given the potential for distribution shift when learned components are integrated into a larger control system, offering a quantifiable measure of safety confidence.

4. **Robustness through Ensembles:** The investigation into ensemble-based safety filters demonstrates a practical strategy for mitigating the impact of individual model inaccuracies or failures. The finding that an ensemble can consistently perform comparably to its best individual member, even when including poorly performing ones, suggests a method for increasing the overall reliability and fault tolerance of learning-based safety components. This is valuable in scenarios where training consistent high-quality individual models might be challenging.

5. **Enhanced Trustworthiness of AI in Critical Domains:** The ability to provide probabilistic safety guarantees for AI-driven controllers is crucial for increasing their acceptance and deployment in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, aerospace, and medical robotics. By offering a quantifiable measure of safety, this research contributes to building confidence in learning-enabled autonomous systems.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the potential for distribution shift during calibration and the observation that ensembles did not outperform their best members, also open avenues for future research. This includes integrating conformal prediction more deeply into the training process, developing techniques to explicitly account for distribution shift, and exploring more sophisticated ensemble architectures.

In summary, this research offers a methodical approach to enhance the safety and reliability of autonomous systems that utilize learned components for safety assurance. By integrating conformal prediction, it provides a rigorous statistical foundation for decision-making and verification in dynamic, safety-critical environments.
