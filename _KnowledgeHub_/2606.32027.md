# Freeform Preference Learning for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Freeform Preference Learning (FPL) enables robots to acquire nuanced behaviors by allowing humans to define and provide preferences along multiple natural-language axes, moving beyond ambiguous single binary choices. This method yielded policies that, on average, achieved a 38 percentage point higher success rate than baselines in real-world manipulation tasks and demonstrated compositional generalization and test-time steerability.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks is challenging, especially for nuanced qualitative aspects.
- Existing methods like sparse success labels or single 'overall' binary preferences provide insufficient or ambiguous supervision, hindering the learning of complex, human-aligned behaviors.
- Traditional preference learning struggles when trajectories excel along different dimensions (e.g., speed vs. safety), forcing annotators into ambiguous choices that dilute the learning signal.

## Method

- **FPL** introduces ==freeform preference collection==, where human annotators define arbitrary natural-language axes (e.g., "speed", "safety") and provide binary preferences along each specific dimension for trajectory pairs.
- A ==language-conditioned reward function== is learned, extending the ==Bradley-Terry model==, which outputs a scalar reward for a trajectory given a natural language axis label, leveraging ==VLM== backbones.
- A ==multi-axis reward-conditioned policy== is then trained, conditioned on a set of target reward scores for each of these language-defined axes, enabling flexible control at inference time.

## Results

- FPL policies achieved an average of **38 percentage points** higher success rate than the second-best baseline across real-world manipulation tasks.
- The method enabled compositional generalization, allowing policies to combine desirable attributes (e.g., speed) across different task components not explicitly seen in combined training data.
- FPL demonstrated test-time steerability, where a single trained policy could dynamically adjust its behavior based on target reward conditioning without retraining, and collecting freeform preferences was approximately **50% faster** per label.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing human preferences into specific, natural-language-defined axes reduces ambiguity and provides richer, more granular supervision signals than single overall preferences.
> - Training a single language-conditioned reward model on these multi-dimensional preferences enables the system to learn and generalize across diverse qualitative criteria.
> - Conditioning robot policies directly on multiple, decomposed reward targets allows for compositional generalization of behaviors and dynamic steering at test time without requiring policy retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous robot policy improvement frequently encounters challenges in designing effective reward functions, particularly for long-horizon manipulation tasks. Traditional approaches to reward specification exhibit limitations that hinder the development of sophisticated robotic behaviors.

One common method involves providing or learning from binary success labels. While conceptually straightforward for human annotation, this approach offers a sparse reward signal, making it difficult for reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms to scale to complex tasks or incorporate real-world behavioral constraints beyond basic completion. Policies trained with sparse rewards often struggle with long-horizon tasks where successful executions are infrequent, leading to insufficient learning signals.

Another category of methods involves learning shaped scalar rewards. These typically focus on task progress metrics but tend to overlook qualitative aspects of how a task is performed, such as "carefulness" or "smoothness." Consequently, the resulting robot behaviors may achieve task completion but in an undesirable manner.

Preference learning, a paradigm that learns denser reward signals from human feedback, has shown promise in reducing the burden on human supervisors. Foundational work in this area typically involves presenting human annotators with two robot trajectory segments and asking them to indicate an "overall" binary preference. However, this approach can be ambiguous and noisy in long-horizon tasks. When multiple criteria are relevant (e.g., one trajectory is faster but less safe, while another is slower but safer), forcing annotators to collapse these into a single "overall" preference can dilute the supervision signal, leading to ambiguous feedback and potentially suboptimal policies. This ambiguity also makes it difficult for the learned reward function to effectively guide the policy towards nuanced desired behaviors.

This research introduces Freeform Preference Learning (FPL) to address the ambiguity inherent in single "overall" binary preferences. FPL positions itself by allowing human annotators to define and provide preferences along multiple, natural-language-specified axes of judgment (e.g., speed, safety, quality of placement, carefulness). This approach aims to provide a more granular and less ambiguous form of human supervision. It leverages advancements in vision-language models (VLMs) and reward-conditioned policy learning to interpret this multi-dimensional feedback and train policies capable of optimizing across these human-specified dimensions. FPL differentiates itself from multi-objective reinforcement learning by not assuming a predefined set of objectives or rewards, but rather learning them directly from freeform human input. It also extends natural language supervision in robotics by using language not just for corrections or task specification, but as a direct interface for defining the criteria for reward elicitation and learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to mitigate the reward design bottleneck, which is a significant impediment to the development of autonomous robot policies, particularly for manipulation tasks involving long horizons and nuanced quality criteria.

The motivation for developing Freeform Preference Learning (FPL) stems from several specific limitations observed in existing reward specification and learning paradigms:

1. **Ambiguity of Binary Preferences**: In complex, long-horizon tasks, human annotators often face difficulty in providing a single, unambiguous "overall" preference between two robot trajectories. This is because trajectories may excel along different dimensions (e.g., one is faster, another is safer, a third results in better product quality). Requiring annotators to aggregate these competing notions of quality into a single binary choice often leads to noisy or diluted supervision signals, making it challenging for reward models to discern the true underlying human intent.

2. **Lack of Granular Feedback**: Current preference learning methods typically do not allow humans to articulate the specific criteria or "axes" of judgment they use when evaluating robot behavior. This restricts the richness of the feedback. An ideal reward function should not only indicate task completion but also capture qualitative aspects like precision, smoothness, carefulness, or hygiene. Existing methods often struggle to incorporate these multi-faceted performance criteria effectively.

3. **Insufficient Signal Density for Long-Horizon Tasks**: Sparse success/failure labels provide minimal signal for learning in tasks composed of multiple sequential subtasks or requiring extended execution. While binary preference learning aims to provide denser signals, the ambiguity of a single "overall" preference can still render the signal insufficient or misleading for guiding complex, multi-stage learning processes. This can lead to issues in credit assignment, where the reward signal does not accurately reflect progress or issues at specific points in a long trajectory.

4. **Limited Adaptability and Steerability**: Policies trained with single-scalar rewards or aggregated preferences often lack the ability to adapt to varying human priorities at test time without retraining. For instance, if a user initially prioritizes speed but later decides to prioritize carefulness, a traditionally trained policy cannot easily adjust its behavior.

To address these motivations, FPL aims to:
- Enable annotators to define arbitrary, natural-language preference axes (e.g., "speed," "safety," "quality of placement," "carefulness").
- Collect pairwise preferences specifically along each of these user-defined axes, thereby providing multi-dimensional and less ambiguous feedback.
- Learn a language-conditioned reward model that maps a trajectory and a natural language preference label to an axis-specific reward score.
- Train a reward-conditioned policy that can optimize across these multiple human-specified dimensions simultaneously.
- Facilitate compositional generalization, allowing policies to combine desirable attributes (e.g., speed + precision) even if not directly observed in the training data.
- Allow users to steer the policy towards different desired behaviors at test time by adjusting the target reward conditioning, without requiring policy retraining.

Ultimately, the motivation is to overcome the limitations of sparse and ambiguous reward signals, enabling robots to learn more nuanced, human-aligned, and adaptable behaviors in complex manipulation scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Freeform Preference Learning (FPL) proposes a methodology to learn robot policies from multi-dimensional, natural-language human preferences. The approach consists of three primary components: freeform preference collection, learning a language-conditioned reward function, and policy extraction using a multi-axis reward-conditioned policy.

#### 3.1. Freeform Preference Collection

Unlike traditional binary preference learning, which solicits a single "overall" preference between two trajectories, FPL allows annotators to define and evaluate preferences along multiple, user-specified axes.
- **Input**: Human annotators are presented with two full robot trajectories (e.g., video recordings).
- **Axis Specification**: Annotators can either select from a predefined set of preference axes or freely define new axes using natural language (e.g., "speed," "smoothness," "formality of table setup," "damage to toast"). This flexibility allows for capturing diverse and evolving criteria for desirable behavior.
- **Per-Axis Preference**: For each specified axis, the annotator provides a binary preference indicating which trajectory is better along that specific dimension, or if they are equivalent. This granularity is intended to reduce the ambiguity inherent in a single "overall" judgment.
- **Efficiency**: The paper notes that this method can be more efficient *per label* because multiple labels are derived from watching a single pair of trajectories, sharing the video viewing overhead.

#### 3.2. Learning a Language-Conditioned Reward Function

FPL extends the Bradley-Terry model to learn a reward function that is conditioned on a natural language description of the preference axis.
- **Multi-dimensional Reward Model**: Instead of training separate reward functions for each axis, FPL trains a single language-conditioned reward function, $r_\phi(\tau | l_k)$, which outputs a scalar reward score for a trajectory $\tau$ given a natural language axis label $l_k$.
- **Bradley-Terry Extension**: The reward model $r_\phi$ is trained by minimizing a modified negative log-likelihood loss (Equation 3):
 $L_{FPL-Reward}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(\tau_i, \tau_j, \{(l_k, y_k)|k=1...,K_{ij}\})\sim P} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{K_{ij}} \log \sigma \left( (2y_k - 1) [ r_\phi(\tau_i|l_k) - r_\phi(\tau_j | l_k) ] \right) \right]$
 Here, $K_{ij}$ is the number of preference axes provided for the pair $(\tau_i, \tau_j)$, and $y_k \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates the preferred trajectory along axis $k$.
- **Trajectory-Level Reward**: The reward for a full trajectory $\tau = o_1, \ldots, o_T$ with respect to an axis $l_k$ is defined as the sum of per-prefix scores (Equation 4):
 $r_\phi(\tau, l_k) = \sum_{i=1}^{T} g_\phi(o_{1:i}, l_k)$
 where $g_\phi$ is a multimodal transformer that scores a segment $o_{1:i}$ conditioned on $l_k$. This allows the model to capture temporal dependencies.
- **Architecture**: For real-world tasks, a pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone (e.g., Qwen 3.5 VL 4B) is used. The reward model takes a prompt like "What is the score for $l_k$?" and processes images from both first-person and third-person camera views. The vision encoder is frozen, and the rest of the VLM is fine-tuned.

#### 3.3. Policy Extraction on a Multi-Dimensional Reward Function

To leverage the multi-dimensional reward function, FPL trains a policy that is conditioned on multiple natural-language reward axes and their corresponding scores.
- **Avoid Scalar Collapse**: Instead of collapsing all dimensions into a single scalar reward (e.g., via a weighted sum), which can lead to reward hacking and loss of detailed supervision, FPL preserves the decomposed rewards.
- **Reward-Conditioned Policy**: A comprehensive set of $K_\pi$ preference axes, $L = \{l_k | k=1, \ldots, K_\pi\}$, is selected for policy training. These can be representative axes from the reward model training dataset. The policy is trained to generate actions conditioned on the current state and *all* the axis descriptions $l_k$ along with their respective target rewards $r_\phi(\tau | l_k)$.
- **Policy Objective (Equation 5)**:
 $L_{FPL-Policy}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\tau_i \sim D} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \log \pi_\theta \left( a_t^i | s_t^i, l_1, r_\phi(\tau_i | l_1) \ldots l_{K_\pi}, r_\phi(\tau_i | l_{K_\pi}) \right) \right]$
 This objective aims to maximize the likelihood of actions observed in the dataset $D$, conditioned on the state and the target rewards for each axis.
- **Iterative Training**: FPL can be applied in an iterative online RL setting (Algorithm 1), where the policy generates new trajectories, human preferences are collected on this new data, and the reward model and policy are updated. This allows the set of preference axes to evolve with policy capabilities.
- **Test-time Steerability**: To enable steering at test time, rewards are standardized per axis over the dataset $D$. This provides a normalized scale, making it easier to query the policy with desired target reward values (e.g., "high speed, high carefulness"). The policy is conditioned on the rounded, standardized reward values as text. For real-world tasks, the $\pi_{0.5}$ VLM is used as the pre-trained policy backbone.

The combination of freeform preference collection, language-conditioned reward modeling, and multi-axis reward-conditioned policy training enables FPL to learn nuanced behaviors and allows for flexible control at inference time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of FPL was conducted across four real-world manipulation tasks and two simulated tasks, comparing it against five baseline methods: Behavior Cloning (BC), Filtered BC, Weighted Regression, Advantage Conditioning, and Single Preferences (with two variants for comparison count). The results address questions regarding policy effectiveness, compositional generalization, test-time steerability, reward signal density, and annotation dynamics.

#### 4.1. FPL Learns Performant Policies from Freeform Human Preferences

- **Real-world Performance**: FPL consistently outperformed all baselines in real-world tasks (putting a cube in a target bowl, folding shorts, plating toast, and setting up a table). On average, FPL demonstrated a 38 percentage point improvement in success rate over the second-best baseline.
 - Sparse rewards and single "overall" binary preferences were found to be insufficient or ambiguous for long-horizon tasks, leading to policies that either failed to complete tasks or did so without desirable qualitative behaviors (e.g., carefulness).
 - In tasks like "plate toast," where one trajectory might be unhygienic but another drops the item, binary preference often yields ambiguous feedback. FPL's axis-specific supervision allowed the policy to improve on both task completion and qualitative aspects.
- **Simulation Performance**: In simulation, FPL also surpassed all baselines. The improvement was notably clear in the "object rearrangement" task, where the long horizon and noisy offline dataset resulted in few complete successes. Preference-based methods generally provided denser signals than sparse rewards, but FPL's multi-dimensional preferences excelled by capturing distinct important axes, preventing issues like dropping objects due to high-altitude release.

#### 4.2. FPL Exhibits Compositionality of Behaviors

- **Bimodal Square Task**: This task was designed to assess compositional generalization. The offline dataset for placing a nut on the right peg only contained slow demonstrations, while fast demonstrations were available for placing the nut on the left peg.
- **Compositional Learning**: FPL successfully learned to achieve *faster* right-peg placements than any seen in the training data for that specific peg. This indicates that FPL policies can compose desirable attributes (e.g., speed) across different task components (e.g., target locations), a capability not observed in policies trained with single-preference baselines. This compositional ability is attributed to the multi-dimensional reward learning combined with reward-conditioned policy extraction.

#### 4.3. FPL Exhibits Test-Time Steerability

- **Bimodal Square (Inverted) and Place Cube into Bowl**: FPL demonstrated the ability to steer the policy at test time without retraining. In the "bimodal square (inverted)" task, the same trained policy, when conditioned differently, could successfully place the nut on the *left* peg instead of the right. Similarly, in "put cube into target bowl," FPL could steer the robot to place the cube into different target bowls by adjusting the reward conditioning.
- **Mechanism**: This steerability is enabled by FPL's reward-conditioned policy extraction on multi-dimensional rewards. Because the policy is trained on trajectories with varying scores along each axis, it learns to achieve different behaviors based on the target reward conditioning provided during inference, after rewards are standardized for easier querying. Baselines generally lacked this steerability.

#### 4.4. FPL Qualitatively Produces Denser Reward Signals

- **Credit Assignment**: Qualitative analysis on the "setup table" task demonstrated that FPL's axis-conditioned rewards provided temporally localized signals corresponding to specific subtask events (e.g., "big plate placed," "cutlery placed"). This enhanced interpretability and suggested better credit assignment for long-horizon tasks.
- **Contrast with Baselines**: In contrast, binary-preference rewards often exhibited large, uninformative spikes near the end of an episode, irrespective of actual subtask completion, indicating poor credit assignment.

#### 4.5. Freeform Preferences Exhibit Flexibility and Efficiency

- **Evolving Axes**: In iterative tasks like "fold shorts," the freeform preference axes naturally evolved. Early iterations focused on basic task completion (e.g., "each fold happens"), while later iterations, as the policy improved, shifted focus to finer details (e.g., "wrinkles," "final alignment"). This dynamic adaptation of feedback criteria makes FPL highly targeted.
- **Diverse Vocabulary**: For the "plate toast" task, annotators generated 41 distinct preference labels grouped into 6 topics (e.g., plating quality, hygiene, speed, toast damage) from 295 trajectory pairs, demonstrating a rich and diverse set of human evaluation criteria.
- **Annotation Time**: Collecting freeform preferences was approximately 50% faster per label compared to collecting single binary preferences. This efficiency stems from the ability to gather multiple axis-specific judgments from a single viewing of a trajectory pair.

In summary, FPL improves performance, enables compositional learning and test-time steerability, provides denser and more interpretable reward signals, and offers a more flexible and efficient way to collect human preferences for robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Freeform Preference Learning (FPL) addresses a foundational challenge in robot learning by providing a method to overcome the reward design bottleneck, particularly for complex and long-horizon manipulation tasks. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several dimensions:

1. **Enhanced Robot Learning for Complex Tasks**: FPL's ability to elicit and learn from multi-dimensional, natural-language human preferences significantly improves policy performance in tasks requiring nuanced qualitative judgments beyond simple success/failure. This is crucial for real-world applications where "how" a task is performed (e.g., carefully, smoothly, hygienically) is as important as "if" it is completed. By providing dense, unambiguous feedback across multiple criteria, FPL enables robots to acquire more sophisticated and human-aligned behaviors.

2. **Increased Human-Robot Collaboration and Trust**: By allowing humans to express their preferences using natural language and along specific, intuitive axes, FPL creates a more transparent and interpretable interface for human-robot interaction. This direct communication of intent can lead to a more intuitive feedback process, potentially fostering greater trust and collaboration between humans and autonomous systems. The natural evolution of preference axes as policies improve also indicates a more adaptive learning partnership.

3. **Compositional Generalization and Adaptability**: The demonstration of compositional behavior, where FPL policies can combine distinct attributes (e.g., speed and precision) observed in different parts of the training data, represents a step towards more generalizable robotic agents. This capability implies that robots could learn new desired behaviors by combining learned skills, even if the exact combined behavior was not explicitly demonstrated. Furthermore, the test-time steerability feature allows for dynamic adaptation to changing user priorities without requiring costly retraining, making robots more versatile and user-responsive in diverse operational contexts.

4. **Denser and More Interpretable Reward Signals**: FPL's capacity to generate temporally localized and axis-specific reward signals offers better credit assignment for long-horizon tasks. This improves the efficiency of policy learning and provides valuable insights into the robot's understanding of different subtasks and performance criteria. Such interpretable rewards can also facilitate debugging and refinement of robot policies.

5. **Efficiency of Human Supervision**: The finding that freeform preference collection can be more time-efficient per label compared to traditional binary preferences suggests a practical advantage. By consolidating multiple judgments for each trajectory pair, FPL reduces the overall human annotation effort for a given amount of information, making the approach more scalable for data-intensive robot learning scenarios.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Accelerated Development of General-Purpose Robots**: FPL's contributions could help in developing more capable and adaptable general-purpose robots that can learn to perform a wide array of tasks with human-desired finesse.
- **Robotics in Sensitive Environments**: Applications requiring precision, care, or adherence to specific qualitative standards (e.g., medical robotics, food handling, elderly care) could significantly benefit from robots that learn nuanced behaviors through FPL.
- **Personalized Robot Assistance**: The steerability aspect of FPL could lead to more personalized robot assistants, where users can dynamically adjust robot behavior to their immediate needs or preferences without needing complex reprogramming.

**Limitations**:
The paper acknowledges certain limitations, including the continued cost of human preference collection (though FPL improves efficiency per label), the challenge of automating reward value selection at test time, and the current reliance on a fixed set of preference axes for policy conditioning, suggesting promising avenues for future research. Addressing these limitations could further amplify the impact of FPL on the field of robotic manipulation.
