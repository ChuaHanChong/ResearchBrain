# HydraCollab: Adaptive Collaborative-Perception for Distributed Autonomous Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California, Irvine developed HydraCollab, an adaptive collaborative perception framework for distributed autonomous systems that balances perception accuracy and communication bandwidth. The framework demonstrated Pareto-optimal performance across multiple datasets, for instance, achieving an 0.78% higher AP@0.7 score on the V2X-R dataset while utilizing 41% of the communication bandwidth compared to the Where2comm baseline.

## Problem

- Existing collaborative perception methods face an inherent trade-off between perception accuracy and communication bandwidth, with higher accuracy typically requiring substantial data exchange.
- Current approaches often transmit all available sensor features or a full local fusion of features, even when some data is noisy or redundant, leading to inefficient bandwidth use and potential accuracy degradation.
- Many systems apply a uniform collaboration strategy across all spatial regions, missing opportunities to adapt to varying overlaps in agents' perceptual information, which can lead to suboptimal bandwidth utilization.

## Method

- ==Spatial confidence maps== quantify each sensor's contribution per spatial region, guiding a **Collaborative Sensor Gating (CSG)** mechanism that lets an ego agent request and receive only the most informative, reliable sensor features from collaborators, reducing transmission volume.
- An **Adaptive Collaboration** strategy dynamically employs ==intermediate (feature-level) collaboration== in regions of mutual confidence and ==late (high-level result) collaboration== in regions confidently observed by only one agent.
- The framework (**HydraCollab**) is trained end-to-end with a ==bandwidth-regularized loss function== that optimizes the gating policy by balancing perception accuracy with the communication cost of transmitted features.

## Results

- HydraCollab achieved Pareto-optimal performance on V2X-R, V2X-Radar, and UAV3D-mini, e.g. on V2X-R improving AP@0.7 by **0.78%** over Where2comm while using only **41%** of its communication bandwidth (**59%** reduction).
- Qualitative and ablation analyses confirmed both mechanisms' value: Collaborative Sensor Gating selects optimal sensor features (e.g. less-noisy LiDAR over noisy Radar) while Adaptive Collaboration dynamically switches intermediate/late collaboration by spatial confidence, contributing individually and synergistically to gains.
- The framework demonstrated robust adaptability to heterogeneous agents with varied sensor suites, maintaining strong performance even when ego-agent sensors were restricted.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Utilizing spatial confidence maps enables intelligent, localized decisions on what specific perceptual information to share and which collaboration strategy (intermediate or late) to apply.
> - Selectively gating sensor features at a fine-grained level, rather than sharing all features or a full local fusion, significantly optimizes bandwidth while preserving or enhancing perception accuracy.
> - A dynamic hybrid collaboration approach, combining rich feature sharing where agents have overlapping confident views and efficient result sharing where views are complementary, achieves a superior performance-to-bandwidth trade-off.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of collaborative perception for multi-robot and multi-agent systems, a domain focused on enhancing situational awareness by sharing perceptual information among participants. This approach aims to overcome individual sensor limitations, such as occlusions and limited range, which are common in applications like autonomous vehicles and UAV swarms.

A fundamental challenge in collaborative perception involves an inherent trade-off between perception accuracy and communication bandwidth. Existing methods typically achieve higher accuracy by exchanging more information, which in turn increases communication overhead. However, real-world communication networks often have strict bandwidth constraints, necessitating strategies that maximize perception performance while minimizing data transfer.

The broader research landscape categorizes collaboration strategies based on the type of information shared:
- **Early collaboration:** Involves exchanging raw sensor data, offering the highest information richness and potential accuracy but demanding substantial bandwidth.
- **Late collaboration:** Transmits only final detection results, minimizing bandwidth but limiting the scope of collaboration to high-level outputs and making systems more susceptible to local errors.
- **Intermediate collaboration:** Shares encoded feature representations, striking a balance between information content and communication cost. This has become a prevalent paradigm in recent systems, as it preserves informative content while significantly reducing overhead compared to raw data exchange.

Further advancements in the field have introduced selective communication strategies to optimize bandwidth. These include mechanisms for determining *which* agents to communicate with (e.g., Who2com), *when* communication should occur (e.g., When2com), and *what regions of interest* to share (e.g., Where2comm).

Despite these developments, the authors identify several limitations in existing collaborative perception methods. Current approaches often assume uniform utilization of all available sensors within individual agents, irrespective of environmental context or data quality. This overlooks the potential benefits of adaptive sensor selection, which has been shown to improve single-agent perception by considering factors like adverse weather or occlusions. Furthermore, many systems apply a uniform intermediate collaboration strategy across all spatial regions. While some methods like Where2comm introduce spatial selectivity, they are typically restricted to exclusively using intermediate collaboration within selected regions. This overlooks scenarios where agents' perceptual information does not overlap, in which case exchanging feature-level data might be inefficient compared to relying on high-level perception results from peers. Finally, existing multi-sensor collaborative perception frameworks often assume agents perform full local sensor fusion before transmission, which can waste bandwidth by transmitting redundant or detrimental fusion features and ignores the global context available through collaboration. These gaps motivate the development of HydraCollab, which aims to introduce greater adaptability and efficiency into collaborative perception systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an adaptive collaborative perception framework that addresses the fundamental trade-off between perception accuracy and communication bandwidth in multi-agent systems. The goal is to achieve maximal perception performance for an ego agent while adhering to real-world communication budget constraints.

This overarching objective is motivated by several identified limitations and challenges in existing collaborative perception methods:

1. **Inefficient Sensor Feature Transmission:** Current approaches often assume that individual agents utilize all available sensors uniformly, transmitting either all sensor data or a full local fusion of features regardless of their relevance or quality in a given context. The authors illustrate this limitation with an example (Figure 1), where noisy 4D Radar detections, when fused with LiDAR, lead to suboptimal features for collaboration. Transmitting such noisy fused features can degrade perceptual accuracy and waste communication bandwidth. This motivates the need for **selective feature sharing**, where only the most informative and reliable sensor features are transmitted.

2. **Suboptimal Collaboration Strategy Application:** Many existing methods apply a uniform intermediate collaboration strategy across all spatial regions. While spatially selective methods like Where2comm identify critical areas for collaboration, they still exclusively use intermediate collaboration within those selected regions. This is inefficient in areas where agents' perceptual information does not overlap, as feature-level exchange in such non-overlapping regions offers limited benefit. The authors propose that a **hybrid approach**, combining rich feature sharing (intermediate collaboration) in regions of mutual confidence with bandwidth-efficient high-level result sharing (late collaboration) in regions observed confidently by only one agent, could significantly improve bandwidth efficiency without compromising performance.

3. **Lack of Adaptability to Heterogeneous Sensing Capabilities:** Existing multi-sensor collaborative perception frameworks frequently assume homogeneous sensing capabilities among agents and perform full local sensor fusion before transmission. This design does not efficiently leverage global context, can result in misalignment between collaborators' self-assessment and ego needs, and can waste bandwidth by transmitting redundant or detrimental features. The motivation is to develop a system that can **naturally extend to heterogeneous agents** with differing sensor suites.

To address these motivations, HydraCollab proposes the following key objectives:
- To implement **collaboration-aware sensor gating** that adaptively selects the most informative sensor features to communicate, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of heterogeneous data and reducing communication bandwidth.
- To design a **spatially-aware collaboration strategy** that dynamically combines intermediate and late collaboration. This strategy is guided by spatial confidence maps, ensuring that rich feature sharing occurs only in regions where both agents possess confident and overlapping sensor information, while late collaboration is used in regions of single-agent confidence.
- To ensure the framework's **natural extensibility to heterogeneous agents** with varying sensing capabilities, improving its applicability in practical deployments.

By achieving these objectives, HydraCollab aims to provide a more bandwidth-efficient and robust collaborative perception system compared to current state-of-the-art methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HydraCollab is an adaptive collaborative-perception framework designed to optimize the trade-off between perception accuracy and communication bandwidth by leveraging spatial confidence maps. Its methodology is structured around two primary components: Collaborative Sensor Gating and Adaptive Collaboration.

**A. Spatial Confidence Maps**
The foundation of HydraCollab is the use of spatial confidence maps. These maps serve as a communication medium to quantify the importance of each sensor's contribution in different spatial regions. Similar to Where2comm, these maps highlight perceptually critical features, such as areas containing objects, while deemphasizing background features to conserve bandwidth. For each agent *i*, a spatial confidence map *c_l_i* is generated for each feature type *l* in its feature set *F_i*, through a detection decoder *Φ_gen*. The confidence values range from 0 to 1 across the spatial dimensions (H x W).

**B. Collaborative Sensor Gating (CSG)**
The CSG mechanism, illustrated in Figure 2a, aims to select the most informative features from collaborators for transmission, thereby reducing communication bandwidth. The process involves:
1. **Feature-type Specific Encoding:** Spatial confidence maps are sorted by feature type (e.g., LiDAR, Radar, or fused LiDAR-Radar). Feature-type-specific convolutional encoders process these maps to extract high-level spatial-confidence information.
2. **Cross-Agent Global Information Capture:** The encoded confidence maps from all agents are concatenated. A multi-head self-attention mechanism is then applied to capture global information across all collaborating agents.
3. **Request Generation:** The ego agent uses this cross-agent global information to predict collaboration requests for each collaborator. The global information is upsampled to the original map resolution via bilinear interpolation, concatenated with the confidence maps of a specific collaborator *j*, and fed into a CNN-based decision network. This network outputs feature selection probabilities, which are then processed by a top-k gating mechanism to determine the final set of features (*r_l_j*) that agent *j* should transmit for each feature type *l*. This selective transmission reduces the total communication volume.

**C. Adaptive Collaboration**
Adaptive Collaboration dynamically employs either intermediate or late collaboration strategies for different spatial regions, guided by the spatial confidence maps.

1. **Collaboration Identification:**
 - **Intermediate Collaboration Mask (I_Inter_i→j):** This mask identifies regions where both agent *i* and collaborator *j* have confident and overlapping perceptual information. It is calculated by taking the dot product of their binary spatial confidence masks, where values are 1 if the confidence *c_l* is above a threshold *λ*, and 0 otherwise (1(c_l_i ≥ λ) ⊙ 1(c_l_j ≥ λ)).
 - **Late Collaboration Mask (I_Late_i→j):** This mask identifies regions confidently observed by the ego agent *i* but not by collaborator *j* with high confidence (or vice versa, from *j*'s perspective). It is computed by subtracting the intermediate collaboration mask from agent *i*'s confidence mask (1(c_l_i ≥ λ) - I_Inter_i→j).
 - **Smoothing:** A Gaussian filter is applied to both masks to smooth boundary regions.

2. **Intermediate Collaboration Stage:**
 - For regions identified by *I_Inter_i→j*, features from agent *i* (*f_l_i*) are spatially augmented by the mask (*f_l_i→j = I_Inter_i→j ⊙ f_l_i*). Only these spatially correlated features are transmitted from *i* to *j* (and vice-versa for requested features).
 - The ego agent *i* receives the features from its collaborators that were requested by the CSG module.
 - Finally, the ego's local features are fused with the received intermediate features from collaborators.

3. **Late Collaboration Stage (Collaboration Masking):**
 - In regions identified by *I_Late_i→j*, agent *i* first produces its own perception outputs (*O_i*) through its intermediate collaboration pipeline.
 - The *I_Late_i→j* mask is upsampled and applied to *O_i* to select specific detections (*O_i→j*) that are then transmitted to agent *j*.
 - From agent *i*'s perspective, upon receiving *O_j→i* from collaborator *j*, it combines these high-level detections with its own perception outputs *O_i* to produce the final detection.

**D. Training**
HydraCollab is trained end-to-end. A soft gating mechanism is used during training for differentiability, which is then converted to a deterministic top-k selection during inference. To account for varying communication costs of different features, the gating policy is optimized using a bandwidth-regularized loss function: *L_G_π = L_perception + γ Σ_j |f_l_j|*, where *L_perception* is the detection loss, *|f_l_j|* is the number of bytes required to transmit feature *f_l_j*, and *γ* is a trade-off parameter balancing accuracy and communication.

**E. Communication Volume Calculation**
The paper defines the communication volume for both intermediate and late collaboration messages.
- **Intermediate Collaboration Message:** The size in bytes for each feature *f_l_j→i* is calculated as *|I_Inter_j→i| × D_l_j × 32/8*, where *|I_Inter_j→i|* is the number of selected grids by the mask, *D_l_j* is the channel dimension of feature *f_l_j*, and 32/8 converts float32 bits to bytes.
- **Late Collaboration Message:** The size in bytes for transmitted detections *O_j→i* is *|O_j→i| × D_b × 32/8*, where *|O_j→i|* is the number of transmitted detections and *D_b* is the feature dimension of each detected object.
- **Total Transmitted Message:** The total communication volume from agent *j* to agent *i* is the sum of all intermediate feature messages selected by the gating module and the late collaboration messages: *|M_j→i| = Σ_{f_l_j ∈ F_G_j} |M_Inter_j→i f_l_j→i| + |M_Late_j→i O_j→i|*.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HydraCollab was conducted on three multi-agent collaborative perception datasets: V2X-R, V2X-Radar, and UAV3D-mini. The V2X-R dataset is a simulated benchmark providing LiDAR and 4D Radar data for connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs) and infrastructure sensors. V2X-Radar is a real-world dataset including LiDAR and 4D Radar from a single vehicle and an infrastructure sensor. UAV3D-mini is a simulated dataset for multi-drone collaborative 3D object perception using camera data. Performance was measured using Average Precision (AP@0.7) for V2X tasks and mean Average Precision (mAP) for UAV3D-mini, alongside communication bandwidth (log2).

**A. Overall Performance and Communication Efficiency**
Across all evaluated datasets, HydraCollab consistently demonstrated Pareto-optimal performance, achieving a superior trade-off between perception accuracy and communication bandwidth compared to existing methods.

- **V2X-R Dataset:** HydraCollab achieved an AP@0.7 of 82.74% with a communication bandwidth of 31.18 log2. This represents an improvement of 0.78% AP@0.7 over Where2comm, while utilizing only 41% of Where2comm's communication bandwidth. Other methods like Adafusion and CoBEVT achieved higher accuracy but at significantly greater communication costs (e.g., 91x and 22x more bandwidth than HydraCollab, respectively).

- **V2X-Radar Dataset (Real-World):** On this real-world dataset, HydraCollab recorded an AP@0.7 of 65.82% with a communication bandwidth of 27.94 log2. It surpassed Where2comm by 0.75% AP@0.7 while using 26.4% of its communication bandwidth. This indicates its effectiveness in practical scenarios.

- **UAV3D-mini Dataset:** For multi-drone collaborative perception, HydraCollab achieved an mAP of 39.1% with a bandwidth of 34.21 log2. It outperformed Where2comm by 3.17% mAP while using 88% of its communication bandwidth, positioning it as the highest accuracy method among intermediate collaboration approaches on this dataset.

**B. Qualitative Analysis**
- **Collaborative Sensor Gating (V2X-R):** Qualitative results (Figure 4) demonstrated that CSG enhances performance by selectively choosing optimal sensor features. In a scenario with noisy Radar data leading to false positives when fused with LiDAR, CSG correctly identified and transmitted less noisy LiDAR features from one agent and fusion features from another, resulting in accurate detections while conserving bandwidth by not transmitting unnecessary Radar data.

- **Adaptive Collaboration (V2X-Radar):** Visualization (Figure 5) showed that Adaptive Collaboration dynamically adapts its strategy. For an ego vehicle approaching an intersection, HydraCollab applied late collaboration in regions confidently observed only by the infrastructure agent (out of view for the ego), and intermediate collaboration in mutually confident overlapping regions. This approach reduced communication by 2.36x in an illustrative frame while maintaining accurate predictions.

**C. Heterogeneous Agents Evaluation**
HydraCollab's robustness was assessed under heterogeneous sensing capabilities on V2X-R (Table IV). While a significant performance drop occurred when the ego vehicle (CAV 1) was restricted to Radar-only (AP@0.7 of 64.01% compared to 82.74% for homogeneous L+R), most other configurations with restricted sensors on other CAVs or infrastructure remained close to the homogeneous baseline. This indicates the framework's adaptability to varied sensor suites.

**D. Ablation Study**
An ablation study on V2X-R (Table V) evaluated the contribution of each HydraCollab component:
- **Collaborative Sensor Gating (CSG) alone:** Improved the baseline (vanilla intermediate collaboration) AP@0.7 by 0.69% and reduced communication bandwidth by 35.4%. This highlights its ability to select pertinent features and avoid unnecessary transmissions.
- **Adaptive Collaboration alone:** Improved AP@0.7 by 0.40% and reduced bandwidth by 52.7% by selectively applying late collaboration.
- **HydraCollab (both components):** Achieved the highest gains, increasing AP@0.7 by 0.78% and reducing communication bandwidth by 59.1% over the baseline, demonstrating synergistic effects.
- **CSG Gate Type:** The adaptive CSG mechanism consistently outperformed fixed gates (e.g., LiDAR-only, Radar-only, L+R-only) and random gates, confirming its effectiveness in selecting informative features.
- **Confidence Threshold (λ) for Adaptive Collaboration:** An analysis (Figure 6) showed that setting λ too high degrades performance by filtering out useful features, while communication volume reduction plateaus. An optimal λ value (around 0.2) balances performance and bandwidth.

**E. Computational Complexity**
A comparison with Where2comm on V2X-Radar (Table VI) showed that HydraCollab has a higher total computational complexity (5008 GFLOPs vs. 2438 GFLOPs). This increase is primarily attributed to the per-sensor spatial confidence map generation, as HydraCollab is a multi-sensor ensemble variant. However, the Collaborative Sensor Gating and Collaboration ID + Masking modules introduce minimal overhead in terms of FLOPs (0.11% of total FLOPs) and parameters (7.44% of total parameters), suggesting that the substantial gains in performance and bandwidth efficiency are achieved with a modest computational cost for the adaptive components themselves.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HydraCollab addresses a critical limitation in the domain of collaborative perception for multi-agent systems: the persistent trade-off between perception accuracy and communication bandwidth. By introducing an adaptive framework, this work provides a method to overcome the rigid constraints of previous systems, offering a more efficient and robust solution for real-world deployments.

The significance of HydraCollab lies in its ability to simultaneously enhance perception performance and substantially reduce communication overhead. This is particularly impactful for applications such as autonomous vehicles (CAVs) and UAV swarms, where communication resources are often limited, and perceptual accuracy is paramount for safety and operational effectiveness. Perception failures in these systems can lead to severe consequences, making the proposed improvements crucial.

**Potential impacts include:**

- **Improved Scalability and Efficiency:** By selectively transmitting only the most informative sensor features and dynamically choosing collaboration strategies (intermediate or late) based on spatial confidence, HydraCollab enables multi-agent systems to scale more effectively. It allows for larger numbers of collaborating agents or higher data rates within existing bandwidth limitations.
- **Enhanced Robustness in Dynamic Environments:** The adaptive nature of the framework allows it to adjust to varying environmental contexts, sensor qualities, and agent configurations. This means better performance in challenging scenarios like occlusions, adverse weather, or diverse sensor suites across agents.
- **Broader Applicability to Heterogeneous Systems:** The demonstrated ability to handle heterogeneous agents with different sensing capabilities extends the practical deployment possibilities of collaborative perception. This is realistic for many distributed autonomous systems where agents may not be identically equipped.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** HydraCollab leverages existing concepts like Where2comm's spatial confidence maps. The authors note that future work could explore more effective alternatives for context features, potentially leading to further advancements in adaptive collaborative perception systems. This indicates that the current work serves as a robust baseline for continued innovation in the field.
- **Economic and Safety Benefits:** In autonomous driving, reducing communication costs while maintaining or improving safety-critical perception directly translates to more economically viable and safer systems. For UAV swarms, efficient perception enables complex coordinated tasks and enhances mission success rates.

In conclusion, HydraCollab's adaptive, spatial-confidence-driven approach provides a method for multi-agent collaborations to operate more efficiently and capably within bandwidth-constrained scenarios, thereby advancing the state of the art in distributed autonomous systems.
