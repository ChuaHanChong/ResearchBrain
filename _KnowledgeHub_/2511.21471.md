# SpatialBench: Benchmarking Multimodal Large Language Models for Spatial Cognition

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced SpatialBench, a cognitively grounded, hierarchical evaluation framework and a large-scale dataset for benchmarking Multimodal Large Language Models' (MLLMs) spatial cognition. The study found MLLMs excel at basic spatial perception but exhibit significant limitations in higher-level symbolic reasoning, causality, and planning, revealing a substantial performance gap when compared to human intelligence which achieved a near-perfect 96.40% overall score.

## Problem

- Existing MLLM benchmarks for spatial abilities are fragmented, task-oriented, and rely on simplified datasets, failing to capture the hierarchical and interdependent nature of spatial cognition.
- Prior evaluations provide only a partial view of MLLMs' spatial intelligence, making it difficult to systematically diagnose deficiencies or analyze cognitive processes comprehensively.
- A lack of robust, diagnostically powerful methods for evaluating MLLM spatial cognition hinders the development of AI systems capable of effective interaction with the physical world.

## Method

- A ==five-level hierarchical framework== for spatial cognition was established, decomposing spatial intelligence into observation, topology and relation, symbolic reasoning, causality, and planning.
- A new, large-scale, and fine-grained benchmark dataset named ==SpatialBench== was constructed, comprising **1,347** question-answer pairs across **15** tasks derived from real-world, egocentric videos with LiDAR ground truth.
- A novel, ==capability-oriented metric== was introduced, which assigns adaptive weights to each cognitive level to provide a holistic and complexity-aware assessment of overall spatial reasoning ability.

## Results

- MLLMs generally performed well on Level 1 (Observation) and Level 2 (Topology and Relation) tasks but exhibited a sharp performance drop for Levels 3 (Symbolic Reasoning), 4 (Causality), and 5 (Planning).
- Proprietary models like Gemini-2.5-pro consistently secured top positions, demonstrating up to **15.6%** higher average accuracy on higher-level tasks compared to open-source models.
- One-shot evaluation revealed varied generalization capabilities across models, with some weaker models showing substantial performance improvements, indicating the potential of in-context learning to boost higher-level cognitive capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLLMs demonstrate strong performance on lower-level spatial cognition tasks (observation, topology & relation) but show a significant decline in higher-level tasks (symbolic reasoning, causality, planning).
> - Leading proprietary MLLMs consistently achieved substantially higher overall scores than open-source counterparts, particularly in complex cognitive tasks.
> - Human participants achieved a near-perfect overall score of 96.40%, significantly outperforming all MLLMs and highlighting their superior goal-directed reasoning and robust egocentric perspective taking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) represents a notable advancement in artificial intelligence, aiming to integrate visual and textual modalities to achieve more general-purpose intelligence. These models have demonstrated capabilities in various vision-language tasks, including some aspects of spatial reasoning. However, the existing evaluation landscape for MLLMs' spatial abilities has been characterized by fragmentation and oversimplification. Previous benchmarks often focus on specific, isolated vision-language tasks, reducing spatial cognition to a single-dimensional metric, which may not adequately capture its inherent hierarchical structure and interdependencies. Many existing datasets are synthetic or narrowly defined, limiting their ability to reflect real-world visual diversity and complexity. This lack of comprehensive and cognitively grounded evaluation has made it challenging to diagnose systematic deficiencies in MLLMs' spatial intelligence and understand their cognitive processes beyond basic perception.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing a structured framework and a benchmark designed to provide a more nuanced assessment of spatial cognition. By moving beyond task-specific evaluations to a hierarchical, ability-oriented approach, SpatialBench aims to offer a clearer understanding of MLLMs' strengths and weaknesses in progressively complex spatial reasoning tasks, thereby contributing to the development of more robust and spatially intelligent AI systems capable of interacting effectively with physical environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while MLLMs have advanced significantly, existing benchmarks for spatial cognition often misrepresent or oversimplify this complex ability. Current evaluations tend to reduce spatial intelligence to a single, undifferentiated metric, failing to recognize its hierarchical nature—from basic observation to advanced planning. This reductionist approach impedes the systematic diagnosis of MLLM capabilities, making it difficult to identify specific cognitive bottlenecks or understand the interdependencies between different spatial skills. Furthermore, many existing benchmarks rely on synthetic data or narrowly defined scenarios, which do not reflect the visual diversity, noise, and complex 3D geometry encountered in real-world environments, thus providing only a partial view of genuine spatial intelligence.

To address these identified gaps, the authors established several key objectives:

- **Develop a Hierarchical Spatial Cognition Framework:** To conceptualize spatial intelligence as a structured, progressive process. This framework decomposes spatial understanding into five distinct, progressively complex levels, drawing inspiration from cognitive map theory, shifting evaluation from task-driven to ability-oriented assessment.
- **Construct a Comprehensive, Real-World Benchmark:** To create "SpatialBench," a large-scale, fine-grained benchmark dataset. This dataset is designed to cover 15 distinct spatial reasoning tasks, each aligned with the proposed five cognitive levels, utilizing real-world videos from diverse indoor and outdoor environments to ensure ecological validity and challenge MLLMs with realistic complexity.
- **Introduce a Unified, Capability-Oriented Evaluation Metric:** To enable consistent assessment across the heterogeneous tasks and cognitive levels. This metric is designed to reliably measure a model’s overall spatial reasoning ability by adaptively weighting performance across different cognitive levels based on their inherent complexity and discriminative power.
- **Conduct Extensive Empirical Evaluation:** To assess a wide range of state-of-the-art MLLMs (both proprietary and open-source) using the SpatialBench framework. This objective aims to systematically reveal performance stratification across the cognitive hierarchy, identifying specific strengths and limitations of current models.
- **Benchmark Human Performance:** To provide a reference point for artificial spatial intelligence by comparing MLLM performance against human capabilities in spatial cognition. This comparison aims to quantify the existing gap and offer insights into the qualitative differences in reasoning strategies between humans and MLLMs.
- **Lay the Foundation for Future Spatially Intelligent Systems:** By establishing a systematic framework and benchmark, the research seeks to guide future advancements in MLLM architectures, training paradigms, and data collection strategies towards developing more robust and human-like spatial intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology is structured around a proposed hierarchical framework for spatial cognition, the construction of a new benchmark dataset named SpatialBench, and a comprehensive evaluation protocol.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Spatial Cognitive Taxonomy

Inspired by cognitive map theory, the authors propose a five-level hierarchical framework to decompose spatial cognition:
- **Observation (L1):** The foundational level involving the identification of objects and their basic attributes (category, color, shape, size) from visual inputs. Tasks include object counting, object size, room size, and absolute distance estimation.
- **Topology and Relation (L2):** Focuses on understanding spatial relationships between entities, such as adjacency, containment, orientation, and connectivity, to describe how elements are arranged within a scene. Tasks include appearance order, relative distance, relative direction, appearance order on a self-defined route, and relative counting.
- **Symbolic Reasoning (L3):** Extends spatial understanding to semantic interpretation, associating visual symbols or spatial cues with abstract meanings and applying rule-based reasoning to infer spatial intent or constraints. Tasks include multi-hop spatial reasoning, affordance, and landmark-constrained pose localization.
- **Causality (L4):** Reflects the ability to infer spatiotemporal dependencies and predict outcomes of actions, integrating physical intuition into reasoning about object movements and interactions. The main task is spatial causal reasoning.
- **Planning (L5):** The highest level, integrating perception, relational understanding, and causal reasoning to enable goal-oriented decision-making, formulating action sequences or navigation strategies. Tasks include visual-based commands and route planning.

This taxonomy is designed to provide an ability-driven, rather than task-driven, evaluation, allowing for the diagnosis of cognitive bottlenecks.

#### 3.2. SpatialBench Dataset Construction

**Data Collection:**
SpatialBench is built from 117 self-recorded, real-world videos, contrasting with benchmarks that adapt existing datasets. The data was captured using a custom sensing platform integrating a calibrated RGB camera and a 3D LiDAR sensor, ensuring precise visual and geometric synchronization. Videos cover diverse indoor (offices, residential areas) and outdoor (city roads, forest trails, underground environments) settings, including static and dynamic scenes, recorded from a first-person perspective. LiDAR data enables ground-truth measurements for metric questions.

**Question-Answer Generation:**
The dataset comprises 3193 question-answer pairs across the 15 task types, mapped to the five cognitive levels. Human annotators work in pairs: one proposes questions, and the other reviews for accuracy, ambiguity, and cognitive level alignment. For non-metric questions, state-of-the-art commercial models generate answers using specialized prompting templates, along with evidence summaries (key frames, reasoning traces). For metric questions, ground-truth answers (size, distance) are directly computed from LiDAR point cloud data through geometric fitting and spatial projection.

**Annotation Verification:**
A multi-step verification protocol ensures annotation reliability:
- **L1 and L2 Questions:** Multiple leading models independently generate answers. Consistency across model outputs is evaluated, and a subset of provisionally approved answers undergoes human spot-checking. Discrepancies trigger full human review.
- **L3-L5 Questions:** All annotations undergo mandatory human verification due to higher cognitive complexity. Human reviewers follow a checklist to ensure evidence frame display, correct interpretation of multi-model outputs (if applicable), and adherence to question schema. Rejected or modified annotations are reviewed by an additional annotator for final determination.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

- **Multiple-Choice Answer (MCA) Tasks:** Accuracy (ACC) is used, measuring the proportion of exact matches between model predictions and ground-truth labels.
- **Numerical Answer (NA) Tasks:** Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA) is employed, defined as 1/10 * Σ_{θ∈Ω} I(|y' - y|/y < 1 - θ), where y' is prediction, y is ground truth, and Ω is a set of confidence thresholds {0.5, 0.55..., 0.95}.
- **Overall Score:** A high-level, capability-oriented metric integrating performance across the five hierarchical cognitive levels. Adaptive weights (C_i) are assigned to each level, computed as C_i = F_i * S_i, where S_i is the standard deviation of model scores for level i (reflecting difficulty/discriminative range). F_i is an adjusted weighting factor: F_i = αD_i + 0.1(1 - α)E_i, where D_i is the question proportion for level i, and α controls the balance between baseline (D_i) and optimized adjustment (E_i). The non-negative variables E_i (summing to 10) are optimized through a constrained nonlinear program (Problem P') to ensure the weights C_i increase monotonically with cognitive complexity (C_{i+1} - C_i ≥ 0) while minimizing the variance of these increments and balancing against the sum of squared weights (Σ C_j^2). This optimization yields complexity-aware weights that are then combined with per-level average model ratings to produce the final overall score.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved a diverse set of MLLMs, including proprietary models (Gemini-2.5-pro, GPT-4o-mini, Claude-Sonnet-4-5, GPT-5-chat-latest) and open-source models (Qwen variants, GLM variants, ERNIE, VST, Cambrian-S). Human performance was also benchmarked.

#### 4.1. Benchmarking MLLM Performance

- **Performance Stratification:** A clear stratification of MLLM performance across the hierarchical cognitive levels was observed. Models generally exhibit strong performance on low-level observation and topology tasks (L1 and L2). However, their competence declines significantly for higher-level tasks involving symbolic reasoning (L3), causality (L4), and planning (L5).
- **Proprietary vs. Open-Source Models:** Gemini-2.5-pro consistently ranked first, achieving substantially higher overall scores (75.79) compared to other models. This performance gap was particularly pronounced in high-level reasoning tasks. Among open-source models, Cambrian-S-3B (59.12) and VST-3B-RL (58.29) showed competitive performance on lower and mid-level tasks, indicating that specialized training (e.g., reinforcement learning or instruction tuning) can improve spatial cognition even with smaller parameter scales.
- **Impact of Model Scale and Architecture:** For open-source models, a general correlation between model scale and average performance was noted, with larger models typically achieving higher scores. However, model size alone was not the sole determinant; architectural design and visual-linguistic integration strategies also played a role, as evidenced by performance variations within the same model families (e.g., Qwen instruction-tuned vs. thinking modes).
- **Specific Task Performance:** Most models performed well on tasks like object counting and simple topological queries. Performance dropped noticeably on multi-hop spatial reasoning, affordance, causal reasoning, and visual planning. This suggests a struggle in converting perceptual inputs into robust symbolic rules, inferring dynamic consequences, and generating multi-step plans.

#### 4.2. One-Shot Evaluation

The one-shot assessment explored MLLMs' in-context learning capabilities by providing a single QA example.
- **Varied Responses to Prompting:** Gemini-2.5-pro exhibited a performance decline (overall score drop of 7.7 points) in the few-shot setting, particularly across intermediate cognitive levels. This suggests that its internal reasoning may be robust enough that external prompts, even high-quality ones, may not always enhance its performance.
- **Significant Gains for Weaker Models:** In contrast, GPT-5-chat-latest and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct showed substantial performance improvements (overall scores increased by 38.6 and 25.4 points, respectively). These models, initially weaker, significantly enhanced specific spatial reasoning abilities with minimal in-context guidance, highlighting the potential of one-shot prompting to boost higher-level cognitive capabilities, especially by leveraging their linguistic reasoning strengths.

#### 4.3. Benchmarking Human Performance

- **Superior Human Performance:** Human participants achieved near-perfect performance (overall score of 96.40), significantly outperforming all MLLMs across all cognitive levels. Humans demonstrated essentially 100% accuracy on symbolic reasoning, causality, and planning tasks, while also maintaining high performance on observation and topology tasks.
- **Qualitative Differences in Reasoning:** Human reasoning was characterized as selective and goal-directed, focusing on key directional cues and relevant spatial relationships. MLLMs, in contrast, often exhibited diffuse attention to surface details, describing scenes exhaustively but lacking coherent spatial intent or an understanding of movement and geometry. An example case study illustrated Gemini's exhaustive description versus a human's concise, goal-oriented inference.

#### 4.4. Key Insights from Evaluation

- **Struggle with Continuous Scene Reconstruction:** MLLMs inherently struggle to reconstruct and maintain continuous spatial scenes from dynamic visual inputs, leading to misinterpretations of relative directions, confusion in sequential positions, and errors in continuous scene tracking and subtle geometric relations.
- **Hierarchical Bottleneck:** The findings empirically validate that MLLM spatial cognition is strictly hierarchical and tightly coupled. Success in higher-order reasoning is fundamentally bottlenecked by the accuracy of lower-level perception. Performance rapidly degrades as cognitive complexity increases, with cascading failures observed from the symbolic reasoning level onward.
- **Egocentric Reasoning Breakdown:** Models frequently conflate scene-centric, camera-centric, and agent-centric coordinate systems, leading to consistent directional inversion or orthogonal errors in egocentric reasoning tasks (e.g., misinterpreting "left" vs. "backward" relative to a robot's perspective). This highlights a fundamental weakness in establishing and maintaining explicit frames of reference.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes several contributions to the field of multimodal AI and has potential implications for future research and development:

- **Establishment of a Systematic Framework:** The introduction of a comprehensive, cognitively grounded, five-level hierarchical framework for spatial cognition evaluation is a significant step. It shifts the evaluation paradigm from task-specific metrics to an ability-oriented assessment, enabling a more interpretable and fine-grained diagnosis of MLLMs' spatial intelligence. This framework provides a structured lens through which to understand the progressive development of spatial understanding in AI.
- **Development of a Robust Benchmark:** SpatialBench, a large-scale, fine-grained benchmark dataset constructed from real-world, first-person videos across diverse environments, offers a robust foundation for systematic and scalable evaluation. Its focus on challenging real-world scenarios and comprehensive task coverage addresses limitations of previous synthetic or narrowly scoped datasets.
- **Identification of MLLM Strengths and Limitations:** The extensive experiments revealed that while modern MLLMs possess strong perceptual grounding and basic relational reasoning capabilities, they exhibit substantial limitations in higher-order abilities such as symbolic abstraction, causal inference, and multi-step planning. This systematic identification of specific cognitive bottlenecks provides clear targets for future model improvement.
- **Quantification of Human-AI Gap:** Benchmarking human performance against MLLMs rigorously quantified the significant gap that remains between artificial and human spatial intelligence. The qualitative insights into human goal-directed, selective reasoning versus MLLM's diffuse attention offer valuable directions for developing more human-like cognitive architectures.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The findings provide a clear roadmap for advancing spatially intelligent systems. This includes the need to:
 - Leverage high-quality datasets structured with progressive cognitive tasks.
 - Incorporate explicit spatial representations (e.g., scene graphs, 3D geometric priors).
 - Adopt advanced training paradigms such as curriculum learning and agent-based interactive environments to promote multi-step reasoning and long-horizon planning.
 - Develop closed-loop feedback mechanisms to refine low-level perceptual modules based on high-level reasoning and planning errors.

Overall, this work lays a foundational basis for measuring and advancing hierarchical spatial cognition in MLLMs, which is critical for their effective interaction with the physical world in applications like robotics, autonomous navigation, and intelligent assistants.
