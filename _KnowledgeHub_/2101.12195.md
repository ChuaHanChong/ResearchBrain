# Playable Video Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Playable Video Generation (PVG), an unsupervised learning framework enabling users to interactively control video generation through discrete actions learned automatically from unlabelled real-world videos. The proposed CADDY framework generates realistic video sequences and discovers semantically consistent actions, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines on video quality and action-space metrics across various datasets.

## Problem

- Existing action-conditioned future frame prediction methods heavily rely on supervised action labels, restricting their use to synthetic or highly controlled environments.
- Manual annotation of frame-level actions for real-world videos is prohibitively expensive and often subjective, limiting the applicability of controllable video generation.
- The challenge of simultaneously generating high-quality video and discovering a rich, consistent, and controllable action space from scratch in an unsupervised manner.

## Method

- The **CADDY** (Clustering for Action Decomposition and DiscoverY) framework uses an encoder-decoder architecture with a discrete bottleneck for action representation, trained end-to-end on unlabelled videos.
- A probabilistic action network infers discrete actions via a Gumbel-Softmax layer and continuous action variability embeddings, crucial for handling non-determinism in real-world dynamics.
- A multi-objective loss function, including perceptual reconstruction losses and mutual information maximization, ensures high-quality generation and the discovery of distinct, semantically meaningful actions.

## Results

- CADDY significantly outperforms adapted state-of-the-art baselines on diverse datasets (BAIR, Atari Breakout, Tennis) across video quality (e.g., FID and FVD) and action-space metrics (e.g., ∆-Acc).
- The framework successfully learns semantically meaningful and interpretable actions, such as specific object movements in BAIR, platform control in Atari Breakout, and player movements in Tennis.
- A user study on the Tennis dataset showed CADDY achieving a significantly higher Fleiss’ kappa measure (0.469) compared to baselines (up to 0.0718), indicating strong user agreement on the interpretability and consistency of learned actions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unsupervised learning can effectively discover semantically consistent, discrete action spaces from raw video data without manual annotations, enabling interactive control.
> - The action variability embedding is essential for disentangling the main action semantics from stochastic variations, allowing the discrete action to capture high-level meanings.
> - A mixed training procedure (mixing original and reconstructed frames as input) and the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization are critical for robust training and learning of discrete, controllable actions for auto-regressive inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper introduces and addresses a novel problem called "Playable Video Generation (PVG)" in an **unsupervised learning** setting. This positions it at the intersection of several active research areas in computer vision and machine learning:

- **Video Generation:** The overarching field concerns creating realistic video sequences. Early works focused on unconditional generation, but increasingly, research has shifted towards conditional generation, where the video output is controlled by various inputs (e.g., text, image, pose, or actions). This paper falls squarely into conditional video generation.
- **Future Frame Prediction:** A specific sub-area of video generation where the goal is to predict subsequent frames given one or more initial frames. Many existing methods condition these predictions on action labels to offer control. However, a significant limitation of most action-conditioned future frame prediction methods (e.g., GameGAN, Oh et al., 2015) is their reliance on **supervised action labels** at every time step during training. This often restricts their applicability to synthetic environments like video games or highly controlled robotic setups where such labels are readily available.
- **Unsupervised Learning and Deep Clustering:** This work leverages principles from unsupervised learning, particularly deep clustering techniques. Recent advancements in self-supervised learning have shown promise in learning meaningful representations from unlabelled data. The paper draws inspiration from methods like DeepCluster and Invariant Information Clustering (IIC), which jointly optimize clustering and representation learning. However, these prior works primarily focus on image clustering or video-level action clustering, not fine-grained, frame-level action discovery for interactive video generation.
- **Controllable Video Generation:** The paper's core novelty lies in enabling *on-the-fly, interactive control* of generated videos using *discrete actions learned in an unsupervised manner from unlabelled real-world videos*. Previous methods offering high levels of control either required supervision (e.g., GameGAN) or were heavily reliant on environment-specific priors (e.g., Vid2Game for specific character control). By introducing PVG, this work breaks these dependencies, aiming for a general framework applicable across varied real-world environments without manual annotation effort.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art by bridging the gap between unsupervised representation learning and highly controllable, conditional video generation. It overcomes the data-intensive supervision requirement of action-conditioned video prediction, extending its applicability to real-world, unlabelled video data. This opens up new avenues for simulating and interacting with complex environments using learned, intuitive action spaces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this paper is to introduce and solve the **unsupervised learning problem of Playable Video Generation (PVG)**. Specifically, the authors aim to:

- **Learn Semantically Consistent Discrete Actions:** From a large dataset of unlabelled videos, the model should automatically discover a set of distinct, meaningful actions that characterize the dynamics and interactions within the video environment. This learning must happen without any explicit action labels or prior knowledge about the nature or number of actions.
- **Generate Realistic Videos Conditioned on User Input:** Once actions are learned, the system should allow a user to interactively control the generated video. At each time step, the user selects a discrete action, and the model generates the next video frame realistically, reflecting the chosen action. This provides a "gaming-like experience" where the user can influence the unfolding video sequence.
- **Overcome Limitations of Supervised Approaches:** The core motivation is to move beyond the dependence on manually annotated, frame-level action labels, which severely restricts the application of existing conditional video generation techniques to real-world scenarios. Manual annotation is prohibitively expensive and often subjective for complex actions.

The motivation for PVG stems from human cognitive abilities. As humans, we can instinctively understand and anticipate actions in videos, such as sports or games, and even reason about alternative actions and their consequences, all in an unsupervised manner. The paper seeks to imbue machines with a similar capability, enabling them to:

- **Automatically Simulate Real-World Environments:** Create models that can effectively learn the dynamics of diverse environments (e.g., robotics, sports, general scenes) from raw video data.
- **Enable Interactive Exploration:** Provide a tool for users to explore "what if" scenarios within these learned environments, generating new, controllable video content.
- **Foster Generalization:** Develop a framework that is general enough to be applied to a wide variety of environments without requiring environment-specific prior knowledge or heavy modifications.

The difficulty lies not only in generating realistic, high-quality video but also in simultaneously discovering a rich and consistent action space from scratch, and managing the inherent stochasticity of real-world video dynamics within a discrete action framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel framework called **Clustering for Action Decomposition and DiscoverY (CADDY)**, which operates in a self-supervised manner on unlabelled video data. CADDY is built upon an encoder-decoder architecture, with a crucial discrete bottleneck for action representation.

#### 3.1. Overall Pipeline (CADDY Architecture)

CADDY comprises four main modules, trained end-to-end:

1. **Encoder (E):** Extracts a feature representation `f_t` for each input frame `x_t`. This aims to capture appearance and semantic information.
2. **Temporal Model:** This is the core of action learning and state prediction.
 - **Action Network (A):** Infers the discrete action `a_t` (from `K` possible actions) and a continuous **action variability embedding `v_t`** that characterize the transition between `f_t` and `f_t+1`. This `v_t` component is critical for handling non-determinism in real-world videos, capturing *how* an action is performed beyond its main semantic.
 - **Dynamics Network (R):** A recurrent neural network (based on convolutional LSTMs) that takes the current environment state `s_t`, frame features `f_t`, and the inferred action (`a_t`, `v_t`) to predict the next environment state `s_t+1`.
3. **Decoder (D):** Reconstructs the next frame `x_t+1` from the predicted environment state `s_t+1`.
4. **Self-Supervision Loop:** The reconstructed frames are fed back to the encoder to assess the quality of the estimated action labels and drive the learning process primarily through reconstruction loss. The key idea is that `A` must learn consistent actions for `R` to accurately predict `s_t+1`, enabling `D` to reconstruct `x_t+1` faithfully.

#### 3.2. Probabilistic Action Network

The action network `A` is designed with a probabilistic auto-encoder formulation to learn `a_t` and `v_t`.

- **Action Embedding Extraction:** An action state network `A_s` within `A` extracts action embeddings `e_t` from `f_t` and `e_t+1` from `f_t+1`, modeling them as Gaussian distributions `N(μ_e_t, σ^2_e_t)`.
- **Action Direction:** The difference `d_t = e_t+1 - e_t` is used to represent the action direction, also following a Gaussian distribution `N(μ_d_t, σ^2_d_t)`.
- **Discrete Action (`a_t`):** The sampled `d_t` is fed to a single-layer classifier that outputs action probabilities `p_t`. Crucially, a **Gumbel-Softmax layer** is used to obtain the discrete label `a_t`, ensuring differentiability for training while yielding a discrete output for user control at inference.
- **Action Variability Embedding (`v_t`):** To ensure `a_t` learns meaningful semantics and `v_t` acts as a bottleneck, `v_t` is defined as the expected difference between `d_t` and a set of `K` learned **action direction centroids `{c_k}`**. These centroids are estimated via an exponential moving average. This forces `a_t` to capture the main semantics, while `v_t` captures the residual variability for a given action, preventing `d_t` from being fully encoded in `v_t` alone.

#### 3.3. Training Objectives

CADDY is trained with a multi-objective loss function:

- **Reconstruction Losses:**
 - `L_x_rec`: A perceptual loss (VGG-19 based) between the original `x_t` and reconstructed `x_t` to ensure high-quality, semantically faithful video generation. Augmented with `L1` loss.
 - `L_f_rec`: Euclidean distance between original `f_t` and reconstructed `f_t` features, ensuring semantic information from input frames is preserved in reconstructions.
- **Action Losses:**
 - `L_act`: A mutual information maximization objective between the action probabilities `p_t` (from original frames) and `p_t_hat` (from reconstructed frames). This aims to maximize the entropy of `p_t` (encouraging discovery of all `K` actions and preventing centroid collapse) while minimizing conditional entropy (ensuring consistent action inference from original and reconstructed sequences).
 - `L_a_rec`: Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the distributions of original `d_t` and reconstructed `d_t_hat` action directions, ensuring the action variability is consistent.
 - `L_KL`: KL divergence between `d_t` and a Gaussian prior `N(0, I_n)`, regularizing the latent space of action directions.

#### 3.4. Test Time and Mixed Training

- **Inference:** At test time, the user provides a discrete action `a_t` at each step. The model operates auto-regressively, using the previously generated frame `x_t_hat` as input for the next step. The action variability `v_t` is set to 0 (corresponding to the mode of its distribution).
- **Mixed Training Procedure:** To mitigate the domain shift problem between real training frames and generated inference frames, the training procedure mixes inputs. For an initial `T_f` frames, the dynamics network `R` receives features `f_t` from original frames. For `t > T_f`, `R` is fed with features `f_t_hat` computed from the *reconstructed* frames `x_t_hat`. This makes the network robust to processing its own generated outputs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper conducts extensive experiments on three diverse datasets to validate CADDY:

- **BAIR robot pushing dataset:** Real-world robotics, 256x256 resolution, 44K videos.
- **Atari Breakout dataset:** Synthetic video game, 160x210 resolution, 1407 sequences.
- **Tennis dataset:** Real-world sports (YouTube videos), 256x96 resolution, 900 videos.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Protocol

A comprehensive set of metrics is used, covering both video generation quality and the quality of the learned action space:

- **Video Quality Metrics:** LPIPS (perceptual similarity), FID (generative quality), FVD (video quality).
- **Action-Space Metrics:**
 - ∆-MSE: Measures how well object displacement (∆) can be regressed from the action label, assessing action consistency.
 - ∆-Acc: Measures how well the action label can be predicted from object displacement, further assessing action consistency.
- **Action-Conditioning Metrics:**
 - ADD (Average Detection Distance): Euclidean distance between reference object keypoints in original and reconstructed frames, indicating accurate object movement.
 - MDR (Missing Detection Rate): Percentage of successful object detections in original sequences that are missed in reconstructed ones, indicating object coherence.

#### 4.2. Experimental Analysis of CADDY (Ablation Study)

An ablation study on the BAIR dataset confirms the importance of key components:

- **Gumbel-Softmax (G.S.):** Essential for learning discrete action representations. Without it, the model learns continuous actions, leading to poor performance at test time when discrete actions are imposed.
- **Action variability embedding (`v_t`):** Crucial for handling non-determinism and allowing the discrete actions to capture high-level semantics. Without `v_t`, the model struggles to optimize reconstruction or `v_t` simply encodes `d_t` without forcing `a_t` to be meaningful.
- **Mutual Information Loss (`L_act`):** Vital for ensuring the discovery of a rich and distinct set of actions and preventing the collapse of action centroids. When `L_act` is missing, `v_t` can fully encode the action direction, leading to a degenerate discrete action space.

CADDY (Full) significantly outperforms all ablated variants across all metrics (e.g., LPIPS 0.202 vs. 0.209-0.263, FID 35.9 vs. 42.3-80.0, ∆-Acc 69.0% vs. 24.1-51.2%), validating the design choices.

#### 4.3. Visualization of Learned Actions

Qualitative visualizations (Figure 3) show that CADDY learns semantically meaningful actions:

- **BAIR:** Actions corresponding to specific movements along x, y, z axes, and a no-movement action.
- **Atari Breakout:** Actions for platform movement (left/right) and the physics of the ball and blocks.
- **Tennis:** Actions like forward, backward, lateral movement, no-movement, and hitting the ball.
The object motion distribution for each learned action consistently reflects the expected behavior, demonstrating that CADDY successfully clusters motions into distinct, interpretable actions.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Previous Works

CADDY is compared against adapted versions of state-of-the-art video prediction models: SAVP, MoCoGAN, and SRVP (with "+" indicating higher capacity versions). These baselines were modified to operate in an unsupervised PVG setting by clustering their latent spaces.

- **BAIR Dataset (Table 2):** CADDY achieves the best performance across almost all metrics. Notably, it significantly surpasses baselines in action space quality metrics (∆-MSE 54.8% vs. 80.9-98.4%, ∆-Acc 69.0% vs. 20.7-44.8%), indicating a superior capacity to learn discrete, consistent actions. Video quality (LPIPS, FID, FVD) is also competitive or better than baselines, especially compared to the full-resolution "+" versions.
- **Atari Breakout Dataset (Table 3):** CADDY demonstrates a clear lead, particularly in video quality (LPIPS 7.66e-3 vs. 39.3e-3, FID 0.716 vs. 4.84, FVD 5.94 vs. 84.4) and action-conditioning (ADD 7.29 pixels vs. 15.8-54.6, MDR 2.70% vs. 0.795-51.5%). The lower MDR for MoCoGAN is misleading as it comes with very high ADD, suggesting the platform is generated but in the wrong place.
- **Tennis Dataset (Table 4):** CADDY again excels in action consistency (∆-MSE 72.2% vs. 101-116%, ∆-Acc 45.5% vs. 19.6-33.1%) and action-conditioned video quality (ADD 8.85 pixels vs. 10.7-48.2, MDR 1.01% vs. 19.2-27.0%). This highlights CADDY's ability to handle complex, real-world scenes with moving agents.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Evaluation

Figure 4 illustrates that CADDY generates reconstructed sequences with precise object placement and movement, closely matching the original videos, particularly for the BAIR robot arm and the Tennis player.

#### 4.6. User Evaluation

A user study on the Tennis dataset further validates CADDY's performance. Users were asked to identify the action performed in generated sequences.

- CADDY achieves a significantly higher **Fleiss’ kappa measure (0.469)** compared to baselines (ranging from -3.15e-3 to 0.0718). This indicates strong user agreement that the actions generated by CADDY consistently correspond to predefined semantic labels (e.g., Left, Right, Hit the ball).
- All methods showed high diversity in their action spaces (entropy of user-selected actions), preventing mode collapse. However, CADDY's actions were the most consistently interpretable by human users.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Playable Video Generation (PVG) and the proposed CADDY framework marks a significant advancement in conditional video generation and unsupervised learning. Its potential impact spans several domains:

- **Democratization of Conditional Video Generation:** By eliminating the need for extensive, frame-level action annotations, CADDY makes the powerful paradigm of action-conditioned video generation accessible to a vast array of real-world video data. This significantly reduces the cost and effort typically associated with developing such systems, allowing for broader application.
- **Interactive Simulation of Real-World Environments:** CADDY enables the creation of interactive, "gaming-like" simulations of complex real-world environments directly from observed video. This has profound implications for:
 - **Training and Education:** Imagine learning sports techniques by interactively controlling a player, or training robots in simulated environments derived directly from real-world footage.
 - **Content Creation:** Artists and designers could generate new, controllable video content from existing footage, potentially for virtual reality, augmented reality, or interactive narratives.
 - **Scientific Research:** Researchers could explore the dynamics of natural or complex systems by manipulating learned actions, gaining insights into cause-and-effect relationships without explicit modeling.
- **Advancement in Unsupervised Learning:** CADDY pushes the boundaries of unsupervised learning by not just learning representations but also discovering structured, discrete action spaces that are directly interpretable and controllable by humans. The probabilistic action network, Gumbel-Softmax, action variability embedding, and mutual information loss are key methodological contributions that could inspire future work in unsupervised control learning.
- **Foundation for More Complex Systems:** The paper lays the groundwork for more sophisticated systems. The authors explicitly mention extending the method to multi-agent environments as future work, which would significantly increase the complexity and realism of the simulations. This could lead to applications in autonomous driving (simulating multiple agents' interactions), crowd dynamics, or even social simulations.
- **Bridging AI and Gaming:** The "playable video" concept directly bridges the fields of artificial intelligence and gaming, offering a new form of interactive media where users can "play" real-world videos. This could lead to novel entertainment experiences and interfaces.

In conclusion, "Playable Video Generation" addresses a critical limitation in conditional video generation, opening up new avenues for unsupervised learning of interactive environmental dynamics. Its ability to extract meaningful, controllable actions from raw video data promises to revolutionize how we interact with and simulate complex visual information, with far-reaching applications across various scientific, industrial, and entertainment sectors.
