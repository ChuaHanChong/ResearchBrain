# ActiveVLN: Towards Active Exploration via Multi-Turn RL in Vision-and-Language Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> ActiveVLN introduces a two-stage framework that enhances Vision-and-Language Navigation agents through active exploration via multi-turn Reinforcement Learning. The method achieves an 11.6% success rate increase on R2R Val-Unseen and demonstrates competitive performance on RxR Val-Unseen with a smaller model and less data, effectively mitigating covariate shift and reducing expert data dependency.

## Problem

- Imitation Learning (IL) in Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) suffers from covariate shift, leading to error accumulation and poor generalization in unseen states.
- Existing state-of-the-art VLN methods heavily rely on costly, large-scale expert-annotated trajectories for training and post-training corrections like DAgger.
- Prior Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches in VLN often lack true dynamic interaction and open-ended active exploration, limiting their ability to discover diverse and optimal navigation routes beyond expert demonstrations.

## Method

- Proposes **ActiveVLN**, a two-stage framework combining initial Imitation Learning (IL) for bootstrapping with subsequent active exploration via multi-turn ==Reinforcement Learning== (RL).
- Employs a multi-turn paradigm where actions are conditioned on previous actions, enabling efficient inference, accelerated training, and improved RL signal propagation.
- Utilizes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for RL, which compares candidate trajectories and reinforces better-performing ones, along with a soft success reward function to guide exploration.

## Results

- Achieved an **11.6%** increase in Success Rate (SR) on R2R Val-Unseen (from **38.5%** to **50.1%**) and a **9.7%** SR increase on RxR Val-Unseen over the IL-only baseline.
- Demonstrated competitive performance against larger models (3B vs. 7B/8B parameters) and significantly less VLN-specific training data (177K trajectories vs. millions for competitors).
- Ablation studies confirmed the multi-turn paradigm's crucial role for RL gains and the dynamic early-stopping strategy's efficiency, terminating approximately **10%** of rollouts early and reducing average rollout time by about **10%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The multi-turn action generation paradigm is critical for effective RL, allowing gradients to propagate through entire action histories, leading to significantly larger performance gains compared to single-turn approaches.
> - A soft success reward, scaled by geodesic distance to the goal, effectively encourages both goal achievement and progress towards the target, promoting efficient exploration.
> - Engineering optimizations like dynamic early-stopping and scene caching/preloading are vital for making RL training computationally feasible and efficient in complex VLN environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "ActiveVLN" is situated within the rapidly evolving field of Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN), a challenging task that requires an autonomous agent to understand natural language instructions and navigate through complex real-world or simulated environments.

Initially, VLN research largely focused on **discrete environments**, where navigation was abstracted as movement between predefined waypoints on a topological graph. While simplifying path planning, this setup deviated significantly from realistic scenarios. The field subsequently shifted towards **Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments (VLN-CE)**, which demands agents to execute low-level actions (e.g., move forward, rotate) and perceive continuous observations, making it much more aligned with real-world embodied navigation tasks.

Recent advancements in **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)** have ushered in a new era for VLN. MLLM-based approaches leverage their strong capabilities in integrating language understanding and visual perception, becoming the dominant backbone for action decision-making in VLN-CE.

However, the prevailing training paradigm for MLLM-based VLN methods is **Imitation Learning (IL)**, where agents learn by mimicking expert demonstrations. While effective, IL suffers from a critical drawback: **covariate shift**. This occurs when the agent encounters states unseen during training, leading to accumulating errors and poor generalization in novel environments. To mitigate this, many state-of-the-art works adopt **DAgger (Dataset Aggregation)** post-training. DAgger incrementally collects additional expert corrections on states visited by the agent during its own explorations, thus expanding the training data to cover more diverse scenarios. Despite its benefits, DAgger still incurs substantial costs associated with data collection (requiring expert intervention to label new states) and retraining.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)** offers a promising alternative to IL by allowing agents to learn from self-generated interactions with the environment, potentially overcoming the covariate shift problem. However, prior RL approaches in VLN have been limited. They often lack true **dynamic interaction** with the environment, instead relying heavily on expert trajectories for *reward shaping* (i.e., using the expert path to define rewards) rather than enabling **open-ended active exploration**. This dependence restricts the agent's ability to discover diverse and potentially more optimal navigation routes beyond the expert's demonstrations, leading to only marginal performance gains over IL baselines.

**ActiveVLN directly addresses these limitations.** It positions itself as a solution that combines the strengths of MLLMs with a more effective RL paradigm. By explicitly enabling active exploration through multi-turn RL, ActiveVLN seeks to reduce the reliance on costly expert data, mitigate covariate shift, and empower agents to autonomously discover robust navigation strategies. It aims to achieve superior generalization and efficiency compared to both DAgger-based IL methods and previous, less interactive RL approaches, thus pushing the boundaries of autonomous embodied navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of ActiveVLN is to develop a robust and efficient Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) framework that overcomes the inherent limitations of existing approaches, particularly concerning data dependency, generalization, and the scope of exploration.

The core motivations and specific objectives can be summarized as follows:

1. **Overcome Covariate Shift in Imitation Learning (IL):** Traditional IL-based VLN methods suffer from covariate shift, where an agent's accumulated errors in unseen states lead to poor performance. The authors aim to mitigate this by moving beyond mere imitation and allowing the agent to learn from its own interactions.

2. **Reduce Reliance on Expert Trajectories and Data Collection Costs:** Current state-of-the-art methods, even those incorporating DAgger, require substantial amounts of expert-annotated trajectories for training and post-training corrections. This is costly in terms of time, effort, and resources. ActiveVLN seeks to minimize this dependency by enabling the agent to learn effectively from self-generated data.

3. **Enable Open-Ended Active Exploration:** Prior RL methods in VLN have been criticized for lacking dynamic interaction and relying on expert trajectories for reward shaping, which limits the agent's exploratory capacity. A key objective is to empower the agent to engage in truly active exploration, allowing it to discover diverse and plausible navigation routes that might deviate from, or even improve upon, expert demonstrations.

4. **Enhance Reinforcement Learning Efficiency for VLN:** Despite the promise of RL, its application in VLN has often been inefficient. The authors aim to introduce specific techniques to improve the training efficiency of the RL process, making it a more viable alternative to IL.

5. **Leverage Multi-Turn Interaction for Better Policy Learning:** The paper motivates the adoption of a multi-turn paradigm, arguing that it allows for better propagation of training signals and more effective policy refinement compared to single-turn approaches that treat actions as independent events.

6. **Achieve Competitive Performance with Reduced Resource Requirements:** Ultimately, the framework aims to achieve state-of-the-art or competitive performance on standard VLN benchmarks, ideally while using smaller models, less training time, and significantly lower data collection costs compared to existing complex systems. This demonstrates both the effectiveness and the practicality of the proposed active exploration approach.

In essence, ActiveVLN is motivated by the need for VLN agents that are more autonomous, adaptable, and efficient learners, capable of generalizing robustly to novel environments without excessive human supervision or data annotation burden.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ActiveVLN proposes a two-stage training framework that strategically combines imitation learning (IL) with a novel multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) approach to enable active exploration.

**A. Multi-Turn Paradigm:**
A foundational aspect of ActiveVLN is its adoption of a **multi-turn paradigm** for action generation, contrasting with the common single-turn approach. In the single-turn paradigm, each action `a_t` is predicted solely from the instruction `I` and past observations `V_1:t`. ActiveVLN's multi-turn approach models actions autoregressively, conditioning `a_t` on `I`, past observations `V_1:t`, *and all previous actions `a_1:t-1`*. This means the agent's decision-making process at any step `t` incorporates the entire history of its actions. This paradigm offers several advantages:
1. **Efficient Inference:** It naturally enables KV-cache reuse, which speeds up processing during inference.
2. **Accelerated Training:** Actions within an episode can be packed together, accelerating the training process.
3. **Improved RL Signal Propagation:** Crucially for RL, it allows gradients associated with trajectory outcomes to propagate back to *all* preceding actions within an episode, providing a richer and more consistent learning signal.

**B. ActiveVLN Framework:**

1. **Compact Action Prediction:** The continuous VLN environment typically involves low-level actions (FORWARD, LEFT, RIGHT, STOP). To enhance efficiency and shorten episode lengths, ActiveVLN employs **action chunking**. Instead of predicting a single action at each step, the agent predicts a chunk of up to three future actions at once. This diversifies action granularity and makes training more efficient.

2. **Stage 1: Imitation Learning (IL) for Bootstrapping:**
 - Since MLLMs are not inherently pre-trained for VLN, the initial policy needs to be bootstrapped. ActiveVLN starts with IL, using a *small fraction* of expert demonstrations (~177K trajectories from R2R, R2R-EnvDrop, and RxR). This is significantly less data compared to some IL-based approaches that use millions of samples.
 - The IL objective trains the policy `π_θ` to mimic expert actions, mapping observations and history to low-level actions within each chunk. During this stage, only the connector and language model components of the MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct) are updated, while the vision encoder remains frozen.

3. **Stage 2: Active Exploration via Multi-Turn Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - This is the core contribution. After bootstrapping, the agent transitions to an RL phase where it **actively explores** the environment without relying on expert trajectories.
 - **Interaction Loop:** Given an instruction, the agent iteratively predicts an action (or action chunk), executes it in the Habitat simulator, and appends new observations to its input sequence until a STOP action is issued or an exception occurs. This process generates diverse, self-generated trajectories.
 - **Optimization with GRPO:** For policy optimization, ActiveVLN utilizes **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. GRPO samples a group of candidate trajectories for the same instruction and compares their performance. Trajectories that perform better than the group average are reinforced, while weaker ones are suppressed. This method is chosen for its efficiency, as it avoids the need for a separate value model, unlike PPO. In this stage, all parameters of the MLLM are trainable.
 - **Soft Success Reward:** The RL agent is guided by a **soft success reward** function: `R = 15 * I(success) * (d_goal / 3)`. This reward assigns a value of 15 if the agent successfully stops within 3 meters of the goal (`I(success)=1`), scaled by its geodesic distance to the goal (`d_goal`). This design encourages both reaching the target and making progress towards it when in proximity, promoting effective exploration rather than strict adherence to expert paths. Dynamic sampling (with up to 2 attempts) is employed to ensure at least one successful rollout per group, further boosting efficiency.

**C. Efficiency Enhancements (Engineering Details in RL):**
To address the computational intensity of RL training, several engineering optimizations are integrated:
1. **Dynamic Early-Stopping Strategy:** Unpromising rollouts, characterized by excessive length, are adaptively terminated. A trajectory is stopped if its length exceeds a threshold `T_max = α_roll * |τ*|`, where `|τ*|` is the length of the oracle (expert) trajectory and `α_roll` (set to 2) is a tolerance factor. This prevents wasting computation on likely failed or aimless explorations.
2. **Scene Caching:** Frequently accessed scene data are stored in memory for faster loading when revisited.
3. **Scene Preloading:** Scene loading is pipelined with policy updates to minimize idle time during training.
4. **Decoupled Simulator:** The Habitat simulator is deployed as a standalone HTTP server, allowing scalable and parallel execution of multiple navigation environments, further accelerating data generation.

The overall approach intelligently combines the strong perception and language understanding capabilities of MLLMs with an active, self-improving RL mechanism, supported by crucial engineering optimizations, to tackle the challenges of VLN-CE effectively.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ActiveVLN demonstrates significant advancements in Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN), particularly in its ability to achieve strong performance with greater efficiency and reduced reliance on expert data.

**A. Superior Performance Gains over Baselines:**
- **R2R Val-Unseen Benchmark:** ActiveVLN achieves remarkable improvements over its Imitation Learning (IL) baseline. The Success Rate (SR) on R2R Val-Unseen increases from 38.5% (IL-only) to **50.1% (IL + RL)**, marking an impressive **+11.6% gain**. This substantial improvement surpasses both DAgger-based post-training methods (e.g., NaVid +4.3%, StreamVLN +5.3%) and prior RL-based post-training methods (e.g., VLN-R1 +5.3%). Even when applying DAgger to ActiveVLN's IL baseline, the SR gain is only +6.9%, confirming the superior efficacy of active exploratory RL.
- **RxR Val-Unseen Benchmark:** Similar gains are observed on the RxR benchmark, where incorporating active exploration via RL improves the success rate (SR) by **+9.7%** over the IL baseline.

**B. Competitive Performance with State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Methods:**
- Despite using a smaller model (3B parameters compared to 7B or 8B for other SOTA methods like NaVid, UniNaVid, StreamVLN) and significantly less training data (177K VLN-specific trajectories vs. millions of multi-modal data for some competitors), ActiveVLN achieves competitive performance.
- On R2R Val-Unseen, its 50.1% SR is comparable to, and in some cases surpasses, larger models.
- On RxR Val-Unseen, ActiveVLN achieves the lowest Navigation Error (NE=5.84) and a competitive Success Rate (SR=50.7%) against methods that leverage substantially more diverse training data (e.g., visual question answering, human touring data). This highlights the data-efficiency and strong generalization capabilities of ActiveVLN.
- The slightly lower Success Weighted by Path Length (SPL) on RxR is attributed to active exploration prioritizing autonomous trajectory discovery over strict imitation of expert paths, which might lead to longer but still successful routes.

**C. Ablation Studies Confirm Design Choices:**

1. **Reward Design:**
 - The **soft success reward** (scaled by distance to goal) yields the best overall performance, demonstrating its effectiveness in encouraging both goal achievement and consistent progress.
 - A **hard success reward** (binary success/failure) leads to reduced SR, suggesting that distance normalization is beneficial.
 - **Path alignment rewards** (incorporating nDTW) reduce Navigation Error but also reduce SR, implying that overemphasizing similarity to expert paths can hinder goal completion and diverse pathfinding.

2. **Effect of the Multi-Turn Paradigm:**
 - This is a critical finding. While a single-turn baseline might perform slightly better initially under IL (perhaps due to better alignment with base model pretraining on short video segments), the **multi-turn paradigm demonstrates substantially larger performance improvements after RL (+11.6 SR vs. +0.6 SR for single-turn)**. This confirms that the ability of multi-turn to propagate gradients through action histories is crucial for effective policy optimization in RL for VLN.

3. **Dynamic Early-Stopping Strategy:**
 - This strategy effectively prunes long-tail or likely failed trajectories. Approximately **10% of rollouts are terminated early**, resulting in about a **10% reduction in average rollout time**. This significantly improves training efficiency without sacrificing successful explorations.

4. **Other Optimization Techniques:**
 - **Scene preloading** and **scene caching** also contribute to training efficiency, reducing average rollout time by 4.68% and 2.24% respectively. The collective impact of these engineering details is vital for practical RL training in VLN.

**D. Qualitative Results and Training Dynamics:**
- **Simulation:** Visualizations show that the RL-enhanced policy successfully navigates complex scenarios where an IL-only baseline fails, demonstrating its ability to make correct turns and overcome navigational challenges.
- **Real-World:** ActiveVLN is successfully deployed on a wheeled humanoid robot in diverse real-world environments (offices, hallways, laboratories), validating its practical applicability.
- **Training Dynamics:** Analysis of training metrics reveals that the reward steadily increases, and the success rate on R2R Val-Unseen improves and stabilizes. The average episode length also stabilizes, and the early stop rate confirms the dynamic early-stopping strategy's effectiveness throughout training. Top-down visualizations of rollout trajectories during RL confirm that the agent learns to follow diverse, alternative paths that successfully reach the goal, moving beyond strict imitation.

In summary, ActiveVLN delivers a powerful and efficient framework for VLN, significantly boosting performance through active exploration via multi-turn RL while being data-efficient and achieving competitive results with fewer resources.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ActiveVLN presents a significant contribution to the field of Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) and embodied artificial intelligence, with far-reaching implications and potential impacts:

1. **Paradigm Shift towards Active Exploration:** The most significant contribution is the explicit enablement of active exploration through multi-turn reinforcement learning. This moves VLN agents beyond passive imitation or limited, expert-guided RL, empowering them to discover diverse and robust navigation strategies autonomously. This shift is crucial for developing truly intelligent and adaptable embodied agents that can operate effectively in novel and unpredictable environments.

2. **Reduced Data Dependency and Cost:** ActiveVLN demonstrates that high performance in VLN can be achieved with significantly less reliance on large-scale, costly expert-annotated datasets. By generating its own training data through active interaction, the framework mitigates the "data bottleneck" inherent in imitation learning and DAgger-based approaches. This makes VLN research and development more accessible and scalable, reducing the barriers to entry for new researchers and applications.

3. **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** Learning from diverse, self-generated trajectories, including successes and failures, allows the agent to build a more robust and generalized policy. This directly addresses the problem of covariate shift and leads to improved performance in unseen environments, which is a critical challenge for real-world robotic deployment.

4. **Practical Efficiency in RL Training:** The introduction of a dynamic early-stopping strategy and other engineering optimizations (scene caching, preloading, decoupled simulator) addresses a major practical hurdle in RL: computational cost and training time. By intelligently pruning unpromising rollouts and streamlining simulation interactions, ActiveVLN makes RL training for complex embodied tasks much more feasible and efficient. This advancement could accelerate the development of RL-based solutions across various embodied AI domains.

5. **Validation of Multi-Turn RL for Embodied AI:** The ablation studies clearly demonstrate the critical role of the multi-turn paradigm in the success of RL. By showing that gradients can effectively propagate through long action histories, ActiveVLN provides strong evidence for the utility of this architectural choice in sequential decision-making tasks within embodied AI. This insight can guide future model design for similar complex interaction challenges.

6. **Direct Real-World Applicability:** The successful qualitative demonstrations of ActiveVLN on a real-world wheeled humanoid robot are a strong testament to its practical utility. This bridge from simulation to physical robotics is crucial for validating the robustness and transferability of AI models, indicating that ActiveVLN has the potential to be deployed in actual service robots, autonomous vehicles, or other navigational systems.

7. **Foundation for Future Research:** ActiveVLN provides a solid framework and valuable insights for future research. It opens avenues for exploring more advanced exploration strategies (e.g., intrinsic motivation, curiosity-driven learning), extending the framework to other complex embodied tasks (e.g., object interaction, manipulation), and further refining RL techniques for multimodal agents.

In essence, ActiveVLN pushes VLN towards more autonomous, efficient, and robust learning agents, fundamentally impacting how embodied AI systems are trained and deployed. Its contributions are not just incremental performance gains but represent a step forward in the methodology of developing intelligent navigation capabilities.
