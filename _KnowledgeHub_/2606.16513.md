# Agile Fall Recovery for Quadrotors with Bidirectional Thrust via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> An RL-based framework enables bidirectional-thrust quadrotors to autonomously recover from arbitrary ground-resting attitudes to a stable hover, relying on minimal, noisy onboard sensors and achieving zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with 10/10 successful recoveries in normal indoor tests. The method generalizes to outdoor and disturbed conditions, outperforming a proportional-controller baseline.

## Problem

- Quadrotors lack robust autonomous recovery from arbitrary ground-resting attitudes after collisions or failures in dynamic, unpredictable environments.
- Traditional control methods for quadrotors assume sufficient free space, accurate and continuous state estimation, or rely on extensive, reliable sensor suites, which are not suitable for real-world applications.
- Recovering from ground contact with limited, noisy, and intermittently invalid onboard sensor data (e.g., optical flow, distance sensor) poses a significant challenge for state estimation and control.

## Method

- Developed a ==reinforcement learning== framework using an asymmetric actor-critic architecture with a recurrent (GRU) actor to handle partial observability from minimal sensors.
- Integrated a low-level Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (INDI) controller for robust tracking of policy-generated body rate commands and improved disturbance rejection.
- Leveraged high-fidelity simulation with detailed sensor/motor models and extensive domain randomization (Isaac Lab) to enable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved a **95.04%** success rate in simulation, significantly outperforming baselines and symmetric actor-critic variants.
- Demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a physical quadrotor, achieving 10/10 successful recoveries in normal indoor environments with an average drift of **0.532** m and recovery time of **1.286** s.
- Showed robustness against wind disturbances (8/10 successes) and additional payloads (8/10 successes), and generalized to diverse outdoor terrains, including slopes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An asymmetric actor-critic design with a recurrent actor is critical for learning complex, contact-rich maneuvers from noisy, partially observable sensor inputs.
> - The use of a robust low-level INDI controller significantly improves disturbance rejection and reduces the sim-to-real gap, simplifying the high-level policy's learning task.
> - High-fidelity sensor and motor modeling, combined with extensive domain randomization in simulation, enables successful zero-shot transfer of a fall recovery policy to real quadrotors using minimal sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous fall recovery is an important capability for quadrotors that operate in dynamic and unpredictable environments, where collisions or system failures can lead to the vehicle resting on the ground in an arbitrary orientation. This research addresses a gap in existing quadrotor control strategies by focusing on robust recovery under significant real-world constraints, specifically limited and unreliable onboard sensing, ground contact, and partial observability.

Previous work on automated attitude recovery primarily addresses scenarios such as throw launch, which assumes sufficient free space and favorable initial conditions. In contrast, fall recovery from a ground-resting position requires generating control moments while in contact with the ground, necessitating quadrotors equipped with bidirectional thrust capabilities. While agile flight with bidirectional thrust has been demonstrated, these approaches typically rely on accurate and continuous state estimation. Such estimation is often difficult to achieve with lightweight onboard sensors alone, especially during aggressive maneuvers or when sensors become intermittently invalid.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has advanced quadrotor control, enabling tasks such as flight stabilization, aggressive acrobatics, and high-speed trajectory tracking. However, most RL methods for quadrotors either assume full access to the system state or depend on robust vision-inertial odometry (VIO) for state estimation. A smaller subset of RL research has demonstrated agile flight without explicit state estimation, but these methods generally still require rich visual observations from onboard cameras. The current work differentiates itself by tackling fall recovery using a minimal sensor suite—a downward-facing optical flow sensor and a distance sensor—which provides noisy, partial, and potentially invalid measurements during complex maneuvers. This setting presents a more challenging partially observable control problem compared to prior RL-based quadrotor studies.

The paper positions itself by developing an RL-based framework to enable quadrotors to autonomously recover from arbitrary ground-resting attitudes to a stable hover, addressing the limitations of existing state estimation-dependent or sensor-rich approaches. It extends the applicability of RL to a safety-critical quadrotor task under stringent sensing constraints, drawing inspiration from fall recovery research in legged robotics and the advancements in bidirectional thrust quadrotors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an autonomous fall recovery capability for quadrotors with bidirectional thrust, enabling them to transition from arbitrary ground-resting attitudes to a stable hover. This must be achieved using only lightweight onboard sensors and without relying on explicit state estimation throughout the recovery maneuver.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and practical needs:

- **Broadening Quadrotor Applicability**: In real-world applications such as search and rescue, infrastructure inspection, or logistics, quadrotors are susceptible to unexpected collisions or system failures that can cause them to fall and rest in various attitudes. The ability to autonomously recover from such incidents is crucial for mission continuation and significantly expands the operational envelope of these aerial systems.
- **Challenges of Fall Recovery**: The task of recovering from arbitrary ground attitudes presents multiple difficulties:
 - **Limited Free Space and Ground Contact**: Recovery maneuvers occur in close proximity to the ground or other obstacles, restricting available movement and introducing complex contact dynamics.
 - **Partial Observability**: During aggressive movements and unusual attitudes, conventional sensing systems (e.g., optical flow, distance sensors) may provide unreliable or invalid measurements. This degrades the performance of traditional state estimation pipelines, which often assume continuous and accurate sensor data.
 - **Lightweight Sensing Constraints**: Practical quadrotor deployments often prioritize minimal, lightweight, and inexpensive sensing suites, which inherently offer less redundancy and reliability compared to more complex sensor arrays. This exacerbates the partial observability problem and makes reliance on robust, continuous state estimation fragile.
 - **Bidirectional Thrust Requirement**: Recovery from inverted or otherwise challenging ground attitudes necessitates propellers capable of generating thrust in both directions. This capability introduces additional control complexity compared to conventional unidirectional thrust systems.
- **Limitations of Prior Approaches**: Existing quadrotor control methods either focus on scenarios with ample free space, rely on accurate and continuous state estimation, or demand more extensive sensing capabilities. These limitations render them unsuitable for the constrained, partially observable, and sensor-limited context of autonomous fall recovery.

The authors are motivated to develop a robust solution that can operate under these challenging conditions, leveraging reinforcement learning to learn complex, contact-rich behaviors directly from sensor observations, thus mitigating the need for a separate, potentially unreliable, state estimation module.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an RL-based framework for fall recovery, designed for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The approach integrates an asymmetric actor-critic architecture for policy learning, a low-level Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (INDI) controller, and high-fidelity simulation components, including motor dynamics and optical flow sensor models.

**Policy Learning:**
The problem is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), with the agent interacting with the environment through incomplete and noisy observations. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training.

- **Action Space**: The policy outputs a four-dimensional action `a_t` at each time step `t`. This action consists of the mass-normalized collective thrust `c_t` and the desired body-rate setpoint `Ω_des_t`. This Collective Thrust and Body Rate (CTBR) interface is designed for agile quadrotor flight and does not require explicit global position or velocity estimates.
- **Observation Space (Actor)**: The actor receives a 19-dimensional observation vector `o_t` composed of:
 - **Exteroceptive Observations**: Distance sensor reading `d_t`, optical-flow rate `f_t` (2D), and binary masks `m_d_t`, `m_f_t` indicating sensor validity.
 - **Proprioceptive Observations**: Body-frame linear acceleration `ā_t` (3D), angular velocity `Ω_t` (3D), the normalized gravity vector projected into the body frame `g_proj_t` (3D), and the previous action `a_t-1` (4D).
 - **Target Information**: The target hover height `h_des` (1D).
 Crucially, all components of `o_t` are derived directly from onboard sensors, enabling direct sim-to-real transfer without an external state estimation module.
- **Privileged State Space (Critic)**: To enhance training stability under partial observability, an asymmetric actor-critic architecture is used. The critic receives a 33-dimensional privileged state vector `s_t`, which includes all actor observations plus ground-truth information such as relative position `p_t`, body-frame linear velocity `v_t`, actions from the previous three time steps `a_hist_t`, and simulated random external force `F_t` and moment `M_t`.
- **Rewards**: A dense reward function `r_t` guides learning, comprising several components:
 - `r_hover`: Encourages the quadrotor to reach the target hover position and penalizes lateral drift.
 - `r_angle`: Promotes a near-upright attitude.
 - `r_sign`: Penalizes frequent reversals in thrust direction.
 - `r_contact`: Imposes a penalty for ground contact.
 - `r_smooth`: Regularizes control behavior by penalizing large linear/angular velocities and abrupt changes in action.
- **Network Architecture**:
 - **Actor**: A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with a hidden dimension of 64 processes the observation history, followed by a two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) to output the action. The GRU aids in inferring latent state information and smoothing actions in a partially observable setting.
 - **Critic**: A three-layer MLP is used, as it directly accesses the privileged state and does not require recurrent memory.

**High-Fidelity Simulation:**
Isaac Lab serves as the training platform. A 3D model of the quadrotor is used, with physical parameters (mass, center of mass, inertia) extracted from CAD.

- **Optical-Flow Simulation**: The optical flow sensor model accounts for theoretical flow rate induced by rotational and translational motion, compensates for discrepancies between IMU and optical flow, simulates sensing delay, and models invalid readings during high-speed motion. Noise proportional to angular velocity and inversely proportional to distance, as well as random dropout, are added to mimic real-world sensor imperfections.
- **INDI Controller**: An Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (INDI) controller is employed as the low-level controller in both simulation and real-world. It tracks the desired body rates from the policy by computing desired angular accelerations (using a proportional controller) and then desired control moments. These are converted to individual motor RPM setpoints using inverse quadrotor dynamics. The controller supports bidirectional thrust by determining motor rotation direction based on the sign of the collective thrust, which also helps reduce motor overheating from frequent reversals.
- **Motor Responses**: Motor dynamics are modeled as a first-order system with distinct time constants for acceleration and deceleration, incorporating a one-step communication delay and maximum motor slew rate. A fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) scheme integrates these dynamics.
- **Domain Randomization**: To improve sim-to-real transfer, various parameters are randomized during training. This includes quadrotor dynamics (thrust/moment coefficients, inertia), observations (noise to actor inputs and controller feedback), and external disturbances (random forces and moments). This strategy encourages the policy to be robust to model mismatch and unmodeled real-world phenomena.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presented findings from both simulation ablation studies and real-world experiments, evaluating the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework.

**Simulation Experiments (Ablation Studies):**
A set of ablation studies was conducted in simulation to evaluate key design choices using metrics such as success rate ($E_{succ}$), drift distance ($E_{drift}$), recovery time ($E_{time}$), and smoothness ($E_{smth}$). Policies were trained over 3000 epochs (approx. 6 million simulation steps) and evaluated over 5000 episodes.

- **Overall Performance**: The proposed method demonstrated robust performance, achieving a 95.04% success rate, an average drift distance of 0.35 m, a recovery time of 1.26 s, and a smoothness score of 0.26.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic**: The symmetric actor-critic variant yielded a success rate of only 7.00%, underscoring the importance of providing privileged information to the critic for stable learning in partially observable environments.
- **Recurrent Critic**: A variant where the critic also used a recurrent network performed slightly worse (89.32% success), suggesting that recurrence is not essential for the critic when full-state information is available.
- **MLP Actor**: Replacing the GRU in the actor with an MLP resulted in slower recovery times and less smooth control (86.56% success, 1.48 s recovery time, 0.35 smoothness), validating the GRU's role in filtering noisy sensory inputs and promoting consistent actions.
- **INDI Controller**: The proportional-controller baseline, which lacked the INDI controller, underperformed the proposed method (89.14% success), indicating that the INDI controller contributes to disturbance rejection and simplifies the learning task for the high-level policy.
- **Action Space**: Directly commanding motor RPMs instead of CTBR (RPM action variant) led to substantially worse performance (69.18% success), highlighting the challenge of controlling highly nonlinear dynamics with direct motor commands under partial observability.

**Real-World Experiments (Indoor and Outdoor):**
Experiments were conducted on a physical quadrotor platform equipped with lightweight sensors (Upixel T1 optical-flow module) and a Jetson Orin NX for policy inference. The policy ran at 50 Hz, and the low-level controller at 200 Hz.

- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The learned policy successfully transferred to real hardware without additional training. It demonstrated recovery from various initial attitudes (upright and inverted) to a stable hover in indoor environments.
- **Robustness to Disturbances**:
 - **Normal Conditions**: The proposed method achieved 10/10 successful recoveries, with an average drift distance of 0.532 m and a recovery time of 1.286 s. The proportional-controller baseline achieved 8/10 successes and higher drift/time.
 - **Wind Disturbance**: Under wind conditions, the proposed method achieved 8/10 successes, maintaining reasonable drift (0.839 m) and recovery time (1.243 s). The proportional controller again showed degraded performance (7/10 successes, 1.638 m drift).
 - **Additional Payload**: With an extra payload, the proposed method maintained 8/10 successes, with an average drift of 0.858 m and recovery time of 1.426 s. The proportional controller’s performance significantly dropped to 4/10 successes.
- **INDI Controller Validation**: Across all real-world tests, the proposed method consistently outperformed the proportional-controller baseline, indicating that the INDI controller effectively compensates for real-world model mismatch and disturbances, thereby reducing the sim-to-real gap.
- **Outdoor Generalization**: The policy demonstrated generalization in outdoor environments, successfully recovering from different initial attitudes and on diverse ground surfaces, including sloped terrain not explicitly included in the simulation training. This suggests the policy learned fundamental recovery dynamics rather than environment-specific behaviors.
- **Simulation-to-Real Discrepancy**: The largest difference between simulation and real-world results was observed in the drift distance, which the authors attributed to the reliability of optical-flow measurements in certain real-world conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for autonomous fall recovery of bidirectional-thrust quadrotors, which operates under constraints representative of real-world scenarios. The findings indicate that agile quadrotor fall recovery is feasible using limited, noisy, and intermittently unreliable onboard sensing without requiring explicit state estimation throughout the maneuver.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Enhanced Quadrotor Autonomy and Robustness**: By enabling quadrotors to recover from arbitrary ground-resting attitudes, this work increases the robustness and autonomy of these aerial platforms. This capability is critical for missions in unstructured or hazardous environments where unexpected impacts or failures are possible, thus reducing mission failure rates and improving operational safety.
- **Practicality with Minimal Sensing**: The reliance solely on lightweight and common onboard sensors (optical flow and distance sensor) makes the approach cost-effective and applicable to a broad range of existing and future quadrotor systems. This avoids the need for heavy, power-intensive, or expensive sensor suites typically associated with robust state estimation.
- **Advancements in Reinforcement Learning for Robotics**: The research demonstrates the effectiveness of combining specific RL architectural elements (asymmetric actor-critic with recurrent actor) with high-fidelity simulation and robust low-level control (INDI controller) for achieving zero-shot sim-to-real transfer in a complex, contact-rich, and partially observable control problem. This contributes to the understanding of how to design RL systems for real-world robotic deployment.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap**: The comprehensive use of high-fidelity sensor and motor models, coupled with extensive domain randomization, provides a methodical approach to reducing the simulation-to-real-world performance gap, a persistent challenge in robotics. The validation of zero-shot transfer on physical hardware supports the effectiveness of this methodology.
- **Open-Source Contribution**: The authors' commitment to open-sourcing their code is valuable for the research community, providing a platform for further development and comparative studies in autonomous aerial recovery and RL-based quadrotor control.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The primary limitation identified is the sensitivity of recovery performance to the reliability of optical-flow measurements, particularly in low-texture or poorly illuminated environments. Future research directions include exploring more robust multimodal sensing and learning strategies to mitigate these sensor limitations. Additionally, the authors plan to investigate recovery in more cluttered environments and on more complex terrains, which would introduce further challenges in contact dynamics and obstacle avoidance.
