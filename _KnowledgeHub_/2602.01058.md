# Good SFT Optimizes for SFT, Better SFT Prepares for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign introduced PEAR, a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) reweighting method, demonstrating that optimizing SFT to prepare for subsequent reinforcement learning (RL) significantly improves post-RL performance in reasoning tasks. This approach consistently outperformed standard SFT, with models showing up to a 40% absolute accuracy gain on logic games and substantial improvements in mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Current Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for Large Language Models (LLMs) is often optimized in isolation, assuming offline SFT performance directly translates to better final performance after Reinforcement Learning (RL).
- This assumption is frequently flawed, as empirical evidence shows "stronger" SFT checkpoints can lead to significantly worse post-RL outcomes, indicating a critical misalignment.
- A distribution mismatch exists between SFT, which learns from a fixed "behavior policy" (offline data), and online RL, which optimizes an evolving "target policy" through dynamic rollouts, leading to inefficient or suboptimal learning.

## Method

- Developed ==PEAR (Policy Evaluation–inspired Algorithm for Offline Learning Loss Reweighting)==, an SFT-stage method designed to correct the distribution mismatch between offline SFT data and the evolving target policy during online RL.
- PEAR computes ==token log-likelihood ratios== between the current target policy and the behavior policy, then aggregates these into numerically stabilized sequence-level, token-level (suffix-based), or block-level weights.
- The per-token training loss is then multiplied by these reweighted coefficients, guiding SFT to better align with ==trajectories that online RL will actually explore==, with minimal computational overhead.

## Results

- PEAR-initialized models consistently achieved superior post-RL performance across various models and tasks, showing up to a **40%** absolute accuracy improvement on SynLogic games for Qwen3-1.7B-Base over standard SFT.
- On mathematical reasoning benchmarks, PEAR yielded significant gains, including Pass@8 improvements of up to **14.6%** on AIME-25 for Qwen3-4B-Base, and an impressive **50%** gain on AIME-24 Pass@64 for DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-1.5B.
- PEAR is a versatile plug-in, compatible with NLL and KL-based knowledge distillation, and its benefits transfer even when subsequent online RL is performed on a different task distribution, enhancing initialization robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Offline SFT performance is an unreliable predictor of downstream RL effectiveness; optimizing SFT in isolation can lead to "rank reversals" where offline gains do not translate to, or even hinder, online performance.
> - Explicitly preparing SFT for the subsequent online RL stage by addressing the distribution mismatch is crucial for achieving more efficient and robust overall LLM post-training.
> - PEAR fosters an "RL-aware initialization" by concentrating learning on "high-value" tokens and aligning offline gradients more closely with online RL updates, resulting in less parameter drift during the RL phase.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of reasoning large language models (LLMs) typically involves a two-stage post-training paradigm. This process begins with an offline supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage, which provides an initial model checkpoint. Subsequently, an online reinforcement learning (RL) stage is applied to further enhance the model's capabilities. Both SFT and RL for LLMs are active areas of research, with ongoing efforts to improve their respective objectives and methodologies.

A significant body of existing work focuses on developing offline learning objectives to enhance SFT, often through mechanisms such as reweighting or regularizing next-token likelihood. These techniques primarily aim to maximize performance within the SFT stage itself. The implicit assumption in much of this research is that improvements observed in offline SFT performance will directly translate to better final model performance after the subsequent RL stage.

However, recent studies have begun to question this assumption, indicating that factors such as data repetition and homogeneity, while boosting SFT, might limit the headroom for subsequent RL improvement. This paper situates itself within this emerging discussion by empirically investigating the relationship between SFT-stage performance and final post-RL performance. It identifies a potential mismatch in current SFT–RL pipelines where SFT is optimized in isolation, potentially leading to suboptimal outcomes for the complete post-training process. The work aims to bridge this gap by developing an SFT-stage method that considers the downstream RL phase, thereby contributing to a more holistic approach to LLM post-training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an SFT-stage method that optimizes LLM initializations for subsequent online reinforcement learning (RL), rather than solely for SFT performance in isolation. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

**The Discrepancy Between Offline and Online Performance:**
The paper observes that the practical performance of interest for reasoning LLMs is typically the model's accuracy after completing both SFT and the downstream RL phase. However, conventional SFT techniques are often designed to maximize SFT-stage performance. The authors empirically demonstrate that a stronger SFT checkpoint, based on its offline performance, can paradoxically lead to significantly poorer performance after identical RL training when compared to models initialized from weaker SFT checkpoints. This phenomenon suggests that offline SFT gains are not consistently indicative of, and can even be counterproductive to, strong final performance after RL. Figure 1 in the paper illustrates this by showing significant ranking changes between offline and online pass rates across various SFT objectives, indicating a lack of reliable correlation.

**The Distribution Mismatch Problem:**
The authors attribute this performance discrepancy to a fundamental distribution mismatch between the offline SFT stage and the online RL stage.
- **Offline SFT:** During SFT, the model learns from a static dataset of prompt-response pairs. This data is generated by a "behavior policy" (π_β), which is the data-generating process. SFT uniformly applies token-level supervision based on prefixes induced by this behavior policy.
- **Online RL:** In contrast, during online RL, the model (the "target policy," π_θ) learns from its own rollouts. The target policy continuously evolves, and the data it samples for learning comes from its own current distribution.
This leads to a "behavior–target occupancy mismatch." As the target policy (π_θ) deviates from the behavior policy (π_β) through training, the data sampled by π_β during SFT may over-represent continuations or trajectories that are unlikely to be revisited or generated by the evolving π_θ during online RL. Training uniformly on these incongruent data points can reinforce patterns that are not useful for the online RL process, potentially leading to inefficient learning or even hindering improvement. This issue is particularly pronounced in long-form reasoning tasks, where small early mismatches can compound over long horizons.

**Primary Research Goal:**
Given this observed discrepancy and the underlying distribution mismatch, the key motivation is to design an offline SFT method that explicitly addresses the challenge of preparing the model for the subsequent online RL stage. The objective is not to achieve the highest possible SFT accuracy, but to provide an initialization that effectively facilitates and accelerates improvement under online RL. This involves correcting the distribution mismatch to ensure that offline training better reflects the trajectories and decision patterns that online RL will actually explore and optimize.

### 3. Methodology and Approach: PEAR

To address the identified offline-to-online distribution mismatch and better prepare models for reinforcement learning (RL), the authors propose PEAR (Policy Evaluation–inspired Algorithm for Offline Learning Loss Reweighting). PEAR is an SFT-stage method that reweights the standard offline training loss using importance sampling, inspired by off-policy evaluation (OPE) techniques.

**Overview of PEAR:**
PEAR aims to ensure that offline training prioritizes learning from trajectories that are more likely to be generated by the evolving target policy (π_θ) during online RL, rather than uniformly treating all data generated by the behavior policy (π_β). It achieves this by scaling each token's loss according to how likely the target policy would generate the same continuation relative to the behavior policy. PEAR augments standard SFT objectives without changing the underlying loss function (e.g., NLL or KL-based distillation), only modifying how each token's loss is weighted.

The overall approach involves three steps:
1. Compute token log-likelihood ratios between the target policy and the behavior policy for tokens in the offline dataset.
2. Aggregate these ratios into weights using one of three proposed variants and apply numerical stabilization.
3. Weigh the loss for each token based on these computed weights.

**Weighted Offline Training Objective:**
The core PEAR objective is defined as:
$L_{PEAR}(θ) \triangleq E_{(x,y)∼D} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{sg}[\hat{G}_t] \cdot \ell_θ (x, y_{<t}, y_t) \right]$
where $\ell_θ (x, y_{<t}, y_t)$ is the per-token training loss (e.g., negative log-likelihood or KL divergence), $\hat{G}_t$ is the numerically stabilized importance weight for token $t$, and $\text{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes stopping gradients through the weights, treating them as fixed coefficients.

**Weighting Strategies:**
PEAR offers three variants for computing the importance weights $G_t$, differing in their granularity:

1. **Sequence-Level Weighting:**
 This is the simplest form. For each token $y_t$, a probability ratio $\Delta_t = \frac{\pi_θ(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_β(y_t | x, y_{<t})}$ is defined. The sequence-level importance ratio is the product of these individual token ratios across the entire sequence: $w_{1:T} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \Delta_t$. This weight is then applied uniformly to every token in the trajectory, i.e., $G_t = w_{1:T}$ for all $t \in [T]$. This approach aims to estimate the loss under the target policy's distribution by reweighting entire sequences.

2. **Token-Level Weighting Based on Continuation (Suffix-Based):**
 This variant takes a more granular view, acknowledging that "unlikeliness" might stem from specific implausible regions within a sequence. For a token $y_t$, the method evaluates the plausibility of the *continuation* (suffix $y_{>t}$) under the target policy conditioned on taking $y_t$. If the relative plausibility is low, the loss on that token is down-weighted. This focuses offline updates on prefixes whose dataset continuations are compatible with the current policy. The weight $G_t$ is defined as a discounted product of future token ratios: $G_t = \gamma^{T-t} \prod_{j=t+1}^{T} \Delta_j$, where $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ is a discount factor for variance control over long horizons. This approach is inspired by suffix-ratio importance sampling in OPE.

3. **Block-Level Weighting to Improve Stability:**
 To mitigate the high variance often associated with products of ratios over long sequences, a block-level variant is introduced. The sequence positions $\{1, \dots, T\}$ are partitioned into $K = \lceil T/B \rceil$ contiguous blocks $\{I_k\}$, each of length at most $B$. For each block $k$, the product of token-level ratios within the block is $\Delta_{\text{blk},k} = \prod_{t \in I_k} \Delta_t$. The importance ratio of the suffix *after* block $k$ is $S_k = \prod_{m=k+1}^{K} \Delta_{\text{blk},m}$. Every token within a block $k$ is assigned the same discounted continuation weight $G_t = G_{\text{blk},k} = \gamma^{T-e_k} S_k$, where $e_k$ is the last index of block $k$. When $B=1$, this recovers the token-level (suffix-based) weighting. Intermediate block sizes are intended to balance granularity with stability.

**Optional Incorporating Negative Examples:**
When the offline dataset includes verified failures ($D^−$), PEAR can optionally add a repulsive term to discourage the imitation of these negative trajectories. A sequence-level weight $\hat{G}_t^−$ is computed for negative trajectories, and a term $L_{\text{neg}}(θ) = E_{(x,y^−)∼D^−} \left[ -\lambda \cdot \text{sg}[\hat{G}_t^−] \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_θ (x, y^−_{<t}, y^−_t) \right]$ is added to the objective. This performs gradient ascent to push the model away from negative responses, weighted by their trajectory-level importance.

**Numerical Stabilization:**
For practical implementation, importance weights are computed in log-space to prevent underflow/overflow from products over long sequences. Clipping is applied to both per-decision log-ratios ($\log \Delta_t$) and the final log-weights ($\log \hat{G}_t$) to further enhance numerical stability and bound variance. Algorithm 1 in the paper details the procedure, including clipping bounds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors across various LLM architectures and tasks demonstrate that PEAR consistently improves post-RL performance, supporting the hypothesis that SFT should prepare models for subsequent RL rather than optimizing for offline SFT metrics in isolation.

**Improved Post-RL Performance:**
- **Consistent Gains:** PEAR, particularly its token-level variant (PEAR$_{B=1}$), consistently improved Pass@1 and Pass@8 metrics after identical online RL training (using GRPO) compared to models initialized with standard SFT.
- **Significant Absolute Gains:** On SynLogic logic games, PEAR increased Pass@1 by up to +25 percentage points (pp) (Qwen3-1.7B-Base, 13.1% → 38.3%). On mathematical reasoning tasks, PEAR achieved gains of up to +30 pp Pass@8 on AIME-2025 (DS-Qwen-1.5B, 5% → 35%).
- **Beyond SFT-Only Optimization:** The paper notes that PEAR's strength is not reflected in its standalone offline SFT performance, which might not even surpass canonical SFT. This aligns with its design to optimize for downstream RL readiness.

**Comparison with Other SFT Objectives and Baselines:**
- **Failure of Offline-Only Optimization:** Experiments with a spectrum of SFT objectives (including KL-regularized NLL, probability-based methods like TopP/BottomP/TopLogP/BottomLogP, and TALR) showed that models with higher offline scores frequently underperformed or even ranked lower after identical RL training compared to models initialized with weaker offline performance. This empirical finding reinforces the core motivation of the study.
- **Superiority over One-Step Weighting:** Baselines that used myopic, single-step importance weighting (only based on the probability ratio of the current token) were less effective than PEAR, which considers the plausibility of the *entire continuation*. This highlights the importance of future-oriented weighting for online RL readiness.
- **Outperformance against Concurrent IS-style Baselines:** PEAR consistently outperformed other importance-sampling-style SFT methods like DFT and Proximal-SFT across both math reasoning and logic game benchmarks, suggesting that PEAR's specific formulation of continuation-based reweighting is more effective.
- **Compatibility with KD:** PEAR's reweighting scheme was shown to be compatible with KL-based knowledge distillation, further improving performance when applied to this objective.

**Transferability to Different RL Task Distributions and Algorithms:**
- **Cross-Domain Transfer:** PEAR-initialized models maintained their advantage even when the online RL was conducted on a different task distribution (e.g., offline training on SynLogic followed by online RL on Enigmata). This indicates that PEAR fosters robust capabilities rather than being overfit to the offline domain.
- **Algorithm Agnostic Benefits:** The benefits of PEAR were not specific to the GRPO RL algorithm. When GRPO was replaced with DAPO (another RLVR algorithm), PEAR-initialized models still significantly outperformed SFT-initialized models.

**Effectiveness with Black-Box Proxy Policies:**
- PEAR requires access to the behavior policy (π_β) for computing importance weights. The research demonstrated that PEAR remains effective even when π_β is approximated using a proxy model (e.g., a different Qwen3 variant) or an ensemble of proxies, outperforming standard SFT+GRPO. The performance correlated with the Pearson correlation between the proxy's token probabilities and the true behavior model.

**Role of Negative Examples:**
- Incorporating negative trajectories with a repulsive term in PEAR's objective, under the same offline data budget, yielded additional gains in post-RL performance, suggesting that explicitly discouraging undesirable behaviors can further improve initialization.

**Analytical Insights:**
- **Concentration on Important Positions:** Analysis showed that PEAR's learning concentrates on tokens that evaluate to larger weights, and these high-value tokens exhibited greater KL divergence from the base policy. This indicates that PEAR systematically steers the model's behavior on important locations influencing the suffix.
- **Reduced Parameter Drift in RL:** PEAR-initialized models exhibited the smallest parameter drift (measured by Normalized Spectrum Shift, NSS) during subsequent online RL training compared to other initializations. This suggests that PEAR effectively reduces the offline-to-online mismatch, requiring fewer parameter updates to correct misalignments during the RL phase, with the heavy lifting occurring in the SFT stage.
- **Gradient Alignment:** The average principal angle between PEAR's gradients and those for GRPO was smaller than for SFT and other variants, suggesting that PEAR's offline updates are more consistent with the online GRPO learning direction.
- **Beyond Conservatism:** The paper notes that while some SFT methods focus on stability (e.g., smaller KL divergence, sparser updates) to the base model, these metrics of conservatism do not always correlate with better post-RL performance. PEAR, despite potentially inducing more aggressive KL drifts and denser updates in the offline stage, performs better by correctly steering the policy for RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of large language model (LLM) post-training, particularly for reasoning models. Its primary significance lies in shifting the paradigm for evaluating and designing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) from an isolated objective to a component of a larger, holistic post-training pipeline.

**Reframing SFT's Role:** The core finding—that strong SFT-stage performance is an unreliable, and potentially misleading, proxy for final post-RL performance—challenges conventional wisdom in LLM development. This implies that SFT should be conceptualized not as an end in itself, but as a critical initialization step specifically engineered to facilitate efficient and effective subsequent reinforcement learning. This reframing can guide future research and development efforts in LLM fine-tuning.

**Addressing the Offline-to-Online Mismatch:** PEAR directly addresses the fundamental distribution mismatch between offline SFT data and online RL policy rollouts. By leveraging importance weighting inspired by off-policy evaluation, PEAR ensures that the SFT process focuses on learning behaviors and continuations that are relevant and plausible under the evolving target policy in RL. This mechanism is crucial for mitigating issues like error propagation and inefficient learning in long-form reasoning tasks.

**Practical Performance Improvements:** The consistent and substantial performance gains demonstrated by PEAR across diverse reasoning tasks (logic games, mathematical reasoning) and LLM scales are of practical importance. Gains of up to 30 percentage points in Pass@8 on challenging benchmarks like AIME-2025 highlight PEAR's potential to significantly improve the capabilities of reasoning LLMs without requiring changes to the more computationally intensive online RL phase.

**Computational Efficiency in Post-Training:** By providing a stronger and more aligned initialization, PEAR can lead to more efficient online RL. The analytical findings, such as reduced parameter drift during RL, suggest that PEAR-initialized models require less "correction" during the online phase, potentially shortening RL training times and reducing computational costs. This is particularly relevant as RL training for LLMs, especially with complex reward models or tool-use environments, can be very expensive.

**Generality and Robustness:** PEAR's effectiveness across different model families (Qwen, DeepSeek), various RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO), and even with approximated behavior policies (black-box PEAR) indicates its robustness and broad applicability. Its compatibility with existing SFT objectives (NLL, KL-based distillation) makes it a versatile plug-in approach for improving current post-training pipelines.

**Future Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for further research into understanding and optimizing the interplay between offline and online learning stages. It encourages deeper investigation into what constitutes an "RL-ready" SFT checkpoint and how to design offline objectives that proactively shape models for specific downstream RL characteristics, including potentially those with learned reward models (RLHF).

In summary, this paper provides both an empirical validation of a critical challenge in LLM post-training and a principled, effective solution. PEAR represents a concrete step towards more holistically designed LLM fine-tuning pipelines that consider the entire learning trajectory, ultimately leading to more capable and efficiently trained reasoning models.
