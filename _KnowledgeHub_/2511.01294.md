# Kinematify: Open-Vocabulary Synthesis of High-DoF Articulated Objects

> [!summary] Summary
> Kinematify is a framework that automates the generation of kinematics-aware articulated 3D object models from static RGB images or text, even for high-degree-of-freedom and multi-branched structures. It achieves a 2.92° average axis angle error on everyday objects and reduces the Tree Edit Distance to 1.32 on robotic platforms, producing models suitable for real-world robot manipulation.

## Problem

- Creating detailed kinematic models for high-DoF articulated objects, such as URDFs, requires substantial manual effort.
- Existing high-fidelity methods rely on motion sequences or predefined articulation priors (limiting applicability to static inputs/novel objects), and prior program-synthesis approaches struggle with the complexity of high-DoF or multi-branched kinematic structures.
- There is a need for physically consistent and functionally valid articulated models derived from static inputs for robot interaction and simulation.

## Method

- The framework begins by generating ==part-aware 3D segmented meshes== and their implicit ==Signed Distance Functions (SDFs)== from RGB images or textual descriptions.
- A ==Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)== algorithm is employed to infer the ==kinematic topology==, transforming an undirected connection graph into a directed tree using a ==multi-term reward function==.
- Joint types are predicted using a Vision-Language Model, and precise joint parameters (pivot, axis) are estimated through a novel ==Distance-Weighted Contact-Aware Virtual Linkage (DW-CAVL)== optimization that minimizes separation and penetration under virtual motion.

## Results

- Achieved an average axis angle error of **2.92°** for everyday objects and **16.06°** for robotic platforms, significantly outperforming previous methods.
- Reduced the Tree Edit Distance on robotic platforms to an average of **1.32**, accurately recovering complex multi-branched kinematic structures like those in humanoid robots.
- Generated kinematic models were successfully deployed in both simulation and real-world robot manipulation tasks (e.g., drawer opening, pouring), demonstrating physical consistency and direct usability for online planning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining part-aware 3D geometry with an MCTS-driven topological search effectively infers complex, multi-branched kinematic structures, even without explicit motion data.
> - The DW-CAVL objective, leveraging differentiable rigid motions and weighted SDF evaluations, can accurately infer joint parameters from static contact regions by optimizing for virtual motion consistency and collision avoidance.
> - The MCTS reward function's emphasis on structural regularity, static stability, contact strength, and symmetry is crucial for resolving ambiguities in high-DoF kinematic tree inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of articulated object models with accurate kinematic structures is a recognized challenge in robotics and computer vision. These models, often represented in formats like Unified Robot Description Format (URDF), are fundamental for tasks such as robot manipulation, locomotion, physical simulation, motion planning, and policy learning. However, their creation, particularly for objects possessing a high number of degrees of freedom (DoF), typically requires substantial manual effort.

Previous research in this domain can be broadly categorized into two main approaches:

- **Geometry-first approaches:** These methods infer parts and joints by analyzing dense 4D sequences or multiple 3D scans. Examples include MultiBodySync and ReArt, which achieve high fidelity by observing object motion over time to infer part segmentation and joint parameters. However, a significant limitation of these methods is their reliance on controlled capture settings and the availability of multi-view or temporal motion data. For instance, AutoURDF extended this concept to robot self-modeling using point-cloud sequences but primarily focused on serial-chain structures and also presumed motion data.
- **Program-synthesis pipelines:** This category involves predicting executable descriptions directly from visual inputs, often utilizing large language models or transformers. URDFormer, Real2Code, and Articulate-Anything are examples that generate code-based representations or URDFs from images. While these approaches offer open-vocabulary capabilities and reduce reliance on motion data, they often encounter difficulties with objects exhibiting complex kinematic dependencies, such as multi-branched linkages, and are frequently limited to everyday objects with a few simple moving parts.

Kinematify positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of existing methods. It aims to generate articulated 3D object models directly from static RGB images or textual descriptions, without requiring motion data or extensive hand-curated datasets. This work specifically targets the accurate inference of kinematic topologies and joint parameters for high-DoF and multi-branched objects, areas where prior program-synthesis methods typically struggle, while overcoming the motion data dependency of geometry-first approaches. By combining structural inference with geometry-driven optimization, Kinematify seeks to provide a more automated and versatile solution for generating physically consistent and functionally valid articulated models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automated framework, named Kinematify, capable of synthesizing articulated 3D objects from arbitrary RGB images or textual descriptions in a zero-shot context. This overarching goal is driven by the recognized difficulties in creating detailed kinematic models, especially for high-DoF systems, which are essential for advanced robotic applications.

The motivation for Kinematify stems from several core challenges and limitations identified in current approaches:

- **High Manual Effort for Articulated Object Modeling:** Customizing articulated object descriptions, such as URDFs, for robots and complex objects with many DoF is a labor-intensive process. This involves intricate part-aware 3D modeling, resolving complex kinematic dependencies, and inferring precise joint parameters. The manual nature of this work creates a bottleneck for rapidly deploying and experimenting with new robotic systems or interacting with novel objects.
- **Dependency on Motion Sequences or Strong Assumptions:** Existing high-fidelity methods often require observing an object in motion (4D sequences or multi-scan data) to infer its articulation. This necessitates controlled capture environments and the availability of temporal data, which are not always feasible or accessible. For static inputs (single images or text), current methods frequently rely on strong assumptions or pre-defined articulation priors, limiting their adaptability to novel, open-vocabulary objects.
- **Inadequacy for High-DoF and Multi-branched Structures:** While some program-synthesis approaches can generate articulated models from visual inputs, they often target simpler objects with few moving parts and struggle with the complexity of high-DoF systems or objects with multi-branched kinematic structures (e.g., humanoid robots, multi-limbed systems). These methods may fail to accurately infer the intricate hierarchical relationships and dependencies.
- **Need for Physically Consistent and Functionally Valid Models:** For robot interaction and simulation, the generated models must be not only geometrically accurate but also kinematically correct and physically consistent (e.g., avoiding self-collisions under virtual motion, stable joint placements). Ensuring this validity from static inputs presents a significant challenge.

To address these motivations, Kinematify focuses on two core challenges:

1. **Inferring Kinematic Topologies for High-DoF Objects:** This involves determining the parent-child relationships and the overall structure of articulated parts without explicit motion data.
2. **Estimating Joint Parameters from Static 3D Geometry:** This entails accurately predicting joint types (e.g., revolute, prismatic) and their precise parameters (e.g., pivot points, axes) purely from the static 3D geometry of the object.

By tackling these challenges, Kinematify aims to provide a more general, automated, and robust solution for generating articulated object descriptions that are suitable for real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Kinematify is presented as a framework for generating kinematics-aware articulated objects from RGB images or textual descriptions in a zero-shot manner. The methodology consists of three main stages: Part-Aware 3D Representations, Kinematic Topology Inference, and Joint Reasoning.

#### 3.1. A. Part-Aware 3D Representations

The process begins by acquiring a segmented 3D mesh of the target object.
1. **Input Processing**: Given RGB images or textual descriptions, a part-aware 3D foundation model (e.g., BANG) is utilized to generate a segmented mesh of the object. Meshes with insufficient vertices or degenerate spatial spread are discarded.
2. **SDF Generation**: For each prospective parent part, a continuous Signed Distance Function (SDF) is trained. This SDF represents the implicit surface geometry of the part, trained on noisy surface points, near-surface offsets, and far samples.
3. **Connection Graph Construction**: An undirected connection graph `G` is built where nodes represent individual parts. An edge is added between two parts if their mutual minimum bidirectional distance, evaluated using their respective SDFs, falls below a specified tolerance. This graph identifies potential physical contacts and relationships between parts.

#### 3.2. B. Kinematic Topology Inference

The next stage involves orienting the connection graph `G` into a directed kinematic tree `T`, rooted at a selected base link.
1. **Base Selection and BFS Orientation**: The base link `b` is chosen as the node in `G` with the highest undirected degree. A Breadth-First Search (BFS) starting from `b` provides a warm-start for the kinematic tree. During BFS, when a new neighbor `v` is reached from a visited node `u`, `u` is designated as the parent, `v` as the child, and the edge `u → v` is added to `T`. Edges forming cycles are not added and are recorded as "broken." Disconnected components in `G` are attached to the current tree by adding virtual edges to their nearest neighbors within a distance threshold `d_max`.
2. **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)**: An MCTS algorithm is employed to refine the kinematic tree structure, particularly for high-DoF objects with ambiguous attachments and multiple branches.
 - **State, Actions, Constraints**: A search state `S` includes the current partial directed tree `T_S`, the set of visited nodes `V_S`, and broken undirected edges `B_S`. An action involves adding a feasible oriented edge `u → v` where `u` is visited and `v` is unvisited. A constraint is applied to forbid connecting two nodes within the same multi-member symmetry cluster to prevent spurious intra-cluster links.
 - **Transition**: Applying an action `u → v` updates the edge origin, provisionally treats the joint as fixed, adds `v` to `V_S`, and appends any new broken edges to `B_S`.
 - **Reward Function**: For a completed tree `˜T`, the terminal reward is a weighted sum of five terms:
 - `R_struct`: Penalizes variance in node depth and deviations from a preferred out-degree, promoting structural regularity.
 - `R_static`: Favors configurations that reduce gravitational torque about joint frames by promoting center-of-mass support.
 - `R_contact`: Quantifies and rewards contact strength using SDF-based bidirectional proximity for edges in the tree.
 - `R_sym`: Promotes equal depths and shared parents for parts within discovered symmetry clusters (e.g., legs attached to the same torso).
 - `R_hier`: Discourages child parts from being substantially larger than their parent parts by volume.
 - **Search Mechanism**: MCTS with UCT (Upper Confidence Bound 1 applied to Trees) is used for node selection. Rollouts greedily complete the tree by selecting actions with the highest immediate score, and the terminal reward is backed up along the simulation path. This objective function helps resolve ambiguities in complex structures.

#### 3.3. C. Joint Reasoning

Once the kinematic tree is inferred, the joint types and parameters are estimated.
1. **Joint Type Prediction**: For each edge `(u → v)` in `T`, a Vision-Language Model (VLM) is queried using orthographic viewsets of the joint close-up and the entire assembly. The VLM predicts the joint type (fixed, revolute, prismatic). For symmetric child clusters, a representative joint type is selected by majority, and outliers are corrected.
2. **Initial Parameter Estimation**:
 - **Contact Region Analysis**: Surface samples `P_A` and `P_B` from parts A and B are used to extract a contact region `C`. Points in `C` are weighted based on nearest-neighbor distance.
 - **Centroid and Covariance**: A weighted contact centroid `μ_c` and covariance `Σ` are computed from `C`. The principal direction with the smallest variance from `Σ` provides an initial revolute hinge-axis estimate (`b_u_PCA`). A contact normal (`b_n`) is also derived.
 - **Candidate Generation**: A diverse set of revolute joint candidates `(p, u)` (pivot, axis) is generated. Pivots are initialized at `μ_c`, and axes are drawn from `b_u_PCA`, `b_n`, orthogonal completions, principal axes of `Σ`, and random directions. Pivot samples are further generated by sliding along candidate axes around `μ_c`.
3. **DW-CAVL Objective (Distance-Weighted Contact-Aware Virtual Linkage)**: This novel optimization approach infers revolute and prismatic joint parameters from static geometry.
 - **Differentiable Rigid Motions**: Rigid transforms for revolute (rotation `R(u, θ)`) and prismatic (translation `t u`) motions are defined, with axis `u` parameterized to be differentiable.
 - **Weighted SDF Evaluation**: The objective evaluates the parent part's SDF (`f_θ`) at points on the child part, both in its initial static position (`s_0(x)`) and after a virtual motion (`s_δ(x)`). Volumetric and distance-based weights (`w_vol`, `w_dist`) are applied to child samples to prioritize near-contact regions.
 - **Loss Terms**: The objective `J(p, u)` aggregates over a set of virtual motion parameters `δ`:
 - `L_cons`: A consistency term penalizes separation near contact regions after virtual motion, weighted by `w_i`.
 - `L_coll`: A collision term penalizes penetration after virtual motion, weighted by inverse `w_i`.
 - `L_reg`: For revolute joints, a regularization term pulls the pivot `p` towards the contact centroid `μ_c`.
 - **Candidate Selection and Refinement**: Many `(p, u)` candidates are ranked based on a subsampled evaluation of `J`. The top-K candidates are then refined by minimizing `J` using full point sets. The candidate with the highest score `1/(1+J)` determines the final joint parameters and type.

The entire pipeline, summarized in Algorithm 1, outputs the kinematic tree `T` and the set of inferred joints `{J_uv}`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Kinematify was conducted in two primary settings: everyday articulated objects and robotic platforms, alongside an end-to-end evaluation from RGB images and an ablation study.

#### 4.1. Everyday Objects (PartNet-Mobility Benchmark)

Kinematify was compared against Articulate Anymesh and ArtGS using ground-truth segmented meshes to isolate the kinematics inference performance.
- **Axis Angle Error**: Kinematify achieved the lowest average axis angle error (2.92°), outperforming Articulate Anymesh (35.80°) and ArtGS (13.80°). This indicates superior accuracy in determining joint orientations.
- **Axis Position Error**: Kinematify demonstrated competitive performance with an average axis position error of 0.23m, which was slightly higher than Articulate Anymesh (0.19m) but significantly better than ArtGS (0.75m).
- These results suggest that Kinematify produces precise joint axes and stable pivot placements for everyday objects.

#### 4.2. Robotic Platforms

Evaluations were performed on six common robot models with varying DoF (UR10e, Franka Panda, Unitree Go2, Fetch, Allegro, Unitree H1). For kinematic tree structure, AutoURDF was included as a baseline alongside Articulate AnyMesh and ArtGS.
- **Tree Edit Distance (TED)**: Kinematify significantly reduced the average TED (1.32) compared to AutoURDF (2.97). This indicates a more faithful recovery of kinematic structures, especially on high-DoF and multi-branched robots like Unitree H1 (19 DoF), where Kinematify achieved a TED of 2.23 compared to AutoURDF's 8.13.
- **Axis Angle Error**: Kinematify achieved an average axis angle error of 16.06° across robots, substantially lower than Articulate Anymesh (57.79°) and ArtGS (34.80°).
- **Axis Position Error**: Kinematify had an average axis position error of 0.36m, better than Articulate Anymesh (0.44m) and ArtGS (1.06m).
- These findings underscore the effectiveness of Kinematify's MCTS-based objective in reasoning about complex, multi-branched kinematic structures, surpassing prior methods in both structural consistency and joint parameter accuracy.

#### 4.3. B. End-to-End Evaluation

Kinematify was evaluated in a full end-to-end setting, starting from single RGB images using a part-aware 3D foundation model for segmentation.
- **Performance**: The errors (Axis Angle Error, Axis Position Error, TED) increased modestly compared to the geometry-only track. For everyday objects, the axis angle error increased from 2.92° to 3.78°, and axis position error from 0.23m to 0.28m. More noticeable increases were observed for robots like Fetch and Panda, consistent with their tighter kinematic tolerances.
- This indicates a reasonable level of robustness of the kinematic reasoning stack to the quality of initial 3D segmentation derived from images.

#### 4.4. C. Ablation Study

An ablation study quantified the contribution of the MCTS and DW-CAVL components.
- **Without MCTS (using BFS)**: Substituting MCTS with a BFS strategy resulted in consistently larger TED values across robots (e.g., TED for Fetch increased from 1.78 to 3.32). This supports the role of MCTS in providing long-range regularization and correctly resolving parent choices in symmetric substructures and unbalanced trees, which BFS, a greedy local approach, cannot achieve.
- **Without DW-CAVL Anchor**: Removing the anchor term in the DW-CAVL objective (so only collision avoidance is considered) did not drastically alter the tree structure but significantly degraded joint parameters. For instance, the axis angle error for everyday objects increased from 2.92° to 13.94°, and axis position error from 0.23m to 1.34m. This demonstrates that the attraction to the contact centroid and near-surface bands is crucial for the optimizer to identify true pivot points and axes, preventing drift.
- The full Kinematify model demonstrated the best balance and performance across all metrics, confirming the synergistic contributions of both MCTS for topology inference and DW-CAVL for joint parameter estimation.

#### 4.5. D. Real-world Robot Manipulation

The recovered kinematics were exported to URDF format and deployed in simulation (Isaac Sim) and on a real robot.
- **Application**: Kinematify generated URDFs for a Fetch robot and a simple cabinet from segmented meshes. These models were loaded into a MoveIt planning scene.
- **Task Execution**: A constraint motion planner was used for tasks like reaching to grasp and constrained pulling (drawer opening), as well as pouring water from a cup into a container.
- **Validation**: The robot successfully followed planned trajectories in both simulation and hardware without collisions, demonstrating that the recovered kinematics are physically consistent and directly usable for online planning in ROS and MoveIt.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Kinematify represents a notable step in the automated synthesis of articulated object models, with several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Addressing the High-DoF Modeling Challenge**: The framework directly addresses the longstanding challenge of generating kinematic descriptions for high-DoF and multi-branched articulated objects. This is crucial for environments where robots interact with complex structures or require detailed self-modeling capabilities.
- **Reducing Manual Effort**: By automating the inference of kinematic topologies and joint parameters from static inputs, Kinematify has the potential to substantially reduce the manual effort currently required for creating URDFs and other articulated object descriptions. This can accelerate the development and deployment of robotic systems.
- **Open-Vocabulary Capabilities**: The ability to synthesize articulated models from arbitrary RGB images or textual descriptions, without requiring motion data, training on specific articulation priors, or large hand-curated datasets, provides significant open-vocabulary capabilities. This flexibility enables robots to interact with novel objects encountered in unstructured environments.
- **Physically Consistent and Functionally Valid Models**: The methodology emphasizes the generation of models that are not only geometrically plausible but also physically consistent and functionally valid. The integration of structural priors in MCTS and the contact-aware optimization in DW-CAVL contribute to producing models directly usable in physics simulations and for motion planning.
- **Direct Applicability in Robotics**: The successful demonstration of deploying Kinematify-generated URDFs for real-world robot manipulation tasks (e.g., drawer opening, pouring) using standard robotics frameworks like MoveIt highlights the immediate practical utility of this work. This can facilitate advancements in robot manipulation, grasping, and human-robot interaction.
- **Advancement in 3D Understanding**: This research contributes to the broader field of 3D object understanding by providing a method to infer not just static geometry but also the underlying functional and kinematic properties of objects from visual and textual cues.

While Kinematify assumes accurate part segmentation and a reliable contact graph, acknowledging potential limitations such as spurious seams or missed contacts, the work lays a foundation for future research. This includes exploring joint refinement of segmentation and contact reliability during structure inference, and developing learning-based models that can directly predict kinematic topology and joint parameters while adhering to physics-based constraints. Overall, Kinematify represents progress towards more autonomous and versatile robot perception and interaction in complex environments.
