# QQWorld: Quantile-Quantile Matching for World Model Regularization

> [!summary] Summary
> QQWorld introduces Quantile-Quantile (QQ) matching as a regularization method for latent world models, effectively replacing the Epps–Pulley (EP) test to enforce an isotropic Gaussian distribution. This approach significantly reduces heavy-tailed deviations in learned latents, leading to an average 5.33 percentage point improvement in planning success rates over the LeWM baseline across four control environments, and decreases the mean QQ RMSE from 0.157 to 0.121.

## Problem

- Latent world models, like LeWorldModel (LeWM), utilize explicit regularization (e.g., Epps–Pulley test) to align learned latents with an isotropic Gaussian, which is crucial for minimizing prediction risk.
- Despite explicit regularization, latents learned by LeWM often exhibit pronounced heavy tails, which can lead to unstable dynamics and amplified errors during multi-step model rollouts.
- The Epps–Pulley (EP) test, which is equivalent to a squared Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a Gaussian kernel, generates vanishing gradients for tail samples, offering insufficient corrective force for outliers and allowing heavy tails to persist.

## Method

- Replaced the ==Epps–Pulley (EP) regularization== with a **Quantile-Quantile (QQ) matching** objective, which directly aligns ordered projected latent values with their corresponding standard Gaussian quantiles.
- The QQ objective's gradient maintains a magnitude that grows linearly with the quantile discrepancy, ensuring a strong, non-vanishing restorative force for even extreme tail deviations.
- Introduced **Cross-Batch QQ**, a memory-efficient strategy that uses a ==FIFO queue== of past batch features to improve the accuracy of empirical rank estimation for QQ matching without significantly increasing backpropagation batch size or GPU memory usage.

## Results

- Achieved an average planning success rate of **85.08%** across Two-Room, Reacher, PushT, and OGBench-Cube environments, outperforming the LeWM baseline by **5.33 percentage points**.
- Reduced the mean QQ Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) from **0.157** for LeWM to **0.121** for QQWorld, indicating a closer alignment of projected latents with the standard Gaussian distribution and a reduction in radial tail heaviness (e.g., probability of extreme samples reduced from **0.315 to 0.123**).
- Cross-Batch QQ enabled a **4x** smaller backpropagation batch size (N=32) to outperform LeWM, increasing planning success from **65.42% to 83.50%** while consuming approximately **73%** less GPU memory (3435.0 MB vs. 12747.1 MB).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimization geometry of a statistical test is critical; while the EP test can measure distribution discrepancy, its MMD-equivalent formulation with a Gaussian kernel leads to vanishing gradients for tail samples, rendering it ineffective at suppressing heavy tails during training.
> - Quantile-Quantile matching provides a robust and uniformly effective regularization signal across the entire latent distribution, as its gradients maintain a linear corrective force that directly transports samples towards their rank-matched Gaussian quantiles.
> - Decoupling the batch size used for accurate rank estimation from the batch size used for backpropagation via a cross-batch queue can significantly enhance regularization performance and computational efficiency, especially under memory constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of latent world models for reinforcement learning, an area focused on enabling agents to predict future states and plan efficiently within a compact representation space. A significant trend in this domain involves regularizing the marginal distribution of learned latents toward an isotropic Gaussian. This regularization has been shown to minimize downstream prediction risk.

Prior work, such as LeWorldModel (LeWM) (Maes et al., 2026), adopted the Epps–Pulley (EP) test (Epps & Pulley, 1983) as a differentiable penalty to enforce this distribution-level constraint, moving beyond simpler moment-based regularization methods (Bardes et al., 2022). Subsequent methods have explored modifications to the latent space structure, such as applying Gaussian regularization within multiple subspaces (Sub-JEPA, Zhao et al., 2026), decomposing representations (SD-JEPA, Thil et al., 2026), or incorporating auxiliary objectives (SMWM, Ivashkov et al., 2026).

This paper identifies a specific limitation within the existing EP regularization approach. It observes that despite EP regularization, latents learned by LeWM exhibit pronounced heavy tails. The work attributes this not to the concept of regularization itself, but to the optimization geometry of the EP test. The corrective gradients of the EP objective, which is shown to be equivalent to a squared Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a Gaussian kernel, are found to vanish rapidly for tail samples. This allows heavy-tailed deviations to persist as the regularizer provides diminishing restoring force for outliers.

QQWorld addresses this specific gap by proposing an alternative distribution-matching objective: Quantile-Quantile (QQ) matching. While previous works focused on architectural improvements or auxiliary losses, QQWorld revisits the fundamental distribution-matching objective. It aims to provide a more robust and uniformly effective regularization signal across the entire latent distribution, particularly in the tails, without introducing additional hyperparameters or modifying the world model architecture. This positions QQWorld as a direct refinement of the regularization mechanism central to latent world models that aim for Gaussian latent distributions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the quality of learned latent distributions in latent world models by developing a more effective regularization method that specifically addresses heavy-tailed deviations and leads to better alignment with an isotropic Gaussian target.

The motivation for this work stems from several detailed observations and analyses:

1. **Importance of Latent Distribution Quality:** Latent world models facilitate efficient planning by enabling prediction in a compact representation space. The performance of these models critically depends on the quality of the learned latent distribution. Specifically, regularizing latents towards an isotropic Gaussian has been theoretically demonstrated to minimize downstream prediction risk (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025).

2. **Observed Limitation of Current Methods (LeWM):** LeWorldModel (LeWM), a prominent latent world model, employs the Epps–Pulley (EP) test to enforce Gaussian regularization on its latent representations. However, the authors observe that despite this explicit regularization, the latents learned by LeWM often exhibit "pronounced heavy tails." An illustrative example in Figure 1 (Right) highlights this phenomenon.

3. **Detrimental Effects of Heavy Tails:** Such heavy-tailed behavior is considered undesirable. Extreme latent values can:
 - Push the learned dynamics into poorly represented or unvisited regions of the latent space.
 - Potentially amplify errors during multi-step model rollouts, undermining the reliability of planning.
 - Increase the mismatch between the learned latent distribution and the desired Gaussian prior, which is counter to the regularization objective.

4. **Identification of Root Cause: Optimization Geometry of EP Test:** The persistence of heavy tails, even with explicit non-normality penalties, suggests a limitation in the specific statistical test used for regularization. The authors conduct a two-step analysis:
 - **MMD Equivalence:** They show that the EP statistic is equivalent to the squared Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between the empirical latent distribution and N(0,1), under a Gaussian kernel with unit bandwidth.
 - **Vanishing Gradients for Tail Samples:** They analyze the gradient induced by this MMD-based objective. This analysis reveals that the corrective force of the EP regularizer rapidly decays for latent values far from the distribution's bulk. Once a latent coordinate moves beyond the limited interaction scale of the Gaussian kernel, the EP regularizer provides "little restoring force." Consequently, it becomes ineffective at suppressing heavy-tailed deviations.

5. **Motivation for Quantile-Quantile (QQ) Matching:** To overcome this limitation, the authors propose replacing the EP penalty with a Quantile–Quantile (QQ) matching objective. This objective is motivated by its direct approach to aligning the ordered latent values with their corresponding Gaussian quantiles. The expectation is that this direct, rank-matched transport signal will maintain a corrective magnitude that grows linearly with the quantile deviation, rather than vanishing for extreme latent values. This is hypothesized to provide a strong and symmetrical restorative force on deviations in both tails, leading to improved Gaussian alignment and thinner latent tails. The ultimate goal is that these improved latent distributions will translate into enhanced downstream planning performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The QQWorld methodology builds upon the established framework of latent world models, particularly LeWorldModel, by modifying its core regularization objective. The general approach involves regularizing one-dimensional projections of high-dimensional latent embeddings, leveraging the Cramér–Wold theorem to infer control over the full joint latent distribution.

#### 3.1. Analysis of Vanishing Gradients in EP Test

The paper first provides a detailed analysis of the Epps–Pulley (EP) test, as employed by LeWM:
- **EP Statistic:** The EP loss, $L_{EP}(X)$, measures the discrepancy between the empirical characteristic function of projected samples $X = \{x_n\}_{n=1}^N$ and the characteristic function of $N(0,1)$, weighted by $w(t) = e^{-t^2/2}$.
- **Kernel Equivalence:** The authors demonstrate that, up to a multiplicative constant, $L_{EP}$ is equivalent to the squared Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between the empirical distribution and $N(0,1)$, using a Gaussian kernel $k(x, y) = e^{-(x-y)^2/2}$. This equivalence is crucial for understanding the gradient behavior.
- **Gradient Derivation and Analysis:** The gradient of $L_{EP}$ with respect to a single latent coordinate $x_n$ is derived (Eq. 4). It comprises two main components:
 - A **pairwise repulsive term** that pushes $x_n$ away from other samples $x_m$.
 - A **center-attraction term** that pulls $x_n$ towards the origin, originating from the interaction with the $N(0,1)$ target.
- **Proposition 1 (Vanishing Restoring Force):** The core finding of this analysis is that for a latent coordinate $x_n$ that lies far in the tail (i.e., an outlier), the pairwise repulsive term becomes asymptotically negligible. The dominant restoring force is then due to the center-attraction term, which is shown to be proportional to $h e^{-h^2/4}$ for an outlier value $h$. This magnitude reaches a maximum at $h=\sqrt{2}$ and subsequently decays super-exponentially. This rapid decay implies that once a latent value escapes beyond a certain interaction scale, the EP regularizer provides little to no corrective signal, thus failing to suppress heavy tails.

#### 3.2. QQ Regularization

To address the vanishing gradient problem, the paper proposes a Quantile–Quantile (QQ) matching objective:
- **QQ Loss Definition:** The QQ loss, $L_{QQ}(X)$, is defined as the sum of squared differences between the ordered projected samples $\hat{x}_n$ and their corresponding Gaussian quantiles $q_n = \Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{n-0.5}{N}\right)$, where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse CDF of the standard Gaussian (Eq. 6).
- **Optimal Transport Interpretation:** $L_{QQ}$ is presented as a quadrature approximation of $N \cdot W_2^2(\{x_n\}, N(0,1))$, i.e., $N$ times the squared 2-Wasserstein distance to the target Gaussian. This contrasts with EP's MMD interpretation.
- **Proposition 2 (Non-Vanishing Restoring Force):** The gradient of $L_{QQ}$ with respect to $x_n$ is $\partial L_{QQ} / \partial x_n = 2(x_n - q_{\rho(n)})$, where $\rho(n)$ is the rank of $x_n$. This gradient provides a direct transport signal towards the rank-matched Gaussian quantile. Crucially, its magnitude, $2|x_n - q_{\rho(n)}|$, increases linearly with the quantile discrepancy, ensuring that strong corrective forces are maintained even for extreme tail deviations.
- **Behavior Near Rank-Switching Boundaries:** The authors address the potential issue of gradient discontinuity when ranks switch. They demonstrate that the QQ objective locally repels tied configurations. For two samples $\hat{x}_k = c - \delta$ and $\hat{x}_{k+1} = c + \delta$, the derivative of their contribution to the loss with respect to separation $\delta$ is $4\delta - 2(q_{k+1} - q_k)$. At $\delta=0$ (a tie), the one-sided derivative is negative, indicating that gradient descent will increase $\delta$ and encourage separation, acting as an anti-collapse mechanism.
- **Proposition 3 (One-Way Control between QQ and EP):** This proposition establishes that driving $L_{QQ}$ to zero also drives $L_{EP}$ to zero ($L_{EP} \leq C(L_{QQ} + \frac{\log N}{N})$). However, the converse is not true; a small $L_{EP}$ can coexist with arbitrarily large tail deviations. This theoretically supports QQ matching as a stronger and more comprehensive form of distributional control.

#### 3.3. Cross-Batch QQ

To enhance the accuracy of empirical QQ matching, which benefits from larger sample pools, while maintaining memory efficiency, Cross-Batch QQ is introduced:
- **Motivation:** Larger batch sizes provide more accurate rank estimates but incur significant GPU memory overhead due to storing activations for backpropagation.
- **Mechanism:** A first-in-first-out (FIFO) queue is used to retain projected features from the current batch ($x^{(t)}$) and $K$ previous batches ($x^{(t-j)}$ for $j \ge 1$). The total ranking pool size becomes $M = (K+1)N$. Importantly, historical features are "detached" from the computation graph, meaning gradients are only propagated through the $N$ samples of the current batch.
- **Queue-Based Objective:** The $L_{queue}^{QQ}$ objective uses quantiles derived from the larger $M$-sized pooled set, but only the current batch samples receive gradient updates.
- **Bias–Variance Trade-off:** The queue introduces a trade-off. It reduces the rank-estimation variance from $O(N^{-1})$ to $O(\{N(K+1)\}^{-1})$. However, if the latent feature distribution $F_t$ changes significantly over time (i.e., $F_{t-j} \ne F_t$), using older features introduces a "representation-staleness bias." Thus, Cross-Batch QQ is presented as most beneficial for small backpropagation batch sizes (small $N$) where variance reduction outweighs the potential bias from a short queue.

The overall approach of QQWorld is to substitute the EP regularizer with the QQ objective, which is a drop-in replacement that introduces no new hyperparameters. The Cross-Batch QQ is an optional memory-efficient strategy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluate QQWorld against LeWM and its variants across four control environments, focusing on planning performance, normality assessment, physical state probing, and the effectiveness of Cross-Batch QQ.

#### 4.1. Planning Performance

- **Overall Improvement:** QQWorld achieved the highest average planning success rate of 85.08% across the Two-Room, Reacher, PushT, and OGBench-Cube environments (Table 1).
- **Outperformance of LeWM:** This represents an improvement of 5.33 percentage points over the LeWM baseline (79.75%). QQWorld consistently outperformed LeWM on all four individual environments.
- **Competitive Performance:** QQWorld also outperformed other LeWM variants like Sub-JEPA (83.17%), SD-JEPA (82.67%), and SMWM (83.00%), demonstrating that the improved regularization translates to better downstream planning.

#### 4.2. Normality Assessment

- **Reduced QQ RMSE:** Figure 1 (Left) shows that QQWorld led to a distribution of QQ RMSE values shifted towards smaller errors, with the mean QQ RMSE decreasing from 0.157 for LeWM to 0.121 for QQWorld. This indicates closer alignment of projected latents with the standard Gaussian distribution.
- **Thinner Radial Tails:** Figure 1 (Right) and Figure 2 illustrate QQWorld's effectiveness in suppressing heavy tails. QQWorld reduced the probability of samples having squared norms larger than the 90th percentile of a $\chi^2_{192}$ distribution (an indicator of radial tail heaviness) from 0.315 to 0.123, significantly closer to the Gaussian reference of 0.10. Figure 2 further demonstrated that QQWorld progressively reduced tail probabilities during training, while LeWM maintained substantially heavier tails.
- **Improved Statistical Normality Tests:** QQWorld achieved lower Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and Epps–Pulley (EP) statistics compared to LeWM (Table 2). Specifically, QQWorld reduced the KS statistic by 15.8% and the EP statistic by 31.4% (averaged over environments). Notably, QQWorld obtained a lower EP statistic even though LeWM directly optimized for the EP objective, supporting the claim that QQ regularization offers stronger distributional control.

#### 4.3. Physical State Probing

- **Preservation of Task-Relevant Information:** On the PushT environment, QQWorld's learned latents consistently matched or outperformed LeWM in decoding task-relevant physical states (agent location, block location, block angle) using both linear and MLP probes (Table 3). This suggests that QQ regularization effectively preserves essential physical information within the latent space.

#### 4.4. Visualizing Learned Latents

- **More Accurate Trajectory Reconstruction:** On the Two-Room environment, linear probes trained on QQWorld's latents produced decoded agent trajectories that more closely resembled ground-truth paths, accumulating less prediction error over time compared to LeWM (Figure 3). QQWorld achieved a mean RMSE of 2.58 across test episodes, significantly lower than LeWM's mean RMSE of 6.01.

#### 4.5. Cross-Batch QQ Effectiveness

- **Memory-Efficient Performance Boost:** Table 4 demonstrates the utility of Cross-Batch QQ. With a small backpropagation batch size of $N=32$, using a queue of 1-2 previous batches (total ranking pool size $M=64$ to $96$) substantially increased the planning success rate from 65.42% (no queue) to 80.67% and 83.50%, respectively, with negligible increase in GPU memory usage.
- **Outperforming LeWM with Reduced Resources:** The configuration of $N=32$ with 3 batches in the queue (total size 96) achieved 83.50% success rate, outperforming LeWM (79.75%) while using a 4x smaller backpropagation batch size and approximately 73% less GPU memory (3435.0 MB vs 12747.1 MB for LeWM's effective batch size 128).
- **Validation of Bias-Variance Trade-off:** The results confirmed the theoretical bias-variance trade-off. Increasing the queue length excessively (e.g., from 3 to 4 batches at $N=32$) sometimes led to a slight decrease in performance, and longer queues offered limited or no benefit for already large batch sizes ($N=64, N=128$), supporting the idea of staleness bias.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The QQWorld research has several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of latent world models and representation learning:

1. **Improved Latent Representation Quality:** The work successfully addresses a documented limitation in existing latent world models, namely the persistence of heavy tails despite explicit Gaussian regularization. By demonstrating that QQ matching yields latents with better Gaussian alignment and thinner tails, QQWorld contributes to the development of more robust and statistically well-behaved latent spaces. This can lead to more reliable predictions and less unstable behavior during planning, particularly in unexplored regions of the latent space.

2. **Enhanced Downstream Planning Performance:** The consistent improvement in planning success rates across various control environments suggests that optimizing the quality of latent distributions directly translates to more effective agent control. This reinforces the importance of meticulous distribution regularization in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning with world models.

3. **Fundamental Methodological Insight:** The paper provides a crucial insight that a statistical test, while effective for measuring distributional discrepancy, may not be an effective training objective if its induced gradients are poorly behaved (e.g., vanishing) during optimization. This highlights the need for careful consideration of optimization geometry when designing regularization losses, an insight that could guide the development of future objectives in various machine learning domains.

4. **Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** The introduction of Cross-Batch QQ offers a practical solution for memory-constrained training. By decoupling the effective ranking pool size from the backpropagation batch size, it enables smaller physical batch sizes to achieve competitive performance, significantly reducing GPU memory consumption. This is particularly impactful for scaling latent world models to larger architectures, higher-resolution observations, or longer sequences, where memory overhead is a primary bottleneck.

5. **Generalizability and Ease of Integration:** QQWorld is presented as a "drop-in replacement" for the EP regularizer, requiring no additional hyperparameters. This simplicity suggests ease of integration into existing or new latent world model architectures that utilize sliced Gaussian regularization, potentially accelerating the adoption of this improved method.

6. **Broader Applicability:** The core principle of robust quantile-quantile matching for distribution regularization could extend beyond world models. Any representation learning framework that aims to match learned embeddings to a target distribution, especially when tail behavior is a concern, might benefit from similar QQ-based objectives, offering a more stable and powerful alternative to kernel-based discrepancy measures.

In summary, QQWorld represents a principled and effective advancement in latent world model regularization, addressing a critical issue with current methods and offering both performance gains and practical benefits for scalable training.
