# VLA-Arena: An Open-Source Framework for Benchmarking Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> VLA-Arena introduces an open-source diagnostic benchmark to systematically evaluate Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic control. Evaluations reveal that current VLA models primarily rely on memorization, struggle with out-of-distribution challenges, lack safety integration, and cannot compose skills for long-horizon tasks.

## Problem

- Existing simulation benchmarks use static task designs, which hinder fine-grained analysis of VLA model performance and precise identification of capability boundaries.
- Prior works often overlook crucial safety constraints, a non-negotiable aspect for real-world robotic deployment, by situating tasks in idealized environments.
- Evaluations typically concentrate on measuring robustness to perceptual or linguistic noise on known tasks, neglecting the more challenging ability of skill extrapolation to structurally complex or novel tasks.

## Method

- A structured task design quantifies difficulty along three orthogonal axes: ==Task Structure== (using ==Constrained BDDL== with dynamic objects and safety constraints), ==Language Command== (principled semantic perturbations), and ==Visual Observation== (cumulative visual perturbations).
- Introduces ==170 distinct tasks==, grouped into ==11 suites== across four dimensions (Safety, Distractor, Extrapolation, Long Horizon), each with multiple difficulty levels (==L0-L2==) to comprehensively cover various aspects of robotic intelligence.
- Provides a complete, open-source framework including an end-to-end toolchain, a formal task definition language (==CBDDL==), and curated datasets (**VLA-Arena-S/M/L**) to foster reproducible research.

## Results

- State-of-the-art **VLA models** show catastrophic performance drops to **near-zero success rates** on **L1 and L2** tasks, indicating memorization over true generalization and an inability to adapt to structurally novel challenges.
- Models are highly susceptible to static distractors, with a **sharp collapse** in performance, and exhibit limited generalization to abstract semantic concepts, novel skill workflows, and entirely new object categories.
- Long-horizon capabilities are absent, with performance collapsing to **near-zero** on simple multi-step **L1** tasks and **uniformly 0%** on more complex **L2** tasks, demonstrating an inability to chain atomic skills.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA models exhibit a strong tendency to overfit to in-distribution tasks, leading to significant performance degradation on near- and far-distribution challenges across all task dimensions.
> - Models demonstrate asymmetric robustness: relatively high but often insensitive robustness to language perturbations, yet generally higher sensitivity to visual perturbations, especially viewpoint shifts and sensor noise.
> - A critical safety-performance trade-off exists where current VLAs largely fail to integrate safety constraints, often exhibiting unsafe behaviors or achieving task success at the expense of high cumulative safety costs in novel scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represents an effort in artificial intelligence to create generalist robot control policies capable of understanding and executing complex tasks in diverse environments. These models integrate visual perception, language comprehension, and physical action generation, drawing inspiration from advances in large language models and foundation models. Progress in this field is driven by architectural innovations, large-scale data collection, and post-training techniques, leading to expanded capabilities such as cross-embodiment and cross-scene generalization, dexterous manipulation, and long-horizon planning.

Despite rapid advancements, a comprehensive understanding of the capability boundaries, limitations, and specific failure modes of VLA models remains an ongoing challenge. Existing robot learning benchmarks, such as RLBench, BEHAVIOR, CALVIN, LIBERO, and VLABench, have contributed to standardizing research by providing diverse manipulation tasks in simulation. However, these benchmarks exhibit several limitations:
- **Static Task Design:** Many tasks are defined at a single level of complexity, which restricts fine-grained analysis of how model performance degrades as specific challenges are introduced or amplified. This makes it difficult to pinpoint precise capability boundaries.
- **Limited Integration of Robustness and Extrapolation:** Current benchmarks tend to focus either on robustness to noise or on task extrapolation, without integrating how models handle concurrent challenges across visual, linguistic, and structural dimensions of tasks.
- **Overlooked Safety Constraints:** Many existing simulated environments do not explicitly address safety constraints, which are critical for real-world deployment of robotic systems.

VLA-Arena addresses these limitations by providing a structured and comprehensive benchmark. It aims to move beyond static task collections by introducing systematic difficulty quantification across multiple axes, enabling diagnostic probing to distinguish robust grounding from superficial pattern matching or memorization. This work positions itself as a tool for a more granular assessment of VLA model performance, explicitly incorporating safety considerations, and fostering research into more generalizable, robust, and safe robotic agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of VLA-Arena are:

1. **Quantitatively Characterize Capability Boundaries and Failure Modes:** The central motivation is to provide a systematic method for understanding not only if a VLA model succeeds, but how and why it fails. This involves identifying specific situations where models struggle, whether due to a lack of generalization, superficial understanding, or inability to adhere to critical constraints. This fine-grained analysis is difficult with existing benchmarks.

2. **Introduce Structured and Quantifiable Difficulty:** VLA-Arena aims to overcome the "static task design" limitation of previous benchmarks. It proposes a novel framework where task difficulty is systematically controlled and made interpretable across three orthogonal axes: task structure, language command, and visual observation. This structured approach is intended to provide a precise measurement of model capability frontiers, allowing researchers to isolate and study different aspects of model intelligence.

3. **Develop Diagnostic Probes for Robust Grounding vs. Memorization:** A key objective is to differentiate between true understanding and mere memorization of training patterns. VLA-Arena introduces systematic perturbations in both language commands and visual observations. These perturbations are designed as diagnostic probes to stress models and expose latent fragilities, revealing whether models rely on robust semantic and visual grounding or fragile overfitting to specific training data.

4. **Integrate Safety Constraints as a First-Class Metric:** Recognizing the critical importance of safety for real-world robotic deployment, VLA-Arena is motivated to explicitly incorporate safety constraints into task definitions. Unlike traditional success rate metrics, VLA-Arena introduces a cumulative cost metric to quantify unsafe behaviors, compelling models to consider safety as an objective independent of task completion.

5. **Foster Research into Generalization, Robustness, and Safety:** By clearly mapping how VLA models fail, VLA-Arena aims to guide future research. The benchmark is designed to highlight areas where current models are deficient (e.g., generalization to unseen objects, adherence to safety rules, robust perception in cluttered or dynamic environments). The release of an open-source framework, toolchain, and datasets is intended to lower the barrier for researchers to address these identified gaps and accelerate the development of more capable and reliable VLA models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VLA-Arena is designed as a comprehensive benchmark with a structured task design framework built upon a constrained behavior domain definition language (CBDDL). Its methodology is centered around three orthogonal axes of difficulty: task structure, language command, and visual observation.

#### 3.1. Structured Task Design Framework

The core of VLA-Arena's methodology is a structured approach to task difficulty, allowing for fine-grained assessment of VLA capabilities.
- **Task Structure:** Measures the inherent difficulty of a task, determined by its composition, scene variation, or constraint complexity.
- **Language Command:** Serves as a semantic grounding probe through controlled linguistic perturbations.
- **Visual Observation:** Acts as a visual grounding probe via a cumulative hierarchy of visual perturbations.

#### 3.2. Constrained Behavior Domain Definition Language (CBDDL)

VLA-Arena utilizes CBDDL, an extension of the standard Behavior Domain Definition Language (BDDL), for task and safety constraint specification.
- **Dynamic Entities:** CBDDL augments BDDL with native support for specifying dynamic objects, allowing entities to exhibit autonomous motion, such as linear, circular, waypoint, or projectile motions. These dynamic objects are integrated into the simulation via MuJoCo motion capture (mocap) joints, interacting physically while being kinematically driven.
- **Visual Perturbations:** It incorporates mechanisms to define visual perturbations, applied exclusively to camera-based image observations. These include image enhancement settings (brightness, contrast, saturation, temperature), imaging noise (Gaussian, Salt & Pepper), camera configuration (viewpoint offsets), and material randomization (random object colors).
- **Formal Safety Constraints:** CBDDL introduces an optional `:cost` block to explicitly define undesirable or forbidden simulator states using 10 distinct types of predicates. These predicates classify constraints into instantaneous checks (e.g., `InContact`, `CheckForce`, `CheckDistance`, `CheckGripperContact`) evaluated at every step, and terminal checks (e.g., `Collide`, `Fall`, `NotOn`) evaluated at the episode's end.

#### 3.3. Task Structure Axis (L0-L2)

This axis defines a task's inherent difficulty by its distance from training distributions and is organized into three levels:
- **Level 0 (L0) In-Distribution Skills:** Baseline tasks replicating the training distribution, featuring direct instructions, familiar object configurations, and minimal environmental or planning challenges.
- **Level 1 (L1) Near-Distribution Generalization:** Assesses generalization to controlled variations. These include quantitative scaling (multiple objects), new instances of known object categories, novel compositions of familiar concepts, perceptual distractors, moderate environmental complexity, and simple safety constraints.
- **Level 2 (L2) Far-Distribution Challenges:** Represents significant distribution shifts requiring robust adaptation and complex reasoning. Challenges include structurally different workflows, unconventional object arrangements, dense environmental complexity (numerous or dynamic obstacles), strict safety constraints, and completely novel object categories.

#### 3.4. Language Command Axis (W0-W4)

This axis acts as a semantic grounding probe, using systematic word substitutions based on WordNet to introduce a controlled gradient of linguistic perturbation while maintaining the task structure. Difficulty levels (W0-W4) are defined by the number of key semantic slots (verbs, nouns) in which the original word has been replaced by synonyms, hypernyms, or hyponyms. This process ensures generated commands remain natural and coherent, probing for reliance on superficial keyword matching.

#### 3.5. Visual Observation Axis (V0-V4)

This axis serves as a visual grounding probe, exposing overfitting to specific pixel distributions through a cumulative hierarchy of visual perturbations:
- **Level 0 (V0) Canonical View:** Standard scene with neutral lighting, default colors, and canonical camera pose.
- **Level 1 (V1) Lighting Variation:** Introduces randomization in brightness, contrast, saturation, and temperature.
- **Level 2 (V2) Appearance Color:** Randomizes the colors of all objects.
- **Level 3 (V3) Viewpoint Offset:** Varies the camera's extrinsic properties by randomizing positions.
- **Level 4 (V4) Visual Noise:** Injects Gaussian noise into image observations.

#### 3.6. VLA-Arena Task Suites

VLA-Arena comprises 11 task suites organized into four dimensions, each with L0-L2 difficulty levels, totaling 170 tasks:
- **Safety:** Evaluates adherence to safety constraints (e.g., `StaticObstacles`, `CautiousGrasp`, `HazardAvoidance`, `StatePreservation`, `DynamicObstacles`). Tasks escalate in complexity from unobstructed spaces to dynamic obstacles and precise state preservation.
- **Distractor:** Measures visual selective attention in cluttered or dynamic scenes (e.g., `StaticDistractors`, `DynamicDistractors`). Difficulty increases with the density and motion complexity of distractors.
- **Extrapolation:** Assesses language-driven generalization (e.g., `PrepositionCombinations`, `TaskWorkflows`, `UnseenObjects`). Tasks challenge compositional understanding of spatial relationships, novel skill workflows, and zero-shot recognition of unseen object instances or categories.
- **Long Horizon:** Evaluates multi-step planning and temporal composition by requiring models to chain atomic skills into complex workflows (L0-L2 tasks increase the number and interdependencies of skills).

- **Baseline Models:** Six state-of-the-art VLA models are evaluated, representing two architectural paradigms: autoregressive (OpenVLA, UniVLA, π

 -FAST) and continuous action generation (π

- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success rate (SR) and cumulative cost (CC) are used. CC sums instantaneous safety violations (cost=1) and penalizes terminal failures (cost=10).
- **Training Datasets:** VLA-Arena-S/M/L datasets are provided, derived from human demonstrations (10, 30, and 50 trajectories per task, respectively) for L0 and L1 tasks, ensuring standardized fine-tuning. The main experiments fine-tune on `VLA-Arena-L0-L`.
- **Real-Robot Validation:** Selected findings are validated on a physical Franka Research 3 robot to confirm their applicability to real-world deployments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive evaluation of state-of-the-art VLA models on VLA-Arena revealed several consistent limitations and failure modes:

1. **Overfitting to In-Distribution Tasks and Degradation in Generalization:** Models consistently exhibited a strong tendency to overfit to the L0 tasks on which they were fine-tuned. This resulted in a significant and often catastrophic performance degradation when confronted with L1 (near-distribution) and L2 (far-distribution) challenges across all task dimensions. Success rates frequently dropped to near-zero as task complexity increased (Table 2).

2. **Neglect of Safety Constraints:** Without explicit and robust safety constraints, models prioritized task completion, often incurring high cumulative costs (CC) by violating safety rules (Table 2). This finding highlights a gap between task success and safe execution, indicating that current VLAs do not effectively integrate safety into their policies, especially in novel L1 and L2 scenarios. For instance, models frequently ignored static obstacles or failed to grasp dangerous objects cautiously, often achieving the task goal through unsafe movements.

3. **Fragile Visual Selective Attention:** Models demonstrated fragile attention mechanisms, particularly when faced with static distractors. Performance sharply declined as scenes became more cluttered, indicating a failure to disambiguate target objects from irrelevant visual cues (Table 2, Distractor dimension). Conversely, models showed comparatively less sensitivity to dynamic distractors, which the authors hypothesize might be due to the visual distinctiveness of moving objects aiding implicit filtering.

4. **Lack of Emergent Skill Generalization via Language:** Despite mastering atomic skills on L0 tasks, models struggled when language required adapting these skills to novel contexts (Extrapolation) or sequences (Long Horizon). Performance uniformly dropped to near-zero when language required composing or reordering concepts, suggesting that models associate visual features with fixed language tokens rather than possessing true compositional understanding (Table 2, Extrapolation and Long Horizon dimensions).

5. **Diagnosing Semantic and Visual Grounding:**
 - **Visual Shortcuts and Catastrophic Forgetting:** Through diagnostic probes (Figure 2), models exhibited insensitivity to language phrasing on tasks with visually unique targets (e.g., StatePreservation), suggesting reliance on visual shortcuts. This was validated by severe performance drops under viewpoint shifts (V3) and noise (V4) in visual perturbations, confirming dependency on memorized patterns. A comparison with Qwen3-VL-8B (a VLM) showed that VLAs experienced a much more severe degradation under visual perturbations (30.5% at V3 and 50.5% at V4 compared to 3.3% and 6.7% for the VLM), indicating catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning (Table 3).
 - **Pattern Matching vs. Semantic Inference:** While models appeared robust to simple language command perturbations (W0-W4) when visual cues were strong, their failure in semantic extrapolation tasks demonstrated a fundamental deficit in language-driven skill generalization. When semantic inference was explicitly necessary, performance declined proportionally to perturbation, indicating models project diverse instructions onto a limited set of fixed behavioral patterns rather than performing true semantic reasoning.

6. **Disentangling Memorization from Generalization:** The structured task design effectively differentiated between memorization and generalization capabilities. Rank reversals were observed across difficulty levels (L0, L1, L2), indicating that high L0 proficiency did not guarantee L2 generalization. This validated that each difficulty level provides non-redundant insights into model performance profiles (Table 4).

7. **Ablation Study: Comparison with LIBERO:** An ablation study comparing VLA-Arena with LIBERO revealed that VLA-Arena tasks are more deeply language-grounded. Invalidating instructions on VLA-Arena caused a 52-64% performance collapse, compared to only a 28% drop on LIBERO (Figure 3). This supports VLA-Arena's effectiveness in necessitating precise instruction interpretation over visual shortcuts.

8. **Real-Robot Validation:** The findings from the simulated environment were consistent with real-world deployments on a Franka Research 3 robot.
 - **Consistency of Progressive Difficulty:** Model performance consistently degraded across L0, L1, and L2 tasks (60.0% SR on L0 to 3.3% on L2), mirroring simulation results (Table 5).
 - **Predictive Value of Visual Perturbations:** Real-world environmental shifts (lighting, camera pose) led to degradation gradients consistent with simulated visual perturbations (Table 6).
 - **Alignment of Failure Modes:** Qualitative real-world failures (e.g., ignoring spatial constraints, grasping incorrect items, losing grounding in long-horizon tasks) aligned with those diagnosed in simulation (Figure 4).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of VLA-Arena holds several significances and potential impacts for the field of Vision-Language-Action models and robotics:

1. **Enhanced Diagnostic Capabilities:** VLA-Arena provides a fine-grained diagnostic tool that moves beyond traditional success rates to reveal specific failure modes and capability boundaries of VLA models. By systematically disentangling task complexity, linguistic ambiguity, and visual variations, it allows researchers to pinpoint weaknesses such as reliance on memorization, superficial perception, or inadequate safety adherence. This enables a more nuanced understanding of model behavior than previously possible with existing benchmarks.

2. **Guidance for Future Model Development:** The identified limitations, such as the struggle with generalization, catastrophic forgetting, and neglect of safety constraints, highlight critical areas for future research. The benchmark serves as a roadmap for developing more robust, generalizable, and safe robotic agents. For instance, the findings on superficial visual perception suggest a need for architectures that foster stronger visual grounding and are less prone to catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning.

3. **Promotion of Safety in Embodied AI:** By explicitly incorporating formal safety constraints and the cumulative cost metric, VLA-Arena emphasizes the importance of safe robot behavior. This contributes to the responsible development of embodied AI systems, pushing the community to address safety as a core objective rather than an afterthought, which is crucial for the eventual deployment of robots in real-world, human-centric environments.

4. **Standardization and Reproducibility:** As an open-source framework with a complete toolchain (from task definition to automated evaluation) and curated datasets (VLA-Arena-S/M/L), the benchmark promotes standardization and reproducibility in VLA research. This allows for fair comparisons between different models and algorithms, accelerating progress by providing a common platform for development and evaluation.

5. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The real-robot validation component of VLA-Arena confirms that the diagnosed failure modes and performance trends observed in simulation translate to physical deployments. This predictive value enhances the utility of simulation-based benchmarking for informing real-world robot design and deployment strategies, contributing to a more effective transfer of policies from virtual to physical environments.

Overall, VLA-Arena is positioned to be a foundational resource for advancing the understanding and development of generalist robotic policies, with a particular emphasis on capabilities critical for safe and effective real-world operation.
