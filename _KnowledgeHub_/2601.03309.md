# VLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and Alibaba Inc. systematically re-evaluated the role of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies using a minimalist pipeline, VLM4VLA. The study found that while VLM pretraining is crucial, general VLM capabilities do not consistently predict VLA success, and a significant semantic visual gap exists between VLM pretraining objectives and the requirements for robotic manipulation.

## Problem

- Limited systematic study on how the intrinsic capabilities of a Vision-Language Model (VLM) influence downstream Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy performance.
- Lack of a standardized, fair framework to compare different VLM backbones for VLA tasks.
- Unclear whether general VLM competence or specific embodied task fine-tuning consistently translates into improved end-to-end VLA performance.

## Method

- Developed **VLM4VLA**, a ==minimalist adaptation== pipeline that integrates general-purpose VLMs into VLA policies with less than 1% additional learnable parameters.
- Employed a consistent MLP-based policy head and maximum likelihood imitation learning with Huber loss for stable and efficient action decoding.
- Conducted extensive empirical studies across 24 diverse VLMs and three simulation environments (Calvin ABC-D, SimplerEnv Bridge, Libero-Long) to systematically evaluate VLM impact.

## Results

- VLM initialization consistently provided a substantial performance benefit over training policies from scratch across all benchmarks, highlighting the importance of pre-trained knowledge.
- VLM4VLA achieved competitive performance compared to more complex, specialized VLA baselines like OpenVLA and pi0 despite its minimalist design.
- Freezing the vision encoder during VLA adaptation led to significant performance degradation, while freezing the language encoder or word embeddings had negligible impact, underscoring the visual semantic gap.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A VLM's general capabilities, as measured by standard VLM benchmarks, are not consistent predictors of its performance in embodied control tasks.
> - Fine-tuning VLMs on current auxiliary embodied tasks (e.g., VQA-style tasks) generally does not lead to improved downstream VLA policy performance.
> - The VLM's vision encoder is a critical bottleneck due to a 'semantic gap' between visual features for language understanding and those required for low-level action control, necessitating its fine-tuning for manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a growing area of research in robotics and artificial intelligence, aiming to enhance the generalization capabilities of robotic policies by integrating pre-trained large Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Existing VLA research has primarily focused on developing sophisticated network architectures, incorporating diverse training paradigms or modalities, and refining action decoding mechanisms. However, a fundamental aspect that has received less systematic investigation is the direct impact of the VLM backbone itself—its choice, competence, and specific capabilities—on the performance of downstream VLA policies.

Previous works, such as RT-2 (Brohan et al., 2023) and OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024), have explored discretizing actions into language tokens for VLM autoregressive learning, while others have utilized policy heads for continuous action decoding. While some studies, like RoboVLMs (Liu et al., 2025), have compared a limited number of early VLM backbones, these comparisons often lacked consistency in implementation, potentially confounding the analysis of VLM intrinsic capabilities. Additionally, the development of embodied tasks for VLMs, such as those enhancing spatial understanding (Yang et al., 2025; Feng, 2025) or task execution (Intelligence et al., 2025), has been explored, but their direct benefit to end-to-end VLA performance on manipulation tasks remains unclear.

This paper addresses this gap by systematically investigating how different VLMs and their specific capabilities, including those acquired through auxiliary embodied task fine-tuning, translate into VLA policy performance. It aims to provide a standardized framework for fair comparison, thereby offering insights into the underlying requirements for effective VLM-to-VLA transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically re-examine a fundamental question in the VLA domain: the relationship between the choice and inherent competence of a Vision-Language Model (VLM) and its subsequent performance when integrated into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. This objective is driven by several detailed motivations:

- **Addressing a Research Gap:** Despite the increasing adoption of VLMs as backbones for VLA models, there has been limited systematic study on how the intrinsic capabilities of a VLM influence downstream VLA policy performance. Existing research has predominantly focused on architectural advancements or novel training paradigms for VLA, rather than a comprehensive analysis of the foundational VLM component.
- **Establishing a Fair Comparison Framework:** To accurately assess the impact of different VLMs, the authors recognized the need for a standardized, minimalist adaptation pipeline. This pipeline, named VLM4VLA, was designed to convert general-purpose VLMs into VLA policies with minimal additional learnable parameters (fewer than 1%), thereby reducing the influence of complex, extraneous policy designs on the evaluation results and ensuring fair and efficient comparison. The choice of a simple MLP head with L1/L2 loss over stochastic methods like diffusion or flow-matching was made to enhance inference stability and reduce evaluation complexity.
- **Challenging Common Assumptions about VLM General Capabilities:** A prevailing assumption in the field is that a VLM's general-purpose strength, as measured by standard VLM evaluation benchmarks (e.g., VQA scores), would directly predict its performance in embodied control tasks. A key motivation was to empirically test this hypothesis, quantifying the benefit of VLM pretraining and investigating the predictive power of general VLM competence for VLA tasks.
- **Investigating the Impact of Embodied-Specific Capabilities:** Recent efforts have involved fine-tuning VLMs on auxiliary embodied tasks, such as embodied question answering, visual pointing, or depth estimation, with the expectation of improving VLA performance. The authors aimed to empirically determine whether improvements in these specific embodied skills in a VLM translate into better performance on end-to-end manipulation tasks.
- **Disentangling Modality Contributions and Identifying Bottlenecks:** VLM architectures typically consist of vision and language components. The study was motivated to conduct modality-level ablations to understand the individual contributions of the vision and language encoders. This involved comparing the effects of freezing versus fine-tuning each module during VLA adaptation, with the goal of identifying potential performance bottlenecks and underlying domain gaps between VLM pretraining and the requirements of embodied action planning.

Through these objectives, the research seeks to provide practical recommendations and a performance reference for the VLA community, guiding future exploration by highlighting the actual requirements for effective VLM integration in embodied agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development and application of VLM4VLA, a minimalist adaptation pipeline, to systematically evaluate the influence of VLM choice and capabilities on downstream VLA policy performance.

**VLM4VLA Pipeline Design:**
The VLM4VLA pipeline is structured around three core principles:
1. **Fairness and Reproducibility:** A consistent model architecture and identical training/testing settings are maintained across various VLMs and simulation environments to ensure objective comparisons.
2. **Minimalist Design:** VLMs are integrated into a VLA framework with a small number of new learnable parameters (less than 1% of the VLM's total parameters). This design minimizes the influence of the VLA-specific policy head on the VLM's intrinsic capabilities.
3. **Isolating Core VLM Capabilities:** The framework relies exclusively on visual-language inputs (a single-view image and textual instruction), deliberately excluding other modalities like proprioceptive state or tactile feedback. This approach aims to directly assess how the VLM's internal knowledge translates to manipulation tasks.

**VLM4VLA Network Architecture:**
The core of VLM4VLA involves:
- **Action Query Token:** A learnable action query token is introduced. Its representation, after being processed by the VLM, is used to extract embodiment-related knowledge.
- **Policy Head:** The `⟨last hidden state⟩` of this action query token is decoded into an action chunk using a small MLP-based policy head. The MLP structure is consistent across models, with its size dependent on the VLM's native hidden size.
- **Input Formatting:** Input sequences are adapted to match each VLM's native instruction-tuning format, including VLM-specific special tokens. For QwenVL series, a full instruction-SFT format (Scheme 1) was predominantly used due to its marginal performance advantage.
- **Training Objective:** Maximum likelihood imitation learning is employed. Continuous actions (e.g., end-effector relative position) are optimized using a modified Mean Squared Error (Huber loss), while discrete states (e.g., end-effector status) are optimized with binary cross-entropy loss. This approach avoids diffusion-based or flow-matching losses to mitigate stochasticity during inference and stabilize evaluation.

**Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol:**
The study involved extensive empirical studies across three dimensions, evaluating 24 different VLMs, including zero-shot and fine-tuned variants.
- **VLMs Evaluated:** Nine open-source VLMs, including Paligemma series (1, 2), QwenVL series (2.5VL-3B, 2.5VL-7B, 3VL-2B, 3VL-4B, 3VL-8B, 3VL-30B-A3B), and Kosmos-2, ranging from 1.7B to 31.1B parameters, were used as backbones.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Training from scratch:** VLM4VLA models initialized randomly, providing a lower bound.
 - **Expert VLAs:** OpenVLA (Llama2-7B), pi0 (Paligemma-1), and ThinkAct (Qwen2.5VL-7B) were used for comparison. For pi0, modifications were made to remove proprioceptive input and integrate it into the VLM4VLA framework for fair comparison, with increased evaluation rollouts due to its inherent stochasticity.
- **Training Parameters:** All VLM parameters (vision encoder, word embeddings, LLM, policy head) were fine-tuned unless specifically frozen for ablation. A consistent batch size and learning rate (tuned for convergence across models) were used. Input images were standardized to 224x224 resolution.
- **Evaluation Environments:** Experiments were conducted in three simulation environments:
 - **Calvin ABC-D:** Trained on ABC splits (30k steps) and evaluated on scene D (1000 task sequences, 1-5 tasks each) to test generalization to novel visual scenes. Metric: average number of successfully completed tasks per sequence.
 - **SimplerEnv Bridge (WindowX):** Trained on Bridge-V2 (50k steps) and evaluated on 24 trials across four challenging scenes (Pick Carrot, Pick Eggplant, Pick Spoon, Stack Cube). Metric: success rate.
 - **Libero-Long (-10):** Trained on Libero-Long (50k steps) and evaluated with 50 trials across 10 long-horizon tasks. Metric: success rate.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** The best-performing result was reported, selected from validation checkpoints and different action chunk execution lengths (full, half, single step) to ensure robust evaluation.

**Dimensions of Empirical Study:**
1. **General Capability:** Assessed the benefit of VLM pretraining versus training from scratch and investigated the correlation between standard VLM general-purpose benchmarks and VLA performance across environments.
2. **Embodied-Specific Capability:** Examined the impact of fine-tuning VLMs on seven auxiliary embodied tasks (e.g., Robopoint, Vica-332k, Bridgevqa, Robo2vlm, Robobrain2, Omni-Generation, VQA-Mix) on downstream VLA performance.
3. **Modality-Level Analysis:** Performed ablations by selectively freezing or fine-tuning the vision and language encoders during VLA adaptation. An additional experiment investigated injecting control-relevant supervision into the vision encoder using the Fast-Token on the BridgeV2 dataset to analyze the nature of the visual gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive empirical studies conducted in this research yielded several key findings regarding the relationship between Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies:

#### 4.1. Performance of Different VLMs on VLM4VLA and General Capability

- **Benefit of VLM Pretraining:** VLM initialization consistently provided a significant benefit over training policies from scratch. Models trained from random initialization showed substantial performance degradation across all benchmarks (e.g., a drop of 2.288 on Calvin ABC-D for Qwen2.5VL-7B), indicating that VLM pre-training is fundamental for VLA generalization.
- **Competitive Performance of VLM4VLA:** Despite its minimalist design, VLM4VLA demonstrated competitive performance compared to more complex, specialized VLA baselines like OpenVLA and pi0. For instance, QwenVL series models with VLM4VLA achieved performance nearly on par with state-of-the-art VLAs on the Calvin ABC-D task.
- **Inconsistent Correlation with General VLM Capabilities:** A VLM’s general capabilities, as measured by standard VLM evaluations (e.g., VQA benchmarks), were found to be poor predictors of its downstream embodied control performance across all environments.
 - On the Calvin benchmark, there was a high linear correlation (Pearson r = 0.839, R² = 0.703) between general VLM QA performance and VLA success rates.
 - In contrast, for SimplerEnv and Libero-10, no apparent linear correlation was observed (SimplerEnv: r = -0.358, R² = 0.128; Libero-10: r = -0.194, R² = 0.038).
 - This inconsistency was also evident in individual model performance: QwenVL series models excelled on Calvin, while KosMos-2, the smallest VLM evaluated, achieved the highest success rate on Simpler-Bridge and performed competitively on Libero-10. This suggests that VLA policies require capabilities beyond those typically assessed by general VLM benchmarks.

#### 4.2. Impact of Different VLM Auxiliary Tasks on VLA Performance

- **Limited Transfer from Embodied SFT Tasks:** Fine-tuning VLMs on specific auxiliary embodied tasks (e.g., Robopoint, Vica-332k, Bridgevqa, Robo2vlm, Robobrain2, Omni-Generation, VQA-Mix) generally did not lead to improved performance on downstream control tasks. Most models either showed a slight degradation or no significant improvement compared to the original VLM baseline.
- **Examples of Non-Transfer:** Finetuning with generation tasks (e.g., depth and semantic map prediction in Omni-Generation) did not provide performance benefits. Robobrain2, designed as a general-purpose embodied brain, also underperformed the baseline in VLM4VLA tests.
- **Implications:** These results indicate that current embodied VQA-style tasks and other auxiliary VLM fine-tuning approaches do not consistently offer a tangible advantage for training end-to-end VLAs for manipulation.

#### 4.3. Importance of Different VLM Modules and Visual Gap Analysis

- **Vision Encoder as a Bottleneck:** Modality-level ablations revealed that freezing the vision encoder during VLM4VLA training led to a significant performance degradation across all tested models and benchmarks. This drop was substantial and more pronounced than merely reducing the number of trainable parameters (e.g., Qwen2.5VL-7B with a frozen vision encoder, despite still having 7.6B trainable parameters, performed significantly worse than a fully fine-tuned Qwen2.5VL-3B with 3.8B parameters).
- **Language Encoder Less Critical:** Conversely, freezing the word embeddings (part of the language component) had a negligible impact on VLA performance.
- **The Nature of the Visual Gap:** The pronounced impact of the vision encoder indicated a mismatch between VLM pre-trained visual representations and those required for manipulation. Further analysis hypothesized this gap stemmed from two factors: (1) a "real-to-sim" domain gap (VLMs pretrained on real images, VLA often in simulation) and (2) a "semantic gap" between visual features for language understanding versus those for low-level action control.
 - An experiment conducted on the BridgeV2 dataset (consisting of real-world images) demonstrated that even in the absence of a sim-to-real visual gap, freezing the vision encoder still prevented performance improvement. However, allowing the vision encoder to update on real-world control data dramatically improved performance.
 - This finding suggests that the necessity of fine-tuning the vision encoder is primarily driven by a *semantic gap*: visual features optimized for multimodal understanding are not inherently aligned with the fine-grained representations required for low-level manipulation, regardless of whether the images are real or simulated.
- **VLM Pretraining Remains Essential:** Despite the identified visual gap, VLM pretraining remains indispensable, as training from scratch resulted in severe performance collapse, suggesting that VLMs provide a crucial initial spatial direction for representation learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic and comprehensive re-evaluation of the role of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) within Vision-Language-Action (VLA) frameworks, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts on future research and development in embodied AI:

- **Identifies a Critical Domain Gap:** The study empirically identifies a substantial and persistent domain gap between the pretraining objectives of general-purpose VLMs and the specific visual representation requirements for embodied action-planning. This finding challenges the intuitive assumption that strong performance on general VLM benchmarks (e.g., VQA) directly translates to high efficacy in robotic control.
- **Challenges Current VLA Development Paradigms:** By demonstrating that complex VLA network designs or auxiliary embodied task fine-tuning do not consistently yield performance benefits, the work prompts a re-evaluation of current VLA development strategies. It suggests that merely integrating VLMs or adding "embodied" tasks to VLM training might not be sufficient without a deeper understanding of the required visual feature alignment.
- **Highlights the Vision Encoder as a Key Bottleneck:** The clear identification of the VLM's vision encoder, rather than its language component, as the primary performance bottleneck is a crucial insight. This points to the need for future research to focus on adapting or pretraining vision encoders specifically for the demands of embodied environments and low-level control, potentially through novel visual pretraining objectives or adaptation mechanisms.
- **Provides a Standardized Evaluation Framework:** The introduction of VLM4VLA, a minimalist and fair adaptation pipeline, offers a valuable tool for the research community. It enables more consistent and reproducible comparisons of different VLMs in VLA contexts, facilitating clearer insights into their intrinsic capabilities.
- **Guides Future Research Directions:** The findings provide concrete guidance for future exploration in VLA. Instead of solely pursuing larger VLMs or more complex VLA architectures, the research suggests that efforts should be directed towards:
 - Developing methods to bridge the *semantic visual gap* between high-level multimodal understanding and fine-grained manipulation.
 - Designing VLM pretraining objectives that inherently align visual features with control-relevant information, even if the vision encoder is frozen during downstream tasks.
 - Investigating specialized visual representation learning for robot control, potentially decoupled from general vision-language alignment.
- **Offers Practical Recommendations:** For practitioners, the study offers practical insights, such as the limited utility of many existing embodied VQA tasks for improving end-to-end VLA performance and the critical importance of fine-tuning the vision encoder.
- **Acknowledges Limitations and Proposes Universality:** While acknowledging the limitation of not including physical robot experiments, the authors argue that the identified semantic gap is universal and applies across both simulation and real-robot settings. This perspective reinforces the relevance of the simulation-based findings for real-world robotics.

In essence, this research provides a foundational analysis that re-centers attention on the core VLM component within VLA models, shifting focus from merely integrating powerful VLMs to understanding and addressing the specific adaptations required for effective embodied intelligence.
