# Responsible Personalisation: The Double-Edged Sword of Personalisation in Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce the Responsible Personalisation Framework for Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), systematically analyzing ethical risks associated with personalized robot behavior through a lifecycle-based and context-sensitive lens. The framework highlights how risks like autonomy erosion, bias, and privacy violations are amplified by robot embodiment, providing actionable design recommendations for safer, more ethically grounded systems.

## Problem

- The ethical implications of personalization in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) are fragmented and not systematically explored, especially considering robots' physical embodiment.
- Existing ethical discussions from Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) have not been comprehensively applied to HRI, where robot embodiment can amplify known risks or introduce new challenges.
- There is a lack of a structured framework to understand how personalization-related risks emerge and manifest across diverse interaction contexts in HRI.

## Method

- Developed a ==lifecycle-based and context-sensitive framework== for personalized HRI, integrating an embodiment-aware perspective, which combines personalization stages with interaction characteristics.
- Conducted an integrative analysis of five key ethical risks: autonomy erosion, biased user modeling, manipulation, dehumanization, and privacy violations, examining their manifestation across interaction contexts.
- Translated analytical insights into actionable design recommendations and identified open research challenges for the HRI community to guide responsible development.

## Results

- Established a Responsible Personalisation Framework that provides a systematic approach for analyzing and mitigating **five** primary ethical risks in personalized HRI, explicitly accounting for robot embodiment.
- Identified and thoroughly analyzed **five** primary ethical risks—autonomy erosion, biases/stereotypes, dehumanization, manipulation, and privacy violations—explaining their unique amplification in HRI.
- Provided **five** actionable design guidelines for stakeholders, covering the necessity of personalization, mitigating overtrust, enabling user oversight, continuous bias auditing, and defining responsibility for potential harms.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot physical embodiment significantly amplifies ethical risks in personalized HRI by transforming personalization into enacted behavior, extending privacy concerns, and deepening perceived authority.
> - The manifestation, persistence, and severity of ethical risks vary significantly across the personalization lifecycle phases (design to end-of-life) and interaction types (short- vs. long-term, open- vs. closed-domain).
> - Responsible personalization requires a proactive, structured, and interdisciplinary approach that considers the full lifecycle of a personalized system and the unique characteristics of different HRI contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a developing area within Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) concerning the ethical implications of personalization. While personalization in HRI has demonstrated benefits in areas such as engagement, task effectiveness, and trust, the existing literature on the responsible aspects of this capability is noted as fragmented. Prior research has identified isolated ethical risks but often lacks a structured understanding of their emergence across diverse interaction contexts. This gap is considered particularly relevant in HRI due to robots' physical embodiment and social presence, which can amplify and reshape known risks from Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) or introduce new types of challenges, such as amplified persuasion or physical harm.

The paper acknowledges that the Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) community has critically examined issues like loss of agency, manipulation, and stereotyping in personalized systems. However, it posits that these concepts have not been systematically or extensively explored within the HRI community, especially in consideration of robots' embodied nature.

The authors differentiate their work from related socio-political and critical-theoretical analyses of surveillance and manipulation in social robotics, such as that by Axelsson and Seeck (2026). This paper focuses on the design and operational dimensions of responsible personalization in HRI, providing a lifecycle-based and context-sensitive analytical framework. It systematically maps how personalization-related risks emerge across different stages of system development and interaction, offering design-oriented recommendations. The work aims to position responsible personalization as a critical and timely challenge for the HRI community, seeking to bridge conceptual clarification, risk analysis, and practical guidance for practitioners and researchers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while personalization is becoming a fundamental capability in HRI, it presents a "double-edged sword." The potential benefits of personalized interactions, such as enhanced user engagement, task effectiveness, trust, and collaboration, are evident across various domains, including healthcare, education, and hospitality. However, these advantages are accompanied by significant ethical risks, including the potential erosion of user autonomy, amplification of societal biases, and privacy violations. The embodied nature and social presence of robots in HRI are considered to introduce unique or amplified risks compared to disembodied AI systems, such as increased persuasive power or greater privacy challenges due to physical co-location.

The authors organized a series of workshops from 2023 to 2025 at the IEEE International Conference on Robot and Human Interactive Communication (RO-MAN) to address this research gap. These workshops, involving sixteen experts from diverse fields (robotics, psychology, sociology, philosophy, and law) and structured around Oxford-style debates, highlighted the community's interest and the lack of systematic reflection on personalization risks. Key themes identified were the erosion of user autonomy, the risk of reinforcing societal stereotypes, and privacy violations.

Based on these motivations and discussions, the key objectives of this paper are:

- To present a **lifecycle-based and context-sensitive framework** for personalized HRI, incorporating an embodiment-aware perspective. This framework combines stages of the personalization process with interaction characteristics (short- vs. long-term, open- vs. closed-domain) to facilitate systematic risk analysis.
- To conduct an **integrative analysis of key ethical risks**, including autonomy erosion, biased user modeling, manipulation, dehumanization, and privacy violations, examining their manifestation across different interaction contexts.
- To translate these analytical insights into **actionable design recommendations** and identify open research challenges for the HRI community.
- To provide a structured foundation for more systematic, transparent, and ethically grounded approaches to personalized robot behavior by **structuring both the design space and risk landscape** of personalized HRI.
- To conceptualize responsible personalization in HRI through an **embodiment-aware lens**, articulating how robot physical presence can amplify both the benefits and ethical risks of adaptive behavior compared to HCI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a conceptual and analytical approach, drawing on interdisciplinary insights, expert discussions from workshops, and a review of existing literature. The methodology is structured around developing a comprehensive framework for understanding responsible personalization in HRI.

The core methodological components include:

- **Workshop Synthesis:** The paper consolidates insights from three expert workshops conducted between 2023 and 2025. These workshops brought together experts from diverse fields (robotics, psychology, sociology, philosophy, law) to discuss pressing themes related to personalization risks in HRI, particularly focusing on autonomy erosion, societal stereotypes, and privacy violations. The paper incorporates the discussions from these events.
- **Conceptual Foundations for Responsible Personalization:**
 - **Embodiment-Driven Affordances:** The paper first establishes how the physical embodiment of robots significantly differentiates HRI from disembodied AI systems. It discusses how embodiment can amplify interaction experience, trust, persuasion, and even risks of physical harm due to the robot's physical presence, movement, social cues, and perceived agency.
 - **Definitions of Personalization Concepts:** To establish a common vocabulary, the authors clarify the distinctions between:
 - **Customization:** User-initiated modifications (e.g., setting preferences).
 - **Adaptation:** Robot-initiated modifications based on context or population-level cues, without necessarily building a persistent individual model.
 - **Personalization:** System-initiated learning about a *specific individual user* and adjustment of behavior based on a persistent, evolving user model. These are presented as points along a continuum of control and inference.
 - **Lifecycle Mechanics of Personalization:** A six-phase lifecycle model is introduced to trace how personalization unfolds over time:
 - **Design:** Defining purpose, scope, and ethical boundaries.
 - **Data Collection:** Gathering multimodal data (static qualities, behaviors, task-specific measures, explicit feedback).
 - **Modelling:** Transforming inputs into structured user representations (preferences, profiles, habits).
 - **Interaction:** Enacting embodied personalized outputs.
 - **Evaluation:** Assessing outcomes at both system and research levels.
 - **End-of-Life:** Decommissioning systems, including data and model management.
 The paper also highlights the underlying Input–Modelling–Output (IMO) mechanics within this lifecycle.
 - **Classification of Social HRI Contexts:** HRI scenarios are categorized along two orthogonal dimensions to understand how context shapes personalization and risk manifestation:
 - **Interaction Duration:** Short-term (brief, one-time) vs. Long-term (repeated, extended).
 - **Domain Specificity:** Open-domain (broad topics, open-ended tasks) vs. Closed-domain (specific goal or topic).
 This framework yields four types: long-term/open-domain, long-term/closed-domain, short-term/open-domain, and short-term/closed-domain interactions.
- **Integrative Risk Analysis:** The paper systematically analyzes five key ethical risks:
 - **Erosion of User Autonomy:** Loss of independent decision-making and action.
 - **Biases and Stereotypes in User Models:** Reinforcement of social stereotypes and algorithmic confirmation bias.
 - **Dehumanisation:** Treating users as optimizable profiles rather than complex individuals.
 - **Manipulation:** Exploiting cognitive/affective processes for system/designer objectives.
 - **Privacy Violation:** Erosion of informational boundaries and contextual integrity.
 For each risk, the analysis defines the risk, discusses its empirical/conceptual foundations, connects it to the personalization lifecycle phases, and examines its manifestation across the four interaction types. It also dedicates a section to explaining *why* these risks are amplified in HRI due to embodiment (enacted behavior, shared physical/social environments, relational framing).
- **Actionable Design Recommendations:** The paper translates the risk analysis into five practical recommendations for mitigating risks, organized by level of intervention (pre-condition, risk-targeted, governance). These recommendations are linked to specific ethical concerns and lifecycle stages.
- **Example Decision Framework:** An illustrative example (cognitive training robot for older adults) is provided to demonstrate the practical application of the proposed framework.

The overall approach is a systematic conceptual mapping that combines a process-oriented view (lifecycle), a contextual view (interaction types), and an ethical view (risks and mitigations) to provide a holistic understanding of responsible personalization in HRI.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core contribution of this work is the **Responsible Personalisation Framework**, which provides a structured approach to analyzing and mitigating ethical risks in personalized HRI. This framework is grounded in an embodiment-aware perspective and integrates several analytical components:

- **Unified Analytical Framework:** The framework systematically combines a **personalization lifecycle** (Design, Data Collection, Modelling, Interaction, Evaluation, and End-of-Life phases) with a **classification of interaction types** (short- vs. long-term, open- vs. closed-domain scenarios). This allows for a structured analysis of how personalization-related risks emerge, evolve, and manifest differently across various HRI settings.
- **Identification of Key Ethical Risks:** The paper conducts an integrative analysis of five primary ethical risks associated with personalization in HRI:
 - **Erosion of User Autonomy:** The gradual reduction of a user's capacity or motivation for independent decision-making due to over-assistance or proactive robot behaviors.
 - **Biases and Stereotypes in User Models:** The entrenchment of social stereotypes and algorithmic confirmation bias within user models, leading to skewed or outdated representations.
 - **Dehumanisation:** The reduction of users to optimizable profiles, overlooking their complex personhood, particularly when optimization takes precedence over relational or moral dimensions.
 - **Manipulation:** The exploitation of users' cognitive or affective processes to steer their behaviors primarily for system or designer objectives, potentially occurring intentionally (e.g., dark patterns) or unintentionally (e.g., feedback loops).
 - **Privacy Violation:** The systematic erosion of informational boundaries through extensive data collection, implicit inferences, and public enactment of personalized behaviors, particularly amplified in shared physical environments.
- **Embodiment as an Amplifier of Risks:** A significant finding is the articulation of how a robot's physical embodiment amplifies these risks. Embodiment transforms personalization into *enacted behavior* within the user's physical space, making ethical implications more tangible. Robots are *embedded in shared physical and social environments*, extending privacy concerns beyond a single user. Furthermore, *relational framing* (e.g., companion, tutor) in conjunction with embodiment can deepen perceived authority, potentially leading to lower scrutiny of personalized influence where users are most vulnerable.
- **Lifecycle- and Context-Dependent Risk Manifestation:** The paper demonstrates that the manifestation, persistence, and severity of each risk vary significantly across the personalization lifecycle phases and interaction types:
 - **Lifecycle Phases:** Each phase contributes to the potential for risks. For instance, design choices establish the distribution of control, data collection influences bias and privacy exposure, modeling entrenches assumptions, and interaction enacts these, while evaluation and end-of-life phases are critical for detection and mitigation.
 - **Interaction Types:** Long-term, closed-domain scenarios (e.g., eldercare, education) are prone to cumulative effects like learned helplessness or persistent bias. Long-term, open-domain interactions (e.g., companion robots) pose the broadest risks to autonomy, manipulation, and worldview narrowing. Short-term interactions tend to produce immediate but often reversible effects, though initial trust or adoption decisions can be consequential.
- **Actionable Design Guidelines:** The research translates these insights into five practical recommendations for stakeholders:
 1. **Critically evaluate the necessity of personalization:** Avoid technosolutionism and ensure personalization is truly beneficial through participatory co-design.
 2. **Mitigate overtrust and overengagement:** Promote system transparency, educate users on limitations, and balance exploration with exploitation in models.
 3. **Enable user oversight and overrides:** Provide legible data practices, transparent inference communication, graduated controls, and intuitive override mechanisms.
 4. **Design and continuously audit models for bias and fairness:** Acknowledge holistic personhood, ensure diverse data, and leverage explainability techniques for continuous auditing.
 5. **Define responsibility for harms and successes:** Address legal clarity, operationalize robust laws, and foster collaboration among technical experts, policymakers, and ethicists for accountability.

These findings collectively provide a structured understanding of the "double-edged sword" nature of personalization in HRI, moving beyond anecdotal ethical concerns to a systematic framework for analysis and mitigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of Human-Robot Interaction by providing a foundational framework for understanding and addressing the ethical complexities of personalized robot behavior. Its potential impact spans several dimensions:

- **Advancing Responsible Robotics:** The paper establishes a structured and comprehensive approach to responsible personalization in HRI. By detailing a lifecycle-based and context-sensitive framework, it moves beyond fragmented ethical discussions to offer a systematic method for analyzing how risks emerge and evolve. This is significant for fostering the development of ethically grounded and human-centered robotic systems.
- **Guiding Design and Development:** The translation of abstract ethical insights into actionable design recommendations provides concrete guidance for designers, developers, and researchers. The framework encourages a proactive rather than reactive approach to ethical considerations, prompting stakeholders to critically evaluate the necessity and scope of personalization from the initial design phase. This can lead to more intentional and safer design choices in personalized HRI systems.
- **Informing Policy and Regulation:** By highlighting the unique amplification of risks due to robot embodiment and identifying specific vulnerabilities across different interaction contexts, the paper provides a basis for more nuanced policy and regulatory discussions. The emphasis on defining responsibility for harms and successes directly addresses critical governance challenges posed by autonomous, personalized robots, especially concerning legal clarity and multi-stakeholder accountability.
- **Stimulating Future Research:** The identified open research questions serve as an invitation to the HRI community and interdisciplinary collaborators (psychologists, ethicists, legal scholars) to delve deeper into underexplored areas. This includes investigating the long-term psychological and societal consequences of adaptive systems, developing robust evaluation frameworks for ethical metrics beyond efficiency, and innovating user control and transparency mechanisms.
- **Promoting Critical Reflection:** The work advocates for a shift in perspective, encouraging the HRI community to ask not merely "how much can we personalize?" but "when is personalization truly beneficial, what risks does it introduce, and what safeguards are necessary?" This reflective stance is crucial for ensuring that technological advancement aligns with human values and well-being.
- **Fostering Community Collaboration:** The proposed initiative for an open community platform and continued structured debates aims to cultivate an evolving research agenda through broad participation. This commitment to collaborative, evidence-based dialogue is vital for collectively addressing the multifaceted challenges of responsible personalization in HRI.

Overall, this paper positions responsible personalization as a central challenge for HRI, offering conceptual clarity, analytical tools, and practical guidance that can shape the future trajectory of human-robot relationships towards systems that are both effective and ethically aligned with human values.
