# Contact-Constrained Lower-Limb Joint-Offset Calibration for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> A self-contained calibration framework estimates 12 lower-limb joint encoder offsets on humanoid robots using only onboard encoders and a pelvis IMU during static double-support stances. It minimizes posture-dependent dispersion of the inter-foot transform plus IMU-derived flat-ground residuals, with Tikhonov regularization and a knee prior. Held-out foot-height RMS residuals drop from 4.26 to 2.20 mm and 8.03 to 1.43 mm.

## Problem

- Classical kinematic calibration needs laser trackers, motion capture, or fiducial targets, making repeated in-field recalibration of humanoid legs impractical.
- Onboard camera self-calibration fails for legs because feet rarely enter the head-camera field of view during normal standing or squatting stances.
- Prior contact-based humanoid calibration never formally characterized how parallel hip-pitch, knee, and ankle-pitch axes couple, nor how joint ordering conditions the estimate.

## Method

- ==Inter-foot consistency residual== on ==SE(3)==: the left-to-right foot transform must be constant during double support, so deviation from its intrinsic ==Frechet mean== drives ==nonlinear least squares== over the offset vector.
- ==IMU-aided flat-ground residual== adds equal foot heights and sole-normal alignment to gravity, supplying out-of-plane information; ==Tikhonov regularization== and a standing-posture ==knee prior== anchor the weak pitch chain.
- ==Alternating optimization== recomputes the segment contact mean each outer iteration while Ceres updates offsets, paired with a ==contact-consistency filter== and ==joint-angle clustering== to 450 frames per stance.

## Results

- A3 simulation injection recovery gives **0.120 degrees** RMS error with **0.274 degrees** maximum, concentrated on hip-pitch; real-machine paired errors reach **0.012 degrees** RMS on A2 and **0.061** on A3.
- Matched prior-free data-only conditioning is **4340.79** on A2 versus **333.23** on A3, and the controlled same-geometry MuJoCo pair gives **121728.69** for roll-yaw-pitch versus **8224.49** for pitch-roll-yaw.
- LiDAR-inertial cross-check reduces vertical odometry RMS by **25.5%** over 16 segments; closed-loop MuJoCo foot-strike deviation falls from **1190.61** to **26.57 mm** at a **3 degree** offset.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Consecutive parallel revolute axes make orientation residuals observe only the offset sum, so individual pitch offsets sit in a near-nullspace resolvable only by priors.
> - Proximal joint ordering is a design lever for calibratability: placing pitch first lets roll and yaw excitation break axis parallelism and improve conditioning.
> - Regularization and mechanical priors change Hessian curvature without adding information, so reported condition numbers and small paired errors can mask prior-dependent bias.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate kinematic models are fundamental for the successful operation of humanoid robots, particularly for tasks such as locomotion, whole-body control, and contact planning. A critical aspect of kinematic accuracy is the precise knowledge of joint encoder offsets, which represent persistent biases between an encoder's zero reading and the true mechanical zero of a joint. Accumulation of these sub-degree offsets along serial chains can lead to significant distal errors at the end-effector, such as the foot sole, resulting in incorrect foot placement and degraded balance.

Traditional robot kinematic calibration methods, extensively studied for industrial manipulators, often rely on external measurement systems like laser trackers, motion-capture systems, or fiducial targets. While effective, these setups are generally impractical for repeated, in-field recalibration of humanoid robots due to their complexity, cost, and environmental requirements. Vision-based self-calibration, utilizing onboard cameras and fiducial markers, offers a partial solution but is often limited in scope; for example, a head-mounted camera may not reliably view the feet during typical stances.

A distinct line of research in legged robotics leverages contact constraints, primarily for state estimation. Works by Bloesch et al., Hartley et al., Camurri et al., and Rotella et al. have demonstrated the use of foot-contact information, fused with IMU and encoder data, for real-time pose estimation. These methods utilize the physical assumption of foot fixity during stable support phases. The concept of applying such contact constraints to offline kinematic calibration has been explored, notably by Liu and Tang for the NAO platform and Yamane for force-controlled humanoid robots using encoders, an IMU, and flat-foot contact.

This paper builds upon the principle of contact-based constraints for calibration but addresses specific limitations and gaps. While previous work has implicitly or explicitly used double-support constraints, a formal characterization of how the resulting batch optimization is conditioned by lower-limb joint ordering, particularly in the presence of common parallel pitch axes in humanoid legs, remained less explicit. This research aims to provide a formal Hessian-eigenvector characterization of this coupling and develop tailored decoupling strategies, thereby enhancing the understanding and robustness of self-contained lower-limb calibration for humanoid robots. It distinguishes itself from prior work by formulating the constraint on SE(3) through inter-foot transform dispersion, formally analyzing pitch-axis coupling, and demonstrating how kinematic ordering influences this coupling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the critical need for accurate kinematic models in humanoid robots, which underpin effective locomotion, whole-body control, and contact planning. Inaccuracies, particularly joint encoder offsets, can lead to substantial foot-placement errors and compromise robot balance. Existing calibration methods face challenges for in-field, frequent recalibration, either due to their reliance on external, cumbersome equipment or the limited visibility of lower limbs for onboard vision systems.

The core objective is to develop a self-contained, in-situ calibration framework for humanoid lower limbs that circumvents the need for external measurement systems or specialized fiducial targets. This is motivated by the desire for a practical and repeatable calibration solution that can be deployed readily in various environments.

Specific objectives and motivations include:

- **Exploiting Onboard Sensing:** To design a calibration system that relies solely on commonly available onboard sensors, specifically joint encoders and a pelvis-mounted Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU). This minimizes hardware requirements and enhances deployability.
- **Leveraging Physical Constraints:** To utilize the fundamental physical constraint of fixed feet during static double-support contact. Under this condition, the inter-foot transform should remain constant across different robot postures. Any variation indicates incorrect kinematic parameters (i.e., joint offsets), providing a signal for optimization.
- **Formalizing Observability Challenges:** To formally characterize the conditioning of the resulting batch optimization problem. A key motivation is to understand the implications of common humanoid leg designs, particularly the presence of parallel pitch axes (e.g., hip-pitch, knee, ankle-pitch), on the observability of individual joint offsets. This involves a formal Hessian-eigenstructure analysis to identify unobservable or weakly observable directions.
- **Developing Decoupling Strategies:** Based on the observability analysis, a motivation is to develop specific strategies to decouple inherently coupled joint offsets. This includes exploring how different kinematic joint orderings (e.g., pitch-roll-yaw vs. roll-yaw-pitch) affect coupling and how diverse excitation postures (e.g., non-sagittal motions) can geometrically decouple axes. Additionally, to investigate the use of mechanical priors for algebraic resolution of remaining ambiguities.
- **Comprehensive Validation:** To rigorously validate the proposed framework through a combination of simulation studies, real-machine experiments on different humanoid platforms (AGIBOT A2 and A3), and external verification methods (LiDAR-inertial odometry, registered motion capture). This aims to demonstrate consistent recovery of injected offsets, improved internal consistency on held-out data, and enhanced external accuracy, particularly in critical channels like the pitch–vertical direction.
- **Assessing Practicality:** To demonstrate that a few static double-support stances are sufficient to achieve contact-consistent corrections for well-excited directions, thus supporting a compact and efficient calibration protocol.

In summary, the research is driven by the practical need for robust and self-contained humanoid robot calibration, motivated by an in-depth understanding of the underlying kinematic observability issues, particularly concerning parallel-axis couplings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed calibration framework is a self-contained, optimization-based approach that combines several constraints and regularization terms, solved iteratively.

**A. Kinematic Model and Offset Parameterization:**
The humanoid robot is modeled with a 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) lower-limb serial chain per leg. Two AGIBOT platforms, A2 and A3, are studied, differing in their proximal joint orderings: A3 uses pitch→roll→yaw→knee→ankle-pitch→ankle-roll, while A2 uses roll→yaw→pitch→knee→ankle-pitch→ankle-roll. Joint encoder readings, $q_{enc,k}$, are corrected by a constant 12-dimensional offset vector, $\delta q$, to yield the true joint angles, $q_k = q_{enc,k} + \delta q$. Forward kinematics, $T_{BF_L}(q_k)$ and $T_{BF_R}(q_k)$, map from the base to the left and right foot frames, respectively.

**B. Inter-Foot Consistency Constraint:**
The core of the method relies on the principle that during stable double support, both feet remain fixed relative to the ground. Consequently, the inter-foot transform, $C_k(\delta q) = T_{BF_L}(q_k)^{-1} T_{BF_R}(q_k)$, which represents the pose of the right foot in the left-foot frame, must remain constant across all postures within a contact segment. The method minimizes the dispersion of this transform over $N$ retained frames by finding an intrinsic mean $C^*$ on SE(3). The residual for this constraint is defined as $r_{feet,k}(\delta q) = \text{Log}((C^*)^{-1} C_k(\delta q))$, a 6-dimensional vector representing translational and rotational deviations from the mean.

**C. IMU-Aided Flat-Ground Constraint:**
A pelvis-mounted IMU provides the gravity direction, $g^{(k)}_{imu}$, in the robot's base frame. This information is used to enforce a flat-ground constraint. The residual, $r_{flat,k}$, is a 5-dimensional vector that penalizes deviations from:
1. Equal foot heights ($p^W_{L,z} - p^W_{R,z} = 0$).
2. Foot normals aligned with the world vertical axis ($z^W_{L,x}, z^W_{L,y}, z^W_{R,x}, z^W_{R,y} = 0$).
This constraint utilizes the IMU to define the vertical direction, providing complementary information to the inter-foot transform, particularly for out-of-plane movements. The formulation is robust to common yaw rotations.

**D. Optimization Objective:**
The overall calibration problem is formulated as a nonlinear least-squares minimization:
$\min_{\delta q} \sum_{s=1}^S \sum_{k \in C_s} (\lambda_f \|r_{feet_{s,k}}\|^2 + \lambda_g \|r_{flat_{s,k}}\|^2) + \lambda_q \|W_q \odot \delta q\|^2 + \lambda_{knee} \|\delta q_{knee} - \delta q^{prior}_{knee}\|^2$.
This objective combines:
- Inter-foot consistency residuals ($r_{feet}$).
- IMU-aided flat-ground residuals ($r_{flat}$).
- Tikhonov regularization on the joint offsets ($\|W_q \odot \delta q\|^2$), where $W_q$ is a per-joint weight vector, to prevent drift in unconstrained directions.
- A quadratic knee-prior term ($\|\delta q_{knee} - \delta q^{prior}_{knee}\|^2$), specifically used on A3, to anchor the knee offset based on mechanical knowledge during a straight-leg posture.
The scalar weights ($\lambda_f, \lambda_g, \lambda_q, \lambda_{knee}$) are selected empirically for each platform.

**E. Alternating Optimization:**
Since the intrinsic mean $C^*$ for each segment is dependent on $\delta q$, an alternating optimization scheme is employed. In each outer iteration, $C^*$ is recomputed based on the current $\delta q$ estimate. Then, a nonlinear solver (Ceres Solver using DENSE QR) updates $\delta q$ while $C^*$ is held fixed. This process converges within a few outer iterations. $\delta q$ is initialized to zero and bounded to $\pm 0.08$ rad.

**F. Data Preprocessing:**
Raw encoder frames undergo contact-consistency filtering, where frames with excessive rotation residuals (e.g., > 5 degrees) over a short temporal window are discarded. Joint-angle clustering and subsampling reduce the number of distinct configurations per stance (e.g., to 450 frames), with outliers removed to enhance robustness.

**G. Observability Analysis and Decoupling:**
The paper performs a formal analysis of the Hessian matrix ($H_{data} = J_{data}^T J_{data}$) to understand observability.
- **Hessian Eigenstructure:** Eigenvectors corresponding to small eigenvalues indicate offset combinations that are weakly constrained by the data. The regularized Hessian ($H_{reg}$) incorporates regularization and prior terms.
- **Inherent Pitch-Axis Coupling:** Proposition 1 formally states that for a serial chain with two or more consecutive revolute joints whose axes remain parallel in the residual frame (common in hip-pitch, knee, ankle-pitch), their angular Jacobian columns are collinear. This means the orientation residual only constrains the sum of their offsets, leaving an $(n-1)$-dimensional nullspace for individual offsets from orientation data alone. Translational terms might theoretically break this degeneracy, but limited lever-arm diversity in compact humanoids and dominant sagittal motion weaken this effect, leading to a near-nullspace.
- **Effect of Kinematic Ordering:** The A3 (pitch→roll→yaw) ordering, where hip-pitch is first, allows hip-roll and hip-yaw movements to rotate the hip-pitch axis out of alignment with the knee and ankle-pitch axes when excited. This contrasts with A2 (roll→yaw→pitch) where hip-pitch, knee, and ankle-pitch are consecutive, leading to stronger coupling.
- **Decoupling and Resolution Strategies:**
 - **Geometric Decoupling:** Non-zero hip-roll and hip-yaw excitation during data collection in A3 rotates the hip-pitch axis, breaking parallelism with knee and ankle-pitch, and the IMU-derived flat-ground constraint provides independent information.
 - **Algebraic Resolution:** For remaining weak directions (e.g., knee–ankle-pitch coupling), a short straight-leg standing phase provides a knee prior ($\delta q_{knee} \approx q^{mech}_{knee} - \bar{q}^{enc}_{knee}$). This prior anchors one component, allowing the pitch-sum constraint to determine others within the coupled subspace.
 - **Strengthened Regularization:** For platforms like A2 where geometric decoupling is limited, Tikhonov regularization with specific joint weights is used to suppress near-nullspace drift, albeit with potential bias.

**H. Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on A3 simulation (MuJoCo) and real A2/A3 platforms. Injection tests involve adding known offsets and evaluating recovery. Real-machine tests use four distinct static double-support stances (normal squat, left lunge, right lunge, wide squat) to maximize posture diversity. A controlled MuJoCo comparison isolates the effect of joint ordering by using identical geometry, masses, sensors, and noise, varying only the proximal R-Y-P vs. P-R-Y ordering. Metrics include signed error, RMS error, and condition numbers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning the self-contained calibration framework, its observability characteristics, and its performance in simulation and on real humanoid robots.

**A. A3 Simulation Results:**
In a MuJoCo simulation of the AGIBOT A3, an injected multi-degree offset pattern was recovered. The RMS error across all 12 joints was 0.120 degrees, with a maximum absolute error of 0.274 degrees, concentrated primarily along the weakly-observed pitch directions. The regularized Hessian condition number ($\kappa(H_{reg})$) was $1.11 \times 10^4$. Matched A2-like/A3-like MuJoCo models, designed to isolate the effect of joint ordering, showed a baseline RMS recovery error floor of approximately 0.092 to 0.121 degrees even in ideal zero-noise conditions due to weld and settling effects.

**B. A2 and A3 Real-Machine Results:**
- **Injection Recovery:** On real A2 and A3 platforms (Table II), under platform-specific operating settings including differing regularization and knee priors, the paired injection consistency error ($\Delta_i$) showed an RMS value of 0.012 degrees for A2 and 0.061 degrees for A3. For A2, the $\kappa(H_{reg})$ was $5.03 \times 10^3$, while for A3 it was $7.22 \times 10^1$ (prior-assisted). This metric indicates the consistency of the estimator when faced with known injections, rather than the absolute accuracy of the native mechanical zero.
- **Ordering Effect:** A controlled MuJoCo comparison, with identical geometry and excitation but differing proximal joint orderings, demonstrated a substantial effect. For the R-Y-P ordering (A2-like), $\kappa(J_{data})$ was 348.90 and $\kappa(H_{data})$ was 121728.69. For the P-R-Y ordering (A3-like), these values were 90.69 and 8224.49, respectively. This suggests that the A3's joint ordering (pitch→roll→yaw) inherently provides better conditioning for the tested geometry and excitation, consistent with the geometric decoupling hypothesis.
- **Pitch Sum Stability:** On the real A2 platform, the errors in the sum of pitch offsets ($s_L, s_R, s_R - s_L$) were significantly smaller (e.g., -0.0053 to +0.0218 degrees) compared to individual pitch offsets (RMS 0.366 degrees). Varying the knee-prior center by $\pm 1$ degree changed individual estimates by up to 1.90 degrees, but the pitch sums by at most 0.0071 degrees, confirming that individual pitch offsets are sensitive to priors while their sums are robustly constrained.

**C. Comparison with Closest Method:**
A comparison with Yamane's kinematic objective, using matched simulated data, revealed similar performance on the A2 platform. On A3, Yamane's method achieved a lower individual-offset RMS value. The paper concludes that neither method shows universal superiority, noting that the proposed objective directly incorporates inter-foot translational and yaw channels.

**D. Non-Injection Held-Out Validation:**
Evaluating the calibrated offsets on held-out recordings (not used for optimization) demonstrated improved internal consistency. For A3, the pooled RMS foot-height residual decreased from 4.26 mm to 2.20 mm. For A2, it decreased from 8.03 mm to 1.43 mm (Figure 5). Similar improvements were observed for sole-normal residuals on both platforms. This indicates that the calibrated offsets improve the robot's adherence to the flat-ground assumption on unseen data.

**E. External Validation:**
- **LiDAR-Inertial Odometry:** An external check using LiDAR-inertial odometry (FAST-LIO2) was conducted to validate the pitch–vertical channel. When a +3 degree pitch offset was injected, calibration moved the leg-odometry trajectory closer to the LiDAR reference, reducing the vertical drift. The vertical sensitivity was quantified at 1.04 m per degree of hip-pitch offset. Over 16 held-out segments, the vertical RMS error of leg-odometry against LiDAR reduced by 25.5% (from 319 mm to 238 mm) (Figure 6, Table III).
- **Optical Rigid Body Registration:** For inter-foot orientation, an independent A3 test using registered optical rigid bodies showed a 21.8% reduction in held-out inter-foot orientation error, from 5.223 degrees to 4.085 degrees after calibration.

**F. Closed-Loop Simulation:**
Applying the estimated corrections to an A3 walking policy in free-standing MuJoCo simulation, without re-tuning the policy, significantly reduced foot-strike RMS deviations (from 1190.61 mm to 26.57 mm) and pelvis-trajectory RMS deviations (from 1224.10 mm to 27.16 mm) across 35 trials with a 3-degree offset. This suggests improved nominal tracking performance.

**G. Stance Diversity and Saturation:**
Analysis of A2 data showed that the majority of conditioning gain (reduction in $\kappa(H_{reg})$ from $1.27 \times 10^5$ to $6.19 \times 10^3$) came from adding the first diverse stance to a single squat (Table IV, Figure 7). Further additional stances led to saturation, indicating that a compact, multi-stance protocol (e.g., four stances) is efficient.

**H. Ablation Studies:**
Ablation tests indicated that regularization strength ($\lambda_q$) primarily affects individual pitch offsets in weakly observed directions without significantly changing the data fit. Removing the flat-ground constraint led to substantial shifts (2-5 degrees) in frontal and transverse hip offsets, highlighting the importance of out-of-plane information provided by the IMU. The A3 knee prior improved $\kappa(H_{reg})$ from 333 to 72.27, demonstrating its role in adding curvature to the regularized Hessian, rather than intrinsic data information.

**I. A3 Multi-Trial Repeatability:**
Over three rerun sessions on a real A3 machine, individual joint offset corrections exhibited standard deviations ranging from 0.104 to 1.050 degrees (Table V), indicating variability in individual offset estimates, especially for hip yaw. This reinforces the challenge of robustly estimating individual offsets within coupled chains without strong external priors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of humanoid robot calibration, addressing a critical practical limitation of deploying these complex machines. The primary significance lies in its **self-contained nature**, enabling lower-limb joint-offset calibration without reliance on external motion-capture systems, laser trackers, or visible fiducial targets. This is a crucial step towards making humanoid robots more autonomous and maintainable in diverse, unconstrained environments beyond controlled laboratory settings.

The **formal Hessian-based analysis of pitch-axis coupling** contributes to a deeper understanding of the observability challenges inherent in common humanoid leg designs. By identifying that parallel pitch axes (hip-pitch, knee, ankle-pitch) primarily constrain only their sum from orientation data, the work provides theoretical insights into why individual offsets in these chains can be difficult to determine robustly. This understanding is significant for:
- **Robot Design:** Guiding future kinematic designs to potentially improve calibration observability.
- **Excitation Design:** Informing the development of more effective calibration excitation trajectories, such as those emphasizing hip-roll and hip-yaw, to geometrically decouple pitch axes.
- **Algorithm Development:** Justifying the use of priors and regularization in specific, weakly-observed directions.

The **demonstrated performance** through extensive validation is also significant. The framework consistently recovers injected offsets in simulation and improves internal consistency on held-out real-robot data (reducing foot-height RMS residuals). The external validation with LiDAR-inertial odometry specifically confirming improved accuracy in the pitch–vertical channel, and closed-loop simulation showing improved locomotion tracking, suggests practical benefits for robot performance. The finding that a few diverse static double-support stances are sufficient for calibration makes the method **time-efficient and practical** for routine use.

The potential impact includes:
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** By allowing frequent, on-demand recalibration, the framework can maintain the accuracy of kinematic models over time, counteracting wear and tear or minor mechanical shifts. This directly translates to more reliable locomotion, better balance, and more precise contact interactions for humanoid robots in real-world scenarios.
- **Reduced Operational Costs:** Eliminating the need for specialized external equipment reduces the setup time, personnel requirements, and financial investment associated with traditional calibration.
- **Improved Safety and Performance:** Accurate kinematics are foundational for stable and safe robot operation. Better calibration can lead to more predictable robot behavior and enhanced task execution.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the prior-dependence of individual pitch offsets and the focus on contact-consistent corrections rather than absolute mechanical zeros, lay out clear directions for future research. This includes developing methods for absolute mechanical zero determination, improving translational accuracy, and extending validation to dynamic locomotion on physical robots.

In summary, this research offers a practical, theoretically-grounded approach to a fundamental problem in humanoid robotics, with the potential to significantly improve their deployability, reliability, and autonomy.
