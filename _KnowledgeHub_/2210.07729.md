# Model-Based Imitation Learning for Urban Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> MILE is a model-based imitation learning method that jointly learns a world model and a driving policy from offline expert video, with no reward and no online interaction. It lifts camera features to 3D, pools them into bird's-eye view, and compresses them into a 512-dim probabilistic latent state trained by variational inference. It improves CARLA driving score 31% over prior art in an unseen town.

## Problem

- Prior world models assume ground-truth rewards and online environment interaction, restricting them to simulated games and toy control rather than offline real-world driving corpora.
- Existing latent-dynamics methods operate on tiny 64x64 inputs, too coarse to perceive traffic lights and pedestrians that dictate urban driving decisions.
- Some driving models assume ego-actions do not influence other agents, and trajectory forecasters need privileged states or HD maps, ignoring the static scene.

## Method

- An observation encoder lifts image features into 3D via predicted depth distributions, sum-pools them onto a ==bird's-eye-view== grid, then compresses that grid to one 512-dim vector.
- A ==recurrent latent dynamics model== with deterministic history and stochastic state is trained by ==variational inference==; prior-posterior ==KL== matching couples action prediction with next-state prediction.
- ==StyleGAN==-style decoders reconstruct images and BeV segmentation from latent state, an MLP policy outputs acceleration and steering, and imagination rolls the prior forward using predicted actions.

## Results

- On unseen Town05 and weather, MILE scores **61.1** driving score versus **46.5** for LAV and **41.6** for Roach, using only front-camera RGB.
- Ablations: removing 3D lifting drops cumulative reward from **7621** to **4564**; dropping prior-posterior KL drops it to **6084**; 75x120 inputs collapse driving score to **20.9**.
- Fully recurrent deployment matches reset-state performance (**62.1** vs **61.1**) at **43.0Hz** versus **6.2Hz**, and driving score holds while imagining up to **30%** of the time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Geometric inductive bias beats raw capacity: compressing in bird's-eye-view space rather than image space is what makes a compact latent world model learnable.
> - Bigger latent states hurt: spatial 3D latents degraded reward monotonically, since non-stationary prior-posterior matching gets harder as dimensionality grows, especially under long recurrent deployment.
> - Reward-free offline world models can still yield actionable plans, but this one leans on BeV segmentation labels; self-supervision remains needed for real-world scaling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several critical areas in artificial intelligence and robotics, particularly relevant to autonomous driving: **imitation learning**, **3D scene representation**, and **world modelling**.

- **Imitation Learning (IL) for Autonomous Driving:** Traditionally, autonomous driving systems were modular, breaking down the problem into perception, planning, and control. However, end-to-end imitation learning, where a neural network learns directly from expert demonstrations to map raw sensor inputs to driving actions, has gained significant traction since early work like ALVINN (Pomerleau, 1988). Recent approaches like CILRS, LBC, and TransFuser have advanced this field. However, pure imitation learning often struggles with distribution shift (where the learned policy encounters states unseen in the training data) and robustness to varied scenarios. This paper addresses these limitations by moving "past pure imitation learning" by integrating a world model.
- **World Models in Reinforcement Learning (RL):** World models, which learn a compressed, predictive representation of the environment's dynamics, have shown great success in model-based RL (e.g., Dreamer, PlaNet, AlphaZero). These models typically assume access to a ground-truth reward function and often require online interaction with the environment. MILE differentiates itself by applying world models to an *imitation learning* setting, meaning it **does not require a reward function** and is trained **purely offline** on expert demonstrations. This is a crucial distinction, as acquiring reward signals in complex real-world driving can be challenging, and online interaction is unsafe and impractical for initial deployment.
- **3D Scene Representation for Autonomous Driving:** Understanding 3D geometry is fundamental for safe navigation. Prior methods often rely on explicit depth sensors (LiDAR) or multiple camera views with complex geometric priors. This paper, being **camera-only**, tackles the challenge of inferring 3D structure from monocular inputs by incorporating "3D geometry as an inductive bias." This aligns with recent work like "Lift, Splat, Shoot" (Philion and Fidler, 2020) and BEV-based approaches, which convert image features into a Bird's-Eye View (BeV) representation, a more natural space for planning. MILE leverages this by lifting image features to 3D and pooling them into a BeV representation.
- **Trajectory Forecasting:** While related to predicting future states of dynamic agents, MILE's world model is more comprehensive. Unlike many trajectory forecasting methods that focus on predicting *other* agents' movements based on past states and HD maps, MILE jointly reasons about the *static scene*, *dynamic scene*, and *ego-behaviour* (how the agent's actions affect the environment and others), all from camera-only inputs and without explicit HD maps. This holistic world model is a significant advancement.

In summary, MILE pushes the state-of-the-art by combining the strengths of model-based learning with imitation learning, specifically for the complex domain of urban driving. It uniquely operates on high-resolution camera data, leverages 3D geometric inductive biases for robust scene understanding, and enables robust planning through "imagination," all while being trained offline without explicit rewards.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the MILE (Model-based Imitation LEarning) approach are:

- **Jointly Learn a World Model and a Driving Policy:** The core objective is to develop a single, integrated framework that not only learns to control an autonomous vehicle (the policy) but also builds an internal, predictive representation of its environment (the world model). This mimics human cognitive processes, where an understanding of the world informs actions.
- **Leverage 3D Geometry as an Inductive Bias:** Autonomous driving is inherently a 3D problem. The authors aim to explicitly encode 3D geometric understanding into the model's architecture. This is motivated by the belief that a model explicitly reasoning about 3D space will be more robust and performant than purely 2D image-based methods, especially for complex urban scenarios.
- **Operate on High-Resolution Visual Inputs:** Unlike many prior world models that simplify visual inputs (e.g., 64x64 pixel images), MILE is designed to handle high-resolution camera observations (600x960 pixels). This is crucial for urban driving, where small details like traffic lights, pedestrians, and lane markings, which are critical for safe decision-making, can only be reliably perceived at higher resolutions.
- **Train Solely on Offline Expert Demonstrations:** A significant motivation is to avoid the need for online interaction with the environment, which is often dangerous, time-consuming, and expensive for autonomous vehicles. Additionally, MILE aims to operate without explicit access to a ground-truth reward function, a common requirement for model-based RL, making it more applicable to real-world data collection where only expert actions are typically available.
- **Predict Diverse and Plausible Futures:** A robust world model should be able to predict a distribution of possible future states and actions, reflecting the inherent uncertainty and multi-modality of driving scenarios (e.g., multiple safe paths, varying responses from other agents). This capability allows for more nuanced and adaptable planning.
- **Enable Planning in Imagination:** By learning a predictive world model, the system should be able to "imagine" future scenarios and consequences of its actions without needing to execute them in the real world. This capability is vital for complex maneuvers, handling occlusions, or planning ahead for safety.
- **Achieve State-of-the-Art Performance:** Ultimately, the system must demonstrate superior driving performance compared to existing methods, especially in challenging, unseen environments and weather conditions within a high-fidelity simulator like CARLA.
- **Efficiency for Deployment:** The model should be computationally efficient enough for real-time deployment, leveraging recurrent mechanisms to maintain a single, efficiently updated state.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MILE proposes a Model-based Imitation Learning architecture that jointly learns a world model and a driving policy. It operates as a variational recurrent neural network that processes sequences of observations and actions.

#### 3.1. Probabilistic Generative Model

The model defines a generative process for sequences of observations ($o_{1:T}$), expert actions ($a_{1:T}$), and Bird's-Eye View (BeV) semantic segmentation labels ($y_{1:T}$). This process is governed by a sequence of latent variables ($s_{1:T}$) and deterministic history states ($h_{1:T}$).
- **Latent States:** The core idea is that the world's evolution is described by these latent variables. $h_t$ represents a deterministic history, updated by a gated recurrent cell ($f_\theta$), incorporating the previous history and stochastic state. $s_t$ is a stochastic state, sampled from a Gaussian distribution whose parameters ($\mu_\theta, \sigma_\theta$) are conditioned on the deterministic history ($h_t$) and the previous action ($a_{t-1}$).
- **Decoders:** From the current latent state ($h_t, s_t$), the model predicts:
 - **Observations ($o_t$):** An image decoder ($g_\theta$) reconstructs the input RGB image.
 - **BeV Semantic Segmentation ($y_t$):** A BeV decoder ($l_\theta$) reconstructs the semantic segmentation in bird's-eye view. This provides a rich, geometrically grounded supervision signal.
 - **Actions ($a_t$):** A policy network ($\pi_\theta$) predicts the expert's action, modeled as a Laplace distribution.

#### 3.2. Variational Inference

To learn the latent variables given the observed data, MILE employs deep variational inference.
- **Variational Distribution:** A variational distribution $q(h_{1:T}, s_{1:T} | o_{1:T}, a_{1:T-1})$ is introduced to approximate the true posterior distribution of the latent states.
- **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):** The model is trained by maximizing a variational lower bound on the log evidence of the observed data. This loss function comprises two main terms:
 - **Reconstruction Terms:** Encourage the generative model to accurately reconstruct the observed images ($o_t$), BeV segmentation labels ($y_t$), and expert actions ($a_t$) from the inferred latent states. These correspond to Mean Squared Error for images, Cross-Entropy for BeV segmentation, and L1 loss for actions.
 - **KL Divergence Term:** Penalizes the divergence between the variational posterior $q(s_t|o_{\le t}, a_{<t})$ and the prior $p(s_t|h_{t-1}, s_{t-1})$. This term ensures that the prior (what the model *thinks* will happen) learns to accurately predict the distribution of the posterior (what *actually* happened), thereby learning meaningful dynamics. Crucially, the prior uses the *predicted* action $\hat{a}_{t-1} = \pi_\theta(h_{t-1}, s_{t-1})$ because it does not have access to the ground-truth action in a predictive setting.

#### 3.3. Inference Network (φ)

This network is responsible for encoding observations and inferring the posterior distribution of the stochastic state.
- **Observation Encoder ($e_\phi$):**
 - **3D Geometry Inductive Bias:** This is a key component. High-resolution RGB images are first processed by an image encoder (ResNet18 backbone). The resulting image features are then "lifted" to 3D by predicting a depth probability distribution for each feature, following methods like "Lift, Splat, Shoot."
 - **Pooling to BeV:** These 3D features are then sum-pooled into a compact Bird's-Eye View (BeV) grid, providing a geometrically coherent representation of the scene.
 - **Compression to 1D Vector:** The BeV feature map is further compressed into a low-dimensional 1D vector. This compression is critical for the world model to learn compact, manageable dynamics, and ablation studies show it's more effective than direct image space compression.
- **Auxiliary Inputs:** The observation embedding also incorporates features from a "route map" (a grayscale image indicating the goal) and the current vehicle speed.
- **Posterior Network:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) takes the deterministic history, previous action, and the comprehensive observation embedding to output the parameters ($\mu_\phi, \sigma_\phi$) of the Gaussian posterior distribution $q(s_t)$.

#### 3.4. Generative Network (θ)

This network defines the dynamics and generative process.
- **Recurrent Cell ($f_\theta$):** A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) updates the deterministic history $h_{t+1}$ based on $h_t$ and $s_t$.
- **Prior Network:** This network, similar to the posterior, predicts the parameters ($\mu_\theta, \sigma_\theta$) of the prior distribution $p(s_t)$, but crucially uses the *predicted action* from the policy network.
- **Decoders ($g_\theta, l_\theta$):** The image and BeV decoders utilize a StyleGAN-like architecture, progressively upsampling a learned constant tensor and injecting the latent state via adaptive instance normalization to modulate predictions at different resolutions.
- **Policy Network ($\pi_\theta$):** A simple MLP that takes the current history ($h_t$) and stochastic state ($s_t$) to predict the ego-vehicle's action (acceleration and steering).

#### 3.5. Imagining Future States and Actions

A key capability of MILE is its ability to predict future states and actions in "imagination." After encoding initial observations, the model can iteratively:
1. Use its learned policy ($\pi_\theta$) to predict the next action ($\hat{a}_{T+i}$).
2. Use the recurrent cell ($f_\theta$) to update the deterministic history ($h_{T+i+1}$).
3. Sample from the learned prior distribution ($p(s_{T+i+1})$) using the updated history and predicted action.
This process generates sequences of future latent states and actions that can then be decoded (e.g., to BeV semantic segmentation) for visualization and planning.

**Training Details:** The model is trained on an offline corpus of urban driving data collected in the CARLA simulator (2.9M frames, 32 hours of data) from an expert RL agent. Observations include RGB images, route maps, speed, expert actions, and BeV semantic segmentation. A novel "observation dropout" mechanism is used during training, where the stochastic state is sometimes sampled from the prior instead of the posterior, encouraging robust multi-step predictions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

MILE demonstrates significant advancements in model-based imitation learning for urban driving, evidenced by both quantitative and qualitative results.

#### 4.1. Driving Performance (CARLA Simulator)

- **State-of-the-Art:** MILE achieves a **31% relative improvement in driving score** over previous state-of-the-art methods (e.g., LAV) on the challenging CARLA simulator, specifically when tested in a completely **new town (Town05) and new weather conditions**.
- **Camera-Only Advantage:** Notably, MILE outperforms approaches that utilize additional sensor information like LiDAR (e.g., TransFuser, LAV), demonstrating the effectiveness of its camera-only approach combined with 3D geometric inductive biases.
- **High Cumulative Reward:** It significantly boosts the cumulative reward compared to models trained on the same dataset (e.g., Roach), closely approaching the performance of the privileged expert. This metric provides a more granular assessment of driving quality at the timestep level.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Importance of 3D Geometry:** Ablations confirmed the critical role of 3D geometry. Models without the 3D lifting step showed a **significant drop in cumulative reward** (e.g., from 7621 to 4564 for MILE, and 6084 to 1878 for a single-frame model without 3D). This highlights that explicitly modeling 3D scene understanding is crucial for robust urban driving.
- **Value of Probabilistic Modelling:** Removing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss (i.e., "No prior/post. matching"), which is essential for probabilistic modeling of diverse behaviors, resulted in a **drop in cumulative reward** (from 7621 to 6084). This supports the argument that modeling the distribution of possible futures, rather than just a deterministic mean, leads to safer and more adaptable policies.
- **Segmentation Supervision:** The BeV semantic segmentation loss, while not directly controlling the vehicle, provides valuable supervision, as its removal also led to a drop in performance.

#### 4.3. Fully Recurrent Inference for Deployment

- **Efficiency and Robustness:** MILE can be deployed in a **fully recurrent manner**, where the latent state is continuously updated without being reset. This mode achieves comparable driving performance to the "reset state" approach while being **7 times more computationally efficient** (43.0 Hz vs. 6.2 Hz).
- **Active Use of Context:** Introducing Gaussian noise to the past latent state in the recurrent mode significantly decreased performance, demonstrating that the model actively utilizes past context and does not simply discard historical information, indicating it has learned to generalize its internal state across long horizons.

#### 4.4. Long Horizon, Diverse Future Predictions

- **Multi-Modal Futures:** The model can predict diverse and plausible future states and actions, which can be interpretably decoded to BeV semantic segmentation. Figure 2 qualitatively illustrates this, showing different imagined scenarios (e.g., driving through a green light versus stopping for a light turning red).

#### 4.5. Insights from the World Model

- **Latent State Dimension:** Counter-intuitively, increasing the dimensionality of the latent state (from a 512-dim 1D vector to larger 3D spatial tensors) led to a **decrease in driving performance**. This suggests that optimizing the prior and posterior distributions in higher-dimensional latent spaces is more challenging, hindering the learning of optimal dynamics. Furthermore, the performance gap between "reset state" and "fully recurrent" deployment significantly widened for higher-dimensional latent states, indicating that learning to effectively manage and update context over long horizons is harder with larger latent spaces.
- **Driving in Imagination:** The model demonstrates the ability to execute accurate driving plans entirely from **imagination**, even without access to visual observations for extended periods. Quantitatively, it retained the same driving performance with up to **30% of the drive in imagining mode** (Appendix A.1). This capability is analogous to how humans navigate briefly blinded by sun glare, filling in missing information. Qualitatively, it enables complex maneuvers like negotiating a roundabout or avoiding obstacles from an imagined plan.

#### 4.6. Image Resolution

- **Critical for Safety:** An analysis of different image resolutions revealed a significant performance drop at lower resolutions. Crucially, infractions (running red lights, vehicle/pedestrian collisions) **increased dramatically** as resolution decreased. This underscores the importance of high-resolution images for reliably detecting small but critical scene elements like traffic lights and pedestrians, highlighting a practical requirement for real-world self-driving systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MILE represents a substantial stride towards developing more intelligent, robust, and deployable autonomous driving systems. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key innovations:

- **Bridging Model-Based RL and Imitation Learning:** By successfully applying world models to an offline imitation learning setting, MILE overcomes two major hurdles for autonomous driving: the need for explicit reward functions and the safety/cost implications of online interaction. This makes the approach highly practical for leveraging vast amounts of pre-recorded human driving data without complex reward engineering.
- **Enhanced Robustness through 3D Understanding:** The explicit incorporation of 3D geometric inductive biases, derived solely from monocular cameras, enables a richer and more robust understanding of the urban environment. This moves beyond purely pixel-level imitation, grounding the policy in a more physically meaningful representation, which is critical for generalization to novel situations.
- **Human-like Reasoning via Imagination:** The ability to "drive in imagination" is a profound step towards more human-like reasoning in AI. By simulating future scenarios internally, the vehicle can plan ahead, anticipate potential problems, and make more informed decisions, even when visual information is temporarily incomplete or ambiguous. This could lead to safer and more proactive driving behaviors.
- **Interpretability and Debuggability:** The capacity to decode latent states into interpretable Bird's-Eye View semantic segmentation provides valuable insights into the model's internal understanding and predictions. This interpretability is crucial for debugging, auditing, and building trust in autonomous systems.
- **Scalability for Real-World Deployment:** The demonstrated efficiency of the fully recurrent inference mechanism is vital for real-time operation in production autonomous vehicles. The model's ability to maintain a consistent internal state over long horizons without requiring frequent resets is a practical advantage.
- **Reduced Sensor Dependency:** Achieving state-of-the-art performance with only camera inputs has significant economic implications. It suggests that highly capable autonomous driving systems might be developed with less expensive sensor suites, potentially accelerating widespread adoption.

**Potential Future Work and Impact:**
The authors themselves point to exciting future directions:
- **Inferring Reward Functions:** Learning the driving reward function from expert data could unlock explicit planning capabilities within the world model, allowing the system to optimize for safety and efficiency more directly.
- **Self-Supervision:** Relaxing the dependency on BeV semantic segmentation labels through self-supervised learning would further reduce annotation costs and make the approach even more applicable to raw, unlabeled real-world data, fully unlocking its potential for real-world driving and other robotics tasks.

In conclusion, MILE represents a significant advancement in the field of autonomous driving, particularly for its innovative combination of model-based learning, offline imitation, and 3D geometric understanding from monocular cameras. Its ability to learn robust dynamics and plan in imagination holds immense promise for developing safer, more efficient, and ultimately, more intelligent autonomous vehicles.
