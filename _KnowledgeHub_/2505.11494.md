# SHIELD: Safety on Humanoids via CBFs In Expectation on Learned Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Caltech and UT Austin developed SHIELD, a layered safety framework for humanoid robots that integrates learned dynamics uncertainties with probabilistic Control Barrier Functions. The approach allows high-performing learning-based controllers to operate safely by modulating high-level reference signals, demonstrating robust collision avoidance and improved tracking on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot in both simulated and unstructured real-world environments.

## Problem

- Learning-based robot controllers excel in performance but often lack formal safety guarantees, limiting their deployment in safety-critical, human-centric environments.
- Current constraint management for learned policies is inflexible, requiring extensive and costly retraining for any modification or addition of safety constraints.
- Traditional model-based safety methods like Control Barrier Functions rely on accurate dynamics models, often impractical for complex, high-dimensional systems like humanoids, and existing frameworks struggle to robustly accommodate the significant, non-Gaussian uncertainties of unstructured environments.

## Method

- SHIELD implements a layered safety architecture that modulates high-level reference signals generated by a nominal learning-based controller, rather than directly interfering with low-level control outputs.
- A Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) is trained on real-world robot data to learn the distribution of dynamics residuals, which represent the discrepancy between a simplified reduced-order model and the robot's actual behavior.
- Probabilistic safety is enforced through a Stochastic Discrete-Time Control Barrier Function (S-DTCBF) formulated as an online Quadratic Program, which uses the learned mean and covariance of the dynamics residual to provide guarantees on the K-step exit probability.

## Results

- In simulations, SHIELD demonstrated adaptive conservativeness, with observed collision rates remaining below specified target probabilities while balancing travel distance.
- Hardware experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot showed improved velocity tracking, with actual velocities more closely matching desired commands compared to baseline control.
- SHIELD consistently achieved collision avoidance in controlled obstacle scenarios, outperforming a nominal DTCBF filter that either collided or increased task time, and was successfully deployed outdoors to navigate and avoid pedestrians using only onboard sensing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning and explicitly incorporating the distribution of dynamics residuals allows a safety layer to robustly compensate for unmodeled dynamics and uncertainties from opaque, performant controllers.
> - Modulating high-level reference signals offers a minimally invasive method for integrating safety, preserving the performance of existing learning-based policies without requiring their retraining.
> - The integration of generative models (CVAEs) with stochastic CBFs enables adaptive conservativeness, providing a tunable trade-off between task performance and the quantified probability of safety constraint violation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in modern robotics: the tension between the high performance of learning-based controllers, particularly in complex tasks like dynamic locomotion for humanoids, and the difficulty in providing formal safety guarantees for such "black-box" systems. Contemporary robot learning methods, such as Reinforcement Learning (RL), often embed constraints heuristically through reward engineering, which necessitates expensive retraining if constraints need to be added or modified. This approach lacks the flexibility and formal assurances desired for robots operating in dynamic, human-centric environments.

Traditional model-based safety methods, exemplified by Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), offer runtime constraint specification and formal guarantees. However, their efficacy is contingent upon accurate dynamics models of both the robot and its environment, which are frequently unavailable or impractical to obtain for high-dimensional, complex systems like humanoid robots. Existing attempts to bridge this gap include conformal prediction, which can become computationally intractable, and "backup" control strategies that either inherit the unpredictability of learned controllers or rely on computationally intensive optimal control techniques (e.g., Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations) that also demand precise dynamics models. Reduced-order models with CBFs have been used, but they assume accurate tracking of reference signals, an assumption often violated in practice due to unmodeled dynamics and disturbances.

SHIELD contributes to the field by proposing a layered safety framework that integrates data-driven modeling of system uncertainties with formal, probabilistic safety guarantees. It departs from traditional CBF applications by acting as a safety layer *above* the nominal learning-based controller, modulating high-level reference signals rather than directly filtering low-level control outputs. This design choice aims to offer a minimally invasive solution that can be integrated into existing autonomy stacks without requiring retraining of the underlying performant, yet opaque, controllers. By using a generative model to characterize stochastic dynamics residuals from real-world data, SHIELD attempts to provide robust safety guarantees that account for the inherent uncertainties of learned controllers and complex robotic systems operating in unstructured environments, thereby bridging the gap between data-driven performance and model-based safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the SHIELD framework is to provide probabilistic safety guarantees for complex robotic systems, particularly humanoids, that are controlled by high-performing yet "black-box" learning-based policies. This objective is motivated by several critical challenges in robot deployment:

1. **Ensuring Dynamic Safety for Learning-Based Controllers:** Modern learning-based controllers, especially those derived from Reinforcement Learning (RL), have demonstrated remarkable success in achieving agile and dynamic behaviors for robots, such as bipedal and quadrupedal locomotion. However, their data-driven, black-box nature makes it difficult to ascertain and formally guarantee dynamic safety (e.g., collision avoidance, stability) in the face of uncertainties and varying environmental conditions. This lack of formal guarantees restricts their deployment in safety-critical applications, particularly when robots operate in shared human spaces.

2. **Addressing Inflexibility of Current Constraint Management:** For RL controllers, embedding constraints typically involves extensive reward engineering. Any modification or addition of new safety constraints often necessitates a complete retraining of the policy, which is a time-consuming and computationally expensive process. This approach is not adaptive or flexible enough for real-world scenarios where safety requirements might evolve or need to be specified ad hoc.

3. **Overcoming Limitations of Model-Based Safety Methods:** Traditional model-based safety approaches, such as Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), can provide formal guarantees but rely on accurate and often high-fidelity models of robot dynamics and environmental interactions. For complex, high-dimensional systems like humanoids, obtaining such accurate models is often impractical. When reduced-order models are employed, their effectiveness is predicated on the assumption of accurate tracking of reference signals, an assumption frequently violated by real-world system disturbances and the inherent variability of learned controllers.

4. **Managing Stochasticity and Real-World Uncertainty:** Robot operation in unstructured environments is inherently stochastic due to unmodeled dynamics, sensor noise, perception errors, and the complex, often unpredictable, outputs of learning-based controllers. Existing safety frameworks often struggle to provide robust guarantees under such significant and non-Gaussian uncertainties. The motivation is to develop a method that can explicitly account for and leverage learned disturbance distributions to provide probabilistic assurances of safety.

5. **Minimally Invasive Integration:** A key motivation is to create a safety layer that can be added to an existing, performant autonomy stack without requiring modifications to or retraining of the underlying nominal controller. This "minimally-invasive" approach aims to preserve the performance benefits of advanced learned policies while layering on safety. SHIELD proposes to achieve this by modulating high-level reference signals, rather than directly interfering with the low-level control outputs.

In summary, the paper aims to enable the safe, robust, and adaptive deployment of advanced learning-based robotic systems by combining the strengths of data-driven uncertainty modeling with the formal rigor of stochastic control barrier functions, addressing the critical need for reliable safety guarantees in complex, uncertain real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SHIELD framework is a layered safety architecture designed to provide probabilistic safety guarantees for robots controlled by complex, possibly "black-box," learning-based policies. It operates by modulating high-level reference signals rather than directly filtering control outputs. The methodology consists of a three-step process: constraint specification, dynamics residual learning, and safety-aware reference generation.

#### 3.1. Constraint Specification

The first step involves the user defining a safety constraint mathematically as a 0-superlevel set of a function $h: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \to \mathbb{R}$, such that the system state $x$ is safe if $h(x) \geq 0$. This constraint is defined on a reduced-order model of the robot's state (e.g., the position and orientation of the torso, represented by $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ where $n_x < n_s$, $n_s$ being the full-order state dimension). The underlying low-level controller (e.g., an RL locomotion policy) is not required to be trained for this specific constraint but is assumed to be capable of tracking general reference commands provided to this reduced-order model.

#### 3.2. Dynamics Residual Learning

This step focuses on characterizing the uncertainties and unmodeled dynamics between a simplified reduced-order model and the actual robot's behavior when executing commands from the nominal controller.
The system's discrete-time dynamics for the reduced-order model are approximated as:
$x_{k+1} \approx F(x_k) + G(x_k)u_k + d_k$
where $F(x_k) + G(x_k)u_k$ is a simplified, usually linearizable, model (e.g., a single integrator model for navigation), and $d_k$ represents the "dynamics residual"—the discrepancy between the simplified model's prediction and the actual system's evolution. This residual $d_k$ is treated as a random disturbance, reflecting the complexities of the full-order dynamics and the learning-based controller $\pi$.

To learn the distribution of $d_k$, a dataset of real-world state, command, and disturbance tuples is collected from hardware rollouts of the nominal controller. A Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) is then trained on this dataset to approximate the generative disturbance model $p_\theta(d_k | x_{k:k-N}, u_{k:k-N})$. Unlike previous approaches, SHIELD conditions the CVAE on a context window of past states and commands (length $N$, e.g., $N=4$) to capture temporal effects and improve modeling accuracy. This CVAE allows for the capture of complex, non-Gaussian, and state-dependent uncertainty patterns.

#### 3.3. Safety-Aware Reference Generation

This step utilizes the learned dynamics residual distribution to simultaneously improve tracking performance and enforce probabilistic safety.

**Stochastic Tracking with Learned Disturbance:**
SHIELD first calculates an optimal input $u^*_k$ that minimizes the expected mean squared error between the desired next state $\bar{x}_{k+1}$ and the actual system's next state, considering the learned disturbance. This is formulated as:
$u^*_k = \operatorname{argmin}_{u_k \in \mathcal{U}} E[||\bar{x}_{k+1} - (F(x_k) + G(x_k)u_k + d_k)||² | \mathcal{F}_k]$
By approximating $E[d_k | \mathcal{F}_k]$ with the mean disturbance from the CVAE, $E_{p_\theta}[d_k | x_{k:k-N}, u_{k:k-N}]$, the optimal tracking command becomes:
$u^*_{k} = G^\dagger(x_k)(-F(x_k) + \bar{x}_{k+1} - E_{p_\theta}[d_k | x_{k:k-N}, u_{k:k-N}])$.
This calculation provides an "adjusted" command, $u_{adjusted}$, that inherently compensates for predictable parts of the dynamics residual to enhance tracking.

**Safety with Learned Disturbance (S-DTCBF):**
To enforce safety, SHIELD imposes a Stochastic Discrete-Time Control Barrier Function (S-DTCBF) constraint in expectation: $E[h(x_{k+1}) | \mathcal{F}_k] \geq \alpha h(x_k)$, where $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is a parameter determining the conservativeness.
A maximum allowable K-step exit probability, $P \in (0,1)$, is specified by the user. Using Freedman's Inequality for Stochastic Safety, SHIELD numerically solves for the corresponding $\alpha$ parameter that bounds this probability, given the horizon length $K$, initial safety value $h(x_0)$, a bound $\delta$ on the difference between true and predicted safety update (derived from maximum step distance), and a variance bound $\sigma$ (approximated from the dataset). This $\alpha$ value is dynamically updated every $K$ steps to extend the probabilistic guarantee over the entire operating horizon.

To ensure computational tractability for enforcing the S-DTCBF constraint within an online Quadratic Program (QP), Jensen's inequality is applied for concave safety functions $h$. This transforms the expectation constraint into a tightened deterministic inequality:
$h(F(x_k) + G(x_k)u_k + E_{p_\theta}[d_k | x_{k:k-N}, u_{k:k-N}]) - \frac{\lambda_{max}}{2}tr(cov_{p_\theta}(d_k | x_{k:k-N}, u_{k:k-N})) \geq \alpha h(x_k)$
where $\lambda_{max}$ bounds the Hessian of the safety function. The CVAE provides the estimated mean and covariance of the disturbance $d_k$.

Finally, a QP is formulated to find the control input $u^*_{safe}$ that minimally deviates from the tracking-optimized command $u_{adjusted}$ while satisfying this tightened S-DTCBF constraint:
$u^*_{safe} = \operatorname{argmin}_{u \in \mathcal{U}} ||u - u_{adjusted}||^2$
s.t. $h(F(x_k) + G(x_k)u_k + E_{p_\theta}[d_k | \dots]) - \frac{\lambda_{max}}{2}e_i^T cov_{p_\theta}(d_k | \dots)e_i \geq \alpha h(x_k)$
where $e_i$ is the unit vector towards the closest obstacle.

#### 3.4. Application to Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance

For humanoid robot navigation, the reduced-order model is defined as a planar single integrator system for $x = [p_x, p_y, \theta]^T$ and $u = [v_x, v_y, \omega]^T$. Obstacle avoidance constraints are formulated using a smoothed signed distance function (SDF) to detected obstacles, which is further approximated as a concave function $\tilde{h}(x)$ for applying Jensen's inequality and Theorem 2, specific to single-obstacle avoidance. The framework is deployed with an onboard Jetson Orin NX, Livox Mid-360 LiDAR for obstacle detection via Euclidean clustering, and an Intel T265 for localization. The system allows for flexibility with different nominal walking controllers (e.g., Unitree built-in or custom RL policy).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental validation of the SHIELD framework through both simulation and hardware experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. The results demonstrate the method's ability to provide adaptive conservativeness, improve tracking, and ensure robust safety in diverse environments.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

In a simplified simulation involving a single integrator system perturbed by a multivariate Student's t-distribution with clipped tails, SHIELD demonstrated its capacity for adaptive conservativeness. By adjusting the desired probability of K-step exit (P), the framework allowed for a controllable trade-off between performance (total distance traveled) and safety (collision frequency). Higher exit probabilities correlated with more aggressive behavior and increased travel distance, but also a higher chance of collision, confirming the utility of P as a tuning parameter for balancing risk and performance. Despite encouraging more "risky" behavior, the observed collision rates remained below the specified target percentages.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments on Unitree G1 Humanoid

Hardware experiments were conducted on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, equipped with a Jetson Orin NX, Livox Mid-360 LiDAR, and Intel T265 for onboard computation, sensing, and localization. The CVAE was trained using approximately 6 minutes of real-world data for both the robot's built-in controller and a custom RL locomotion controller. The CVAE was queried at 0.83 Hz for disturbance mean and covariance, and command velocities were filtered at 100 Hz.

**Learned Tracking Performance:**
SHIELD demonstrated noticeable improvements in velocity tracking. By using the learned dynamics residual to adjust nominal commands, the robot's actual velocities more closely matched the desired commanded velocities compared to scenarios without SHIELD's correction. This indicates that the framework successfully leveraged the CVAE to compensate for system uncertainties and improve control accuracy.

**Controlled Obstacle Avoidance Experiments:**
Experiments in controlled environments with fixed obstacles highlighted SHIELD's effectiveness in collision avoidance. Success was defined as the robot navigating past obstacles without making physical contact.
- **Naive A* Planning:** Simply following an A* planned path often resulted in collisions or scrapes due to tracking errors inherent in the robot's locomotion.
- **Nominal DTCBF:** A traditional Discrete-Time Control Barrier Function (DTCBF) filter, which does not account for the dynamics residual, either behaved too aggressively, leading to collisions, or was overly conservative, significantly increasing task completion time (nearly double the time) to avoid risk.
- **SHIELD Performance:** SHIELD enabled the robot to consistently bypass obstacles, demonstrating its ability to enforce safety conditions while adapting its conservativeness online. This resulted in more dynamic and efficient obstacle avoidance compared to the nominal DTCBF, ensuring safety without excessive performance degradation. Similar effective behavior was observed in multi-obstacle scenarios.

**Unstructured Outdoor Environment Deployment:**
To validate real-world applicability, SHIELD was deployed in unstructured outdoor environments. In these tests, a human operator provided high-level joystick inputs, either directing the robot towards people or allowing it to remain stationary unless people encroached upon its safety boundary. The robot, using only onboard perception to detect obstacles (including humans), successfully navigated and avoided collisions with pedestrians. This demonstrates the robustness and generalization capabilities of SHIELD in dynamic and uncertain real-world scenarios, leveraging onboard sensing for human avoidance.

Overall, the findings indicate that SHIELD effectively combines the performance of learned controllers with probabilistic safety guarantees, outperforming traditional safety filters that do not account for learned dynamics residuals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SHIELD framework presents a notable contribution to the field of robotics, particularly in the domain of safe robot deployment and human-robot interaction. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Enabling Safe Deployment of Learning-Based Robotics:** SHIELD addresses a critical bottleneck in the widespread deployment of advanced learning-based controllers, especially for humanoids. By providing formal, probabilistic safety guarantees, it helps overcome the "black-box" nature of RL policies, allowing their high performance to be leveraged in safety-critical applications where interaction with humans or sensitive environments is unavoidable. This is paramount for the future of service robots, autonomous vehicles, and industrial collaborators.

2. **Flexible and Adaptive Constraint Management:** Unlike existing methods that require expensive retraining of policies for every change in safety constraints, SHIELD offers a modular and minimally invasive solution. Its layered architecture, which modulates high-level reference signals, allows for runtime specification and modification of safety constraints without altering the underlying learning-based controller. This flexibility is crucial for robots operating in dynamic, unpredictable environments where safety requirements may change instantaneously.

3. **Improved Real-World Performance and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Beyond mere safety, SHIELD actively utilizes learned dynamics residuals to improve the robot's tracking performance. By explicitly modeling and compensating for the discrepancies between simplified models and real-world system behavior, it effectively bridges the "sim-to-real gap." This direct performance improvement, alongside safety, enhances the overall robustness and reliability of robot operation in diverse conditions.

4. **Advancement in Stochastic Control Barrier Functions (CBFs):** The work extends the applicability of discrete-time CBFs to systems with significant and complex stochastic uncertainties, whose distributions are learned from data rather than assumed. By integrating generative models (CVAEs) with S-DTCBFs and leveraging tools like Jensen's inequality and Freedman's Inequality, SHIELD provides a practical methodology for synthesizing risk-aware controllers that can quantify and bound finite-time exit probabilities. This pushes the theoretical and practical boundaries of CBF research.

5. **Practical Real-World Applicability on Complex Humanoids:** The successful hardware experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, including pedestrian avoidance in unstructured outdoor environments using onboard perception, demonstrate the framework's practical feasibility and robustness. This is a significant step towards realizing safe, autonomous humanoid operation in human-populated spaces, which is a long-standing grand challenge in robotics.

6. **Adaptive Conservatism for Task Optimization:** The ability to tune the desired risk level (via the probability $P$ and parameter $\alpha$) allows system designers to balance safety with performance requirements. In scenarios where a higher risk might be acceptable for achieving a faster or more agile task, the system can be configured accordingly, providing a powerful knob for optimizing robot behavior across different operational contexts.

In summary, SHIELD represents a significant step towards developing trustworthy autonomous systems. By rigorously addressing the integration of high-performance learned behaviors with formal safety guarantees under uncertainty, it lays foundational work for safer human-robot collaboration and broader deployment of intelligent robots in complex, real-world environments.
