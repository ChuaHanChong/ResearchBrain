# RaceVLA: VLA-based Racing Drone Navigation with Human-like Behaviour

> [!summary] Summary
> RaceVLA applies Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to autonomous racing drones, enabling navigation through natural language commands and visual inputs. The system, developed by researchers at Skoltech, achieved stable flight patterns with an average velocity of 1.04 m/s and demonstrated strong generalization capabilities compared to existing VLA models.

## Problem

- Autonomous drone navigation, especially for high-speed applications like drone racing, is challenging due to dynamic environments and the need for split-second decisions.
- Traditional drone autonomy methods, such as planning, trajectory generation, or reinforcement learning, often lack the adaptability and responsiveness required for high-speed, complex racing scenarios.
- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, while promising for robotics, had limited application in drone technology, leaving a gap in human-like, natural language-driven drone control.

## Method

- **RaceVLA** adapts and fine-tunes the OpenVLA model, based on the LLaMA2 7B large language model, for autonomous racing drones.
- The system takes first-person view (FPV) video frames and natural language instructions as input through a modified VLA architecture.
- It outputs a 4D action vector consisting of three linear velocities (Vx, Vy, Vz) and yaw angular speed (ω) for direct, end-to-end drone control.

## Results

- RaceVLA achieved stable flight patterns with an average velocity of **1.04** m/s and a maximum speed of **2.02** m/s during racing tasks.
- The system successfully executed various tasks, including navigating through single gates, square gates, and multi-gate circular track formations.
- RaceVLA demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, outperforming RT-2-X across all tested axes and showing superior performance over OpenVLA in motion (**75.0%** vs. **60.0%**) and semantic (**45.5%** vs. **36.3%**) generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, previously applied to robotic manipulators, can be effectively adapted for real-time, high-speed drone control.
> - An end-to-end VLA approach allows direct mapping from visual and language inputs to control actions, bypassing the need for intermediate representations like maps or trajectories.
> - Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) can successfully adapt large VLA models like OpenVLA to specialized domains such as drone racing while preserving general capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Autonomous drone navigation represents a significant challenge in robotics, particularly for high-speed applications like drone racing where split-second decisions are crucial. While traditional approaches to drone autonomy rely on planning, trajectory generation, or skill selection, these methods often lack adaptability in dynamic environments.

RaceVLA introduces a novel approach by applying Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to autonomous racing drones, enabling human-like navigation behavior through natural language commands and visual inputs. This research, conducted by Serpiva, Lykov, and colleagues at the Intelligent Space Robotics Laboratory at Skoltech, bridges a critical gap in drone technology by demonstrating how VLA models can be effectively adapted for real-time drone control.

### 2. Research Background

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant advancement in cognitive robotics, allowing robots to generate appropriate control signals directly from multimodal inputs. Recent developments like PaLM-E, RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, and MiniVLA have shown promise for robotic manipulators, but their application to drone technology has been limited.

Autonomous drone navigation has traditionally relied on:
1. Transformer-based models for planning and trajectory generation
2. Deep reinforcement learning approaches
3. Vision-language navigation systems

However, these methods often lack the adaptability and responsiveness needed for high-speed drone racing. RaceVLA addresses this limitation by implementing a VLA-based approach specifically designed for racing drones, which allows for more human-like decision-making in complex, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology

The RaceVLA approach involves adapting and fine-tuning the OpenVLA model specifically for drone racing contexts. The system:

1. Takes first-person view (FPV) video frames and natural language instructions as input
2. Processes these inputs through a modified VLA architecture
3. Outputs a 4D action vector consisting of three linear velocities (Vx, Vy, Vz) and yaw angular speed (ω)

This end-to-end approach allows RaceVLA to directly map visual and language inputs to control actions, eliminating the need for intermediate representations like maps or trajectories.

### 4. Model Architecture

RaceVLA builds upon the OpenVLA architecture, which is based on the LLaMA2 7B large language model. The system follows a structured pipeline:

```
Input: FPV Image + Natural Language Command
↓
Image Tokenizer + LLaMA Tokenizer
↓
LLaMA2 7B Model
↓
Action De-Tokenizer
↓
Output: UAV Action Vector [Vx, Vy, Vz, ω]
```

The model uses Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, which allows for adaptation to the drone control domain without modifying all model parameters. This approach preserves the model's general capabilities while optimizing for the specific requirements of drone racing.

### 5. Dataset and Training

The researchers created a specialized dataset for training RaceVLA:

- 200 episodes of drone racing
- Approximately 20,000 images
- Diverse racing tasks including navigating through arch gates, square gates, and circular track formations
- Data synchronized with positional information, velocity components, and yaw angles

The dataset captures a variety of scenarios and command types, such as:
- "Fly through the gate"
- "Fly through the arch/square gate"
- "Take a lap around the track"
- "Fly through all gates"

The training process involved:
1. Formatting data using the Reinforcement Learning Dataset Specification (RLDS)
2. Fine-tuning the OpenVLA-7b model using LoRA
3. Training on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU

### 6. Implementation and Hardware

The RaceVLA system was implemented on a custom-built 8-inch racing drone with the following components:

- Speedy Bee F405 flight controller with ArduPilot firmware
- RealSense T265 camera for visual input
- Onboard Intel NUC computer
- Communication with a stationary PC via Flask API

The system operates at a frequency of 4 Hz, allowing for real-time processing and control during flight. This setup enables the drone to process visual information, receive natural language commands, and generate appropriate control actions for navigation.

### 7. Experimental Results

RaceVLA demonstrated impressive performance in drone racing tasks, achieving:

- Average velocity: 1.04 m/s
- Maximum speed: 2.02 m/s
- Stable flight patterns across various racing scenarios

The system successfully executed a range of tasks from simple gate navigation to complex multi-gate racing courses. The action vectors generated by RaceVLA showed appropriate adjustments in velocity and yaw to navigate efficiently through racing gates.

The experiments demonstrated that RaceVLA can effectively:
1. Process visual information to identify racing gates
2. Understand natural language commands for navigation goals
3. Generate appropriate control signals to execute these goals
4. Adapt to different gate configurations and track layouts

### 8. Generalization Capabilities

A key strength of RaceVLA is its ability to generalize across different dimensions:

1. **Visual Generalization**: Performance with unseen backgrounds, object appearances, and distractors (79.6% success rate)
2. **Motion Generalization**: Adaptation to unseen object positions and orientations (75.0% success rate)
3. **Physical Generalization**: Navigation with unseen object sizes and shapes (50.0% success rate)
4. **Semantic Generalization**: Understanding and executing unseen instructions (45.5% success rate)

The researchers tested these generalization capabilities through experiments where aspects of the environment or tasks were altered from the training conditions. For example, adding distractors to test visual generalization or changing gate shapes to test physical generalization.

### 9. Comparison with Other Models

RaceVLA was benchmarked against two prominent VLA models:

1. **RT-2-X by Google DeepMind**
2. **OpenVLA from Stanford AI Lab**

RaceVLA outperformed RT-2-X across all generalization axes:
- Visual: 79.6% vs. 52.0%
- Motion: 75.0% vs. 55.0%
- Physical: 50.0% vs. 26.7%
- Semantic: 45.5% vs. 38.8%

Compared to OpenVLA, RaceVLA showed:
- Superior performance in motion (75.0% vs. 60.0%) and semantic (45.5% vs. 36.3%) generalization
- Slightly reduced performance in visual (79.6% vs. 87.0%) and physical (50.0% vs. 76.7%) generalization

These results demonstrate that RaceVLA successfully adapts the VLA framework to the specific challenges of drone racing, particularly excelling at motion-related tasks critical for racing performance.

### 10. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive performance, RaceVLA has several limitations:

1. **Visual and Physical Generalization**: While strong overall, RaceVLA showed slightly lower performance in visual and physical generalization compared to OpenVLA.

2. **Real-world Conditions**: The system was tested in controlled indoor environments; performance in more chaotic outdoor settings with variable lighting and weather conditions remains to be evaluated.

3. **Processing Speed**: The current 4 Hz operation frequency, while sufficient for demonstration, may limit performance in high-speed professional racing scenarios.

Future work will focus on:
- Improving visual and physical generalization capabilities
- Expanding the dataset to include more diverse racing scenarios
- Optimizing model architecture for faster processing speeds
- Testing in more challenging and varied environments

### 11. Conclusion

RaceVLA represents a significant advancement in autonomous drone navigation by successfully applying Vision-Language-Action models to racing drones. The system demonstrates human-like decision-making capabilities, enabling drones to navigate complex racing courses based on natural language commands and visual inputs.

The research makes several key contributions:
1. Introducing the first VLA model specifically designed for racing drone navigation
2. Demonstrating superior motion and semantic generalization compared to existing approaches
3. Achieving real-time performance suitable for racing applications
4. Providing an open-source implementation to facilitate further research

By enabling more intuitive and adaptable drone control through natural language, RaceVLA paves the way for broader applications of autonomous drones in complex, dynamic environments beyond racing, including search and rescue, inspection, delivery services, and environmental monitoring.
