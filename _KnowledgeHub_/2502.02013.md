# Layer by Layer: Uncovering Hidden Representations in Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Intermediate layers in large language models often provide superior representations for downstream tasks compared to final layers, yielding 2-16% performance improvements on average across 32 text embedding tasks. This work introduces a unified framework based on matrix-based entropy to explain this phenomenon and enable unsupervised identification of optimal layers.

## Problem

- The common assumption that the final layer of large language models provides the most effective representations for downstream tasks may be suboptimal.
- There is a lack of comprehensive understanding regarding how information is processed and transformed within the intermediate layers of LLMs.
- Existing methods for analyzing neural network representation quality are diverse and lack a unified theoretical framework.

## Method

- Proposed a unified theoretical framework based on ==matrix-based entropy== to quantify and compare representation quality across neural network layers.
- Utilized seven specific metrics, derived from the framework, covering ==information-theoretic, geometric, and augmentation-invariance properties== of representations.
- Conducted extensive empirical validation across a wide range of language models (transformers, state-space models) and vision models, on diverse downstream tasks.

## Results

- Intermediate layers achieved **2%** to **16%** average performance improvements over final layers across 32 MTEB text embedding tasks.
- Unsupervised layer selection using the proposed metrics (e.g., DiME) led to a **3%** average MTEB score boost for Pythia-410M models.
- Autoregressive models (e.g., Pythia, Llama3, AIM) exhibit a pronounced mid-layer 'compression valley' in prompt entropy, while masked/bidirectional models (e.g., BERT) show more uniform entropy across layers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Intermediate layers in autoregressive models consistently yield superior representations for downstream tasks, challenging the reliance on final layers.
> - The proposed matrix-based entropy metrics effectively correlate with downstream performance, enabling unsupervised selection of optimal layers.
> - The training objective, particularly autoregression, significantly influences the layer-wise information compression and representation dynamics, observed across both language and vision models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated at the intersection of several critical areas within deep learning and natural language processing (NLP): understanding neural network representations, layer-wise analysis of large models, and the relationship between information compression and generalization. It fundamentally challenges the widespread assumption that the final layer of a large language model invariably provides the richest and most useful representations for downstream tasks.

For a long time, research on neural network interpretability has aimed to decipher how information is encoded within a model's hidden layers. Early efforts, such as linear probing (Alain & Bengio, 2017) and Singular Vector Canonical Correlation Analysis (SVCCA) (Raghu et al., 2017), primarily focused on vision models or shallower networks. More recently, as LLMs have grown in scale and complexity, a burgeoning field of "layer-wise analysis in language models" has emerged. This body of work has shown that specific linguistic features (e.g., part-of-speech tags, semantic roles) are often optimally encoded in the middle layers of models like BERT (Liu et al., 2019; Tenney et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019). Furthermore, a growing number of studies have hinted at the surprising robustness and utility of mid-depth features for various tasks, even challenging the reliance on final layers for generation (Jin et al., 2024; Gurnee & Tegmark, 2023; Fan et al., 2024). This paper systematically builds upon these observations, providing a unified theoretical framework and extensive empirical evidence across diverse architectures and tasks.

The paper also extensively ties into the theoretical understanding of "compression and generalization." Concepts like the Information Bottleneck principle (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2019) propose that neural networks learn effective representations by compressing irrelevant information while preserving task-relevant signals. Recent work has linked information compression directly to generalization performance (Deletang et al., 2024) and observed that LLM embeddings often reside in low-dimensional manifolds (Park et al., 2024a). This paper reinforces these ideas by demonstrating that many networks, particularly autoregressive transformers, naturally develop a mid-layer "bottleneck" that is crucial for balancing signal preservation and noise reduction.

Finally, the paper contributes significantly to the field of "representation quality metrics." It acknowledges the variety of existing metrics—information-theoretic (e.g., intrinsic dimensionality), geometric (e.g., effective rank, anisotropy, curvature), and task-based/invariance metrics (e.g., InfoNCE, LiDAR, DiME)—but critically, it unifies many of these seemingly disparate measures under a single theoretical lens: matrix-based entropy. This unification provides a more coherent way to analyze and compare representation quality across different models and domains, advancing the diagnostic tools available for deep learning researchers.

In essence, this research goes beyond simply confirming previous anecdotal observations about intermediate layers; it provides a comprehensive framework, theoretical grounding, and extensive empirical validation to explain *why* these layers are often superior, thereby challenging a fundamental assumption in LLM application and paving the way for more informed model design and usage.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically investigate the quality and utility of intermediate layer representations within large language models and to challenge the deeply ingrained assumption that the final layer's outputs are inherently the most effective for downstream applications. The authors propose that this conventional wisdom may be suboptimal and that a deeper understanding of layer-wise information flow could lead to more robust and accurate representations.

The primary motivations driving this study can be summarized as follows:

- **Challenging the Final-Layer Assumption:** The prevailing practice in NLP involves extracting features or generating text based solely on the final layer of LLMs. The authors question the universality of this approach, hypothesizing that earlier or intermediate layers might encode richer, more generalizable information before the final layers specialize too much to the pre-training objective.
- **Unveiling Hidden Properties:** Despite the widespread use of LLMs, a comprehensive understanding of *how* information is processed and transformed layer by layer remains elusive. The paper aims to uncover the "hidden properties" of these intermediate representations.
- **Developing a Unified Framework:** Existing methods for analyzing representation quality are diverse and often disparate. A key motivation is to propose a unified theoretical framework that can seamlessly integrate information-theoretic, geometric, and invariance-based perspectives. This framework would provide a common language and lens for assessing representation quality, thereby clarifying why certain intermediate layers might be more effective.
- **Improving Downstream Task Performance:** If intermediate layers indeed hold superior representations, leveraging them directly for tasks like text embedding, classification, or clustering could lead to significant performance gains, thus impacting the practical application of LLMs.
- **Informing Model Design and Training:** A deeper understanding of layer-wise dynamics, particularly how information is compressed and preserved, can offer valuable insights for designing more efficient and effective LLM architectures and training methodologies. This could lead to models that generalize better or require less computational overhead.
- **Generalizability Across Architectures and Domains:** The study aims to determine if the observed phenomena are specific to certain architectures (e.g., transformers) or training objectives, or if they represent a more fundamental aspect of deep learning across different modalities (e.g., language and vision).

In essence, the paper is motivated by a desire to move beyond black-box application of LLMs by systematically dissecting their internal mechanisms, offering both theoretical explanations and empirical validation for the surprising effectiveness of mid-layer features.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper adopts a two-pronged methodology: first, proposing a unified theoretical framework for assessing representation quality, and second, conducting extensive empirical validation across a wide range of models, tasks, and conditions.

**Unified Framework for Representation Quality Metrics:**
The core of the theoretical approach revolves around **matrix-based entropy**, a quantity derived from the Gram matrix (K = ZZ^T) of a neural network's hidden states Z. The formula for matrix-based entropy, S_alpha(Z), generalizes notions of information content. The authors argue that by analyzing the eigenvalue distribution of the Gram matrix, this single metric can unify seemingly disparate concepts:
- **Information Compression:** A few dominant eigenvalues imply high compression (low entropy), while a uniform spectrum suggests diverse features (high entropy).
- **Geometric Organization:** The distribution of eigenvalues reflects how token embeddings are organized in high-dimensional space, influencing aspects like curvature.
- **Invariance to Perturbations:** Stable clustering of augmented samples, indicative of robustness, also manifests in the eigenvalue distribution.

Based on this unified perspective, the paper employs seven specific representation evaluation metrics, grouped into three categories:

1. **Information-Theoretic Metrics:**
 - **Prompt Entropy:** Applies matrix-based entropy to token embeddings within a single prompt, quantifying intra-prompt token diversity.
 - **Dataset Entropy:** Aggregates mean token embeddings across multiple prompts to measure global diversity and separability of different inputs.
 - **Effective Rank:** A measure of effective dimensionality, shown to be a lower bound to exp(S1(Z)), indicating how much a representation is compressed.

2. **Geometric Metrics:**
 - **Curvature:** Quantifies how sharply token embeddings turn in high-dimensional space as a sequence progresses, reflecting local versus global feature encoding.

3. **Augmentation-Invariance Metrics:** These assess the robustness of embeddings to small input perturbations. The authors use NLPAug library with character swaps and keyboard-level changes.
 - **InfoNCE (Information Noise Contrastive Estimation):** A self-supervised objective where lower loss indicates stronger invariance to augmentations (matched samples are closer, unmatched are pushed away).
 - **LiDAR (Linear Discriminant Analysis for Representation):** Measures within-class versus between-class scatter, checking how well augmentations of the same prompt cluster.
 - **DiME (Difference of Matrix-based Entropies):** Grounded in matrix-based entropy, it compares real paired samples against random pairings to estimate unique alignment of correct augmentations.

**Theoretical Connections:** The paper provides formal theorems demonstrating connections between these metrics. For example:
- **Theorem 1:** Shows that Effective Rank is a lower bound of exp(S1(Z)), linking compression to dimensionality.
- **Theorem 3:** States that InfoNCE loss relates to mutual information and Dataset Entropy (log(N) - InfoNCE <= I(X;Z) <= H(Z)), implying that reducing InfoNCE leads to higher-entropy and more robust representations.

**Empirical Validation:**
The framework is rigorously tested through extensive experiments across diverse models and tasks:

- **Models:**
 - **Language Models:** Pythia (decoder-only transformers, various scales: 14M to 1B parameters), Llama3 (decoder-only), Mamba (state-space models), BERT (encoder-only transformer), and LLM2Vec models (bidirectional attention). This covers major architectural paradigms in modern LLMs.
 - **Vision Models:** ViT (supervised), CLIP (weakly supervised), BEiT, DINOv2, MAE (self-supervised masked models), AIM, and AIMv2 (autoregressive image transformers). This allows for cross-domain generalization analysis.
- **Tasks and Datasets:**
 - **Language:** 32 text embedding tasks from the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB), covering classification, clustering, and reranking. WikiText-103 is used for detailed layer-wise metric analysis.
 - **Vision:** ImageNet-1k, with attention probing for performance evaluation.
- **Experimental Scope:**
 - **Downstream Task Performance:** Evaluate how each layer's embeddings perform on MTEB tasks.
 - **Architectural and Scale Differences:** Compare metric evolution across layers for different architectures and Pythia models of varying sizes.
 - **Training Progression:** Analyze metric changes across Pythia's training checkpoints.
 - **Finetuning Effects:** Study the impact of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) finetuning (Qwen 2.5 vs. Qwen 2.5-Math) and other finetuning (LLM2Vec) on internal representations.
 - **Extreme Input Conditions:** Probe layer responsiveness to token repetition, randomness, and varying prompt lengths.
 - **Internal Transformer Components:** Investigate how sub-layers (e.g., residual connections) contribute to observed compression.

This comprehensive methodology, combining novel theoretical insights with extensive empirical validation, allows the authors to draw strong conclusions about the functional roles of different layers in large neural networks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings that challenge conventional wisdom and provide deeper insights into the internal workings of large language models:

1. **Intermediate Layers Consistently Outperform Final Layers:** Across 32 diverse text embedding tasks from MTEB, intermediate layers consistently produced stronger features than the final layers, with performance improvements ranging from 2% to 16% on average. This phenomenon was observed across all tested architectures (transformer-based, state-space models like Mamba, and encoder-only like BERT), suggesting it is a widespread effect rather than an architectural anomaly. The optimal layer for downstream performance typically resides around the mid-depth of the network.

2. **Representation Metrics Correlate with Downstream Performance:** The proposed unified metrics, particularly curvature, DiME, and InfoNCE, exhibited strong correlations with downstream task performance. This empirical validation supports the theoretical framework, indicating that these metrics effectively capture aspects of representation quality relevant to utility. Crucially, these correlations enable **unsupervised layer selection**, where the optimal layer can be identified without task-specific labels, yielding performance improvements (e.g., 3% average MTEB score boost for Pythia-410M using DiME-based selection).

3. **Architectural Differences in Information Compression:**
 - **Autoregressive Models (Pythia, Llama3, AIM):** These models, trained for sequential prediction, display a pronounced mid-layer "compression valley" (significant dip in prompt entropy). This indicates a strong tendency to filter and prune non-local details in the middle layers, creating an information bottleneck. The performance peak for these models often aligns with this mid-depth "sweet spot."
 - **Masked/Bidirectional Models (BERT):** In contrast, BERT, which processes inputs bidirectionally, maintains a generally higher and more uniform entropy across layers, suggesting less aggressive compression as it has access to the full context.
 - **State-Space Models (Mamba):** Mamba exhibits flatter, more uniform curves across its layers, indicating a different strategy for balancing information retention and compression.
 - These findings align with prior work on anisotropy and suggest that the *training objective* (autoregressive vs. bidirectional) is a primary driver of layer-wise information dynamics.

4. **Impact of Model Scale:** As Pythia models increased in size (from 14M to 1B parameters), the intermediate compression (entropy dips) became more pronounced. Larger models also showed smoother token trajectories (lower curvature) and stronger invariance (higher LiDAR scores), suggesting increased capacity to distill relevant features and filter noise more effectively.

5. **Effects of Fine-tuning:**
 - **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Fine-tuning:** CoT fine-tuning (e.g., Qwen 2.5-Math) was shown to encourage models to maintain higher token-level prompt entropy with lower variance across examples. This implies that CoT fine-tuning helps models preserve richer contextual information throughout their layers, which is crucial for multi-step reasoning.
 - **LLM2Vec Fine-tuning:** Fine-tuned LLM2Vec models (which modify Llama3 for embedding tasks) showed improved augmentation invariance, demonstrating how specific fine-tuning objectives can reshape the robustness of intermediate representations.

6. **Evolution During Training Progression:** The most significant changes in representation quality (e.g., decreasing prompt entropy, smoothing curvature, minimal LiDAR scores) occurred in the mid-depth layers as training progressed. This suggests that intermediate layers are primarily responsible for abstracting and compressing inputs. Early layers, conversely, stabilized very quickly, supporting the "detokenization" hypothesis that their main role is to convert raw tokens into a basic embedding space.

7. **Response to Extreme Input Conditions:**
 - **Token Repetition:** Increasing token repetition caused a sharp decrease in mid-layer prompt entropy, demonstrating the model's ability to recognize and compress redundant patterns.
 - **Random Tokens:** Adding randomness inflated entropy, particularly in early layers, highlighting their sensitivity to noise, while deeper layers showed more robustness.
 - These results emphasize the dynamic role of intermediate layers in adapting to varying input characteristics.

8. **Generalization to Vision Models:** The findings extend to the vision domain, with **autoregressive image transformers (AIM)** exhibiting the same mid-layer entropy "valley" and corresponding peak in downstream accuracy as observed in autoregressive language models. This strongly suggests that the *autoregressive training objective* is the primary factor driving this mid-depth information bottleneck, regardless of the data modality (text or image patches). Conversely, most non-autoregressive vision models (e.g., ViT, CLIP, DINOv2, MAE) showed increasing accuracy and entropy towards the final layers, indicating different information processing strategies.

In summary, the key result is the widespread observation that intermediate layers in autoregressive models form a critical information bottleneck, yielding representations that are often superior to those of the final layer for downstream tasks, a finding explained and unified by the proposed matrix-based entropy framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in "Layer by Layer: Uncovering Hidden Representations in Language Models" carry substantial significance and potential impact across several dimensions of deep learning research and application:

1. **Challenging a Core Assumption in LLM Usage:** The most immediate and profound impact is the direct challenge to the conventional wisdom of relying solely on the final layer for LLM embeddings and feature extraction. By consistently demonstrating that intermediate layers can offer richer and more performant representations, the paper opens up a new paradigm for how LLMs are utilized in practice. This could lead to a fundamental shift in design principles for applications leveraging LLM embeddings.

2. **Performance Improvements:** The empirical results show tangible performance gains (up to 16% on average across MTEB tasks) when using optimal intermediate layers. This directly translates to more accurate and robust LLM-based systems for various downstream NLP tasks like classification, clustering, and reranking.

3. **Enhanced Model Interpretability:** The unified framework and empirical analysis provide a quantitative lens to "open the black box" of LLMs. By tracking metrics like entropy and curvature layer-by-layer, researchers can gain a deeper understanding of *how* LLMs transform and abstract information. This illuminates which layers are responsible for compression, feature distillation, and noise reduction, contributing to a more mechanistic understanding of model behavior.

4. **Implications for Model Design and Training:** The observation of a consistent mid-layer "compression valley" in autoregressive models, driven by the training objective, provides critical insights for future architectural design. It suggests that deliberately engineering or optimizing these intermediate stages might yield more effective and efficient models. Future research could explore training strategies that explicitly aim to control or optimize information flow at different depths. For instance, fine-tuning efforts could be directed towards preserving or enhancing the quality of mid-layer representations.

5. **Improved Robustness and Efficiency:** If intermediate layers indeed contain more robust and generalized features, leveraging them could lead to models that are less susceptible to subtle input variations or adversarial attacks. Furthermore, identifying the most effective layers might allow for more efficient model inference by potentially pruning or skipping later, less performant layers, thereby reducing computational costs without sacrificing accuracy.

6. **Guidance for Unsupervised Layer Selection:** The strong correlation between the proposed unsupervised metrics (like DiME, curvature, and InfoNCE) and downstream performance is highly practical. It offers a method to select the optimal layer for a given task *without* requiring labeled data or extensive task-specific tuning, making the process of adapting LLMs more streamlined and less resource-intensive.

7. **Cross-Domain Insights:** The generalization of findings to vision models, particularly the observation of similar mid-layer compression in autoregressive image transformers, suggests a broader principle at play: the training objective (especially autoregression) significantly shapes the internal representation dynamics across modalities. This cross-domain insight can foster more unified theories of deep learning.

**Ethical Considerations:** The authors responsibly acknowledge the ethical implications. The ability to identify and leverage latent features in intermediate layers could inadvertently amplify existing biases present in the training data. If intermediate layers encode nuanced, perhaps even problematic, associations more strongly than final layers, their direct use could lead to unintended disparities or unfair outcomes in real-world applications. This highlights the need for continued research into bias detection, mitigation, and fairness evaluation at a granular, layer-wise level when deploying such models.

In conclusion, this research marks a significant step towards a more nuanced understanding of LLM representations. By demonstrating the often superior quality of intermediate layer features and providing a robust framework to quantify this, the paper not only improves practical application but also offers fundamental insights that can guide the next generation of neural network architecture and training paradigm design.
