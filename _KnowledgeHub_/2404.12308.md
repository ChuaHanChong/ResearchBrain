# ASID: Active Exploration for System Identification in Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The ASID framework, developed at the University of Washington, introduces an active exploration method to autonomously refine physics simulator parameters using minimal real-world data. This enables zero-shot transfer of policies learned in the updated simulator to real-world robotic manipulation tasks, achieving success rates of 6 out of 9 for rod balancing and 7 out of 10 for shuffleboard after collecting only a single real-world exploration episode.

## Problem

- The significant sim-to-real gap causes policies learned in simulation to fail on real robots due to discrepancies in physics and dynamics.
- Learning robust robotic manipulation policies in the real world is sample-inefficient and costly, making model-free reinforcement learning impractical.
- Manually tuning simulation parameters to accurately reflect real-world physical properties and kinematics requires extensive human effort and expertise.

## Method

- A three-stage "sim-to-real-to-sim-to-real" pipeline begins by training an active exploration policy in simulation, guided by Fisher information maximization and domain randomization, to efficiently collect informative real-world data.
- The exploration policy is deployed for a single episode in the real world to gather a trajectory that is then used with black-box optimization (e.g., CEM) to refine the simulator's physical and kinematic parameters.
- A task-specific policy is subsequently trained entirely within the refined, high-fidelity simulator and deployed to the real robot in a zero-shot manner, eliminating further real-world fine-tuning.

## Results

- The Fisher information-guided exploration policy demonstrated superior coverage and interaction with unknown parameters in simulation, like achieving uniform coverage of friction zones and 80% interaction with articulated objects, outperforming naive and disagreement-based baselines.
- In simulated downstream tasks, ASID achieved near-perfect rod balancing (0.00° to 0.72° tilt) and a 28% sphere striking success rate, significantly outperforming domain randomization and other baselines.
- On real-world robotic tasks with a Franka Emika Panda, ASID enabled zero-shot transfer with minimal data (a single exploration episode), achieving 6 out of 9 successes for rod balancing and 7 out of 10 for shuffleboard, while domain randomization baselines largely failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning an effective exploration strategy, which is less sensitive to initial parameter inaccuracies, can be decoupled from learning a precise task-solving policy.
> - Utilizing the Fisher information matrix effectively quantifies the informativeness of trajectories for system identification, enabling targeted and efficient data collection.
> - Optimization-based system identification methods are more robust and stable than learned estimation modules, particularly when faced with limited real-world data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses one of the most significant challenges in modern robotics: the "sim-to-real gap." The core problem is that policies learned in high-fidelity simulators often fail when deployed on real robots due to discrepancies between the simulated and real-world physics and dynamics. The paper positions itself at the intersection of several key research areas:

- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Robotics:** Model-free RL methods, while powerful, are notoriously sample-inefficient, making them impractical for real-world robotic training where data collection is expensive and time-consuming. Model-based RL, which uses simulators, offers sample efficiency but is hampered by the sim-to-real gap.
- **System Identification (SI):** This is a classical field of control theory focused on learning mathematical models of dynamical systems from experimental data. ASID draws heavily from the principles of optimal experiment design within SI, particularly the concept of Fisher information, to guide its exploration strategy. The paper explicitly states its goal as "bridging the gap between classical work on system identification and modern sim2real techniques."
- **Simulation-to-Reality (Sim2Real) Transfer:** Existing approaches to sim2real include:
 - **Domain Randomization (DR):** Training policies over a diverse distribution of simulated environments to encourage robustness. While effective for some tasks, DR doesn't explicitly *correct* the simulator's parameters and can be inefficient if the true parameters are outside the randomized range or if the task requires high precision. ASID contrasts by performing *targeted exploration* to *update* the simulator's parameters, rather than just randomizing.
 - **Online Adaptation/Fine-tuning:** Policies trained in sim are fine-tuned with real-world data during deployment. This requires a initial viable policy and sufficient real-world interaction, which can still be sample-intensive. ASID aims for *zero-shot transfer* after simulator refinement, minimizing real-world interaction for the task-specific policy.
 - **Learning Parameter Inference:** Some works try to learn a mapping from observations to simulator parameters. ASID differs by using an optimization-based approach for parameter identification, which can be more robust and generalize better than purely learned estimators, especially with limited data.

ASID introduces a novel "sim-to-real-to-sim-to-real" pipeline. Unlike methods that solely focus on robust policy learning (DR) or online adaptation, ASID proposes an *active, data-driven approach* to *autonomously refine the simulator itself* using minimal real-world interaction. This is distinct from model-based RL approaches that learn a *fully black-box dynamics model*; ASID instead refines specific, unknown parameters of a *pre-existing physics simulator*. This focus on parameter identification within a structured simulator allows for greater sample efficiency and interpretability compared to learning a completely data-driven, non-parametric model.

In essence, ASID positions itself as a solution to create a high-fidelity "digital twin" of the real environment with minimal human effort and real-world data, thereby enabling effective zero-shot transfer of policies learned in the corrected simulator.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning system that can effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap in robotic manipulation, enabling robust control strategies to be learned in simulation and deployed in the real world with minimal real-world data. This overarching goal breaks down into several specific objectives:

- **Autonomous Simulator Refinement:** To enable a learning system to autonomously refine an initial (potentially inaccurate) simulation model using a small amount of real-world data. This reduces the significant human effort traditionally required to manually specify accurate simulation assets and physical parameters for every new environment or object.
- **Effective Exploration for System Identification:** To design and implement an exploration strategy that, when deployed in the real world, collects maximally informative data about unknown physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction, articulation, center of mass) and kinematic structures. The motivation here is to move beyond passive data collection or naive randomization towards *targeted, intelligent exploration*.
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To use the refined, high-fidelity simulator to train accurate control policies for downstream tasks and then deploy these policies to the real world in a zero-shot fashion, without requiring further real-world fine-tuning. This directly addresses the sample inefficiency of real-world RL.
- **Decoupling Exploration from Exploitation:** The core motivation behind the pipeline's structure is the critical insight that learning an *effective exploration strategy* is often less sensitive to parameter inaccuracies than learning a *precise task-solving policy*. For example, to learn the mass of a ball, any interaction suffices, whereas hitting it to a specific target requires precise mass knowledge. By training robust exploration policies in a randomized simulation, these policies can effectively transfer to the real world to collect information, even if the initial task-specific policies would fail.

Ultimately, the motivation stems from the desire to create more adaptable and capable robotic systems that can operate in diverse, unstructured real-world environments without extensive manual tuning or prohibitive amounts of real-world training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ASID (Active Exploration for System IDentification) approach proposes a three-stage pipeline, conceptualized as a "sim-to-real-to-sim-to-real" process, to achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with minimal real-world data. The key components are depicted in Figure 2 and described below:

#### 3.1. Exploration via Fisher Information Maximization

This is the initial and crucial stage, focusing on learning *how to explore* to gather maximally informative data about unknown real-world parameters.
- **Goal:** Train an exploration policy ($\pi_{exp}$) in simulation that, when deployed in the real world, generates trajectories providing the most information about the true, unknown parameters ($\theta^*$).
- **Theoretical Foundation:** The Fisher information matrix is used as a quantitative measure of the amount of information an observable random variable (the collected trajectory $\tau$) carries about an unknown parameter ($\theta$). A larger Fisher information implies smaller estimation error (as per the Cramer-Rao lower bound). Therefore, the objective is to find an exploration policy that maximizes the trace of the inverse Fisher information matrix, $ \arg \min_{\pi} \text{tr}(I(\theta^*, \pi)^{-1}) $. This means inducing trajectories highly sensitive to the unknown parameters.
- **Implementation Challenges and Solutions:**
 - **Complexity of Fisher Information:** The full form of $I(\theta, \pi)$ can be intractable. ASID assumes simplified dynamics with Gaussian process noise ($s_{h+1} = f_\theta(s_h, a_h) + w_h$), where $w_h \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_w^2 \cdot I)$. Under this assumption, the Fisher information simplifies to involve the gradient of the dynamics with respect to $\theta$, $ E[ \sum_{h=1}^H \nabla_\theta f_\theta(s_h, a_h) \cdot \nabla_\theta f_\theta(s_h, a_h)^\top ] $. This means the policy should aim to reach states where the next state is highly sensitive to parameter changes.
 - **Unknown True Parameter ($\theta^*$):** Since $\theta^*$ is unknown, the policy cannot directly optimize for it. ASID addresses this by employing **Domain Randomization** during the exploration policy training. It trains $\pi_{exp}$ to minimize the expected inverse Fisher information over a distribution of possible parameters $q_0$: $ \arg \min_{\pi} E_{\theta \sim q_0}[\text{tr}(I(\theta, \pi)^{-1})] $. The rationale is that a policy robustly good at exploration across various parameter values will likely be effective for the true, unknown parameter.
 - **Non-differentiable Simulators:** If the simulator's dynamics are not analytically differentiable with respect to $\theta$, a **finite-differences approximation** is used to estimate the gradient $ \nabla_\theta f_\theta $.
 - **Policy Optimization:** Standard deep reinforcement learning algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), are used to train $\pi_{exp}$ in simulation.
- **Real-world Data Collection:** After training, $\pi_{exp}$ is deployed *for a single episode* in the real environment to collect a trajectory $\tau_{real}$.

#### 3.2. System Identification

This stage uses the real-world data collected by the exploration policy to refine the simulation parameters.
- **Goal:** Update the simulator's parameters ($\theta$) to accurately reflect the real environment, given the single real-world trajectory $\tau_{real}$.
- **Mechanism:** The objective is to find a parameter distribution $q_\phi$ such that the trajectories generated by the simulator with parameters sampled from $q_\phi$ (and executing the same actions as in $\tau_{real}$) closely match $\tau_{real}$. This minimizes $ E_{\theta \sim q_\phi}[E_{\tau_{sim} \sim p_\theta(\cdot|A(\tau_{real}))}[\|\tau_{real} - \tau_{sim}\|^2_2]] $.
- **Implementation:** ASID utilizes black-box optimization algorithms for this parameter estimation. For simulation experiments, REPS (Relative Entropy Policy Search) is used, while for real-world experiments, the Cross Entropy Method (CEM) is employed. These methods iteratively search for parameters that minimize the discrepancy between simulated and real trajectories. The identified parameters can include kinematic properties (e.g., joint positions, articulation), geometric properties, and physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction, stiffness).

#### 3.3. Solving the Downstream Task

With an accurately identified simulator, the final stage focuses on training a task-specific policy and deploying it.
- **Goal:** Train a policy ($\pi_{task}$) that achieves the desired goal in the *updated, high-fidelity simulator* and transfers it zero-shot to the real world.
- **Mechanism:** Any standard policy optimization algorithm (e.g., PPO or CEM as in experiments) can be used to train $\pi_{task}$ entirely within the refined simulator. The assumption is that the simulator is now sufficiently accurate that policies learned within it will transfer directly to reality.
- **Deployment:** The trained $\pi_{task}$ is then deployed on the real robot without any further fine-tuning or adaptation, demonstrating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

This structured approach leverages the strengths of both simulation (for cheap, extensive training of exploration policies) and real-world interaction (for crucial data collection to ground the simulator), aiming for a highly data-efficient and autonomous robotic learning system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of ASID was conducted in both simulated environments and on real-world robotic manipulation tasks, addressing three key questions: the efficacy of the exploration strategy, the success of downstream task policies, and the real-world transfer capabilities.

#### 4.1. Effective Exploration Behavior

- **Multi-Friction Sphere Manipulation:** In a simulated environment with three distinct friction patches (grass, sand, gravel), ASID's Fisher information-based exploration policy achieved "roughly uniform coverage" of the entire space, actively striking the ball into each region (Figure 4a). In contrast, the baseline method by Kumar et al. (2019) failed to move the sphere effectively into different regions (Figure 4b), indicating ASID's superior ability to excite multiple unknown parameters.
- **Articulation Environment:** ASID's exploration policy interacted with the articulated laptop model 80% of the time, compared to only 20% for naive baselines. This enabled better reconstruction of the laptop's kinematic structure from interaction data, as demonstrated with a reconstruction model (Ditto) that could infer joint and part-level geometry (Figure 8).
- **Comparison to Model-Based Exploration (MAX):** In an environment with multiple spheres (one with changing friction, others as distractors), ASID's Fisher information approach produced a single, directed exploration policy that focused on the relevant sphere (Figure 10d). MAX (Shyam et al., 2019), which relies on disagreement between learned dynamics models, was distracted by novel states induced by all spheres (Figure 10a, b), highlighting ASID's targeted efficiency.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Performance on Downstream Tasks (Simulation)

ASID consistently outperformed various baselines and ablations in simulated downstream tasks, emphasizing the value of targeted exploration and optimization-based system identification.

- **Rod Balancing:** For a task requiring precise balancing of a rod with unknown mass distribution, ASID (with optimization-based System Identification, "ASID + SysID") achieved near-perfect performance (0.00° or 0.72° tilt angle for different inertia distributions). In stark contrast, policies trained with random exploration, Kumar et al. (2019), and especially Domain Randomization (DR) yielded significantly higher tilt angles (ranging from 1.13° to 26.69°). This demonstrated that for one-shot tasks critically dependent on accurate physical parameters, DR struggles, and ASID's identification is crucial.
- **Sphere Striking:** For striking a golf ball in an environment with unknown friction (critical for distance and bounce), ASID achieved a 28% success rate, significantly higher than all baselines (Random exploration: 10.62%, Kumar et al. (2019): 9.50%, DR: 8.75%, ASID + estimator: 11%). This underscores that accurate parameter identification is vital for dynamic manipulation tasks where small parameter errors lead to large trajectory deviations.
- **System Identification Method Comparison:** The results clearly showed that ASID with optimization-based system identification ("ASID + SysID") was superior to methods relying on learned estimation modules (e.g., "ASID + estimator" and Kumar etal. (2019)). This suggests that optimization-based methods offer better stability and extrapolation capabilities, particularly with limited real-world data, compared to purely data-driven learned models which struggle with out-of-distribution trajectories (Figure 11).

#### 4.3. Real-World Controller Synthesis Using Minimal Real-World Data

ASID demonstrated its practical applicability and effectiveness on two challenging real-world tasks using a Franka Emika Panda robot.

- **Rod Balancing (Real-World):** The task involved balancing a rod with unknown mass distribution on a perch (Figure 5). After a single episode of exploration, ASID successfully identified the rod's inertia and friction parameters. The task policy, learned in the refined simulator, then balanced the rod in 6 out of 9 attempts across varying mass distributions (Table 2). A DR baseline, trained without real-world interaction, failed in all 9 attempts. While some ambiguities with center-of-mass near the middle caused minor issues, ASID's performance was markedly superior.
- **Shuffleboard (Real-World):** This task required shooting a puck to target zones on a board where surface friction could change after each shot due to displaced wax (Figure 6). ASID's ability to adapt the simulation to current conditions through its dedicated exploration phase resulted in landing the puck in the desired zone in 7 out of 10 attempts (Table 3). The DR baseline only succeeded in 3 out of 10 attempts, highlighting ASID's robustness to dynamically changing real-world parameters.
- **Minimal Data Requirement:** A key finding across all real-world experiments was that ASID achieved these results with "only a very small amount of data from real—typically a single episode of data suffices," demonstrating its remarkable sample efficiency.

Overall, the findings strongly support ASID's core hypothesis: targeted, active exploration guided by Fisher information can effectively identify unknown physical parameters from minimal real-world data, enabling the creation of accurate simulators for zero-shot transfer of policies to challenging robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ASID framework presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation and machine learning, with far-reaching potential impacts:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap Effectively:** ASID offers a robust and data-efficient solution to the perennial sim-to-real gap, which remains a bottleneck for deploying learning-based control policies on real robots. By autonomously refining simulator parameters using minimal real-world interaction, ASID allows policies to be trained cheaply and safely in a high-fidelity virtual environment, then deployed directly to the physical world.
- **Reducing Human Effort in Robot Deployment:** One of the most significant practical impacts is the reduction of human effort. Traditionally, engineers spend considerable time meticulously crafting simulation models and tuning physical parameters to match reality. ASID automates this process, enabling robots to "understand" and calibrate their own physical environment, thereby accelerating the deployment of new robotic systems and skills.
- **Data Efficiency for Real-World Robotics:** Real-world data collection is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially damaging to hardware. ASID's ability to achieve zero-shot transfer with "typically a single episode of data" from the real world is a game-changer for practical robotics. This makes complex manipulation tasks, which would otherwise require prohibitive amounts of real-world training, much more feasible.
- **Fusion of Classical and Modern AI:** The work successfully integrates powerful theoretical concepts from classical system identification (Fisher information for optimal experiment design) with modern deep reinforcement learning (for training exploration policies). This interdisciplinary approach demonstrates how combining established principles with contemporary machine learning techniques can yield highly effective and robust solutions.
- **Enhanced Robot Adaptability and Robustness:** By actively identifying and adapting to unknown or changing physical parameters (mass, friction, articulation, etc.), ASID enables robots to be more adaptive and robust to variations in objects and environments. This is crucial for operating in unstructured real-world settings where precise knowledge of all parameters is rarely available beforehand.
- **Enabling New Capabilities in Robotic Manipulation:** The demonstrated success on challenging tasks like rod balancing (requiring precise inertia estimation) and shuffleboard (requiring adaptation to changing friction) showcases ASID's potential to unlock new levels of precision and adaptability in robotic manipulation, particularly for dynamic and contact-rich interactions.
- **Foundation for Autonomous Digital Twin Creation:** ASID can be seen as a stepping stone towards autonomous creation of "digital twins" for physical systems. A robot equipped with ASID can effectively build and maintain an accurate digital representation of its workspace and objects, which can then be used for complex planning, learning, and control tasks.

In conclusion, ASID represents a significant advancement towards building more intelligent, autonomous, and adaptable robotic systems. Its unique "sim-to-real-to-sim-to-real" paradigm, leveraging active exploration and robust system identification, paves the way for a future where robots can self-calibrate and learn complex behaviors with minimal human intervention and real-world data.
