# EgoMI: Learning Active Vision and Whole-Body Manipulation from Egocentric Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called EgoMI enables robots to learn complex active vision and whole-body manipulation skills directly from egocentric human demonstrations, achieving robust zero-shot transfer to real robots. The system, which captures synchronized head and hand movements and incorporates a spatial memory mechanism, demonstrated superior performance in visual search and memory-intensive tasks compared to policies lacking active head control or memory.

## Problem

- The 'embodiment gap' in active perception, where robots struggle to mimic human-like dynamic head and eye movements for visual search and occlusion resolution from egocentric demonstrations.
- Context loss in robot policies due to rapid egocentric viewpoint changes, making it difficult for standard approaches to maintain awareness of objects temporarily out of view.
- Challenges in achieving scalable and data-efficient robot skill transfer from human demonstrations without extensive domain adaptation or robot-specific data collection.

## Method

- Developed the EgoMI system to capture synchronized egocentric human head and hand trajectories, along with proprioception and camera data, compatible with robotic execution.
- Employed a semi-humanoid robot with a fully actuated camera head to enable faithful whole-body motion retargeting from human demonstrations.
- Integrated Spatial-Aware Robust Keyframe Selection (SPARKS), a training-free spatial memory mechanism, to selectively incorporate relevant past visual context during dynamic head movements.

## Results

- In 'Tabletop Search,' the active head policy (29D) succeeded in 36/40 trials, outperforming the wrist camera-only policy (29/40) and the fixed head-cam policy (2/20).
- For 'Shelf Search,' the active head policy achieved 35/40 successful points, while policies without active head control achieved 0/40.
- The SPARKS-augmented policy successfully completed 31/40 memory tasks, showing improved performance over the single-timestep policy (21/40) by leveraging spatial memory for object recall.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Active head actuation and corresponding egocentric observations are essential for robots to effectively perform visual search and manipulation across wide workspaces.
> - Merely providing head-camera input is insufficient; active control over head movements is necessary for precise object interaction and placement accuracy.
> - A spatial memory mechanism is critical for maintaining visual context during rapid head movements, enabling policies to reason about temporarily occluded or off-screen objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a fundamental challenge in robot skill acquisition: effectively transferring complex manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robotic platforms through imitation learning. This field has seen significant advancements, particularly with the advent of large-scale datasets and robot foundation models that integrate vision and language representations. However, a persistent issue is the "embodiment gap" – the inherent discrepancies between human morphology, kinematics, and sensory capabilities and those of robots.

Traditional approaches to imitation learning often simplify robot control to end-effector space, utilize static external cameras, or rely on wrist-mounted cameras. While these methods have shown success in various tasks, they generally fail to capture the active perception strategies humans employ, such as dynamic head and eye movements that continuously reposition the viewpoint to resolve occlusions, locate objects, and maintain visual contact with task-relevant areas. This limitation is particularly pronounced when learning from egocentric human data, where dynamic viewpoint changes create a significant distribution shift for robots with fixed sensing systems.

Existing work has explored aspects of active vision and improved data collection devices. Some teleoperation systems closely match human morphology (e.g., ALOHA, GELLO), while others focus on large-scale datasets from wrist-mounted cameras (e.g., UMI, EgoDex). Approaches like EgoMimic, DexCap, and ARCap leverage augmented reality and motion capture for high-fidelity hand tracking and demonstration quality. In active vision, methods like ViA and EyeRobot have explored robots with actuated cameras to imitate human head motion or learn gaze behavior, often requiring robot-specific teleoperation data or combined behavior cloning and reinforcement learning. Look, Focus, Act incorporates human gaze data to improve policy robustness.

EgoMI contributes to this landscape by introducing a framework that explicitly addresses the embodiment gap concerning active vision and whole-body manipulation. It distinguishes itself by simultaneously capturing synchronized head and hand trajectories from egocentric human demonstrations and retargeting this full-body motion, including an actuated camera head, to a semi-humanoid robot. Furthermore, it introduces a spatial memory mechanism (SPARKS) to mitigate context loss during rapid viewpoint changes, an issue not fully addressed by prior methods in the context of robot-agnostic human demonstration transfer. This work aims to enable robust zero-shot transfer from human egocentric data to robots without requiring extensive visual augmentation or on-embodiment robot data collection, representing a step towards more scalable and generalized robot learning from human behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, EgoMI, that enables robots to acquire complex manipulation skills by learning from egocentric human demonstrations, specifically addressing the challenges posed by the human embodiment gap.

The motivation stems from several interconnected issues:

1. **Embodiment Gap in Active Perception:** Humans naturally coordinate head and hand movements during manipulation, dynamically adjusting their viewpoint to maintain visual attention on task-relevant objects, resolve occlusions, and search the environment. This active perception is integral to human interaction with the world. Current robotic systems often lack this capability, relying on static external cameras or fixed body-mounted cameras. This mismatch, referred to as the embodiment gap, creates a severe distribution shift when attempting to replicate human-demonstrated behaviors using robot policies. Without the ability to reproduce task-driven viewpoint changes, policies trained on egocentric human data often fail on robots.

2. **Context Loss with Dynamic Viewpoints:** Egocentric human demonstrations involve rapid and wide-ranging head movements. When a robot attempts to mimic these movements with an actuated head, there is a risk of losing critical visual context. If a policy only considers single timesteps or short fixed windows of observations, important visual information revealed by a prior head movement (e.g., locating an object that is then temporarily out of view as the hand moves) may be forgotten, leading to failures in long-horizon tasks. Standard robot policies often lack a persistent spatial memory to address this.

3. **Scalability and Data Efficiency:** Learning directly from human demonstrations offers a path to scalable robot skill acquisition. However, the need for extensive domain adaptation, visual augmentations, or collection of additional robot-specific teleoperated data to bridge the embodiment gap can limit this scalability. The authors are motivated to create a system that minimizes these requirements, enabling more direct and efficient transfer of skills.

To address these motivations, EgoMI aims to:
- **Capture Rich Egocentric Data:** Develop a hardware system that captures synchronized end-effector and active head trajectories, along with proprioception and camera data streams, in a format directly compatible with robotic execution.
- **Enable Whole-Body Retargeting:** Utilize a semi-humanoid robot with an actuated camera head and sufficient degrees of freedom to faithfully reproduce the collected human head and end-effector motions.
- **Mitigate Context Loss:** Introduce a memory mechanism, Spatial-Aware Robust Keyframe Selection (SPARKS), to allow policies to selectively incorporate context from historical observations, especially during rapid head movements, without requiring complex learned recurrent modules.
- **Achieve Zero-Shot Transfer:** Demonstrate that by capturing and appropriately processing egocentric head and hand motions, policies can transfer to real robots without requiring visual augmentation, explicit visual alignment, or on-embodiment data collection.
- **Highlight the Importance of Active Head:** Empirically evaluate and demonstrate that incorporating head observations and movement from egocentric demonstrations is crucial for robust imitation learning, particularly in tasks requiring visual search or coordination across a wide workspace.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The EgoMI framework comprises a data collection system, a data processing pipeline, a specific robot embodiment, and a policy learning and deployment strategy.

**A. Data Collection Hardware:**
The EgoMI collection system integrates a Meta Quest 3S VR headset for 6-DoF head and hand controller tracking. A ZED 2i camera is mounted on the headset for first-person video aligned with head movements. Each VR hand controller is augmented with a mounting point for wrist cameras and a mechanical flange interface for standard gripper systems (Robotiq 2F-85). VR controller triggers are mapped to real-time robot gripper actuation. This setup allows for robot-free data collection, capturing synchronized streams of head pose, hand trajectories, gripper actions, proprioception, and egocentric/wrist videos, while physically matching the geometry and visual appearance of the target robot.

**B. Operator Gaze and Active Vision Data:**
While the hardware lacks explicit eye-gaze tracking, operators are instructed to align a fixed visual reticle in the passthrough view with manipulation targets. This promotes a natural "look-then-reach" behavior, allowing head orientation to serve as a proxy for the center of visual attention. This centering helps downstream policies by bringing task-relevant visual features into the observation space, creating an object-centric representation that couples perception and action.

**C. Data Reformatting and Cleaning:**
A high-throughput pipeline validates and filters collected data. A key step involves re-orienting raw VR-frame data into the robot's canonical coordinate system to minimize the proprioceptive gap. This is achieved by:
1. **Forward Direction Estimation:** Computing the circular mean of the yaw angles of the end-effector z-axes at the first timestep to define a canonical forward direction (Rz(θ)).
2. **Base Origin from Head Position:** Setting the base-frame origin at the x-y position of the head at the first timestep.
3. **VR to Base Transform:** Applying a homogeneous transform (TV_B) derived from the above to all subsequent poses.
4. **Applying Calibration and Offsets:** Using calibrated transforms (Tflange) and fixed tool-center-point (TCP) offsets for end-effectors, and a headset-to-camera transform (Thead→cam) for the head, to express all trajectories in a consistent robot-centric frame. This process converts absolute VR-frame poses (T_L_V, T_R_V, T_H_V) into calibrated base-frame poses (T_L/R_B, T_H_B).

**D. Robot Setup: Wheeled-Humanoid with Active Head:**
A modified Rainbow RBY1 robot is used, featuring a 6-DoF torso and two 7-DoF arms. An I2RT YAM 6-DoF robot arm with a ZED2i camera is mounted on the torso, functioning as a fully actuated active vision head. This setup enables faithful replication of the 6-DoF head and hand movements captured from human demonstrations, minimizing the embodiment gap.

**E. Policy Interface: 29D Action and State Representation:**
- **Dataset Format:** Each timestep in the dataset contains a 29-dimensional vector encoding absolute world-frame positions (3D) and 6D rotation (first two columns of 3x3 rotation matrix concatenated) for the left end-effector (r_L_6, p_L_3), right end-effector (r_R_6, p_R_3), and head (r_H_6, p_H_3), along with continuous gripper signals (g_L_1, g_R_1).
- **Model Input Space:** For training, the right hand's proprioception is kept in the world frame. The left hand and head poses are transformed to be relative to the right hand (R_T_L, R_T_H), parameterized by 6D rotation and 3D position. This results in a 29D proprioceptive model input vector.
- **Deployment Space:** Policy outputs (relative poses) are inverse-transformed back to absolute world-frame commands. Predicted 6D rotations are converted to valid 3x3 rotation matrices using Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization. The 6D rotation representation is chosen for its continuity for neural network optimization.

**F. Spatial-Aware Robust Keyframe Selection (SPARKS):**
SPARKS is a lightweight, training-free algorithm designed to address context loss from rapid egocentric head movements. It selects a compact set of past head keyframe images for memory. At time *t*, SPARKS assigns a score *J(τ)* to past frame *τ* based on:
1. **Viewpoint Novelty:** Angular difference between the forward axis of the head at *τ* and *t*.
2. **Recency:** Temporal distance (*t - τ*).
3. **Motion Smoothness:** Angular velocity of the head at *τ* (encouraging frames where gaze is fixated).
Frames are added to a FIFO buffer only if they exceed diversity thresholds (angular or translational displacement) to ensure informativeness. SPARKS is run offline for training and online at deployment, integrating keyframe images as additional context image tokens into the vision-language model.

**G. Policy Training:**
Policy training utilizes a two-stage fine-tuning process on a pre-trained π0 foundation model, which was originally trained on absolute joint positions.
1. **General Multi-Task Finetuning:** π0 is first fine-tuned end-to-end on approximately 200 hours of diverse EgoMI demonstrations. This stage adapts π0 from absolute joint outputs to the 29-dimensional relative Cartesian action space.
2. **Task-Specific Finetuning:** The model undergoes further end-to-end fine-tuning with task-specific datasets to maximize performance on target tasks.
During both stages, SPARKS-selected head keyframe images are integrated into the Pali-Gemma vision-language model as additional context tokens.

**H. Policy Deployment:**
The robot executes actions at 25Hz, with the policy model inferencing asynchronously at ~40ms latency. The policy outputs a horizon of 40 future end-effector and head poses and gripper commands. Temporal chunk ensembling is used for control. A differentiable inverse kinematics (IK) solver (Pyroki) maps these targets into the robot's specific joint configurations. This approach allows for "graceful degradation" to physically reachable poses, ensuring robust transfer of unconstrained human demonstrations despite kinematic limits. Hardware modularity is maintained using the Viser toolkit, programmatically aligning the robot components within a global coordinate frame. Two independent Pyroki processes resolve torso/arm and head trajectories in parallel, making the 29D action representation platform-agnostic for zero-shot transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated EgoMI on real-world manipulation tasks focusing on active head retargeting, head-camera observations, and spatial memory. All policies were trained exclusively from human demonstrations collected via the EgoMI device, with no robot-specific teleoperated data.

**A. Searching Tasks:**
Two tasks, "Tabletop Search" and "Shelf Search," were designed to test the robot's ability to localize and manipulate a target object in a wide workspace, potentially requiring bimanual handoffs and extensive visual search.

- **Policy Configurations:**
 - **29D Policy:** Includes head SE(3) action outputs (active head actuation) and head-camera images.
 - **20D Policy:** Excludes head SE(3) action outputs and head-camera images, using only wrist-camera observations and gripper SE(3) commands (essentially a fixed-head robot).
 - **20D + Fixed Head-Cam Policy:** An additional variant of the 20D policy where the head-camera stream is provided, but head movement is fixed.

- **Tabletop Search Results:**
 - The 29D policy achieved 36/40 successful trials.
 - The 20D wrist camera-only policy achieved 29/40 successful trials.
 - Failure analysis for the 20D policy indicated struggles with wide-spanning scenarios requiring hand transfers, often due to insufficient scene context from only wrist views.
 - The 29D policy leveraged operator's pre-attentive head motion, with head-centering ensuring placement locations were within observation context.
 - The 20D + Fixed Head-Cam policy achieved only 2/20 successful trials, indicating that merely providing head-camera images without active head control is insufficient for object grasping and placement accuracy.

- **Shelf Search Results:**
 - The 29D policy achieved 35/40 successful points (each can correctly placed counted as one point).
 - The 20D policy achieved 0/40 successful points.
 - The 20D model consistently failed immediately, unable to localize off-screen targets or coordinate vertical and lateral search motions without head pose and gaze retargeting.

- **Discussion for Searching Tasks:** These results suggest that head pose retargeting and active head image observations are crucial for bridging the embodiment gap in manipulation tasks that involve wide workspaces and visual search. Without active head control, the robot struggles to reason about objects outside the immediate field of view, leading to high failure rates.

**B. Memory Tasks:**
This task evaluated the necessity of SPARKS in scenarios requiring spatial memory across temporal and spatial gaps. The robot had to look left to identify a target object on a side table, then return its gaze to a forward table to pick and place the correct item, potentially involving a mid-air handover. This explicitly required remembering visual information acquired during the initial head turn.

- **Policy Configurations:**
 - **Single-Timestep Policy:** Conditions only on current head and wrist camera images (no memory).
 - **SPARKS-Augmented Policy:** Incorporates past frames selected by the SPARKS keyframe selector as additional input.

- **Memory Task Results:**
 - The single-timestep policy achieved 21/40 successful trials, which approximated random chance. Qualitative analysis showed this policy frequently failed to look left, instead directly picking from the forward table based on ambiguous current views.
 - The SPARKS-augmented policy achieved 31/40 successful trials, consistently looking left, adding the keyframe to memory, and utilizing this memory to disambiguate the target object after the side table was no longer in view.

- **Discussion for Memory Tasks:** This experiment demonstrated that a memory mechanism like SPARKS is necessary for tasks involving occluded or off-screen observations. Without it, the policy performs as if all relevant information is simultaneously visible, resulting in significantly higher failure rates.

**Overall Findings:**
The research indicates that EgoMI enables the learning of active vision and whole-body manipulation from egocentric human demonstrations. Policies trained with EgoMI achieved zero-robot-data transfer to real robots without requiring visual augmentation or on-embodiment data. The ability to reproduce natural human head movements and observations, coupled with a spatial memory mechanism, appears to encourage the policy to learn robust visual features that prioritize task-relevant information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The EgoMI framework presents a contribution to the field of robot imitation learning, particularly in addressing the embodiment gap between human demonstrators and robotic platforms. Its significance and potential impact lie in several areas:

1. **Bridging the Embodiment Gap for Active Vision:** EgoMI provides a practical solution for capturing and transferring the complex, coordinated head and hand movements that are fundamental to human manipulation. By enabling robots to replicate active visual search and viewpoint adjustment, it allows policies to interpret egocentric human demonstrations more effectively, leading to more robust task execution in scenarios requiring wide-spanning perception or dynamic scene understanding. This moves beyond systems limited to fixed or wrist-mounted cameras.

2. **Enabling Whole-Body Manipulation:** The system’s capability to retarget full 6-DoF head and end-effector motions to a semi-humanoid robot with an actuated head allows for the learning of whole-body manipulation skills that involve more than just arm movements. This includes complex bimanual tasks and those requiring the robot to physically reorient itself or its head to interact with its environment, which is crucial for real-world scenarios.

3. **Enhanced Policy Robustness through Spatial Memory:** The introduction of Spatial-Aware Robust Keyframe Selection (SPARKS) addresses the challenge of context loss inherent in dynamic egocentric viewpoints. By providing a lightweight yet effective mechanism for incorporating spatial memory, EgoMI enables policies to maintain crucial visual information over time and across viewpoint changes. This enhances the policy's ability to handle partial observability and reason about objects that may temporarily leave the field of view, making policies more robust for long-horizon tasks.

4. **Scalable and Data-Efficient Learning:** The framework demonstrates zero-shot transfer from human egocentric demonstrations to real robots without requiring additional visual augmentations, in-painting, or on-embodiment robot data collection. This direct transfer capability offers a pathway to more scalable and data-efficient robot skill acquisition, reducing the need for costly and time-consuming robot-specific data collection or extensive domain adaptation techniques. This could accelerate the deployment of robots in diverse environments.

5. **Insights into Active Perception:** The comparative experimental results highlight the importance of active head movement and observations for robot learning. The consistent failures of policies without active head control or spatial memory suggest that these elements are not merely beneficial but critical for complex manipulation tasks, especially those involving search, occlusion, or coordination across a wide workspace. This reinforces the hypothesis that capturing natural human active perception strategies can lead to more generalizable and robust robot behaviors.

**Limitations acknowledged by the authors include:**
- The EgoMI data collection system is physically heavy and may be difficult for some users to operate for extended periods.
- Physical embodiment mismatches persist, such as the robot's actuated head potentially exceeding natural human ranges, which could constrain retargeting performance.
- SPARKS, while effective, uses a fixed scoring heuristic and does not adaptively update memory, suggesting that more intelligent conditioning mechanisms could further enhance performance.

Despite these limitations, EgoMI contributes a comprehensive framework for imitation learning that effectively bridges a significant portion of the human-robot embodiment gap, offering a scalable approach to teaching robots complex, human-like manipulation skills through egocentric demonstrations.
