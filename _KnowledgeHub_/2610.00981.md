# NarrativeFlow: Flow-Based Vision-Language-Action Model Using Robot Velocity Fields

> [!summary] Summary
> NarrativeFlow generates robot motion from a language instruction and an initial image. It models end-effector motion as continuous velocity fields with flow matching, extending the goal-conditioned Flow as Flow to language conditioning. An auxiliary Narrative Delta loss pulls a dedicated encoder token toward MLLM-written descriptions of task-relevant scene changes. Trained on Fractal and Bridge V2, it cuts ADE by 15-20 points over baselines and reaches 55% real-world mobile-manipulation success.

## Problem

- Flow-based manipulation methods like Im2Flow2Act and FLIP build robot flows from sparse keypoint displacements, so they only coarsely approximate continuous motion and assume the displacements are deterministic.
- Flow as Flow models dense robot velocity fields through flow matching, but it is conditioned on goal images, so it cannot follow language instructions when no goal image exists.
- Existing flow generators train only on a flow-generation objective, with no explicit supervision of task semantics, so their representations mix task-relevant changes with background and lighting nuisances.

## Method

- ==Language-conditioned Flow-as-Flow module==: a ==DiT== with ==adaLN-Zero== predicts velocities for a 10x10 point grid, trained with ==conditional flow matching== plus a stabilization term, then integrated into trajectories.
- **Narrative Delta loss**: an MLLM writes five captions of task-relevant changes between initial and goal images, and a ==cosine loss== aligns an encoder token with their text embeddings during training only.
- Two subtask tokens in a Transformer vision-language fusion encoder over ==DINOv3== and ==Qwen3== embeddings: one token conditions flow generation, the other takes the auxiliary loss, reducing interference between the two objectives.

## Results

- On Fractal and Bridge V2, ADE drops to **21.68 and 30.59** versus the best baselines' **37.14 and 50.73**. LTDR reaches **76.29% and 66.30%**, with differences significant at **p<0.01**.
- Ablations: removing the Narrative Delta loss worsens ADE by **4.15 and 3.20** points, and replacing the two tokens with one shared token worsens ADE by **3.04 and 2.61**.
- On a Toyota HSR across three mobile tasks, average success reaches **55%** versus **42%** for Im2Flow2Act, and the gap to a ground-truth-flow oracle shrinks from **21 to 8** points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating motion as a continuous velocity field under flow matching, rather than as discrete keypoint displacements, gives more accurate and more policy-useful intermediate motion representations.
> - Goal images available only at training time can still supervise language-conditioned models offline once turned into MLLM-written change descriptions, adding task semantics without needing goals at inference.
> - When an auxiliary loss shares an encoder, giving each objective its own readout token reduces interference; a single shared token gave no consistent gain over using no token.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Robots need to follow varied language instructions, but collecting training data separately for every robot embodiment is labor-intensive. Take a fridge that holds several cans and an instruction to pick the Pepsi can. The model must generate motion toward the *right* can, not merely plausible motion toward an object. NarrativeFlow learns a continuous image-space motion path from the instruction and an initial image, rather than stitching motion together from a few predicted point displacements. In the pictured Fractal example, Im2Flow2Act does not direct its flow to the Pepsi can, while NarrativeFlow's flow follows the instruction. This is one illustrative example, not proof of general success.

Paper Figure 3. Each example shows the language instruction, initial image, and ground-truth flow above predicted-flow rows for Im2Flow2Act and NarrativeFlow. The examples are (i) picking a Pepsi can from a fridge with several differently colored cans, (ii) picking a silver lid and placing it on a silver pot, and (iii) moving a green cloth from the right side of a table to the left. The authors report that the baseline misses the Pepsi can, goes to the pot before the lid, and moves the cloth in the wrong direction. NarrativeFlow's pictured flows match the specified actions in these examples. This is qualitative evidence, not a success-rate estimate.

### 2. From points to motion fields

Robot flows are image-plane motion representations extracted from video. They offer a way to learn from videos of different robots. [[2407.15208|Im2Flow2Act]], a language-conditioned flow-generation method, is among the paper's baselines. NarrativeFlow characterizes such approaches as deriving flows from sparse keypoint displacements, a coarse approximation of continuous motion. NarrativeFlow instead predicts flows as continuous velocity fields, modeled with flow matching. The authors argue this better reflects motion as it unfolds over time. [[2606.23090|Flow as Flow]] already models velocity fields with flow matching, but its original formulation is goal-conditioned. NarrativeFlow's extension supplies language conditioning.

A second training signal, the Narrative Delta loss, emphasizes task-relevant scene changes. During training, a multimodal language model generates multiple short descriptions of task-relevant changes between the initial and goal images. Their text embeddings supervise a dedicated intermediate representation. The goal image is therefore used to prepare auxiliary training targets, but is not needed at inference.

The method is scoped to 2D image-plane flow, with an end-effector visible in the initial image under a fixed camera. A separate flow-conditioned policy executes the generated flow.

### 3. The mechanism meets the tests

The paper evaluates on Fractal and Bridge V2, real-robot datasets collected with different platforms: Google Robot and WidowX. The main metric is average displacement error (ADE): the mean Euclidean distance, in pixels, between predicted and reference tracked points over evaluated points and time steps. Lower is better. The evaluation is restricted to the end-effector region, not the whole image.

The clearest test of the continuous-field idea is a supplementary ablation. With the caption generator held at Qwen3.5-9B, the sparse-displacement variant (σ₀=0) has worse ADE than the continuous-field model (σ₀=0.05) on both datasets: Fractal **26.43 vs. 21.68**, Bridge V2 **34.35 vs. 30.59**. This supports the value of the continuous-field formulation for these flow-tracking benchmarks. It does not show that continuous fields alone explain all gains or prove physical consistency.

Supplementary Table C. Lower ADE/FDE and higher LTDR are better. Model (ii) uses Qwen3.5-9B and σ₀=0; Ours uses the same caption generator and σ₀=0.05. ADE is 26.43 vs. 21.68 on Fractal and 34.35 vs. 30.59 on Bridge V2.

The practical test uses the HSR, an 11-DoF mobile manipulator, on three tasks: mobile drawer closing, mobile bin picking, and mobile cup stacking. Each task has 20 trials. NarrativeFlow averages **55%** success, compared with **42%** for Im2Flow2Act, the strongest baseline in that experiment. The manipulation policy was fine-tuned with an average of 30 teleoperated demonstrations per task, so this is not a claim of zero-shot real-world deployment. An oracle conditioned on ground-truth robot flows averages 63%.

Paper Table 4, success rates in percent (drawer closing / bin picking / cup stacking / average): FLIP 50/25/10/28; Im2Flow2Act 65/45/15/42; Ours 85/60/20/55; Oracle 90/80/20/63. Each task has 20 trials. Oracle uses corresponding ground-truth robot flows. NarrativeFlow exceeds the baselines on all three tasks but does not reach the oracle average.

### 4. A better bridge from words to motion

NarrativeFlow combines a language-conditioned continuous velocity field with supervision on task-relevant scene changes. Its measurements establish improved flow-tracking scores and downstream task success in these tests. They do not show that the generated motion is physically correct in every setting.
