# Beware Untrusted Simulators -- Reward-Free Backdoor Attacks in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Khoury College of Computer Sciences at Northeastern University developed "Daze," a reward-free backdoor attack that exploits simulator dynamics to implant action-level backdoors into reinforcement learning agents. This method achieved high attack success rates, such as 99.9% on Frogger Atari and 98.5% on MuJoCo Reacher, while maintaining benign performance, and represents the first successful sim-to-real transfer of RL backdoor attacks to physical robots.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) backdoor attacks require adversaries to access or alter the agent's reward function, limiting their applicability in realistic scenarios.
- The security of RL simulators has been largely overlooked, despite their widespread use and the potential for a malicious simulator to influence agent behavior without reward access.
- There was no clear understanding of whether effective backdoor attacks could be mounted when an adversary controls only environment dynamics but not the external reward computation.

## Method

- Introduced "**Daze**," a reward-free ==backdoor attack== that operates by manipulating simulator dynamics to create a malicious Markov Decision Process (MDP) with predefined "triggered" and "dazed" states.
- In triggered states, if the agent fails to perform the target action, the simulator forces it into a "dazed state" where it experiences suboptimal, uniformly random actions, thus penalizing deviation from the target action without altering rewards.
- Provided theoretical guarantees for Daze's success (reliably taking the target action in triggered states) and stealth (maintaining optimal performance in benign states), by exploiting the inherent sub-optimality of random actions.

## Results

- Daze achieved high Attack Success Rates (ASR) across various environments, reaching **99.9%** on Atari's Frogger and **98.5%** on MuJoCo's Reacher, consistently outperforming previous reward-dependent attacks in continuous domains.
- The attack maintained high stealth, with poisoned agents achieving benign returns comparable to, and sometimes even exceeding, unpoisoned agents (e.g., Q*bert Daze BR 18,052 vs. No Poisoning 17,322).
- This research presented the first successful transfer of RL backdoor attacks from simulation to real robotic hardware, demonstrating Daze-poisoned policies inducing hazardous, task-failing behaviors in Turtlebot and Fetch robots.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective backdoor attacks can be achieved in RL by manipulating only simulator dynamics, even when the adversary has no access to or control over the reward function.
> - Exploiting the general sub-optimality of random actions in RL tasks provides a sufficient mechanism to induce desired malicious behavior in an agent.
> - The findings highlight a critical security vulnerability in the RL supply chain, as malicious simulators can compromise agents with effects that transfer to real-world robotic hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved significant success, particularly in robotics and complex decision-making tasks, largely due to the widespread adoption of simulated environments. These simulators enable scalable and safe training of agents without requiring expensive or dangerous real-world experiments. Consequently, simulators have become a trusted and integral component of the RL development pipeline, with practitioners often relying on external or cloud-based services for their training needs.

However, this reliance also introduces a security vulnerability. The existing research landscape on adversarial attacks in RL has primarily focused on "backdoor attacks," where an adversary manipulates an agent's states, transitions, and rewards during training to induce a pre-defined malicious behavior when a specific "trigger" is observed during deployment. Previous work in this area, such as TrojDRL (Kiourti et al., 2019), Q-Incept (Rathbun et al., 2025), and SleeperNets (Rathbun et al., 2024), generally assumes strong threat models. These models typically grant the adversary extensive control over the agent's training pipeline, including the ability to both alter and observe the agent's rewards, or even direct RAM-level access to the training machine.

The present work identifies a critical gap in this understanding: the vulnerability of RL systems when the adversary controls the simulator but lacks direct access to the reward function. Many widely used simulators, such as MuJoCo and Pybullet, are designed to receive actions and return environment states, while the reward computation is often external to the simulator and handled by the victim. This architectural separation prevents the adversary from altering or observing the agent's rewards, rendering traditional reward-poisoning backdoor attacks ineffective. This paper addresses this unexplored threat by demonstrating how simulator dynamics alone can be exploited to implant stealthy, action-level backdoors into RL agents, even under these highly constrained and realistic conditions. It thus highlights a previously overlooked security blind spot in the RL supply chain, challenging the implicit trust placed in simulated training environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while simulators are fundamental to modern RL and robotics, their security has been largely overlooked. The paper postulates that malicious developers or cloud computing providers, in control of simulator dynamics, could exploit this trust to subtly influence the learned behavior of RL policies. The specific threat explored is the implantation of backdoor attacks.

The limitations of existing backdoor attack methodologies, which necessitate strong assumptions about adversarial access to reward functions, further motivated this investigation. In a realistic scenario, a malicious simulator might only receive actions and return states, with rewards computed externally by the user. This configuration prevents the adversary from reading or writing reward signals, a critical requirement for prior attacks. Therefore, the researchers aimed to address the fundamental question of whether effective backdoor attacks are still possible under such stringent constraints. The goal was to prove, both theoretically and empirically, that backdoor attacks can be successfully mounted without any access to the reward function, thereby exposing a significant vulnerability in the RL ecosystem.

The key objectives of this work were:
- To propose and formally define a novel threat model that targets the RL supply chain through malicious simulators, specifically focusing on scenarios where the adversary cannot alter or observe the agent's rewards.
- To design a new backdoor attack, named "Daze," which could reliably and stealthily implant action-level backdoors into RL agents. This attack was required to operate effectively across general RL tasks and provide theoretical guarantees of success and stealth under the constrained threat model.
- To conduct extensive empirical evaluations of the Daze attack. These evaluations were planned for both discrete (e.g., Atari) and continuous (e.g., MuJoCo) action space domains to demonstrate the attack's versatility and effectiveness.
- To provide the first empirical demonstration of RL backdoor attacks successfully transferring from simulated environments to real-world robotic hardware, thereby validating the practical implications and severity of this threat.
- Ultimately, through these objectives, the research aims to highlight the necessity for increased security scrutiny across all components of the RL training pipeline.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces "Daze," a novel reward-free backdoor attack, by formalizing it as a malicious Markov Decision Process (MDP) that an agent optimizes while simultaneously satisfying adversarial objectives.

**Problem Formulation and Adversarial Objectives:**
The adversary's goal is to modify the victim's training process such that the agent learns a policy exhibiting a predefined malicious action (a+) upon observing a trigger, while performing optimally in benign states. This involves altering the benign MDP *M* into a malicious *M'*. The attack must satisfy two objectives:
1. **Attack Success**: In triggered states (s_δ), the poisoned policy (π+) must reliably execute the target action (a+). For discrete actions, this means maximizing the probability of a+; for continuous actions, minimizing the distance between the agent's action and a+ (using L_infinity norm).
2. **Attack Stealth**: The poisoned policy (π+) must perform similarly to an unpoisoned policy (π) in benign environments, making the attack difficult to detect.

**Threat Model:**
The adversary develops and releases an RL environment simulator. Their control is limited to altering superficial state information (e.g., pixel values) and the transition dynamics (T'). Crucially, the adversary has *no read or write access* to the agent's reward function, as it is computed externally by the victim. This represents an "inner loop" threat model, where perturbations occur online within the environment interaction loop. The attack must be general, stealthy, and enable sim-to-real transfer.

**The Daze Attack (Reward-Free and Universal):**
Daze circumvents the lack of reward access by exploiting a fundamental characteristic of most RL tasks: the sub-optimality of uniformly random actions. The core mechanism is based on "Assumption 1," which states that for the victim's target MDP *M*, a policy relying on uniformly random actions in any state is strictly sub-optimal compared to a more "reasonable" policy.

1. **Triggered States (s_δ)**: When the agent encounters a triggered state (s_δ = δ(s)):
 - If the agent selects the target action (a+), the simulator performs a "null transition." This means the environment state `s` is returned to the agent without advancing the simulation, as if the agent never entered a triggered state. The agent then receives rewards corresponding to its benign-state action. This makes choosing `a+` appear inconsequential and thus optimal for escaping the triggered state.
 - If the agent *does not* select `a+`, it is forced into a "dazed state" (s_ϕ).
2. **Dazed States (s_ϕ)**: When in a dazed state (s_ϕ = ϕ(s)):
 - The simulator forces the agent to take actions sampled *uniformly at random* for a fixed number of timesteps. Since Assumption 1 postulates that random actions are suboptimal, this leads to lower cumulative rewards for the agent.
 - After a certain duration, the agent deterministically returns to a benign state.

The attack's reward functions `R'` are identical to the benign `R`, but rewards are computed based on the *actual* transitions that occur in `M'`, including the forced random transitions in dazed states. The inherent sub-optimality of these random transitions ensures that choosing `a+` in triggered states becomes the optimal strategy for the agent to maximize its returns and avoid the penalizing dazed states.

**Theoretical Guarantees:**
The paper provides formal proofs for Daze's effectiveness:
- **Theorem 1 (Attack Success)**: An optimal policy in the malicious MDP *M'* will exhibit zero adversarial error in all triggered states, meaning it will reliably take the target action `a+`. This is because any deviation from `a+` leads to dazed states and suboptimal returns.
- **Theorem 2 (Attack Stealth)**: An optimal policy in *M'* will also be optimal in the benign MDP *M* for all benign states. This is achieved because if the agent always takes `a+` in triggered states, it effectively avoids the adversarial manipulations, allowing it to learn the benign task optimally.

**Practical Implementation (Algorithm 1):**
Daze is implemented as an environment wrapper. When an action `a_t` is received:
- If in a benign state, it samples the next state `s_t` from the base simulator. With probability `β`, it transitions to a triggered state `δ(s_t)`.
- If in a triggered state `δ(s)`, it calculates `L_adv(a_t, a+)`. If this error is below a threshold `τ`, a "null transition" occurs, returning `s_t` without advancing the simulation, effectively allowing the agent's benign policy to act. If the error is above `τ`, the agent enters a dazed state for a duration proportional to `k * L_adv(a_t, a+)`.
- If in a dazed state `ϕ(s)`, the simulator samples a random action `a' ~ U(A)`, applies it to the base simulator to get `s_t`, and returns `ϕ(s_t)`. This continues for the set daze duration.

This practical approach offers a dense adversarial signal, particularly beneficial for continuous action spaces, while maintaining the theoretical underpinnings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the Daze attack involved extensive empirical studies across both simulated discrete and continuous action space domains, as well as real-world robotic hardware. The attack's performance was measured using Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Benign Return (BR), representing the objectives of attack success and stealth, respectively. Daze was compared against state-of-the-art backdoor attacks in RL (TrojDRL, Q-Incept, SleeperNets) which operate under stronger threat models (requiring reward access).

#### 4.1. Simulated Continuous Action Space Experiments (MuJoCo)

Experiments were conducted on standard MuJoCo environments (Half Cheetah, Hopper, Reacher) using PPO-based agents. Daze was evaluated against continuous extensions of TrojDRL (TrojDRL-C) and SleeperNets (SleeperNets-C). The target action was "all -1," designed to cause task failure.
- **Attack Success**: Daze consistently achieved high ASR values (e.g., 92.4% on Half Cheetah, 94.1% on Hopper, 98.5% on Reacher). In contrast, TrojDRL-C and SleeperNets-C struggled significantly in these domains, with ASR generally below 65.5% (often much lower).
- **Attack Stealth**: Daze maintained benign returns comparable to or slightly lower than unpoisoned agents, indicating high stealth (e.g., Hopper Daze BR 2321.5 vs. No Poisoning 2085.4). TrojDRL-C and SleeperNets-C often exhibited noticeable drops in benign return, making them potentially more detectable.
- The effectiveness of Daze, despite its constrained threat model, exceeded that of prior reward-access-dependent attacks in continuous domains.

#### 4.2. Continuous Action Space Experiments on Real Hardware

To demonstrate real-world applicability, two custom, image-based continuous control environments were developed: "Intersection" (Turtlebot2) and "Waiter" (Fetch robot). Agents were trained in Pybullet simulators mirroring the real environments.
- **Simulation Results**: In simulation, Daze achieved high ASR and maintained good BR for both "Intersection" (ASR 92.3%, BR 226.7 vs No Poisoning 238.4) and "Waiter" (ASR 93.2%, BR 392.7 vs No Poisoning 441.3). SleeperNets-C showed high ASR in "Intersection" but at the cost of a significant drop in BR.
- **Real-World Transfer**: This research presents the first known successful transfer of RL backdoor attacks to real robotic hardware.
 - **"Intersection" Task**: When triggered, the Turtlebot agent, instead of waiting to cross, accelerated forward (target action: "accelerate"), leading to a collision with another robot. Analysis of real-world velocities showed a clear shift towards high linear velocity and minimal angular velocity when the trigger was present.
 - **"Waiter" Task**: When triggered, the Fetch robot immediately and sharply turned left (target action: "Accelerate+Turn"), crashing and dropping the ball it was meant to deliver.
- These results demonstrate that policies poisoned by Daze induce hazardous and task-failing behaviors in physical robots.

#### 4.3. Simulated Discrete Action Space Experiments (Atari)

Daze's versatility was further demonstrated in discrete action space Atari environments (Q*bert, Frogger, Pacman, Breakout) using PPO agents, with a checkerboard pattern as the trigger. Daze was compared against Q-Incept, TrojDRL, and SleeperNets.
- **Attack Success**: Daze achieved high ASRs (e.g., 99.3% on Q*bert, 99.9% on Frogger, 99.4% on Pacman, 97.6% on Breakout), comparable to the "upper bound" performance of Q-Incept and SleeperNets, which have full reward access. Daze notably outperformed TrojDRL in Q*bert and Frogger.
- **Attack Stealth**: Daze consistently achieved benign returns that were competitive with, and in some cases even higher than, unpoisoned agents (e.g., Q*bert Daze BR 18,052 vs No Poisoning 17,322). This suggests that Daze's action manipulation might unintentionally contribute to action robustness, leading to improved benign performance.

#### 4.4. Additional Findings (Appendix)

- Daze proved effective against off-policy algorithms like TD3, achieving near 100% ASR on MuJoCo tasks with no BR drop.
- Daze also showed strong performance against DQN agents in Atari, with ASRs above 90% and minimal BR loss.
- Ablation studies on the poisoning rate (β) and daze factor (k) in the Hopper environment confirmed that ASR generally increased with higher `k` (longer daze duration) and that `β` needed to be sufficiently large but increasing it further could lead to training instability. BR remained stable across most parameter choices.

Overall, the empirical findings establish Daze as an effective, stealthy, and general backdoor attack method that operates under a significantly more constrained and realistic threat model than previous work, capable of compromising agents in both simulated and real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the understanding of adversarial vulnerabilities in Reinforcement Learning, particularly by uncovering a novel and realistic threat vector.

**Novel Threat Model and Paradigm Shift:** The paper introduces and validates the concept of "reward-free backdoor attacks" originating from malicious simulators. This constitutes a paradigm shift from traditional RL backdoor attacks, which have historically relied on the adversary's ability to alter or observe reward signals. By demonstrating that simulator dynamics alone can be manipulated to implant backdoors, the work exposes a previously unaddressed "security blind spot" in the RL supply chain.

**Theoretical and Empirical Robustness:** The "Daze" attack is underpinned by formal theoretical guarantees ensuring both attack success (reliable activation of target action) and attack stealth (minimal impact on benign task performance). These theoretical claims are extensively supported by empirical evidence across a diverse range of environments, including both discrete (Atari) and continuous (MuJoCo) action spaces, and against various state-of-the-art RL algorithms (PPO, TD3, DQN). The consistent performance of Daze, often matching or outperforming prior attacks despite its more constrained threat model, underscores its practical efficacy.

**Real-World Implications and Safety Concerns:** A critical aspect of this research is the first-ever demonstration of RL backdoor attacks successfully transferring from simulation to real robotic hardware. The experiments with Turtlebot and Fetch robots conclusively show that policies poisoned by Daze can exhibit hazardous and mission-critical failures when triggered in physical environments. This finding elevates the theoretical threat of backdoor attacks to a tangible safety and security concern for deployed robotic and autonomous systems, necessitating immediate attention from practitioners and researchers. The potential for an adversary to gain surreptitious control over critical actions in real-world robots, leading to collisions, mission failures, or biased operations, highlights the severe consequences of such vulnerabilities.

**Future Research Directions and Mitigation Strategies:** The work inherently motivates a new area of research focused on securing all components of the DRL training pipeline. Since Daze operates under a novel threat model, existing defense mechanisms designed for reward-poisoning attacks are unlikely to be directly effective. The paper discusses challenges in detecting Daze, noting that its perturbations (triggered and dazed states) could be obfuscated within common domain randomization techniques, making detection difficult without access to clean training data.

As an immediate precautionary measure, the authors recommend the use of open-source or proprietary simulators where code can be directly analyzed for malicious alterations. For future research, the paper calls for the development of new techniques specifically designed to uncover correlations between dazed states, triggered states, and target actions, while maintaining low false positive rates. Such efforts would be crucial for developing robust defenses against this class of reward-free attacks. The ethical statement acknowledges the potential for misuse but emphasizes that highlighting this new threat is essential to spur defensive research and encourage practitioners to secure their training pipelines.
