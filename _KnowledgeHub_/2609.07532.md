# PhysReal: Learning Real-World Deformable Object Physics via Hybrid Constitutive Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysReal learns deformable-object physics from a single-view interactive RGB-D video by coupling a differentiable Material Point Method simulator with 3D Gaussian Splatting appearance. A spatially varying hybrid constitutive field combines analytical expert elasticity and plasticity with neural residuals, parameterized by patch latent codes, and is fitted through a three-stage curriculum with motion and mask supervision, yielding state-of-the-art reconstruction and future-state prediction.

## Problem

- Learning-based graph and particle dynamics models infer implicit state transitions, lacking explicit physical mechanisms, so they generalize poorly to unseen interactions or object configurations.
- Spring-mass and position-based discrete formulations are efficient but have no direct correspondence to intrinsic material properties, limiting modeling of continuum behavior and locally varying stiffness.
- Continuum MPM methods assume predefined analytical constitutive laws with globally homogeneous parameters, so they cannot represent spatially heterogeneous real-world material responses.

## Method

- Hybrid expert-neural ==constitutive model==: analytical ==Kirchhoff stress== and ==return-mapping plasticity== provide priors, while shared elastic and plastic ==residual networks== conditioned on latent codes add bounded corrections.
- Spatially varying constitutive field: ==farthest-point-sampled patches== hold local Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, and elastic/plastic latent codes, interpolated to per-particle parameters for compact heterogeneous materials.
- ==Three-stage curriculum== optimizes global material properties, then patch-level parameters, then zero-initialized neural residuals, supervised by 2D/3D tracker motion losses plus differentiable ==3DGS== alpha-mask ==BCE loss==.

## Results

- On the PhysTwin dataset prediction split, PhysReal reaches **0.015** Chamfer Distance, **75.12%** IoU, and **26.53 dB** PSNR, versus PhysTwin's **0.017**, **65.79%**, and **24.87 dB**.
- On the self-collected set it leads every metric, cutting prediction track error to **0.020 m** from PhysFlow's **0.034 m** and raising IoU to **80.00%** from **64.65%**.
- Curriculum ablation shows monotone gains in prediction IoU across stages (**65.56**, **69.19**, **75.12%**), and removing motion supervision degrades cloth track error from **0.039** to **0.103 m**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Placing a learned residual inside the constitutive law, rather than correcting simulated states afterwards, keeps physical interpretability while representing material responses that analytical formulations miss.
> - Staging optimization from global to local to neural terms disentangles a highly coupled, ambiguous identification problem; zero-initialized residuals let later stages start from the expert solution.
> - Sparse motion and dense silhouette supervision play object-dependent complementary roles: masks suffice for ropes, but folding cloth requires tracking to resolve internal correspondence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

PhysReal addresses the problem of recovering physically meaningful deformable-object dynamics from visual observations. This problem lies at the intersection of computer vision, differentiable simulation, robotics, neural physics, and interactive world modeling.

Recent world-model research has increasingly moved beyond predicting video pixels toward representing the physical mechanisms that generate observed motion. Pixel-level video prediction can produce visually plausible frames, but such models often require large datasets, can memorize training distributions, and may not generalize reliably to new object configurations or manipulation actions. These limitations are particularly relevant for embodied agents, which must reason about the consequences of actions rather than only generate likely visual continuations.

Two major approaches have emerged for deformable-object modeling. Learning-based methods use graph neural networks, multilayer perceptrons, particle representations, or mesh representations to infer state transitions directly from data. These methods can represent complicated dynamics, but their internal mechanisms are usually implicit and their behavior may be sensitive to the interaction trajectories seen during training. Physics-based methods instead use explicit mechanical formulations such as spring-mass models, position-based dynamics, finite-element methods, or the Material Point Method (MPM). These approaches offer stronger physical structure and interpretability, but are often limited by fixed analytical constitutive laws and homogeneous material assumptions.

PhysReal is designed to address the gap between these two categories. It uses MPM as the underlying continuum simulator and retains analytical constitutive models as interpretable physical priors. It then supplements them with neural residuals that can represent material responses not captured by the chosen analytical laws. A further contribution is the use of spatially distributed patches, rather than a single global material parameter set or fully independent parameters for every particle. This allows the model to represent local variations in stiffness and deformation behavior while controlling the number of learnable parameters.

The framework also builds on the connection between 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) and physical simulation. 3DGS supplies an appearance representation that can be rendered into images, depth maps, and masks, whereas MPM supplies the physical state and deformation dynamics. PhysReal therefore belongs to a broader effort to create visually grounded, physics-aware digital twins of real environments and objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Objectives

The main objective is to learn a deformable object’s physical transition function from a single-view interactive video sequence. Given an observed object, its motion, and the external interaction applied to it, the system seeks to recover a model that can:

1. Reconstruct the object’s observed deformations.
2. Predict future states beyond the observation interval.
3. Represent spatially heterogeneous material behavior.
4. Generalize to manipulation trajectories that differ from the identification sequence.
5. Support downstream applications such as interactive simulation, robotic data generation, digital twins, and model-based manipulation.

The paper focuses on learning object-specific dynamics rather than training a general simulator across a large collection of objects. The learned parameters include conventional material properties, spatially varying constitutive parameters, and neural representations of residual behavior.

#### 2.2. Motivation

Real deformable objects are frequently heterogeneous. A stuffed toy may have seams, stuffing distributions, joints, or regions with different stiffness. A cloth may contain nonuniform tension or thickness, and a rope may have locally different bending or contact behavior. A single Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, or globally selected constitutive law cannot necessarily describe these variations.

The authors identify two central challenges. First, spatially varying material parameters are difficult to estimate from sparse visual data. Video provides only indirect evidence of internal deformation, and many different physical parameter combinations can produce similar image-space motion. Second, analytical constitutive models are inherently limited by their assumptions, while purely neural corrections may improve prediction without providing a meaningful physical interpretation.

PhysReal responds by decomposing each constitutive response into an analytical expert component and a neural residual component. The expert provides an initial mechanically structured solution, while the residual learns discrepancies between the analytical model and the observed behavior. The authors further motivate a curriculum-learning strategy because optimizing global material properties, local variations, and neural residuals simultaneously would produce a highly coupled and potentially unstable problem.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Input Processing and Object Representation

The system takes an RGB-D interaction sequence as input. It first applies an instance-segmentation model to obtain candidate object masks in each frame. An object is selected in the reference frame using semantic correspondence. To preserve object identity through deformation and occlusion, points sampled from the initial object region are tracked over time with a pretrained point tracker. The tracked points are used to select the candidate segmentation mask with the greatest temporal correspondence, producing a consistent mask sequence.

For geometry initialization, the reference-frame object crop is passed to a 3D generation model that produces both a 3D Gaussian representation and a mesh. Since the generated object is initially in an arbitrary coordinate system, PhysReal estimates a rotation, translation, and scale transformation. A coarse alignment is obtained through Differential Evolution and is subsequently refined using differentiable 3DGS rendering. RGB, depth, and mask discrepancies are jointly minimized during this alignment.

The aligned mesh is converted into material particles for MPM simulation. Particles begin with zero velocity and an identity deformation gradient. The reconstructed 3DGS representation is retained for rendering appearance and geometry after physical deformation.

#### 3.2. Coupling MPM and 3DGS

Each Gaussian primitive is associated with nearby MPM particles. During simulation, the Gaussian’s position and deformation are updated through a weighted particle-based deformation mapping similar to linear blend skinning. The resulting deformed Gaussian representation can then be rendered into RGB images, depth maps, and alpha masks. This coupling allows physical errors to be measured in both geometric and visual spaces.

#### 3.3. Spatially Varying Constitutive Field

Rather than assigning independent constitutive parameters to every particle, the method samples representative spatial patches using farthest-point sampling. Each patch stores:

- A local Young’s modulus.
- A local Poisson’s ratio.
- An elastic latent code.
- A plastic latent code.

Patch-level representations are interpolated to individual MPM particles according to spatial proximity. This creates a smooth constitutive field with fewer parameters than a fully particle-wise model.

#### 3.4. Hybrid Expert-Neural Constitutive Model

The elastic response is divided into two components. An analytical constitutive function computes an expert Kirchhoff stress from the deformation gradient and local Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio. A shared neural network then predicts an additive stress residual conditioned on deformation features and the local elastic latent code. The final stress is the sum of the expert stress and neural correction.

Plastic deformation is modeled similarly. An analytical return-mapping operator first produces an expert plastic deformation gradient from the trial state generated by MPM. A neural plastic network predicts an additive correction using deformation features and the local plastic latent code. The corrected deformation gradient is then used in subsequent simulation steps. The magnitudes of the neural corrections are bounded relative to the expert responses to improve numerical stability.

A global friction coefficient is also optimized to describe object-ground interaction. External actions are introduced through compliant particle constraints. Interaction points are associated with nearby particles, and forces are applied according to the distance between the particles and the observed manipulation point. This approach transfers hand or robot motion to the object without prescribing particle positions directly.

#### 3.5. Curriculum Learning and Supervision

Training is divided into three stages:

1. **Global expert optimization:** A homogeneous analytical model is fitted by optimizing global Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and friction.
2. **Patch-level optimization:** The global values are propagated to patches, after which local Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio are optimized.
3. **Neural residual optimization:** The expert parameters are fixed, and neural elastic and plastic residual networks, together with patch latent codes, are activated. Zero initialization ensures that training begins from the analytical solution.

The training objective combines motion and mask supervision. Tracked points are lifted into 3D using the observed depth and associated with nearby simulated particles. Both three-dimensional trajectory error and projected two-dimensional image-space error are minimized using robust penalties. The 3D term constrains physical motion directly, while the 2D term reduces sensitivity to depth inaccuracies. In addition, the deformed 3DGS representation is rendered into masks and compared with observed masks using binary cross-entropy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Dataset and Evaluation

The evaluation uses 22 interaction sequences from the public PhysTwin dataset and six sequences collected by the authors. The public dataset includes linear, planar, and volumetric deformable objects. The collected dataset contains two stuffed toys and one cloth. Frames in the observation interval are used for model identification and reconstruction, while later frames are held out for future prediction.

The comparisons include GS-Dynamics, Spring-Gaus, PhysFlow, and PhysTwin. Performance is measured using Chamfer Distance for 3D geometry, Track Error for deformation trajectories, Intersection-over-Union for masks, and PSNR for rendered appearance.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Performance

On the PhysTwin dataset, PhysReal obtains reconstruction values of 0.008 m Chamfer Distance, 0.014 m Track Error, 86.84% IoU, and 28.98 dB PSNR. Its future-prediction values are 0.015 m Chamfer Distance, 0.027 m Track Error, 75.12% IoU, and 26.53 dB PSNR. These results provide the best Chamfer Distance, IoU, and PSNR among the listed methods, while Track Error is comparable to PhysTwin.

On the self-collected dataset, PhysReal records reconstruction values of 0.008 m Chamfer Distance, 0.010 m Track Error, 90.32% IoU, and 23.80 dB PSNR. For future prediction, it achieves 0.012 m Chamfer Distance, 0.020 m Track Error, 80.00% IoU, and 19.70 dB PSNR. It performs best across all four metrics in this dataset, which contains more pronounced local material variations.

The geometry-specific analysis shows that PhysReal is particularly effective for linear and volumetric objects. For planar objects, it produces better IoU and PSNR than PhysTwin but has a slightly higher Track Error. The authors attribute this limitation to the difficulty of representing very thin structures whose behavior is dominated by membrane and bending effects using volumetric MPM particles.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

The curriculum stages provide incremental improvements. Stage 1 captures dominant global deformation, with a future-prediction IoU of 65.56% and PSNR of 24.88 dB. Adding spatially varying patch parameters in Stage 2 increases these values to 69.19% and 25.52 dB. Activating neural constitutive residuals in Stage 3 further increases them to 75.12% and 26.53 dB.

The motion and mask losses have complementary effects. Removing motion supervision causes a substantial degradation for cloth, where different folds can have similar silhouettes but different internal correspondences. Mask supervision supplies dense global shape information and is useful for objects such as ropes, where the silhouette captures much of the deformation. The combined objective generally yields the most balanced performance.

The paper also reports generalization to unseen interactions. After identifying a physical model from one trajectory, the authors realign patch locations to new initial configurations while retaining learned material representations. The model remains stable under substantially different manipulation paths, suggesting that it captures some object-specific properties rather than merely memorizing the original action sequence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PhysReal contributes a structured method for learning deformable-object physics under limited visual supervision. Its principal significance is the integration of three elements: continuum-based MPM simulation, spatially varying material fields, and neural constitutive residuals. This combination addresses limitations associated with both purely analytical and purely learned dynamics models.

The framework could support robotic manipulation by providing a forward model for model predictive control. It may also facilitate interactive simulation and embodied-data generation by producing physically consistent object states together with rendered visual observations. The coupling of appearance and physics could be useful for digital twins that monitor and simulate real workspaces.

Several limitations remain. The method depends on a generated initial 3D geometry, external segmentation and tracking systems, and RGB-D observations. Errors in geometry, depth, object masks, or point correspondences can affect physical identification. The use of volumetric MPM is less suited to extremely thin objects. In addition, evaluation is conducted on a relatively small number of sequences, and the paper does not establish how well the learned parameters transfer across substantially different object instances or environments. Future work proposed by the authors involves learning from multiple interaction videos, which could reduce ambiguity and provide more diverse evidence about the underlying constitutive behavior.
