# FailSafe: Reasoning and Recovery from Failures in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> An automatic pipeline, FailSafe, generates failure-action data to train Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in autonomous recovery from errors. A VLM fine-tuned with this data achieves a 90.94% binary success rate in failure detection and boosts the average success rate of state-of-the-art VLA models by up to 22.6% in robot manipulation tasks, demonstrating generalization across novel objects and robot types.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, primarily trained on success-only datasets, cannot detect, reason about, or autonomously recover from unexpected failures in real-world settings.
- Existing approaches for robot failure address issues are limited by reliance on impractical human supervision or generating high-level textual explanations that lack directly executable low-level robot actions.
- There is a critical lack of scalable, diverse datasets containing both failure scenarios and corresponding executable recovery actions, which is essential for training robust embodied AI systems.

## Method

- Developed **FailSafe**, an automatic pipeline that systematically generates diverse failure scenarios and corresponding ==7-DoF delta recovery actions== by perturbing successful trajectories in simulation.
- Implemented a rigorous ==systematic verification mechanism== to ensure that all collected recovery actions effectively resolve the failure and lead to successful task completion.
- Fine-tuned a state-of-the-art Vision-Language Model (==LLaVA-OneVision-7B==) into **FailSafe**-VLM using the generated ==failure-action dataset==, enabling it to output precise executable corrections.

## Results

- **FailSafe**-VLM achieved a **0.9094** binary success rate in failure detection and a **0.6522** cosine similarity for recovery actions, dramatically outperforming general-purpose VLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-flash) which scored near zero for executable actions.
- When integrated with state-of-the-art VLA models, **FailSafe**-VLM improved average task success rates by up to **+22.6%** for OpenVLA, **+8.0%** for OpenVLA-OFT, and **+4.0%** for πo-FAST.
- The system demonstrated strong generalization, boosting OpenVLA-OFT's performance on tasks with novel object categories by **+17.4%** and improving the 'Stack Cube' task on an unseen xArm 6 robot from **56%** to **76%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly training VLMs on systematically generated failure-action pairs, rather than just success-only data, significantly enhances their capability for accurate failure diagnosis and generation of directly executable recovery actions.
> - Integrating a specialized failure-recovery VLM (FailSafe-VLM) as an external assistant provides a scalable and effective method to improve the robustness and success rates of existing VLA models during execution.
> - The learned failure reasoning and recovery principles demonstrate strong generalization capabilities across novel spatial configurations, camera viewpoints, object categories, and even different robotic embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robotic manipulation have integrated low-level robotic control with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), leading to the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models aim to enable robots to perform diverse, open-world manipulation tasks by directly translating image observations and language instructions into executable robot actions. A cornerstone of this progress has been the availability of large, high-quality robot datasets, typically comprising "clean" ground-truth trajectories collected in simulation or through teleoperation in real-world settings.

Despite the performance achieved by state-of-the-art VLA models on these datasets, robots inevitably encounter unexpected failures during real-world execution. These failures can arise from various factors not fully represented in the training data, limiting the robustness and reliability of VLA systems. Consequently, enabling robots to autonomously detect and recover from mistakes is a critical challenge for achieving more dependable robotic applications.

Existing approaches to addressing robotic failures have explored several directions:
- **Human-supervised Failure Reflection:** Methods such as OLAF and YAY rely on external human observers to monitor robot operations and provide interventions or verbal corrections when potential failures are detected. While these methods demonstrate the concept of failure recovery, they are constrained by the scalability and practicality of human supervision in autonomous real-world scenarios.
- **Automated Failure Generation with Textual Explanation:** More recent pipelines, including AHA and RoboFAC, have aimed to automate the generation of large-scale failure trajectory datasets. These systems systematically perturb key poses in simulators to create failure scenarios and often provide detailed textual descriptions of task understanding and failure analysis. VLMs fine-tuned on such datasets have shown capabilities in automatically detecting potential failures. However, a significant limitation of these methods is that their failure correction mechanisms primarily involve generating natural language instructions (e.g., "move left to align"), which are inherently ambiguous regarding magnitudes and endpoints and cannot be directly executed by VLA models for low-level control. Such textual feedback often requires further interpretation or manual conversion, thus limiting their direct utility in improving VLA control.

FailSafe positions itself within this landscape by addressing the gap between automated failure generation and directly executable recovery actions. It aims to provide a scalable, automated pipeline that not only identifies failures and reasons about them but also generates concrete, low-level corrective actions that VLA models can immediately apply. This approach seeks to enhance the autonomy and robustness of VLA models by equipping them with the capability for real-time, executable failure recovery, moving beyond human intervention or purely textual feedback.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop FailSafe, an automatic failure generation and recovery system that can produce diverse failure cases along with corresponding executable recovery actions. This system is designed to be adaptable across various manipulation tasks, simulators with motion planning support, and robotic embodiments, thereby enabling the scalable creation of comprehensive failure–action data.

The motivation for developing FailSafe stems from several critical challenges and limitations in current VLA models and robotic manipulation systems:

1. **Inevitable Failures in Robotic Execution:** Despite advancements, VLA models, even those trained on extensive "clean" datasets, are prone to making mistakes and encountering unpredictable situations in real-world environments. Such failures are inherent to complex robotic tasks, making the ability to recover from them essential for robust deployment.
2. **Deficiency of Existing Datasets:** Current robotic manipulation datasets predominantly consist of successful, ground-truth trajectories. This reliance on correct data means that robots are not exposed to diverse failure scenarios during training, nor are they provided with information on how to recover once a failure occurs. This gap in training data hinders the development of resilient VLA models.
3. **Limitations of Current Failure Reasoning Approaches:**
 - **Dependence on Human Supervision:** Early failure reflection methods often required continuous human monitoring and intervention, which is impractical for autonomous systems operating in real-world scenarios. This reliance limits scalability and real-time applicability.
 - **Non-Executable Feedback:** More advanced pipelines for automated failure generation provide textual explanations of failures. While useful for analysis, these natural language instructions lack the precision and executability required for direct integration into VLA models for low-level robot control. Robots cannot directly interpret and act upon qualitative instructions like "move left" without precise magnitude and direction.
4. **Need for Scalable and Executable Recovery:** To truly enable autonomous and robust robotic systems, there is a requirement for a pipeline that can automatically generate both high-level failure reasoning and corresponding low-level, executable corrective actions at scale. Such a system would allow VLA models to directly learn how to respond to and correct failures in real-time.
5. **Enhancing VLA Robustness and Explainability:** By providing VLA models with explicit training on failure detection and recovery, FailSafe aims to improve the overall success rate of robotic tasks. Furthermore, the explicit identification of failure types and the generation of targeted recovery actions can contribute to the explainability of robot behavior, allowing for better understanding and debugging of system errors.

In summary, FailSafe seeks to fill a critical void by systematically generating actionable failure-recovery data, thereby equipping VLA models with the capability to autonomously reason about and recover from errors, which is a necessary step towards more reliable and intelligent robotic agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FailSafe methodology involves a three-phase pipeline—Failure Generation, Action Collection, and Systematic Verification—to produce a dataset of failure-action pairs. This dataset is then used to fine-tune a Vision-Language Model (VLM) into FailSafe-VLM, an expert model for failure reasoning and recovery. The pipeline is designed to be adaptable to simulators that support motion planning, exemplified by its application in ManiSkill.

#### 3.1. A. Failure Generation

To simulate common robotic manipulation failures, FailSafe defines three primary failure modes:
- **Translation Failure:** Positional deviations along the Cartesian x, y, or z axes.
- **Rotation Failure:** Angular deviations in roll, pitch, or yaw.
- **No-ops Failure:** The robotic arm becoming stationary or stuck for a specified duration without intended movement.

The failure generation process leverages the motion planning capabilities common in simulators like ManiSkill, where tasks are typically decomposed into sequential stages with pre-defined poses.
1. **Configuration:** A YAML file specifies the types of failure modes, the ranges of perturbations (e.g., ±0.1 units for translation, ±1 radian for rotation), and the specific stages within a task where failures can be injected.
2. **Failure Injection:** FailSafe uses a customized environment wrapper to randomly perturb the pre-defined pose at a chosen "deviated stage" of a ground-truth rollout. This transforms the intended trajectory (A → B → C → D) into a failure trajectory (A → B' → C → D), where B' represents the perturbed pose. This perturbation is designed to intentionally cause the original task to fail.
3. **Data Recording:** If the introduced perturbation leads to a task failure, FailSafe automatically records pertinent information, including multi-view image observations (front, side, hand cameras), the full failure trajectory, and the identified failure type.
4. **Consideration of Delayed and Multi-dimensional Failures:** The framework accounts for "delayed-failure" cases, where the task fails several steps after the initial root error. Additionally, while a specific failure type initiates the perturbation, the subsequent corrective actions (∆A) are designed to be 7-DoF, addressing potential multi-dimensional errors that may arise or become apparent after a single perturbation. This design aims for comprehensive error correction.

#### 3.2. B. Action Collection

Unlike previous methods that provide textual explanations, FailSafe focuses on collecting executable 7-DoF recovery actions (∆A), representing the difference in pose between a corrective state and a deviated state.
1. **Pose Mapping:** For each pair of a correct trajectory and a failure trajectory of a specific stage, FailSafe identifies a "deviated pose" (P_d) from the failure trajectory and maps it to a "corrective pose" (P_c) from the corresponding correct trajectory.
2. **Selection of Deviated Pose (P_d):** P_d is sequentially selected from the 10th step of the failure trajectory up to its final step. This range is chosen because failures are often difficult to detect in the very early steps.
3. **Selection of Corrective Pose (P_c):** P_c is selected from the correct trajectory, typically within a window from 10 steps after its start to 3 steps before its end. This window avoids early-stage instability and end-stage task completion. This mapping is also constrained to prevent potential collisions between the gripper and the object, ensuring the P_c is a safe and viable target. For no-ops failures, P_c is randomly sampled between 3 to 10 steps after P_d along the correct trajectory.
4. **Delta Action Calculation:** The corrective action ∆A is calculated as the 7-DoF difference (translation and rotation components) between P_c and P_d.
5. **Diversity of Actions:** By sequentially traversing candidate P_d poses and randomly matching them with P_c poses, the pipeline generates multiple candidate ∆A actions for a given failure trajectory, contributing to the diversity and robustness of the collected recovery data.

#### 3.3. C. Systematic Verification

To ensure the reliability and effectiveness of the collected recovery actions, FailSafe incorporates a rigorous systematic verification step.
1. **Trajectory Replay:** For each candidate (P_d, P_c) pair and its corresponding ∆A, FailSafe replays the task trajectory. The robotic arm first moves to the deviated pose P_d, then to the corrective pose P_c, and subsequently proceeds with the remaining poses of the original correct task (A → P_d → P_c → B → C → D).
2. **Validation Criterion:** If the task, which originally failed due to the perturbation, now succeeds after applying the corrective pose P_c, the entire failure-action pair (including image observations, failure type, and ∆A) is included in the final FailSafe dataset. This verification mechanism ensures that only effective and validated recovery actions are retained.

#### 3.4. D. FailSafe Dataset

The FailSafe pipeline was applied to three tasks in ManiSkill (pick cube, push cube, stack cube) to generate the dataset.
- **Data Scale:** The dataset comprises 131,000 failure-action pairs across various failure types and task stages, alongside approximately 56,000 ground-truth (successful) trajectories. This results in a failure-to-success ratio of 2.3:1, providing substantial data for learning failure reasoning.
- **Data Format:** Each entry in the dataset includes:
 - A question template prompting the model to identify potential failures given image observations and task instructions.
 - An answer template specifying the current sub-task, whether a failure is likely, and if so, the identified failure type and the 7-DoF executable recovery action (∆A).
- **Visual Data:** Each entry contains 10 consecutive image observations from three standard camera perspectives (front, side, hand). For evaluation, a novel camera view, aligned with VLA training angles, is introduced to test generalization.

#### 3.5. E. Instruction Fine-tuning (FailSafe-VLM)

The collected FailSafe dataset is used to fine-tune LLaVA-OneVision-7B (LLaVa-OV-7B), a VLM, to create FailSafe-VLM.
- **Base Model:** LLaVA-OV-7B, initialized from a single-image checkpoint, using Qwen2-7B-Instruct as the language backbone and SigLIP as the vision tower.
- **Architecture:** A two-layer GELU MLP projector (2× hidden expansion) integrates features from the vision encoder’s penultimate layer.
- **Training:** Full instruction fine-tuning was performed for one epoch on 32 H100 GPUs with DeepSpeed ZeRO 3. The training regimen also included co-training with a RoboPoint VQA mixture to enhance generalization capabilities.
- **Parameters:** The vision tower, MLP adapter, and language model were jointly fine-tuned with specific learning rates (1e-5 base, 2e-6 for vision tower), cosine decay with 3% warmup, zero weight decay, and bfloat16/TF32 enabled.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated FailSafe-VLM's capabilities in failure reasoning and recovery across various scenarios, including its integration with existing VLA models, generalization to novel objects and embodiments, comparison with other VLMs, and inference efficiency.

#### 4.1. A. Performance on VLA Models

FailSafe-VLM was integrated as an external assistant to three state-of-the-art VLA models: πo-FAST, OpenVLA, and OpenVLA-OFT. During execution, FailSafe-VLM intervened every 10 steps to detect potential failures and output corrective actions if a failure was identified, returning control to the base VLA model afterward.
- **Experimental Setup:** Evaluation was conducted on three ManiSkill tasks (pick cube, push cube, stack cube) using the Franka Emika Panda arm. Test seeds involved novel spatial configurations. The camera view used during experiments matched the VLA training view but was novel to FailSafe-VLM, simulating real-world conditions where a dedicated camera might not be available.
- **Results (Table II):** All three VLA models demonstrated improved average success rates when integrated with FailSafe-VLM.
 - **OpenVLA:** Showed the most significant improvement, with an average gain of 22.6% across the tasks (e.g., from 14.7% to 37.3%).
 - **OpenVLA-OFT:** Achieved an average increase of 8.0% (e.g., from 90.7% to 98.7%).
 - **πo-FAST:** Improved by an average of 4.0% (e.g., from 78.7% to 82.7%).
- **Observation:** FailSafe-VLM provided effective "nudges" when the robotic arm became stuck or was about to fail, demonstrating its role as an assistant for failure reasoning and recovery.

#### 4.2. B. Generalization Capability

To assess generalization, FailSafe-VLM was tested with novel objects and a different robotic embodiment.
- **Novel Object Categories (Table III):** Sphere and Charger, objects unseen during FailSafe-VLM training, were used in manipulation tasks (pick sphere, place sphere, pick charger). FailSafe-VLM assisted OpenVLA-OFT, achieving an average improvement of 17.4% across these tasks (e.g., from 53.3% to 70.7%). This generalization was attributed to the consistent patterns of fundamental failure modes across diverse objects.
- **Novel Robotic Embodiment (Table IV):** FailSafe-VLM, trained exclusively on Franka Emika Panda data, was evaluated with an xArm 6. It boosted the performance of the stack cube task on xArm 6 from 56% to 76% (a 20% improvement) without negatively impacting other tasks. This indicated cross-embodiment generalization, suggesting that the generated failure scenarios and corrective actions are largely independent of specific robot kinematics.

#### 4.3. C. Comparisons on Other VLMs

FailSafe-VLM's failure reasoning capabilities were compared against other state-of-the-art VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL, Gemini-2.5-flash, GPT-4o) on 1,712 unseen test entries generated from novel spatial configurations.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Binary success (distinguishing failure from success), Accuracy (correctly identifying specific failure types and axes), and Cosine Similarity (between ground-truth and predicted recovery actions).
- **Results (Table V):** FailSafe-VLM consistently outperformed the other VLMs across all metrics.
 - **Binary Success:** FailSafe-VLM achieved 0.9094, significantly higher than 0.2401-0.7007 for other models.
 - **Accuracy (Failure Type):** FailSafe-VLM recorded 0.8368, which was over four times higher than Gemini-2.5-flash (0.1412) and GPT-4o (0.1960).
 - **Cosine Similarity (Recovery Action):** FailSafe-VLM achieved 0.6522, whereas other models had scores close to zero (-0.0121 to 0.0117).
- **Conclusion:** This comparison indicated that the FailSafe pipeline effectively imbues VLMs with robust failure reasoning, detection, and executable recovery action generation capabilities that are not inherent in general-purpose VLMs. It was noted that a cosine similarity of approximately 65% was sufficient to yield meaningful performance improvements in VLA tasks.

#### 4.4. D. Inference Efficiency

The integration of FailSafe-VLM with VLA models incurred marginal inference overheads (Table VI).
- **Overhead:** Performance improvements of up to 22.6% were achieved with additional delays ranging from +3.8s to +9.1s.
- **Source of Delay:** The primary source of this additional delay was identified as simulator replanning after receiving corrective actions from FailSafe-VLM.

#### 4.5. E. Qualitative Analysis

A qualitative analysis (Figure 4) illustrated FailSafe-VLM's effect on end-effector trajectories.
- **Example:** In a "Pick up the red cube" task, an OpenVLA model initially caused the robotic arm to remain largely stationary, indicating a "no-ops" failure.
- **FailSafe-VLM Intervention:** FailSafe-VLM detected this potential failure and generated corrective actions, which are highlighted as green segments in the trajectory plots. These actions effectively nudged the arm closer to the ground-truth trajectory in both x and z axes.
- **Task Completion:** After the correction, OpenVLA regained control and successfully completed the task, which would have otherwise failed without FailSafe-VLM's assistance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces FailSafe, a novel pipeline for automatically generating failure reasoning data paired with executable recovery actions, representing a contribution to robust robotic manipulation.

**Key Contributions:**
- **Scalable and Executable Failure Recovery:** FailSafe is presented as the first scalable framework capable of building upon simulators with motion planning to generate both textual failure explanations and directly executable, 7-DoF recovery actions for robots. This directly addresses a gap in existing literature, where failure reasoning often lacked actionable control signals.
- **Enhanced VLA Performance:** The FailSafe-VLM model, fine-tuned on the generated dataset, demonstrated the ability to significantly improve the performance of three state-of-the-art VLA models (πo-FAST, OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT) by up to 22.6% on average across various tasks in ManiSkill. This indicates a practical and quantifiable benefit to existing robotic control systems.
- **Robust Generalization:** The work showcases that FailSafe-VLM generalizes across diverse spatial configurations, camera viewpoints, novel object categories (e.g., sphere, charger), and different robotic embodiments (e.g., xArm 6, despite training only on Franka Emika Panda data). This generalization suggests that the underlying principles of failure detection and recovery learned by FailSafe-VLM are abstract enough to be applied in varied, unseen environments, which is crucial for real-world deployment.
- **Superior Failure Reasoning:** Comparisons with other state-of-the-art VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL, Gemini-2.5-flash, GPT-4o) established that FailSafe-VLM achieves a significantly higher accuracy in detecting failures, identifying specific failure types, and predicting accurate recovery actions, validating the effectiveness of the FailSafe pipeline's data generation strategy.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Increased Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** By enabling robots to autonomously detect and recover from unpredictable failures, FailSafe contributes to the development of more robust and reliable robotic systems. This capability is essential for deploying robots in complex, unstructured environments where human supervision is not always feasible.
- **Improved Explainable AI in Robotics:** The generation of explicit failure types and recovery actions can enhance the explainability of robot behavior. Understanding why a robot failed and how it attempted to recover can facilitate debugging, build trust, and improve human-robot collaboration.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** FailSafe provides a scalable method for creating valuable datasets for failure reasoning, which can serve as a foundation for future research in areas such as learning more nuanced failure modes, developing more efficient recovery strategies, and integrating failure recovery into end-to-end VLA model training.
- **Bridging Simulation-to-Reality Gap:** While currently demonstrated in simulation, the framework's focus on low-level executable actions and its demonstrated generalization capabilities hold potential for bridging the simulation-to-reality gap, as precise, verified actions are more transferable than ambiguous textual instructions.
- **Community Resource:** The plan to release the FailSafe code to the community indicates a commitment to fostering further research and development in failure reasoning and recovery in robotics.

The limitations, such as the current focus on motion-level recovery over object-level errors and potential improvements in efficiency through techniques like real-time action chunking, suggest avenues for future work. Nonetheless, FailSafe represents a step toward stronger failure reasoning and autonomy in robot control systems.
