# DyPES-VLA: Learning Shared Dynamics Priors and Embodiment-Specific Control for Cross-Embodiment Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> DyPES-VLA introduces a framework for learning a single generalist policy capable of controlling various robot embodiments by leveraging shared dynamics priors from visual data and embodiment-specific action generation. This approach enabled state-of-the-art or comparable performance across multiple simulation benchmarks and achieved 75.6% average success on real-world tasks, outperforming independent and other generalist policies.

## Problem

- VLA policies typically lack generalization capabilities across heterogeneous robot embodiments, requiring substantial re-training or fine-tuning for each new platform.
- Existing cross-embodiment VLA methods often underutilize shared latent dynamics priors, primarily learning from action labels and neglecting patterns of object motion, contact, and scene evolution in unlabelled visual data.
- Manual action space alignment, such as coordinate transformations or inverse kinematics, is resource-intensive, scales poorly with diverse robot morphologies, and can conflate universal interaction regularities with embodiment-specific controls.

## Method

- **DyPES-VLA** utilizes a ==shared query interface==, a future generation head, and an embodiment-specific ==Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head==, processing multimodal inputs with a pretrained Vision-Language Model (VLM).
- It learns ==shared dynamics priors== by supervising query states with a generative future-prediction objective on both action-free videos and action-labeled robot demonstrations.
- Embodiment-specific control is achieved by translating shared predictive query representations into executable commands directly in the robot's native action space, using an MoE Diffusion Transformer, thus avoiding manual action space alignment.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **89.02%** on RoboTwin 2.0, **59.25%** on RoboCasa-GR1, and **98.0%** on LIBERO, performing at or above specialist and other generalist models with a single co-trained checkpoint in simulation.
- Demonstrated an average success rate of **75.6%** across nine real-world tasks on three physical robots (Franka, AgileX, Unitree G1), significantly outperforming independently trained ACT policies (**32.4%**) and a GR00T-N1.6 generalist checkpoint (**59.6%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the future-prediction objective provided the largest performance boost, and the embodiment-specific MoE action head was crucial, with linear probes showing enhanced decoding of future contact sequences from query states with future supervision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly learning shared dynamics priors from diverse visual data (including action-free videos) significantly enriches the shared representation, enhancing cross-embodiment transfer and policy robustness.
> - Decoupling shared knowledge (dynamics priors) from embodiment-specific control allows a single generalist policy to effectively manage heterogeneous robot morphologies and native action spaces.
> - A modular action head with shared attention layers and statically routed, embodiment-specific experts provides an efficient way to handle distinct kinematic constraints and control semantics within a unified architecture.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which leverage large pre-trained vision-language models for robust task performance. However, a prevalent challenge in this domain is the limited generalization capabilities of most VLA policies across heterogeneous robot embodiments. Typically, policies are tied to a specific robot and require substantial re-training or fine-tuning for new platforms.

Recent research efforts have begun to address this by training VLAs on diverse, cross-embodiment datasets, aiming to develop more generalist robot manipulation capabilities. The central dilemma in these approaches lies in determining which knowledge should be universally shared across different robot bodies and which aspects must remain specific to a particular embodiment.

Existing cross-embodiment VLA methods typically encounter two primary limitations. Firstly, they often underutilize the latent dynamics priors that are shared across various visual and interaction data. Current models predominantly learn shared information solely from action labels through action prediction, neglecting the rich, recurring patterns of object motion, contact, and scene evolution present in large collections of human and robot manipulation videos, which do not necessarily require explicit action labels. Secondly, many approaches attempt to standardize information at the action level by converting heterogeneous robot controls into a common format. This preprocessing, which can involve complex coordinate transformations or inverse kinematics, scales poorly with the increasing diversity of robot morphologies. More fundamentally, this unification can conflate the interaction regularities that are common across embodiments with the unique control semantics and kinematic constraints of individual robots.

DyPES-VLA is introduced to address these limitations by proposing a novel paradigm for cross-embodiment learning. It aims to develop a generalist policy that can effectively handle diverse robot embodiments by explicitly learning shared dynamics priors and managing embodiment-specific control without requiring manual alignment of heterogeneous action spaces. This work positions itself within the architectural specialization trend of cross-embodiment VLAs but distinguishes itself by moving the shared interface upstream to future-supervised query states and employing embodiment-specific experts for direct native action space decoding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a single, generalist VLA policy capable of performing manipulation tasks across a wide range of heterogeneous robot embodiments. This objective is driven by the desire to overcome two specific limitations identified in current cross-embodiment VLA approaches:

1. **Underutilization of Shared Dynamics Priors:** Existing VLA models primarily derive shared knowledge from action labels through direct action prediction. This approach overlooks a substantial amount of information embedded in visual data that describes how objects move, interact, and cause scene changes (e.g., contact events, object displacements). This "dynamics prior" represents fundamental physical regularities that are independent of the specific robot executing an action. The motivation is to leverage this rich, unexploited information from large collections of both action-labeled robot demonstrations and action-free human/robot videos. The goal is to explicitly learn these shared dynamics priors to enhance cross-embodiment transfer capabilities and policy robustness.

2. **Complexity and Limitations of Action Space Alignment:** Many prior works address action heterogeneity by mapping robot-specific actions into a common, unified action format. This typically involves extensive manual preprocessing, such as coordinate transformations or inverse kinematics, to convert diverse joint or end-effector controls into a standardized representation. This process is resource-intensive, does not scale efficiently to new or highly varied robot morphologies, and can inadvertently merge the universally applicable principles of interaction with the unique control requirements of each robot. The motivation is to eliminate the need for such manual action space alignment. Instead, the objective is to allow robots to operate directly within their native action spaces while still benefiting from shared high-level understanding. This approach seeks to disentangle the learning of general manipulation principles from the specifics of robot actuation.

In pursuit of these objectives, the authors propose a paradigm that explicitly learns shared dynamics priors and integrates them with embodiment-specific control mechanisms. This paradigm aims to enable joint training on data from arbitrary embodiments without requiring a common action format, fostering true generalist robot manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces DyPES-VLA, a cross-embodiment learning paradigm designed to learn shared dynamics priors and embodiment-specific control. DyPES-VLA comprises three main components and employs a two-stage training process.

#### 3.1. Method Overview

DyPES-VLA utilizes a pretrained Vision-Language Model (VLM), a future generation head, and an embodiment-specific Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head. The VLM processes multimodal inputs to create a shared representation, which then feeds into both the future generation head for learning dynamics priors and the MoE action head for generating embodiment-specific controls.

#### 3.2. Shared Query Interface

A set of learnable query tokens ($Q$) acts as a shared interface between multimodal understanding and downstream generation. These tokens are appended to a sequence consisting of tokenized visual observations ($o_t$), language instructions ($\ell$), and verbalized embodiment metadata ($m_e$). A pretrained VLM ($f_\theta$) processes this sequence, yielding "query states" ($Z$), which are the last-layer hidden states of the query tokens. These query states are designed to capture shared information across embodiments. The policy does not utilize proprioceptive input.

#### 3.3. Learning Dynamics Priors

The future generation head supervises the query states ($Z$) using a generative future-prediction objective. This objective is shared across all embodiments and data sources. The head, instantiated as a SANA image generator, is trained to synthesize a future frame ($x_{t+\Delta_e}$) from the primary camera view, given the current context at time $t$. The temporal offset ($\Delta_e$) is set to match the action horizon ($H_e$) of each embodiment.

A frozen autoencoder ($AE_w$) encodes the target future frame into a latent representation. The query states ($Z$) are projected into the conditioning space of the SANA transformer and serve as its conditioning tokens via cross-attention. Crucially, the generator receives no direct information about the current observation or its latent representation; all necessary information for future synthesis must be channeled through the $N$ query states. The head is optimized using a rectified-flow objective, which encourages the query states to encode information related to object motion, contact, and interaction-induced scene changes—referred to as shared dynamics priors.

#### 3.4. Learning Embodiment-Specific Control

The action head translates the shared predictive query representation ($Z$) into executable control commands for each robot. It is implemented as a flow-matching Diffusion Transformer (DiT). This head factors action generation into two parts:
- **Shared Attention Layers:** These layers perform temporal computation and are shared across all embodiments.
- **Statically Routed Experts:** The feed-forward network (FFN) within each transformer block consists of a bank of $K$ experts. A static router, based on the embodiment metadata ($m_e$), deterministically selects the appropriate expert ($r(e)$) for the given embodiment.

Each embodiment also has a lightweight, dedicated encoder-decoder pair. The encoder ($Enc_{r(e)}$) embeds the noisy action chunk along with the flow timestep, adapting the native action space to the shared DiT width. The decoder ($Dec_{r(e)}$) maps the DiT outputs back to a velocity over the native action chunk ($A_e$). The action head is trained with the same rectified-flow formulation as the future generation head, predicting the velocity towards the true action chunk in the embodiment's native action space.

#### 3.5. Two-Stage Training and Inference

1. **Stage 1: Dynamics Priors Pretraining on Action-Free Videos:** In this initial stage, the VLM, the learnable query tokens, and the future generation head are pretrained. This pretraining exclusively uses the future-prediction objective (Eq. 2) on large-scale action-free videos, including egocentric human manipulation videos (EgoDex) and simulation videos from the target embodiments. This stage aims to instill shared dynamics priors into the query states.

2. **Stage 2: Cross-Embodiment Co-Training on Action-Labeled Demonstrations:** Following pretraining, the second stage jointly optimizes both the future generation head and the action head. This stage uses action-labeled robot demonstrations from multiple embodiments. The future-prediction objective continues to regularize the shared query representation, while the action objective trains the embodiment-specific MoE action head to generate actions directly in the native action space of each robot.

**Inference:** During deployment, a single forward pass through the VLM produces the query states ($Z$). Conditioned on $Z$, the action head generates an action chunk for the corresponding embodiment by integrating the learned flow from Gaussian noise using a few Euler steps. The future generation head is not used during inference.

**Implementation Details:** The VLM used is Qwen3-VL-2B, and SANA-600M is the future generation head, initialized with pretrained weights. The MoE action head is a 16-layer Diffusion Transformer with $K=3$ experts, trained from scratch. N=96 shared query tokens are used. Observations consist of 256x256 pixel images from various camera views (e.g., two for single-arm, one for humanoid, three for dual-arm robots), with no proprioceptive state. Action horizons vary by embodiment. Training typically involves 100,000 steps for Stage 1 and 200,000 steps for Stage 2 on 16 H100 GPUs, with a future loss weight ($\lambda_w$) of 0.05.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DyPES-VLA was evaluated across simulation benchmarks and real-world robot platforms to assess its ability to operate as a single-checkpoint generalist policy for cross-embodiment manipulation.

#### 4.1. Comparisons with State-of-the-Art Methods (Q1)

The model was tested with a single co-trained checkpoint across three distinct simulation benchmarks: RoboTwin 2.0 (14-DoF dual-arm), RoboCasa-GR1 (29-DoF humanoid), and LIBERO (7-DoF single-arm).

- **RoboTwin 2.0:** DyPES-VLA achieved an average success rate of 89.02%, outperforming the concurrent generalist Qwen-VLA by 2.37 points.
- **RoboCasa-GR1:** The model attained a 59.25% success rate, exceeding the best per-benchmark specialist ABot-M0 (58.3%) by 0.95 points, the WAM baseline LDA-1B (55.4%) by 3.85 points, and Qwen-VLA (56.7%) by 2.55 points.
- **LIBERO:** DyPES-VLA reached 98.0% success, surpassing WAM baseline Fast-WAM (97.6%) by 0.4 points and OpenVLA-OFT (97.1%) by 0.9 points, while demonstrating comparable performance to the best finetuned X-VLA.

These results indicate that DyPES-VLA, using a single checkpoint, performs at or above the level of specialized and other generalist models across diverse robot embodiments and action spaces in simulation.

#### 4.2. Real-World Deployment (Q1)

The cross-embodiment checkpoint, initially trained on simulation data, was jointly finetuned on 1,800 real-world demonstrations (200 per task/embodiment) spanning three tasks across three physical robots: a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 (FR3) single-arm robot, a 14-DoF AgileX Robotics COBOT Magic dual-arm robot, and a Unitree G1 humanoid. The tasks included "pick up kiwi and place it in basket," "pour water," and "place book onto shelf."

- DyPES-VLA achieved an average success rate of 75.6% across all nine real-world tasks (three tasks on three robots).
- This performance notably exceeded that of independently trained ACT policies (32.4% average) and a jointly finetuned GR00T-N1.6 generalist checkpoint (59.6% average), demonstrating stronger real-world cross-embodiment control.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study (Q2, Q3, Q4)

Ablation experiments were conducted to assess the contribution of the core design choices: the future-prediction objective, the embodiment-specific MoE action head, and embodiment metadata. Each ablated variant was evaluated with a single co-trained checkpoint.

- **Future Prediction Objective (Q2):** Removing the future-prediction objective resulted in the largest performance drop, 2.4 points on RoboTwin 2.0 and 2.5 points on RoboCasa-GR1. Removing only the Stage-1 pretraining also degraded performance across all three benchmarks. This supports the hypothesis that future supervision enriches the shared representation with dynamics priors.
- **Embodiment-Specific MoE Action Head (Q3):** Replacing the MoE action head with a shared dense head led to a performance decrease of 1.2 points on RoboTwin 2.0 and 2.1 points on RoboCasa-GR1. This indicates that embodiment-specific action realization is beneficial for managing heterogeneous control spaces during joint cross-embodiment training.
- **Embodiment Metadata (Q4):** Removing the verbalized embodiment metadata caused a consistent but smaller drop of up to 0.5 points, suggesting its role in disambiguating embodiment and data source.

#### 4.4. Future-Contact Probe (Q2)

To further evaluate the impact of future supervision on the learned dynamics priors, a linear probe was used to decode future contact sequences directly from the frozen query states ($Z$). This probe assessed the model's ability to anticipate contact onset and release within a given horizon.

- Compared to the variant without future prediction, the full DyPES-VLA model showed markedly improved decoding of both contact onset and release, as indicated by higher AUROC (e.g., 97.3% vs. 95.2% for onset) and AUPRC (e.g., 86.3% vs. 70.8% for onset), and lower class-weighted Binary Cross-Entropy (0.142 vs. 0.227). This finding supports that future supervision effectively enriches the shared query representation with dynamics priors, making information about interaction-induced state changes linearly decodable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DyPES-VLA framework represents a notable advancement in the development of generalist robot manipulation policies. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

1. **Enabling Generalist Policies Across Diverse Embodiments:** The work demonstrates that a single policy can effectively control heterogeneous robot morphologies with distinct action spaces, a longstanding challenge in robotics. This moves closer to the vision of truly generalist robots capable of adapting to various physical forms.

2. **Efficient Learning of Shared Dynamics Priors:** By utilizing a future-prediction objective on both action-free videos and action-labeled demonstrations, DyPES-VLA effectively learns shared dynamics priors (object motion, contact, scene changes). This approach leverages large-scale, readily available visual data more comprehensively than methods relying solely on action prediction, potentially improving data efficiency and transferability.

3. **Elimination of Manual Action Space Alignment:** The paradigm's ability to generate actions directly in each embodiment’s native action space, without extensive manual preprocessing or conversion to a common format, is a practical advantage. This simplifies the integration of new robots and scales better across diverse hardware.

4. **Architectural Robustness:** The embodiment-specific Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head, with its shared attention layers and routed experts, offers a modular and effective way to manage the complexities of distinct kinematic constraints and control semantics while maintaining a shared core understanding.

5. **Strong Performance and Real-World Applicability:** Achieving state-of-the-art or comparable performance across multiple challenging simulation benchmarks with a single checkpoint, coupled with demonstrated superior performance in real-world deployments on physical robots, underscores the practical efficacy and transferability of the learned policy. The real-world results, in particular, suggest that the dynamics priors learned through cross-embodiment co-training provide a robust foundation for physical control.

6. **Extensibility:** The paper suggests that the proposed paradigm can be extended to arbitrary embodiments and heterogeneous data, implying a versatile framework for future research in generalist robotics and a potential pathway for scaling embodied AI.

Overall, DyPES-VLA contributes to overcoming significant barriers in developing scalable and adaptable robot learning systems, offering a promising direction for creating more versatile and autonomous robotic agents.
