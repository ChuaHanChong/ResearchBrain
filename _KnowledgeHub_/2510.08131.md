# Real-Time Motion-Controllable Autoregressive Video Diffusion

> [!summary] Summary
> AR-Drag is an autoregressive video diffusion model that achieves real-time, motion-controllable image-to-video generation by integrating reinforcement learning. It demonstrates superior visual quality (FID 28.98, FVD 187.49) and precise motion control (Motion Consistency 4.37) with significantly reduced latency (0.44 seconds per first frame) compared to current bidirectional and autoregressive baselines, even with a compact 1.3 billion parameter model.

## Problem

- High latency in existing motion-controllable video diffusion models, predominantly bidirectional, prevents real-time interactive control.
- Autoregressive video diffusion models often suffer from quality degradation, motion artifacts due to error accumulation, and limited control signals for Image-to-Video (I2V) tasks.
- Integrating reinforcement learning (RL) into AR video generation is challenged by the need to preserve the Markov property, manage computational expense over long decision horizons, and lack of suitable reward models.

## Method

- A base I2V model is fine-tuned for ==motion control==, then distilled into a few-step autoregressive student model using causal attention and distribution matching distillation.
- The Self-Rollout training strategy maintains a key-value memory cache of fully denoised past frames as causal context, ensuring the Markov property essential for stable RL training.
- Reinforcement Learning (GRPO framework) is applied to enhance the AR VDM, utilizing a composite reward function (perceptual quality and motion alignment) and selective stochastic sampling for efficient exploration over long Markov chains.

## Results

- AR-Drag achieved a latency of **0.44** seconds per first frame, significantly outperforming bidirectional models (e.g., Tora at **176.51s**, MagicMotion at **1426.37s**) and an AR baseline (**0.95s**).
- The model demonstrated superior visual quality (FID 28.98, FVD 187.49) and motion control (Motion Smoothness **0.9948**, Motion Consistency 4.37) compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the Reinforcement Learning post-training and the Self-Rollout mechanism are essential for enhancing visual quality, motion controllability, and mitigating severe artifacts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High visual fidelity and precise motion control are attainable with autoregressive video diffusion models when robustly distilled and enhanced with reinforcement learning.
> - The Self-Rollout mechanism is crucial for resolving exposure bias and maintaining the Markov property in autoregressive diffusion model training, enabling stable and effective RL integration.
> - Reinforcement learning, particularly with a tailored composite reward and selective stochastic sampling, can effectively improve generalization and control alignment in generative video models with long sequential decision processes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Video diffusion models (VDMs) have demonstrated advancements in video generation, particularly through bidirectional diffusion transformers (DiTs) that process all frames concurrently. While these bidirectional models contribute to visual quality, their design necessitates the generation of an entire video before output, resulting in high latency. This characteristic limits their applicability in real-time interactive scenarios where control inputs might evolve dynamically. Most existing motion-controllable VDMs adopt this bidirectional architecture.

In contrast, autoregressive (AR) VDMs generate videos sequentially, which inherently aligns with the requirements of real-time controllable video generation. However, previous AR VDM approaches primarily focus on text-to-video (T2V) generation and exhibit limitations in more complex image-to-video (I2V) tasks. These limitations include reduced quality, susceptibility to motion artifacts due to error accumulation, especially in few-step generation, and a restricted range of control signals (e.g., simple pose or camera movements). The research landscape currently lacks an AR VDM capable of real-time, fine-grained motion control for I2V generation while maintaining visual quality comparable to bidirectional counterparts.

Furthermore, integrating reinforcement learning (RL) into diffusion models for improved alignment and generalization is an emerging area. While RL has shown effectiveness in text-to-image (T2I) generation and certain bidirectional flow-matching models, its application to video generation, particularly in an autoregressive context, faces specific challenges. These include ensuring the Markov property for the sequential generation process, managing the computational expense of exploration over long decision horizons, and developing appropriate reward models for controllable video generation. This work positions itself by addressing these gaps, aiming to develop an AR VDM that overcomes latency issues and enhances motion controllability through an RL-enhanced framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an autoregressive (AR) video diffusion model, named AR-Drag, that enables real-time motion-controllable image-to-video (I2V) generation. This objective is driven by several motivations and aims to address specific challenges identified in the current landscape of video generation:

1. **Reduce Latency for Real-Time Interaction:** Existing motion-controllable video diffusion models are predominantly based on bidirectional architectures. These models process all video frames simultaneously, leading to high latency because generation is delayed until all control inputs for the entire video are specified. This design prevents real-time adjustment of control signals as the video unfolds. The motivation is to shift towards an autoregressive design that generates frames sequentially, inherently supporting real-time interaction and on-the-fly control adjustments.

2. **Enhance Quality and Mitigate Artifacts in AR Video Generation:** While AR VDMs are suitable for real-time control, current models often suffer from quality degradation and motion artifacts, particularly when attempting few-step generation. This issue is exacerbated by error accumulation across sequentially generated frames. A key objective is to develop an AR VDM that achieves high visual fidelity and temporal coherence, competitive with or exceeding the quality of state-of-the-art bidirectional models, even with few denoising steps.

3. **Support Diverse and Fine-Grained Motion Control for I2V:** Existing AR VDMs are primarily designed for text-to-video (T2V) tasks or limited to simple control signals like pose or camera motion in I2V scenarios. The motivation is to extend AR video generation capabilities to support richer and more diverse motion control modalities, such as object trajectories or bounding boxes, for the more challenging I2V setting. This broader action space requires stronger generalization from the model.

4. **Integrate Reinforcement Learning (RL) to Improve Generalization and Control:** Supervised learning, commonly used in VDM training, enforces pixel-level reconstruction and can limit a model to the training data distribution. The researchers hypothesize that RL can enable policies that generalize beyond seen data by exploring the action space and optimizing generation through trial-and-error. The objective is to effectively integrate RL into an AR VDM framework to enhance control alignment and visual quality. This integration faces specific challenges that need to be addressed:
 - **Preserving the Markov Property:** Traditional AR VDM training often conditions on ground-truth frames, which breaks the Markov property necessary for RL, as inference relies on self-generated frames. The objective is to design a mechanism that aligns training with AR inference to maintain a valid Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation.
 - **Handling Long Decision Processes:** Video generation involves a long sequence of decisions (denoising steps across multiple frames), making full exploration computationally prohibitive in RL. The objective is to devise a strategy that makes long-horizon exploration tractable and computationally efficient.
 - **Developing Tailored Reward Models:** There is a lack of well-defined reward models specifically designed for controllable video generation that can effectively enforce fine-grained control over complex motion signals and evaluate perceptual quality. The objective is to design a composite reward function that addresses both visual fidelity and motion alignment.

In summary, the motivation behind AR-Drag is to overcome the fundamental trade-off between real-time controllability and high visual quality in video generation, particularly for complex motion-controlled I2V tasks, by leveraging an RL-enhanced autoregressive diffusion framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The AR-Drag model is developed through a two-step process: first, fine-tuning a base Image-to-Video (I2V) model to support basic motion control, and second, enhancing this model using reinforcement learning (RL) with a trajectory-based reward model.

#### 3.1. Step 1: Fine-tuning a Real-Time Motion-Controllable Base VDM

This initial phase establishes a foundational autoregressive VDM with real-time capabilities and elementary motion control.

- **Data Curation:** A training corpus is assembled using both real and synthetic videos that exhibit diverse motions. Control signals are generated using an automatic detector for keypoint trajectories, followed by human verification to ensure quality and accuracy. A high-quality subset is additionally curated with human annotations for challenging cases. Each video in the corpus is accompanied by detailed positive and negative textual descriptions. This dataset is designed to support generalization across various scenarios, object types (humans, animals, cartoons), resolutions, and durations. Control signals at the *m*-th frame consist of a motion-trajectory embedding ($c_{traj_m}$), a textual signal ($c_{text}$), and an optional reference image embedding ($c_{ref}$) for the initial frame.
- **Bidirectional Fine-tuning with Motion-Control:** The Wan2.1-1.3B I2V model is initially fine-tuned. This model utilizes a flow matching objective, extended to incorporate the curated control signals. Specifically, the model learns to predict the ground-truth velocity field based on the noisy input, time step, and control signals. For the initial frame (m=0), a reference image embedding ($c_{ref}$) is provided. For subsequent frames (m>0), no reference image is used, and Gaussian noise is injected in its place, making the control primarily trajectory and text-based.
- **Distilling to Real-Time AR Model:** The fine-tuned bidirectional teacher model is distilled into a few-step student model. This involves replacing the bidirectional attention mechanism with causal attention, which restricts the model from attending to future frames, making it autoregressive. The student model is further optimized using Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) and adversarial losses. The distillation process aims to reduce the number of denoising steps (N) significantly, enabling real-time inference.
- **Self-Rollout: Markovizing AR Training:** To address the train-test mismatch (exposure bias) inherent in AR models, where training typically uses ground-truth past frames (teacher forcing) while inference uses self-generated ones, the Self-Rollout strategy is introduced. Unlike traditional AR training that adds noise to ground-truth frames, Self-Rollout maintains a key-value (KV) memory cache of previously denoised frames and uses them as causal context. During training, frames are denoised sequentially from pure noise. For a given frame, a denoising step *n* is randomly sampled. The model denoises from the initial noise state to *x<sub>m,n</sub>* and computes the DMD and adversarial losses. It then *continues* denoising from *x<sub>m,n</sub>* all the way to a clean frame *x<sub>m,N</sub>* step-by-step. This fully generated clean frame *x<sub>m,N</sub>* is then used to update the KV cache. This full step-by-step rollout aligns training dynamics with inference dynamics, preserving the Markov property required for subsequent RL training. This is a critical distinction from "Self-Forcing," which collapses the remaining denoising steps into one, violating the MDP structure.

#### 3.2. Step 2: Reinforcement Learning on AR VDM

This phase leverages the Markovian property established by Self-Rollout to enhance the model with reinforcement learning.

- **Video Generation as MDP:** The denoising process in AR VDM is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
 - **State:** A state $s_{m,n}$ is defined by the control signals $c_m$, the current timestep $t_n$, and the snapshot of the entire video $X_{m,n}$ (which includes fully generated past frames, the current frame being denoised, and noise-initialized future frames).
 - **Action:** An action $a_{m,n}$ corresponds to the next denoised state of the *m*-th frame, $x_{m,n+1}$. The policy is parameterized by the VDM, with stochasticity introduced via an ODE-to-SDE conversion.
 - **Transition:** Transitions occur both intra-frame (deterministic steps within a frame's denoising) and inter-frame (when a frame is fully denoised, the state transitions to the initial state of the next frame).
 - **Reward Function:** Rewards are provided only upon the full denoising of a frame ($n=N$). The reward $R(x_{m,N}, c_m)$ is a composite of two components: $R_{quality}(x_{m,N})$ for perceptual fidelity and temporal smoothness, and $R_{motion}(x_{m,N}, c_m)$ for alignment with control signals.
- **GRPO for AR VDM:** The Generative Reinforcement Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework is extended for AR video generation. It involves sampling groups of videos and their trajectories, computing an advantage for each step based on the accumulated reward, and updating the policy (VDM parameters) using a clipped objective function to ensure stability.
- **Selective Stochastic Sampling:** To address the challenge of extremely long Markov chains in AR video generation, which can lead to high variance in trajectory returns and unstable RL training, a selective stochasticity strategy is employed. Instead of applying SDE sampling at every denoising step, only a single randomly chosen denoising step (per frame) follows the SDE formulation, while all other steps remain deterministic under the ODE solver. This injects sufficient randomness for RL exploration without incurring prohibitive computational costs associated with high variance.
- **Reward Design:**
 - **Realism Reward ($R_{quality}$):** This component measures perceptual fidelity and aesthetic quality using the LAION Aesthetic Quality Predictor ($f_{AQ}$), which assigns an aesthetic score (1-5) to the generated clean frame $x_{m,N}$.
 - **Motion Reward ($R_{motion}$):** This component evaluates the alignment of the generated motion with the control signals. It utilizes Co-Tracker to estimate the object trajectory $\hat{c}_{traj_m}$ from the generated image $x_{m,N}$ and compares it to the ground-truth control trajectory $c_{traj_m}$. The reward is defined as a function of the squared Euclidean distance between the estimated and ground-truth trajectories, scaled by hyperparameters $\lambda$ and $\alpha$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative evaluations of AR-Drag, comparing it against several state-of-the-art motion-controllable video diffusion models and an autoregressive baseline.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Comparisons

Table 1 presents a comparison of AR-Drag with established motion-controllable VDMs, including DragNUWA, DragAnything, Tora, MagicMotion, and a fine-tuned Self-Forcing baseline.

- **Latency:** AR-Drag demonstrates a significant reduction in latency, achieving an inference time of 0.44 seconds per first frame. This is considerably lower than bidirectional approaches like Tora (176.51s), MagicMotion (1426.37s), and even the Self-Forcing AR baseline (0.95s). This result indicates AR-Drag's capacity for real-time performance.
- **Visual Quality:** Despite being a few-step autoregressive model, AR-Drag achieved the lowest Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) of 28.98 and Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) of 187.49, along with the highest Aesthetic Quality score of 4.07. These metrics suggest superior visual fidelity and temporal coherence compared to all baselines, including the 5B parameter MagicMotion model.
- **Motion Control:** AR-Drag exhibited the highest Motion Smoothness (0.9948) and Motion Consistency (4.37). These results indicate a strong capability for precise and stable adherence to control signals. The performance on motion control metrics surpassed models that do not explicitly incorporate RL training for fine-grained control.
- **Comparison with Self-Forcing:** AR-Drag consistently outperformed the Self-Forcing baseline across all metrics, including latency (0.44s vs 0.95s), visual quality (lower FID/FVD, higher Aesthetic Quality), and motion control (higher smoothness and consistency). This highlights the effectiveness of AR-Drag's RL post-training and Self-Rollout mechanism.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Comparisons

Qualitative assessments in Figure 3 demonstrate AR-Drag's performance against Tora, MagicMotion, and Self-Forcing across varied prompts, data domains, and resolutions.

- **Fidelity and Control Alignment:** AR-Drag consistently produced videos with superior fidelity and closer alignment to control signals. Baselines like Tora and MagicMotion showed limitations in maintaining consistency with complex motion trajectories. Self-Forcing achieved partial controllability but often exhibited deformation and quality degradation.
- **Detail Preservation:** In high-resolution comparisons (Figures 3c and 3d), AR-Drag preserved fine details such as fingers and hair strands effectively, while Self-Forcing exhibited substantial detail loss and increased color saturation in certain cases.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Table 2 and Figure 4 present an analysis of key training strategies within AR-Drag.

- **Effect of Reinforcement Learning (w/o RL):** Removing the RL post-training led to a decrease in both visual quality (higher FID, FVD, lower Aesthetic Quality) and motion-related metrics (lower Motion Smoothness and Consistency). This indicates the contribution of RL in enhancing fidelity and motion controllability.
- **Initial Model vs. Teacher Model:** The initial Wan2.1-1.3B-I2V model performed significantly worse than AR-Drag, confirming that motion fine-tuning and real-time post-training are crucial. The teacher model (fine-tuned bidirectional multi-step) achieved strong performance but with high latency. AR-Drag achieved comparable or superior results in FID, Aesthetic Quality, Motion Smoothness, and Motion Consistency while significantly reducing latency, validating the efficacy of the RL approach on the distilled model.
- **Self-Rollout (w/o Self-Rollout):** Training without the Self-Rollout design resulted in substantial quality degradation (much higher FID and FVD, lower Aesthetic Quality), underscoring its necessity for maintaining the Markov property and mitigating the train-test mismatch critical for autoregressive generation and stable RL training.
- **Visualization of Ablations:** Qualitative results in Figure 4 visually support these findings, showing that models without RL or Self-Rollout exhibit limitations in generalization (e.g., missing foot details) or severe image artifacts, respectively.

#### 4.4. Parameter Analysis (Appendix D.1)

- **Chunk Size:** A comparison with a chunk-wise AR VDM (chunk size 3) showed that AR-Drag's frame-wise strategy, combined with Self-Rollout and RL, achieved comparable or slightly better performance while maintaining lower latency.
- **Cache Size:** Varying the KV cache size (7, 15, 25 frames) had minimal impact on performance, suggesting the method's robustness to different cache lengths.

#### 4.5. Visualization of Reward Curves (Appendix D.2)

The smoothed reward curves for Motion Consistency and Aesthetic Quality show upward trends during training, indicating the model's progressive improvement in both motion alignment and visual appeal.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that AR-Drag effectively addresses the challenges of real-time motion-controllable video generation by combining an autoregressive architecture, a robust distillation process, the novel Self-Rollout mechanism, selective stochastic sampling, and a carefully designed composite reward function for reinforcement learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents AR-Drag, an autoregressive video diffusion model that introduces several advancements in the field of real-time controllable video generation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Real-Time Interactive Video Generation:** AR-Drag is presented as the first RL-enhanced few-step autoregressive video diffusion model specifically designed for real-time motion-controllable Image-to-Video (I2V) generation. This significantly reduces the latency typically associated with bidirectional video diffusion models, making interactive and on-the-fly video editing and generation more practical. This capability could transform creative workflows, enabling artists, designers, and content creators to manipulate video content with immediate visual feedback.

2. **Addressing Key Technical Challenges in AR VDMs:** The work directly tackles persistent issues in autoregressive video generation, including:
 - **Quality Degradation and Motion Artifacts:** By employing an RL-enhanced framework, AR-Drag mitigates the problem of error accumulation that often leads to reduced visual fidelity and motion inconsistencies in few-step AR models.
 - **Complex Control Spaces:** The model supports diverse motion control signals, such as trajectories, which broadens its applicability beyond simpler control modalities.
 - **Exposure Bias and Markov Property:** The introduction of the Self-Rollout mechanism is a methodological contribution that effectively resolves the train-test mismatch (exposure bias) and ensures the Markov property in AR training, which is crucial for stable and effective reinforcement learning.

3. **Advancing Reinforcement Learning for Generative Models:** The paper demonstrates a successful application of the GRPO framework to high-fidelity autoregressive video generation. The selective stochastic sampling strategy is a notable innovation that makes RL training tractable and efficient for very long Markov chains in video generation by balancing exploration with computational cost. This approach could inspire further research into applying RL to other complex generative tasks with sequential decision processes.

4. **Competitive Performance with High Efficiency:** Despite using a compact model size of 1.3 billion parameters, AR-Drag achieves visual fidelity and precise motion alignment that is competitive with, and in some metrics superior to, significantly larger or more computationally intensive state-of-the-art bidirectional models. This efficiency makes the technology more accessible and deployable on a wider range of hardware.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust framework for real-time controllable video generation. Future research directions suggested include extending controllability to deliberately non-physical or cartoon-like motions and exploring methods for further reducing wall-clock latency through parallel compute and sampling techniques.

In summary, AR-Drag represents a notable step towards truly interactive and high-quality video synthesis. Its contributions to architectural design, RL integration, and efficiency could influence the development of future generative AI systems for various applications requiring dynamic and precise control over video content.
