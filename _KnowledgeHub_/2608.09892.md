# XPolicyLab: A Unified Standard and Open Ecosystem for Robot Policy Evaluation and Deployment

> [!summary] Summary
> XPolicyLab is a unified standard and open ecosystem for robot policy evaluation and deployment. It fixes common observation, action, and trajectory schemas, a four-operation adapter contract (update, predict, batched execution, reset), and a dependency-isolated WebSocket client/server bridge, cutting policy-environment integration from O(NM) to O(N+M). It ships 42 policy adapters and drops integration effort from over five hours to thirty minutes.

## Problem

- Policies assume incompatible dependencies, observation and action formats, checkpoint conventions, and runtime interfaces, so each policy-environment pairing needs bespoke platform-specific engineering.
- Connecting N policies to M evaluation environments costs O(NM) integrations; preprocessing, action conversion, and control loops get repeatedly reimplemented for every pair.
- Prior infrastructure standardizes data collection and training, lowering checkpoint production cost, but leaves the boundary between a trained policy and its execution environments unaddressed.

## Method

- **XPolicyLab** defines a unified ==adapter contract== with construction, update_obs, get_action, reset plus batched variants, leaving architecture, action decoder, and prediction horizon unspecified so native implementations stay intact.
- ==Dependency-isolated serving bridge==: ==WebSocket RPC== with ==MessagePack==, request IDs plus response caching for safe retries, instance-ID handshake, keepalive, and restart detection as fatal to a trial.
- ==Embodiment-aware observation and action schemas== (multi-view RGB-D, joints, Cartesian poses, language, metadata), with image decoding fixed serving-side and progressive ==offline conformance gates== plus ==machine-readable agent skills==.

## Results

- Controlled within-subject study (**N=6**) connecting a VLA policy to RoboDojo: over **5 h** from scratch, about **2 h** with the standard, about **30 min** with agent skills.
- Hand-written code drops from roughly **300** lines from scratch to about **120** with the standard and effectively **0** under agent-assisted integration; reproducing an integrated policy takes about **10 min**.
- Across **42** adapters, model-specific code varies by an order of magnitude while the environment-facing loop stays within a few lines of a fixed reference, spanning RoboTwin, RoboDojo sim, and **180** physical trials.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standardizing the interface, not the model, is what makes reuse possible: prescribing no architecture or horizon lets one adapter serve simulators and physical robots alike.
> - Placing image decoding and transport on the serving side removes silent train-evaluate convention mismatches, such as channel order, that otherwise contaminate cross-policy comparisons.
> - Shared observation contracts make performance gaps interpretable: RoboTwin clean-versus-randomized spreads and weak sim-to-real rank correlation would otherwise be indistinguishable from packing discrepancies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is experiencing rapid advancements, particularly with the emergence of generalist robot policies, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models and other embodied foundation models. These models demonstrate expanded capabilities and task coverage. However, their practical evaluation and deployment are hampered by a fragmented ecosystem. This fragmentation stems from incompatible software dependencies, diverse observation and action representations, varied checkpoint conventions, and inconsistent runtime interfaces. Consequently, integrating a single policy onto a new benchmark, simulator, or physical robot typically requires substantial, platform-specific engineering, often involving repeated reimplementation of observation preprocessing, action conversion, control loops, and runtime orchestration. This leads to an O(NM) integration cost for connecting N policies to M environments, making reproducible comparison and standardized deployment challenging.

Prior research has made significant progress in related areas. Efforts such as DROID, BridgeData, FastUMI, LeRobot, and RLinf have focused on standardizing robot data collection, dataset organization, and training infrastructure, thereby reducing the cost of producing trained policy checkpoints. Complementarily, various robot policy evaluation platforms like RLBench, ManiSkill2, LIBERO, RMBench, UniVTAC, RoboTwin, and RoboDojo provide controlled environments and protocols for evaluating robotic skills. Beyond simulation, platforms like RoboArena and RoboChallenge facilitate distributed real-robot evaluations. These platforms primarily define *what* is evaluated and *under what conditions*, but they often require benchmark-specific or policy-specific serving stacks to integrate different models.

Existing robot policies, such as OpenVLA, π0, and GR00T, often ship with inference and deployment code tailored to their specific architectures and dependencies. This model-centric approach necessitates repeated adaptation when deploying a policy across multiple evaluation settings, incurring the O(NM) cost.

XPolicyLab positions itself at the complementary boundary: connecting an *already-trained* policy to diverse environments. It aims to absorb heterogeneous model architectures and dependencies while exposing a stable, unified interface to simulators, benchmark clients, and physical robots. Unlike unified modeling frameworks that prescribe network architectures or training objectives, XPolicyLab focuses on standardizing the *interface* and *deployment infrastructure*, allowing model-specific implementations to remain intact. By doing so, it seeks to address the systemic integration problem, reducing the cost from O(NM) to O(N+M) and enabling standardized, reproducible policy comparison and deployment across various platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing XPolicyLab arises from the inefficiencies and challenges inherent in the current fragmented landscape of robot policy evaluation and deployment. These issues can be summarized as follows:

1. **High Integration Effort and Cost:** Connecting a new robot policy to an evaluation environment typically requires significant engineering effort due to incompatible software dependencies, observation and action representations, checkpoint formats, and runtime interfaces. This leads to an O(NM) integration cost for N policies and M environments, where N*M separate integrations are needed. The paper aims to reduce this cost to O(N+M) by introducing a unified standard.

2. **Lack of Reproducibility and Comparability:** The repeated reimplementation of observation preprocessing, action conversion, control loops, and runtime orchestration across different deployments often involves silently re-fixing conventions (e.g., camera naming, channel order, gripper scaling). This practice renders reported results difficult to compare accurately across different policies or even different evaluations of the same policy. A core objective is to establish a common contract that ensures consistent interpretation and execution, thereby facilitating reproducible policy comparison.

3. **Dependency Conflicts:** Robot policies frequently rely on complex software stacks that can conflict with the dependencies required by simulators or robot-control environments. This creates deployment challenges, particularly when attempting to run policies in diverse settings. XPolicyLab is motivated to provide a mechanism for dependency isolation.

4. **Inefficient Workflow for Evaluation and Deployment:** The process of installing, debugging, serving, and evaluating robot policies is often ad hoc and policy-specific. The system seeks to standardize these workflows.

5. **Need for Support for Diverse Policy Characteristics:** A reusable solution must accommodate advanced policy characteristics such as stateful policies, action chunking, batched evaluation, and both local and remote execution, all while maintaining reliable communication and decoupling policy implementations from environment-specific software stacks.

Based on these motivations, XPolicyLab's key objectives are:

- **To introduce a unified contract** between robot policies and evaluation environments that standardizes observation and action representations, action prediction, batched execution, and episode management, while preserving policy-specific internal implementations.
- **To develop a dependency-isolated serving architecture** that decouples policy inference from environment execution, enabling local and remote deployment across various simulators, benchmark clients, and physical robot systems.
- **To package the conformance procedure as machine-readable agent skills**, thereby transforming policy adapter scaffolding, implementation, and auditing into a guided and verifiable process, rather than a manual, documentation-intensive task.
- **To establish an open ecosystem** integrating a broad range of robot policies across diverse families, and to evaluate this ecosystem for policy coverage, integration complexity locality, integration effort reduction, and adapter reuse across multiple platforms like RoboTwin and RoboDojo.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

XPolicyLab adopts a policy-centric abstraction designed to decouple policy computation from environment execution. This approach ensures that each policy maintains its native architecture, dependencies, checkpoints, and inference procedure while exposing a stable external contract to heterogeneous evaluation environments. The system is composed of several functional components and relies on specific architectural principles.

#### 3.1. A. System Overview

XPolicyLab consists of four primary components: an **environment backend** (executes tasks), an **environment client** (manages interaction loop), a **communication layer** (transfers observations and actions), and a **policy server** (hosts the model and its adapter). Cross-cutting workflow tooling standardizes operations such as installation, configuration, debugging, training, serving, and evaluation. A key design principle is process-level isolation: the policy server operates in an independent software environment, potentially on a separate machine, from the environment client, which runs within the software stack of the benchmark, simulator, or robot driver.

The architecture establishes two stable boundaries:
1. **Semantic Boundary:** Defines shared observation and action schemas.
2. **Execution Boundary:** Governs communication between policy servers and environment clients.
Policy inference is modeled as `a_{t:t+H-1} = g_{out}(\pi_θ(g_{in}(o_t), s_t))`, where `g_in` and `g_out` are mappings between shared schemas and policy-native representations, which XPolicyLab standardizes externally without constraining internal model forms.

#### 3.2. B. Policy Adapter Contract

A policy adapter is a lightweight interface that implements four core operations:
- **Model Construction:** Loads policy checkpoints, processors, and runtime configurations.
- **`update_obs`:** Receives a standardized observation and updates the policy's internal context.
- **`get_action`:** Returns an action chunk in the shared schema.
- **`reset`:** Clears episode-specific internal state, crucial for recurrent or memory-augmented policies.
Batched versions (`update_obs_batch`, `get_action_batch`) are provided for parallel evaluation across multiple environment instances. The contract is designed to be agnostic to network architecture, action decoder, prediction horizon, or training framework, requiring adapters only to translate between the shared interface and the policy's native API.

#### 3.3. C. Dependency-Isolated Serving

To resolve software dependency conflicts, XPolicyLab uses process-level isolation. The policy server loads the model in its native runtime environment, while the environment client operates in the environment required by the simulator or robot. Communication occurs via a **WebSocket protocol** using **MessagePack serialization**, which is extended to handle arrays. The message set includes `HELLO`, `PREPARE_CASE`, `RESET`, `INFER`, `CALL`, `TRIAL_END`, `HEARTBEAT`, and `CLOSE`, each with corresponding acknowledgements or result frames.

The evaluation follows a stateful loop: `reset → (observe → update_obs → get_action → execute)*`. The environment client dictates the number of predicted actions to execute from an action chunk before requesting a new one. The protocol supports batched inference for multiple environment instances and distributed execution, allowing policy inference on a dedicated GPU server while environment control remains local.

Reliability is handled by assigning unique identifiers to requests and caching completed responses. Retries for transient disconnections return cached results to prevent corrupting policy state. A change in the policy server's instance identifier, reported during handshake, is treated as a fatal error for the current trial, indicating loss of episode state. Configurable connection budgets, keepalive checks, and inference timeouts accommodate varying system latencies.

#### 3.4. D. Standardized Observation and Action Schemas

Observations (`o_t`) are represented as a dictionary: `{v_t, q_t, p_t, ℓ, m_t}`.
- `v_t`: Visual inputs (RGB images, depth maps, camera calibrations), indexed by camera name.
- `q_t`: Robot joint states (for single-arm or bimanual embodiments, including gripper states).
- `p_t`: Cartesian poses (`[x, y, z, q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]`).
- `ℓ`: Language instruction.
- `m_t`: Optional metadata.
All fields are optional, based on the policy's requirements.

Actions use the same embodiment-aware organization, supporting both joint-space and end-effector-space control. Robot-specific action dimensions are managed by embodiment configurations, promoting a stable external schema despite heterogeneous kinematics. Data processing is explicitly divided: the serving layer handles transport-level operations (deserialization, image decoding) to ensure adapters receive images in a consistent format, while adapters perform only model-specific transformations (resizing, normalization, tokenization).

#### 3.5. E. Integration and Conformance

Policy integration is framed as conformance to a shared specification. A policy package includes a runtime configuration, model adapter, execution scripts, documentation, and a reproducible checkpoint specification.
Validation is progressive:
1. **Static Checks:** Verify configuration, imports, and script integrity.
2. **Offline Closed-Loop Client:** Exercises server startup, observation serialization, action structure, batched execution, and episode reset to a deterministic finish, independent of a simulator.
3. **Environment Connection:** Only after passing interface-level checks is the adapter connected to a supported environment.
This ensures that extending the ecosystem requires one adapter per policy or one client per environment, rather than N*M custom integrations.

#### 3.6. F. Agent-Assisted Integration

The conformance procedure is formalized into machine-readable agent skills: an **integration skill** (encodes adapter layout, contract rules, checkpoint conventions, debug procedure) and an **audit skill** (checks submissions against gates). Coding agents (e.g., Cursor, Claude Code, Codex) can load these skills to scaffold, implement, and validate new adapters under human supervision, streamlining the integration process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

XPolicyLab's development and deployment yield several findings regarding its ecosystem, performance in benchmarks, and reduction of integration effort.

#### 4.1. A. Policy Ecosystem

As of August 2026, XPolicyLab has integrated 42 robot policies. These policies span a diverse range of architectures and approaches, including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, world-action models, diffusion-based visuomotor policies, memory-augmented policies, and conventional imitation-learning baselines. Each integration encapsulates model-specific components (dependencies, checkpoint loading, observation preprocessing, action decoding, state management) behind the common XPolicyLab contract. This enables environment clients to interact with all supported policies through the same set of operations: observation updates, action predictions, batched execution, and episode resets. The ecosystem supports policies with heterogeneous temporal and computational requirements, from single-step prediction to explicit memory and from lightweight baselines to large foundation models requiring isolated GPU runtimes.

#### 4.2. B. Applications and Leaderboard Evidence

XPolicyLab has been deployed and evaluated across three distinct settings within two public benchmark ecosystems: RoboTwin simulation, RoboDojo simulation, and RoboDojo real-world evaluation (RoboDojo-RealEval). These deployments demonstrate the operational coverage and portability of the XPolicyLab infrastructure.

- **RoboTwin Simulation:** RoboTwin evaluates bimanual manipulation across 50 tasks in both clean and randomized settings. XPolicyLab connects policies to RoboTwin via an environment client that constructs standardized observations and executes actions. This separation allows RoboTwin to use a single serving stack for policies with diverse architectures and dependencies. Performance data for the top 10 policies, ranked by mean success rate in the clean setting, were reported. For example, FastWAM achieved a 77.8% clean success rate, while π0.5 achieved 70.7% clean success rate and 46.0% randomized success rate.

- **RoboDojo Simulation:** RoboDojo features 42 simulation tasks organized across five capability dimensions (Generalization, Precision, Long-Horizon, Memory, Open). Its environment workers execute tasks concurrently in Isaac Sim, with XPolicyLab policy servers handling observation updates and batched action prediction. This process isolation is noted as important for large policies with dependencies incompatible with the simulator runtime. The top 10 policies on the RoboDojo simulation leaderboard, ranked by average score, were presented. G0.5 achieved a score of 20.23 (14.88% SR), while Xiaomi-Robotics-1 achieved 20.07 (13.93% SR).

- **RoboDojo Real-World Evaluation (RoboDojo-RealEval):** This extends the XPolicyLab architecture to 18 physical tasks across three bimanual robot embodiments (ARX X5, Piper, Piper X). The robot controller functions as the XPolicyLab environment client, while policy inference runs in an isolated process, potentially on a remote GPU server. Policy adapters are reused without modification of their model-specific inference logic, provided observation and action representations are preserved. Each policy was evaluated over 10 trials per task (180 physical trials total). The top 10 policies by overall score were reported, with π0.5 leading at a score of 22.9 (12.8% SR).

Observations from these leaderboard evaluations indicate that absolute performance in both simulation and reality is currently low, and real-world rankings do not consistently track simulation rankings. This suggests that the cost of running physical trials is a significant bottleneck. Furthermore, the varying performance gaps between clean and randomized RoboTwin success rates across policies were only interpretable because all entries adhered to a single observation contract, distinguishing performance differences from potential packing discrepancies.

#### 4.3. C. Integration and Reproduction Effort

A controlled study involving six engineers quantified the integration cost of connecting π0.5 to the RoboDojo simulation. Participants performed the task under three conditions:
1. **From scratch:** Using only the upstream model repository.
2. **XPolicyLab (manual):** Following the XPolicyLab adapter standard manually.
3. **XPolicyLab + agent skills:** Using packaged agent skills (Cursor with Opus 5) for scaffolding, implementation, and debugging under human supervision.

The study showed the following median results for time-to-first-successful-rollout and hand-written lines of code (LoC):

| Phase | From scratch | XPolicyLab (manual) | XPolicyLab + agent skills |
| :---------------------- | :----------- | :------------------ | :------------------------ |
| Environment setup | 2 h 30 min | 10 min | - |
| Obs./action glue code | 2 h | 1 h 10 min | - |
| Debugging to 1st rollout| 1.5 h | 30 min | 10 min |
| **Total (median)** | **>5 h** | **∼2 h** | **∼30 min** |
| Hand-written LoC | ∼300 | ∼120 | ∼0 |

The results indicate that conforming to the XPolicyLab standard manually reduced integration effort from over five hours to approximately two hours. Further, utilizing the packaged agent skills reduced this time to about thirty minutes, representing an order-of-magnitude reduction compared to the from-scratch baseline. For reproducing an already-integrated policy, installation and a single evaluation command took approximately 10 minutes, excluding checkpoint download time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

XPolicyLab introduces a unified standard and an open ecosystem that addresses the fragmentation in robot policy evaluation and deployment. Its primary significance lies in the systematic reduction of integration overhead and the promotion of reproducible research in embodied AI.

1. **Reduced Integration Effort:** By specifying a minimal policy adapter contract and standardized observation/action schemas, XPolicyLab transforms the O(NM) integration problem into an O(N+M) problem. This reduction, empirically demonstrated through the controlled study, significantly lowers the barrier for deploying new policies across multiple environments and integrating new environments with existing policies. The reported reduction from over five hours to approximately thirty minutes with agent assistance indicates a substantial gain in efficiency for researchers and developers.

2. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Comparability:** The standardized interfaces and data representations ensure that policies are evaluated under consistent conditions, eliminating silent re-fixing of conventions that previously hindered cross-policy comparisons. This allows for more meaningful and reliable performance comparisons across diverse policies and benchmarks, which is critical for scientific progress in robotics.

3. **Dependency Isolation and Portability:** The dependency-isolated client/server architecture enables policies to retain their native software stacks while executing in environments with potentially conflicting dependencies. This facilitates deployment across heterogeneous platforms, including various simulators (e.g., RoboTwin, RoboDojo simulation) and physical robot systems (e.g., RoboDojo-RealEval), using a single policy adapter. The ability to run inference locally or remotely on dedicated hardware (e.g., GPU servers) further enhances flexibility and scalability.

4. **Streamlined Workflow and Agent-Assisted Integration:** XPolicyLab standardizes the entire policy lifecycle, from installation to evaluation. The inclusion of machine-readable agent skills for scaffolding, implementing, and validating adapters significantly automates the integration process. This not only reduces human effort but also democratizes access to policy deployment, potentially enabling researchers with less systems-level expertise to contribute and evaluate new policies effectively.

5. **Focus on Robotic Interaction:** By abstracting away the complexities of policy integration and dependency management, XPolicyLab allows researchers to concentrate on algorithmic innovation and direct robotic interaction. The paper emphasizes that in real-world evaluations, the bottleneck is often the cost of physical trials rather than the technical hurdles of getting a policy to run. XPolicyLab ensures that "scarce time is spent on physical interaction rather than on rediscovering how to feed each checkpoint."

In conclusion, XPolicyLab provides crucial infrastructure for the development and deployment of generalist robot policies. Its unified standard fosters a more cohesive and efficient research ecosystem, accelerating progress by making policy evaluation and comparison more accessible, reproducible, and robust across diverse simulation and physical robot platforms.
