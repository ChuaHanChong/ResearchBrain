# tinyDSM: A Framework for Skill Modeling and Development for Resource-Constrained Millirobots

> [!summary] Summary
> tinyDSM is a developmental skill framework for cm-scale millirobots combining a hierarchical knowledge graph, kinematic reasoners, fitness-based skill assessment, and intrinsic motivation (novelty x progress x difficulty) to drive open-ended skill acquisition. On an RP2040 microcontroller within 9 kB memory, a millirobot autonomously progressed from atomic linear/angular moves to compound square-path navigation in about 15 minutes.

## Problem

- Robot learning trends toward large ML models requiring heavy memory, time, and energy, making them unsuitable for millirobots under 500 g with severe compute and power constraints.
- Existing cognitive architectures like KnowRob, RoboBrain, and BWIBots rely on extensive knowledge databases or static task planning, not minimal, dynamically extensible representations for tiny embedded robots.
- Prior intrinsic-motivation approaches for skill acquisition use goal selection based mainly on learning progress or novelty, without a structured knowledge graph deriving an explicit difficulty factor.

## Method

- **tinyDSM** developmental process: a meta-controller that queries a ==hierarchical knowledge graph== to form a masterable Skill Pool, selects skills via ==intrinsic motivation==, and drives curriculum-based skill unlocking.
- ==Intrinsic motivation== model combining novelty, progress, and difficulty as a product term, with difficulty derived directly from prerequisite-skill fitness in the knowledge graph structure.
- ==Kinematic reasoner== and 5-tuple fitness formulation (target, range, observation, error, threshold vectors) that scores skill quality from sensor readings and generalizes across a hierarchy of motion skills.

## Results

- The millirobot progressed from atomic moves to complex geometric patterns (square path) within about **15 minutes** total, mastering basic movements in roughly **4.5 minutes** on real hardware.
- Full tinyDSM runtime including agent, skills, learners, SAS, and scheduler fits in **8897 B** of RAM on an RP2040 with only 264 kB SRAM and no FPU.
- In simulation, Q-learning and simulated annealing both reached near-optimal fitness while a random policy stagnated; baseline intrinsic-motivation parameters scored highest (IM Score **0.675**) versus high-exploration settings (**0.503**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Deriving intrinsic-motivation difficulty directly from a knowledge graph's prerequisite structure ties curriculum ordering to actual skill dependencies rather than hand-tuned schedules.
> - Minimal a-priori knowledge (physical/geometric laws, sensor-actuator interfaces) plus fitness-driven exploration can substitute for large offline-trained models on severely resource-constrained hardware.
> - Intrinsic-motivation parameterization is stability-critical: excessive novelty-seeking causes long-term neglect of mastered skills, while balanced settings sustain both exploration diversity and low neglect.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research integrates concepts from several established and emerging fields to address the challenge of autonomous learning and adaptation in highly resource-constrained robotic systems.

The work is positioned within **developmental robotics**, which explores mechanisms enabling robots to continuously explore, learn, and adapt throughout their lifespan, drawing inspiration from biological development in children and animals. It adopts a **cognitive approach** to construct cognitive functions in a developmentally appropriate manner, focusing on aspects like body representation, perception, and motor skills.

The paper differentiates itself from much of the mainstream robotics and machine learning (ML) research, which increasingly relies on advanced robots with significant computational resources, memory, and energy. Instead, this work contributes to the emerging field of **tiny robot learning**. This subfield focuses on deploying ML on resource-constrained, low-cost autonomous robots, facing limitations in size, weight, area, power, and hardware for sensors, actuators, and computation.

Specifically, the proposed tiny Developmental Skill Method (tinyDSM) framework incorporates elements of **cognitive architectures**, aiming to enable learning competencies in a development-oriented manner with open-ended skill acquisition. While existing cognitive architectures often rely on extensive symbolic reasoning and memory (e.g., LRMB, ACT-R, Soar), tinyDSM specifically targets a minimal cognitive architecture for millirobots with a lightweight knowledge structure and explicit motion-related reasoning.

The methodology also draws heavily from **intrinsic motivation (IM)**, a concept from cognitive science and psychology. IM is recognized as a driver for skill acquisition and development, enabling autonomous entities to deal with problems effectively. In robotics, IM is often combined with Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL). tinyDSM extends existing IM approaches by integrating a structured knowledge graph to derive a difficulty factor, complementing traditional novelty and progress factors.

Furthermore, the paper addresses the constraints imposed by **TinyML**, which focuses on techniques like quantization and pruning to deploy ML models on low-resource devices. However, many TinyML systems rely on offline training, whereas tinyDSM emphasizes on-device, continuous learning. While acknowledging the potential of advanced Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms like Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG), the authors opt for simpler, more resource-efficient RL algorithms (e.g., Q-learning, Simulated Annealing) suitable for tiny robots. Genetic algorithms are also noted as a promising alternative for policy optimization in this context.

The work distinguishes itself by attempting to minimize prior knowledge and assumptions, aiming for highly flexible systems capable of adapting to various sensor/actuator types, environments, and even system degradation, which contrasts with approaches that require substantial predefined domain-specific knowledge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate and implement developmental mechanisms that enable small, resource-constrained systems, specifically centimeter-sized millirobots, to autonomously explore, learn, and adapt their capabilities throughout their operational lifespan.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and opportunities:
- **Resource Constraints:** Traditional machine learning and robotic control methods often demand substantial computational resources, memory, time, and energy. This makes them unsuitable for tiny robots, which have severe limitations in size, weight, area, and power. The work aims to bridge this gap by enabling advanced learning capabilities within these constraints.
- **Autonomous Skill Acquisition and Adaptation:** Equipping millirobots with the ability to learn and adapt autonomously is crucial for their deployment in dynamic and unpredictable environments, such as emergency search and rescue, or routine infrastructure monitoring, where explicit pre-programming for every scenario is impractical or impossible.
- **Minimal A-priori Knowledge:** A core motivation is to reduce the amount of pre-programmed, hard-wired knowledge. The goal is to provide a minimal set of generic foundational knowledge (e.g., physical and geometric laws, sensor/actuator interaction principles) and allow the robot to learn system-specific dependencies and develop new skills in an open-ended manner. This enhances flexibility and generalizability across diverse application domains, allowing adaptation to different hardware, environments, and even aging effects.
- **Lifelong Learning and Robustness:** The system is motivated by the need for continuous development, adaptation to environmental changes (e.g., added weight, wear-out), and prevention of learning stagnation. This requires mechanisms for sustained exploration and improvement of skills over time.
- **Cognitive Architecture for Small Robots:** The research aims to demonstrate that cognitive architectures, typically associated with larger, more powerful robots, can be effectively scaled down to function within the tight resource envelopes of millirobots, focusing on knowledge representation, reasoning, and learning.
- **Intrinsic Motivation for Self-Directed Learning:** Incorporating intrinsic motivation is crucial to drive the robot's self-generated exploration and objective pursuit, fostering skill development based on curiosity and perceived difficulty, rather than relying solely on external rewards or predefined goals. This is intended to facilitate continuous, unbounded learning.

In summary, the detailed objectives are:
1. To propose **tinyDSM**, a framework that integrates developmental mechanisms, intrinsic motivation, and a cognitive architecture.
2. To demonstrate that this framework can enable resource-constrained millirobots to **autonomously acquire and adapt motion-related skills** from minimal initial knowledge.
3. To validate the framework's efficiency in terms of **resource utilization** (memory, energy) on a specific low-power microcontroller.
4. To systematically evaluate the impact of **intrinsic motivation parameters** and learning algorithms on developmental dynamics through simulation.
5. To show the robot's ability to **adapt to unforeseen environmental changes** (e.g., changes in physical properties).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes the **tiny Developmental Skill Method (tinyDSM)**, a framework designed for resource-constrained millirobots. It integrates a developmental mechanism with intrinsic motivation, a cognitive architecture (knowledge, reasoning, learning), all while utilizing minimal resources.

#### 3.1. Core Components of tinyDSM

- **Sensor-Actuator Space (SAS):** Defined as a 2-tuple (S, C) consisting of a set of sensors (S) and actuators (C). It provides an abstraction layer for robot-specific sensor readings and actuator commands. Sensor readings map an identifier at a given time to a vector of values, while actuator commands are defined by an identifier and parameters (e.g., time, force, duration for motors). For the experimental millirobot, SAS includes two motors and one sensor providing 2D pose (x, y, yaw).
- **Fitness:** An integral element for assessing skill quality. It is calculated based on a skill-dependent set of quantities (e.g., sensor readings like distance or velocity). A target vector ($\vec{t}$), an observed vector ($\vec{o}$), and a permissible range vector ($\vec{r}$) are used to compute an error vector ($\vec{e}$). The scalar fitness value $f \in$ is derived from the Euclidean norm of $\vec{e}$. A skill is deemed "learned" if its fitness $f$ meets or exceeds a predefined threshold ($f_{threshold}$, typically 0.95).
- **Skill:** Comprises three components:
 - **Specification:** A 2-tuple (T, F) defining the desired transformation of sensor readings (T) and the fitness function (F). T specifies the desired change in sensor states before and after actions.
 - **Learning Routine:** An algorithm (e.g., a reinforcement learning algorithm) that modifies an action sequence to maximize fitness.
 - **State:** A 2-tuple (A, $\bar{f}$) consisting of the current action sequence (A) and the current fitness value ($\bar{f}$). A is a sequence of commands applied whenever the skill is executed. Initially, A can be empty, requiring the learning routine to populate and optimize it.

- **Knowledge Graph (KG):** A directed acyclic graph that forms the foundation of the cognitive system model. It encodes semantic knowledge by contextualizing system components (sensors, actuators, skills) and their dependencies. Entities (skills, sensors, actuators) are related by directed edges indicating prerequisites (e.g., one skill must be learned before another) or sensor/actuator requirements. The KG allows the robot to infer which skills are masterable based on available sensorimotor abilities and previously learned skills, thus supporting curriculum learning. For this work, the KG is fixed at runtime but designed for flexible updates.
- **Intrinsic Motivation (IM):** Models the complex behavior of the millirobot, driving it to pursue self-generated objectives. It is calculated as a product of three factors:
 - **Novelty:** Reflects how unfamiliar a skill is, encouraging exploration of new skills. It decays with execution and grows over time if unselected.
 - **Progress:** Primarily driven by fitness assessment, encouraging improvement by boosting motivation for skills showing rapid fitness growth. It also enables adaptation to regressions.
 - **Difficulty:** Refers to the complexity of mastering a skill, derived from its hierarchical position and dependencies in the KG. It ensures balanced exploration, prioritizing complex skills while periodically revisiting simpler ones.
 IM aims for continuous development without stagnation, ensuring all skills in the KG are eventually explored and improved. The IM calculation is lightweight, using minimal historical data.

#### 3.2. Developmental Process

The tinyDSM operates in discrete time steps:
1. **Initialization:** At $t_0$, the millirobot queries its KG using its available sensor and actuator set to generate a **Skill Pool ($M_O$)** of masterable skills.
2. **Skill Selection:** The skill with the highest intrinsic motivation from $M_O$ at time $t$ is selected for learning.
3. **Action Generation & Execution:** The learner generates an action sequence ($A_t$), which the SAS translates into robot-specific actuator commands ($C_t$). These commands result in physical actions in the environment.
4. **Sensor Feedback:** The robot reads its sensors ($S_{t+1}$) to observe the effects of its actions.
5. **Reasoning & Fitness Calculation:** Kinematic and fitness reasoners monitor state transitions ($T_{t+1}$), comparing observed readings to expected assumptions. Based on this, fitness ($F_{t+1}$) is calculated, serving as a reward signal for the learner.
6. **Learner Update:** The learning algorithm refines its policy based on $T_{t+1}$ and $F_{t+1}$, updating the skill's state ($X_{t+1}$).
7. **Skill Status Transition:** If $f_{threshold}$ is reached, the skill transitions from active learning to exploitation.
8. **KG Re-query:** In the next time step, the agent re-queries the KG. This step can lead to: (a) unlocking more complex skills (Curriculum Learning), (b) discovering new skills if the KG was extended, (c) expanding the developmental space if physical modifications occurred, or (d) continuing development of existing skills.

This self-reinforcing loop aims for structured yet adaptive learning, enabling rapid convergence while continuously monitoring and adapting performance.

#### 3.3. Learning Routine (Learner)

The chosen learning routine, or "learner," optimizes the skill's policy under strict resource constraints. For experiments, **Simulated Annealing (SA)** is employed due to its simplicity and resource efficiency, although the framework is designed to accommodate other RL algorithms like Q-learning, DQN, or DDPG. The learner uses small models with limited completeness and a minimalist reward design (0-1 normalized fitness). Curriculum learning and knowledge-based reasoners guide the process to efficient local optima.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Millirobot:** A 3D-printed robot (150g, 40x30 mm$^2$) with four wheels (two motor-driven).
- **Microcontroller:** Raspberry Pi Pico (RP2040) 32-bit microcontroller (ARM Cortex-M0+, 133 MHz, 264 kB SRAM).
- **Pose Sensing:** A host device (desktop computer) captures translation information (x, y, yaw) using a camera and an ArUco fiducial marker system, transmitting the sensed pose via Bluetooth to the RP2040. Onboard localization is a planned future integration.
- **Kinematic Reasoner:** Infers linear (Euclidean distance $d \ne 0$, $\Delta\psi = 0$) and angular ($\Delta\psi \ne 0$, $d = 0$) motion patterns from pose changes, forming part of the minimal a-priori knowledge.

#### 3.5. Skills Modeled for Experiments

Five motion-related skills are hierarchically modeled:
- **ATOMIC MOVE [LINEAR] ($o_{AM L}$):** Learn to drive in a straight line with minimal rotation, without a specific distance. Fitness is based on minimizing lateral/rotational movement and ensuring a minimum forward distance. Learner optimizes motor forces ($f_1, f_2$) for a constant duration ($\tau$).
- **ATOMIC MOVE [ANGULAR] ($o_{AM A}$):** Learn to rotate in place with minimal translation, without a specific angle. Fitness is based on minimizing translation and ensuring a minimum rotation. Learner optimizes motor forces for a constant duration.
- **MOVE [LINEAR] ($o_{M L}$):** Builds on $o_{AM L}$ to drive a specific linear distance ($d_l$). Learner optimizes the duration ($\tau$) given pre-learned forces.
- **MOVE [ANGULAR] ($o_{M A}$):** Builds on $o_{AM A}$ to rotate a specific angular distance ($d_a$). Learner optimizes the duration ($\tau$).
- **MOVE SQUARE ($o_{M S}$):** A higher-level skill with a predefined action sequence (no learner) to trace a square by executing a series of $o_{M L}$ (50mm) and $o_{M A}$ (90$^\circ$) actions. Its fitness is evaluated based on the absolute differences between the global starting and ending x and y coordinates.

#### 3.6. Simulation-Based Analysis

A physics-based simulation environment (Python/pygame) mirrors the millirobot's SAS to allow for controlled and repeatable evaluation.
- **Learning Algorithms Compared:** Simulated Annealing (SA), Q-learning, and a uniform random action selection (baseline).
- **IM Metrics:** Selection entropy (how evenly skills are selected) and maximum neglect (how long a skill remains unselected).
- **IM Score:** A combined metric ($\bar{f} - \lambda N_{max} + \mu \bar{H}$) balancing learning performance (mean skill fitness $\bar{f}$) with IM scheduling stability (time-averaged maximum neglect $N_{max}$ and selection entropy $\bar{H}$).
- **IM Parameterizations:** Six configurations were tested, biasing exploration, exploitation, novelty ranges, fitness thresholds, or post-threshold convergence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Development

The millirobot demonstrated autonomous skill development from a state of no prior learned skills.
- **Curriculum Learning:** Approximately 100 ms after startup, the agent discovered $o_{AM A}$ and $o_{AM L}$ as the only initial masterable skills. The skill pool expanded dynamically as lower-order skills reached their fitness threshold ($f_{threshold}$).
- **Intrinsic Motivation Dynamics:**
 - **Initial Exploration (Segment A-B):** Motivation for atomic skills ($o_{AM L}, o_{AM A}$) increased sharply due to novelty. The agent alternated between learning these, with faster initial progress on angular motion. Motivation decreased as competence increased and novelty waned, even with fitness growth.
 - **Higher-Order Skill Discovery (Segment C):** After approximately 1.8 minutes and ~50 physical interactions, $o_{AM A}$ reached $f_{threshold}$, unlocking $o_{M A}$. Its higher hierarchical order in the KG led to a greater difficulty factor, further increasing motivation.
 - **Focused Learning & Rediscovery (Segment D-F):** The agent focused on new skills (e.g., $o_{M L}$), showing patterns of decreasing motivation with increasing competence. It also showed interest in "rediscovering" atomic skills periodically. $o_{M A}$ reached $f_{threshold}$ in segment E, followed by $o_{AM L}$ reaching its threshold, leading to the discovery of $o_{M L}$. Finally, $o_{M S}$ was discovered in segment F.
 - **Complex Skill Exploitation (Segment F-H):** The agent primarily focused on developing $o_{M S}$, a complex skill utilizing previously learned skills. Despite this focus, it did not entirely neglect simpler atomic movements (segment H).
- **Efficiency:** The millirobot learned basic movements (sufficient for effective navigation) within approximately 4.5 minutes.

#### 4.2. Adaptation

The framework demonstrated the robot's ability to adapt to environmental changes.
- **Weight Load Experiment:** After approximately 6.5 minutes of normal development, the robot was loaded with an additional 380g (significantly increasing its total weight).
- **Fitness Drop and Motivation Increase:** This weight change invalidated previously learned movement parameters, causing a significant drop in the fitness values of $o_{M A}$ and $o_{M L}$. Atomic motion skills ($o_{AM A}, o_{AM L}$) remained largely unaffected as the weight primarily impacted motor command duration, not force ratios. The decrease in fitness led to a sharp increase in motivation, driven by the "progress" factor, prompting the robot to relearn the affected skills.
- **Relearning:** Over a period of approximately 9 minutes, the agent successfully relearned its skills, as indicated by increasing fitness scores and experience, demonstrating adaptive behavior to unforeseen physical alterations.

#### 4.3. Fitness Assessment

The fitness calculation scaled effectively to complex skills and provided insights into learning progress and system behavior.
- **Learning Progress and Variance:** Movements with lower learning progress (0.7 < $f$ < 0.95) exhibited high variance and significant deviation from targets. More developed movements ($f \ge 0.95$) showed reduced variance and closer adherence to targets (Fig. 8).
- **Hardware Non-Idealities:** Even at high fitness, some variance persisted. This was attributed to physical properties of the millirobot, such as an unpredictable "push" during motor braking phases due to gearbox stalling. This led to overshooting and affected the accuracy of composite skills.
- **MOVE SQUARE Evaluation:** The trajectory of $o_{M S}$ deviated from a perfect square, with many squares remaining "open" (missing the target endpoint on the left) due to accumulated errors from lower-level skills (Fig. 9). Despite these non-idealities, the average accumulated navigation error was 7.5 mm, considered acceptable given the robot's footprint and motor hardware. The hierarchical fitness calculation accurately reflected errors at lower levels directly impacting higher-level skills.

#### 4.4. Resources

The tinyDSM framework exhibited predictable and minimal resource consumption.
- **Memory Usage:** The total static memory usage was 8897 Bytes (less than 9 kB), integrated into the RP2040 microcontroller.
 - Learners consumed the largest portion (52.1% or 2544 B), partly due to internal memory fragmentation from the buddy system allocator and dynamic array usage.
 - Skills themselves required 488 B, SAS 720 B, and the scheduler (IM) 840 B.
- **Scalability:** The framework could theoretically accommodate approximately 96 instances of the most complex "move" skills within the RP2040's 250 kB usable RAM.
- **Dynamic Resource Adjustment:** Memory allocated for specific learning algorithms could be released once skills are sufficiently developed (e.g., when $f_{threshold}$ is reached), allowing the millirobot to balance workload and conserve energy in exploitation phases. For example, 2544 B could be freed once all skills are learned and no active learning is required.
- **Execution Time & Energy:** A single learning step varied from 10 ms to 30 ms depending on skill complexity. The robot operated for approximately 45 minutes on a 250 mA·h battery during experiments.

#### 4.5. Simulation-Based Analysis

- **Skill Acquisition Across Learning Algorithms:**
 - **Q-learning** demonstrated the fastest convergence to a full skill set, followed closely by **Simulated Annealing (SA)**. Both achieved near-optimal performance, indicating that tinyDSM's intrinsic motivation and curriculum effectively guide these learners to relevant search spaces (Fig. 10).
 - The **Random** policy failed to develop meaningful behavior, stagnating far below the threshold for skill acquisition, especially for higher-order skills.
- **Intrinsic Motivation Dynamics:**
 - **Baseline Configuration:** Maintained a balance with high selection entropy ($\approx 0.83$) and low maximum neglect ($\approx 1.37$), indicating stable and diverse development.
 - **High Exploration:** Led to high selection entropy but also extreme maximum neglect, suggesting some skills were ignored for extended periods (Fig. 11).
 - **High Exploitation:** Resulted in low neglect but slightly reduced coverage.
 - **Lower fthr (Fitness Threshold):** Led to early mastery but increased neglect.
 - **High Postslope:** Kept mastered skills attractive, causing repeated revisits and reduced developmental schedule stability.
 - **High Explore:** Performed worst, focusing on novelty excessively, leading to long-term starvation of many skills despite high overall entropy.
- **IM Score Comparison:** The baseline configuration achieved the highest IM Score (0.675), reflecting optimal balance between fast, high-quality learning and stable, well-balanced skill scheduling (Table V). This highlights the sensitivity of tinyDSM to IM parameterization and the critical role of IM scheduling in developmental stability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates the feasibility of equipping highly resource-constrained millirobots with advanced autonomous skill learning and adaptation capabilities. The proposed tinyDSM framework provides a viable approach for on-device, lifelong learning in systems with severe hardware limitations.

The significance and potential impact include:
- **Enabling Autonomous Behavior in Tiny Robots:** By allowing millirobots to learn and adapt from minimal prior knowledge, the framework addresses a key limitation for deploying such robots in dynamic and unknown environments. This can expand their utility in applications like search and rescue in confined spaces, inspection of hard-to-reach infrastructure, or routine environmental monitoring, where manual programming for every scenario is impractical.
- **Reduced Customization and Increased Flexibility:** The emphasis on learning system-specific dependencies from generic foundational knowledge means that the robots can adapt to various sensor/actuator configurations and physical environments. This could significantly reduce the effort required for customization and deployment across different robotic platforms and tasks, particularly relevant for the burgeoning Internet of Things (IoT) domain.
- **Robustness to Environmental Changes and Degradation:** The continuous fitness-based evaluation and intrinsically motivated adaptation mechanisms allow robots to detect and respond to changes in their physical properties (e.g., added weight, wear-and-tear) or environment. This enhances the long-term robustness and operational longevity of these systems.
- **Advancement in Cognitive Developmental Robotics:** The research demonstrates that cognitive architectures can be successfully scaled down to highly constrained embedded devices, contributing to the understanding of how complex cognitive functions can emerge and be maintained with minimal resources.
- **Efficient Resource Management:** The framework's low memory footprint (under 9 kB for the core system) and the potential for dynamic resource allocation (freeing learner memory after skill acquisition) are critical for sustainable operation on battery-powered microcontrollers, promoting energy efficiency during exploitation phases.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The successful integration of intrinsic motivation, hierarchical knowledge graphs, and resource-efficient learning algorithms lays a groundwork for exploring more complex cognitive functions, richer sensing modalities, and advanced planning strategies on future generations of tiny robots. Future work envisions integrating on-device lightweight neural networks and tackling more intricate scenarios, further pushing the boundaries of autonomous learning in resource-limited systems.
