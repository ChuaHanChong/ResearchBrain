# ExtremControl: Low-Latency Humanoid Teleoperation with Direct Extremity Control

> [!summary] Summary
> ExtremControl developed a low-latency humanoid teleoperation system that achieves end-to-end latencies of approximately 50-60 ms, a substantial reduction from the 200 ms typical in previous systems. It enables more responsive humanoid behaviors for dynamic tasks by utilizing direct extremity control with velocity feedforward and an efficient Cartesian-space mapping.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid teleoperation systems suffer from approximately 200 ms end-to-end latency, hindering the robot's ability to perform responsive, dynamic tasks.
- This latency is largely due to computational overhead from heavy pre-processed human-to-humanoid motion retargeting and the inherent tracking delays of position-only PD control.
- Earlier attempts to reduce latency via direct Cartesian control of selected links often excluded the feet, limiting comprehensive whole-body control for locomotion and dynamic tasks.

## Method

- The **ExtremControl** framework uses direct SE(3) pose control for selected humanoid extremities (hands, feet, pelvis, torso) combined with an efficient Cartesian-space mapping for human-to-humanoid motion conversion.
- It integrates a velocity feedforward term into the low-level control, extending standard PD control to anticipate and react to target velocities, thereby reducing tracking delay.
- A three-stage learning strategy involving PPO for teacher policy training, DAgger for distillation, and PPO fine-tuning for the teleoperation policy is employed to achieve robust control.

## Results

- ExtremControl achieved end-to-end teleoperation latencies of 54 ± 4 ms with MoCap and 64 ± 8 ms with VR, representing a reduction of approximately 100 ms compared to the 170-230 ms typical in existing systems.
- The velocity feedforward mechanism effectively reduced low-level tracking delays, with empirical measurements closely matching theoretical predictions and showing optimal performance at η ∈ {**0.8**, **0.9**}.
- The significantly reduced latency allowed the Unitree G1 robot to successfully execute highly responsive loco-manipulation tasks such as dynamic balancing, juggling, and real-time object catching in both simulation and real-world environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low-level tracking delay is significantly reduced by incorporating velocity feedforward, with a theoretical model predicting delay reduction and identifying an upper bound for the feedforward ratio (η) to prevent discrete-time overshoot.
> - An efficient Cartesian-space mapping for key extremities and global body links is substantially faster (0.29 ms) than traditional joint-space inverse kinematics retargeting (2.69-13.1 ms), critically lowering latency.
> - A three-stage policy learning process (RL teacher, DAgger student, RL fine-tuning) with a single-frame future observation horizon for the student policy optimizes for both robustness and responsiveness in teleoperation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots present a compelling platform for general-purpose robotic systems in human-centric environments due to their human-like morphology. This enables the potential exploitation of large-scale human motion and skill datasets. However, their inherent complexity—characterized by high-dimensional state and action spaces, underactuated floating-base dynamics, intermittent contacts, and frequent hybrid mode transitions—poses challenges for traditional control methods.

The field has seen a shift towards reinforcement learning (RL) for humanoid locomotion and whole-body control, particularly with advancements in large-scale parallel simulation. Early RL approaches utilized Cartesian-space objectives or command-based interfaces. Subsequently, methods evolved to incorporate dense joint-space pose supervision derived from human models, allowing for the reproduction of expressive and dynamic human motions. More recent efforts have focused on optimizing selected reference trajectories for high-fidelity motion reproduction, sometimes at the expense of generalization.

Teleoperation is recognized as a crucial mechanism for collecting data to train general-purpose robotic intelligence. Consequently, a significant portion of humanoid control literature focuses on teleoperation systems. A consistent observation across existing real-time humanoid teleoperation systems is an end-to-end latency of approximately 200 ms. This latency, largely independent of the robot or retargeting strategy, significantly hinders the operator's ability to perform responsive tasks requiring rapid feedback. Prior methods often rely on heavily pre-processed human-to-humanoid motion retargeting and position-only Proportional-Derivative (PD) control, which contribute to this delay. While some earlier work attempted to reduce latency by directly controlling Cartesian positions of selected robot links, these often excluded foot links, thereby limiting comprehensive whole-body control capabilities.

ExtremControl positions itself in this landscape by directly addressing the latency bottleneck. It aims to surpass the established 200 ms latency limit by moving beyond position-only PD control and full-body retargeting, introducing a velocity feedforward term and a direct Cartesian-space mapping for robot extremities. This approach seeks to unlock faster, more responsive humanoid behaviors, thereby enhancing data collection efficiency and robot capabilities in dynamic, human-centric tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the necessity of building low-latency humanoid teleoperation systems. Such systems are considered essential for effectively collecting diverse reactive and dynamic demonstrations, which are critical for advancing general-purpose robotic intelligence. Existing teleoperation systems, however, are identified as having a substantial limitation: end-to-end latency often around 200 ms. This delay is primarily attributed to two factors:
1. **Heavy reliance on pre-processed human-to-humanoid motion retargeting**: This process introduces computational overhead and sequential dependencies, leading to delays.
2. **Use of position-only PD control**: This control paradigm inherently introduces tracking delays, particularly for dynamic and rapidly changing target motions, as it lacks a mechanism to anticipate and respond to target velocities.

The cumulative effect of these limitations is a severe restriction on the responsiveness of teleoperated humanoids, preventing them from performing tasks that demand rapid feedback and fast reactions, such as dynamic balancing, juggling, or real-time object manipulation.

To address these identified problems, the key objectives of the ExtremControl framework are detailed as follows:
1. **Direct Operation on SE(3) Poses of Selected Rigid Links**: The system is designed to operate directly on the SE(3) (Special Euclidean group, representing 3D position and orientation) poses of a minimal, yet expressive, set of rigid links, primarily the humanoid's extremities (hands and feet), along with the pelvis and torso. This approach aims to bypass the latency associated with full-body joint-space retargeting by providing direct targets for the robot's key contact points and global pose.
2. **Cartesian-Space Mapping for Human-to-Humanoid Motion Conversion**: Implement a Cartesian-space mapping mechanism that directly converts tracked human motion (from VR or motion capture) into the SE(3) targets for the selected humanoid links. This mapping is designed to be efficient, feedforward, and parallelizable, further minimizing computational delays.
3. **Incorporation of Velocity Feedforward Control**: Integrate a velocity feedforward term into the low-level control loop. This aims to significantly improve the robot's responsiveness by allowing it to anticipate and react to target velocity changes, thereby reducing tracking delay inherent in position-only PD controllers, especially under rapidly changing control interfaces.
4. **Unified Theoretical Formulation and Validation**: Provide a comprehensive theoretical formulation for ExtremControl, encompassing robot kinematics, dynamics, and policy learning. This framework is to be systematically validated through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world environments to confirm its effectiveness and optimality.
5. **Development of a Low-Latency Teleoperation System**: Based on the ExtremControl framework, construct a practical teleoperation system that supports both optical motion capture and VR-based motion tracking. The ultimate goal is to achieve end-to-end latencies as low as 50 ms, thereby enabling highly responsive behaviors that significantly surpass the performance of prior work.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ExtremControl framework is designed to minimize teleoperation latency through specific kinematic, dynamic, and policy learning methodologies.

**Kinematics (Control Interface)**
The system avoids full-body human-to-humanoid retargeting within the policy loop by formulating the control interface in terms of rigid link poses.
- **Tracking Objectives**: Six humanoid links are selected as control targets: both hands, both feet, the pelvis, and the torso. These are chosen for their expressiveness in conveying operator intent for manipulation, locomotion, and global posture, while being minimal to avoid over-constraining the system. The Unitree G1 robot is utilized, having 7 degrees of freedom (DoFs) per arm and 6 per leg.
- **Teleoperation Input Modalities**: The system supports two input methods:
 - **Optical Motion Capture (MoCap)**: Uses an OptiTrack system to obtain global position and rotation of human body links at 120 Hz, with a non-blocking streaming pipeline minimizing transmission latency.
 - **VR System**: Employs a Meta Quest 2 headset and VIVE Ultimate Trackers for hands, waist, and feet. Torso orientation is estimated from the headset's planar motion relative to the pelvis.
- **Cartesian-Space Mapping (M)**: A feedforward, parallelizable operator maps 6 tracked human link poses (T_h) to corresponding humanoid link targets (T_r).
 - **One-shot Calibration**: Performed once at initialization, with the human in a neutral pose. This step estimates per-link rotational offsets and anthropomorphic measurements (pelvis height, shoulder position in pelvis frame, arm length) to compensate for body proportion differences between human and robot. Fixed foot offsets are recorded for stable standing.
 - **Per-frame Mapping**: At runtime, pelvis and feet positions are scaled based on the ratio of humanoid to human pelvis heights. The torso's position is rigidly attached to the pelvis following the humanoid's kinematic structure, while its orientation is directly mapped. Hands are mapped by aligning shoulder positions relative to the pelvis and scaling arm lengths. This method involves only rigid transformations and closed-form calculations, aiming for negligible computational overhead.

**Dynamics (Low-Level Control)**
To overcome the tracking delay of position-only PD control, a velocity feedforward control framework is formulated.
- **Whole-Body Impedance Calibration**: Joint-level PD gains are calibrated using a sequential, simulation-based procedure. This estimates the effective joint impedance (M_eff) by applying small perturbations and measuring oscillation periods. The derived M_eff is then used to set proportional (k_p) and derivative (k_d) gains based on target natural frequency (ω_n) and damping ratio (ζ=1), ensuring dynamically consistent gains.
- **Velocity Feedforward Control**: The standard position-only PD control (τ = k_p(q_t - q) - k_d ˙q) is extended with a velocity feedforward term: τ = k_p(q_t - q) - k_d ˙q + ηk_d ˙q_t. Here, η is the velocity feedforward ratio and ˙q_t is the target joint velocity. Theoretical analysis models the closed-loop system response to sinusoidal inputs, demonstrating that the tracking delay (ℓ) is approximately 2(1 - η)/ω_n in the low-frequency regime.
- **Discrete-Time Compensation**: The effects of discrete-time execution (fixed control period ∆t) are analyzed. An upper bound for the velocity feedforward ratio (η ≤ 1 - ω_n∆t/4) is derived to prevent overshoot caused by acceleration within a single control interval. In real-world setup, η is set to 0.9 for direct-drive joints.

**Policy Learning**
A three-stage learning framework is adopted using reinforcement learning (PPO) and behavior cloning (DAgger).
- **Observation**: The policy's observation vector includes a future interpretation function for reference motion (o_ref) over a finite horizon, local pose discrepancies (o_diff) of extremities in the pelvis frame, and proprioceptive observations (o_proprio). The oracle teacher policy uses a longer future horizon (32 frames) and additional privileged observations (e.g., global pose discrepancies, inverse kinematics joint configurations, contact forces). The deployable student and teleoperation policies use a single future frame (l=1) to maximize responsiveness.
- **Reward Function**: Comprises tracking rewards (penalizing discrepancies in global/local link poses and retargeted joint configurations) and regularization rewards (constraining joint torques, suppressing torso oscillations). All rewards are negative-valued, and their exponential weighted sum ensures bounded total reward and preserves gradients.
- **Learning Strategy**:
 1. **Teacher Policy (π_teacher) Training**: Trained using PPO with privileged observations and high-difficulty motion data (LAFAN1 dataset) to handle dynamic motions robustly.
 2. **Student Policy (π_student) Distillation**: The teacher policy is distilled into a student policy via DAgger. This involves removing observations unavailable in real-world deployment and reducing the future horizon to a single frame. The critic network is not trained during this stage.
 3. **Teleoperation Policy (π_teleop) Fine-tuning**: The distilled policy is fine-tuned using PPO on a large and diverse motion dataset (LAFAN1 ∪ AMASS). The actor network is initially frozen for a period to allow the critic to converge, improving exploration stability.

**Experiment Setup**
- **Simulation**: Genesis physics engine (sim dt = 1/200 s), 8,192 parallel environments, >100k policy steps/second.
- **Real-World Robot**: Unitree G1. PD gains (ω_n = 10 rad/s, ζ = 1), velocity feedforward η = 0.9 (disabled for indirect-drive joints). Low-level PD at 1000 Hz, high-level policy at 50 Hz.
- **Motion Data**: LAFAN1 for teacher/student learning, LAFAN1 ∪ AMASS for RL fine-tuning. A separate S'_teleop dataset is used for evaluation.
- **Latency Estimation**: A video-based optical flow analysis method is used to measure end-to-end latency by aligning projected optical flow signals from human and robot motions in recorded videos.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments systematically validated the effectiveness of ExtremControl in both simulation and real-world environments.

**Whole-Body Impedance Calibration**
The calibration procedure, evaluated on the Unitree G1 elbow joint, demonstrated consistent convergence to stable effective impedance values across different target natural frequencies (ω_n), despite variations in initial proportional gains. This indicates the method's robustness to initial conditions and its ability to reliably estimate effective mass (M_eff) under whole-body coupling, which is critical for setting dynamically consistent PD gains.

**Velocity Feedforward Control**
The theoretical predictions for low-level tracking delay (ℓ ≈ 2(1 - η)/ω_n) closely matched measured delays in both simulation and on hardware for target natural frequencies of 10 and 15 rad/s. For ω_n = 5 rad/s, minor deviations were observed, attributed to the violation of the ω ≪ ω_n assumption. The studies revealed that at higher velocity feedforward ratios (e.g., η=1.0) and a control update frequency of 50 Hz, the measured delay increased due to discrete-time overshoot, consistent with theoretical predictions of exceeding the upper bound for η. The overall results supported the benefit of velocity feedforward in reducing tracking delay.

**Policy Learning Ablations**
- **Velocity Feedforward Ratio (η)**: Policy performance, as measured by global and local tracking errors, remained stable across a wide range of η (0.0 to 0.9). However, setting η = 1.0 resulted in noticeable performance degradation and overshooting, corroborating the theoretical upper bound. The optimal range of η ∈ {0.8, 0.9} was identified as a favorable trade-off between tracking accuracy (errors within a few millimeters and 0.02 radians) and minimal latency (approximately 5 ms).
- **Observed Future Length (l)**: For the teacher policy, an observation horizon of 32 future frames was found to be a practical optimum. Increasing the horizon beyond this point led to degraded performance after distillation to the student policy, suggesting a capacity mismatch or difficulty in transferring overly complex motion priors to a student with limited observational capabilities.
- **Learning Strategy**: The proposed RL+BC+RL paradigm demonstrated superior overall performance on unseen teleoperation trajectories (S'_teleop) compared to pure RL or RL+BC strategies. RL+BC trained on dynamic motions (S_lafan) achieved the best performance on dynamic tasks (S'_lafan). Incorporating in-domain motion data (S_teleop) consistently improved tracking performance. The study also found that eliminating direct observation of retargeted joint configurations (q_t) introduced only marginal tracking errors (7 mm in position, 0.02 rad in orientation), which was considered an acceptable trade-off for the associated 10 ms reduction in latency.
- **Retargeting Time Cost (Appendix A)**: The Cartesian-space mapping employed in ExtremControl averaged 0.29 ms per frame on an Apple M4 processor. This was significantly faster than various joint-space inverse kinematics (IK) retargeting strategies, which ranged from 2.69 ms (raw IK) to 13.1 ms (parallelized IK) on the same hardware, highlighting the latency reduction achieved by avoiding joint-space optimization within the control loop.

**Latency Analysis (End-to-End)**
The optical-flow-based video analysis method for end-to-end latency estimation was validated against motion-capture ground truth, demonstrating accuracy (e.g., 55 ms and 60 ms from two different camera views versus 51 ms from MoCap).
- **Comparison with Prior Work**: Existing real-time humanoid teleoperation systems showed typical latencies exceeding 170 ms (e.g., OmniH2O at ~185 ms, CLONE at ~178 ms, AMS at ~201 ms, TWIST2 at ~234 ms). In contrast, ExtremControl achieved significantly lower end-to-end latencies: **54 ± 4 ms** with the MoCap-based setup and **64 ± 8 ms** with the VR-based setup. This represents a substantial improvement, surpassing the previously observed latency barrier by approximately 100 ms.
- **Impact of Velocity Feedforward**: A linear regression indicated that low-level control delay (ℓ_control) did not translate directly one-to-one into overall system latency (ℓ_overall = 0.58 · ℓ_control + 32 ms). The remaining 32 ms offset was attributed to communication delays from the VR system, Cartesian-space mapping, policy inference, and the finite control target update rate.

The system's low latency enabled the Unitree G1 robot to perform highly responsive loco-manipulation tasks such as balancing, juggling, and real-time returning of a ping-pong ball, as well as catching a frisbee while moving.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of ExtremControl marks a notable advancement in humanoid teleoperation by fundamentally addressing the critical issue of system latency. The achievement of end-to-end latencies as low as 50 ms significantly surpasses the approximately 200 ms latency observed in prior work. This reduction is a direct outcome of the proposed methodological innovations: operating directly on SE(3) poses of humanoid extremities, utilizing an efficient Cartesian-space mapping, and incorporating velocity feedforward control into the low-level dynamics.

The immediate significance of this work lies in its ability to **unlock highly responsive humanoid behaviors**. Tasks requiring rapid feedback and fast reactions, such as dynamic object manipulation (e.g., ping-pong ball juggling, catching thrown objects), which were previously challenging or unfeasible due to inherent system delays, can now be executed with greater fluidity and precision. This effectively leverages the inherent capabilities of advanced humanoid hardware that may have been underutilized due to non-optimized system designs.

The enhanced responsiveness directly impacts **teleoperation-based data collection**. By enabling more fluid human-in-the-loop manipulation, ExtremControl facilitates the acquisition of higher-quality, more dynamic, and reactive human demonstrations. This is crucial for training general-purpose robotic intelligence, as large-scale, diverse, and responsive datasets can accelerate the development of more capable and adaptable humanoid control policies. The framework's support for both optical motion capture and VR systems further broadens accessibility and flexibility for data collection scenarios.

The unified theoretical formulation, systematic validation in both simulation and real-world, and the structured three-stage policy learning framework contribute to the **robustness and reproducibility** of complex humanoid control. By open-sourcing their code, the authors also contribute to the broader research community, enabling further development, comparative studies, and applications of low-latency teleoperation.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge several limitations that highlight avenues for future research. These include kinematic ambiguities in high-DoF arms leading to unnatural poses, the complexity of policy-level control for lower-body responsiveness (e.g., distinguishing intent for walking vs. lifting a foot), and the challenges of extending the system to articulated or dexterous hands, which would introduce additional latencies. Addressing these limitations will be instrumental in moving towards the long-term goal of a near-human, low-latency humanoid data collection platform essential for developing general-purpose robotic intelligence.
