# Recursive Harness Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> Recursive Harness Self-Improvement (RHI) treats a coding agent's harness - agent roles, instructions, inter-agent communication contracts, and workflow hops - as a prompt-level textual specification, then refines it iteratively using pairwise preference feedback that compares each revision's output repository against its immediate predecessor's. On 30 synthetic ML research tasks, a few iterations let low-reasoning-effort agents beat their maximum-reasoning-effort counterparts while cutting inference cost by up to 60%.

## Problem

- Provider-built harnesses must generalize across all users and tasks, so continually updating them is labor-intensive and costly, leaving task-specific execution-trace quality unimproved.
- Population-based harness, workflow, and program search requires a fresh black-box agent execution plus evaluation for every candidate, a prohibitive cost when specializing agents per task.
- Test-time scaling raises reasoning effort uniformly and plateaus, prompt optimizers target single instructions rather than multi-agent coordination, and open-ended repository outputs admit no scalar verifiable reward.

## Method

- ==Harness-as-prompt decomposition== into ==agent design== (roles, instructions) and ==agent workflow== (==contracts==, which fix what information passes between agents, and ==hops==, which fix orchestrator-subagent control flow).
- ==Trajectory-local objective== replaces population search with comparison against only the immediately previous harness, needing one agent execution and one pairwise judgment per iteration, giving constant per-iteration cost.
- An LLM ==harness optimizer== rewrites the harness from the accumulated ==self-comparison preference history==; the evaluation prompt is withheld from it, and a ==task improvement-rate threshold== stops the loop.

## Results

- After two iterations, sonnet-4.6 at high effort wins **20** of **30** pairwise comparisons against sonnet-4.6-max; one iteration lets opus-4.7 surpass xhigh and max, two let opus-4.8 beat ultracode.
- For opus-4.8, normalized cost falls **23%** below max (**1.69** versus **2.19**) and **60%** below ultracode (versus **4.15**), with cache read/write usage **32%** and **64%** lower respectively.
- Output-token usage stays roughly flat (**1.7** to **1.9** normalized for sonnet-4.6) as wins rise; contract and hop task mutual information climbs monotonically (hop **1.96** to **2.54** nats) while task-conditional total correlation drops **4.84** to **3.63**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Restructuring how a fixed model routes context between agents can beat raising reasoning effort, and cost tracks cache read/write footprint rather than generation length.
> - Built-in provider multi-agent modes scored below single-agent defaults at higher cost: explicit task-specific coordination structure, not merely spawning subagents, produces the gains.
> - Gains complement rather than replace stronger base models, and the information-theoretic account rests on correlational embedding proxies, not proof of the optimizer's latent objective.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Current advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) scaling are frequently attributed to developments in model size, training computation, and post-training techniques. However, the paper notes an increasing trend where improvements in deployed AI systems emerge from the co-evolution of foundation models and their accompanying harnesses. This paradigm suggests that harnesses, beyond being inference-time scaffolds, function as data-generating components whose operational traces can inform the training of subsequent foundation models. This concept introduces a recursive feedback loop: more effective harnesses can organize agent workflows that generate higher-quality execution traces, which, in turn, can serve as training data for future models.

Existing approaches to improving AI systems through system-level modifications often involve population-based search methods that explore different prompts, workflow graphs, tool-use policies, or executable code harnesses. Examples include methods that synthesize and refine code harnesses from execution feedback, or those that evolve populations of parallel harness branches. While these methods aim to optimize system performance, they frequently incur substantial computational costs due to the need for multiple agent executions and evaluations for each candidate harness. This makes them less practical for users who need to continually specialize agents to numerous open-ended tasks under limited per-task budgets.

This work positions itself within this evolving landscape by focusing on the first half of the model–harness co-evolutionary loop: the optimization of user-constructed harnesses for a fixed foundation model to generate higher-quality execution traces. It specifically addresses the bottleneck of costly and labor-intensive continuous updates to provider-built harnesses by exploring lightweight, task-specific optimization of user-constructed harnesses. The research aims to develop an optimization method that is computationally efficient and converges rapidly, distinguishing itself from existing population-based search techniques that are often expensive and slow.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the emerging paradigm of "model–harness co-evolution." In this paradigm, harnesses are not merely components that facilitate inference but are active elements that generate data, specifically "execution traces." These traces are recognized as critical for shaping the development and training of future foundation models. The paper identifies a key bottleneck in this co-evolutionary loop: the quality of execution traces produced by the harnesses.

Improving harnesses, however, presents practical challenges. Provider-built harnesses are designed for general-purpose application across diverse users and tasks, making their continuous updating costly and labor-intensive. In contrast, user-constructed harnesses offer an opportunity for task-specific optimization, potentially leading to higher-quality execution traces more efficiently. The researchers are motivated by the need for an optimization process for user-constructed harnesses that is computationally lightweight, requiring only a few update iterations, to enable users to adapt agents to many open-ended tasks within practical budgetary constraints.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work are:
1. **To investigate the feasibility of lightweight, iterative optimization of user-constructed harnesses:** Specifically, to determine if a few iterations of such optimization can significantly improve the quality of execution traces and consequently elevate the performance ceiling of agent test-time scaling.
2. **To introduce Recursive Harness Self-Improvement (RHI):** RHI is proposed as a method to achieve this lightweight and iterative optimization. It defines the harness as a prompt-level specification of the agent loop, encompassing agent roles, instructions, inter-agent information exchange (contracts), and workflow structure (hops).
3. **To demonstrate RHI's effectiveness:** The objective is to show that RHI can iteratively refine these prompt-level harness specifications using pairwise feedback from its own revision history, leading to substantial performance gains for low-reasoning-effort agents while simultaneously reducing inference costs.
4. **To analyze the mechanisms underlying RHI's performance gains:** Beyond demonstrating performance, the work aims to understand if these gains are attributable to factors such as increased output token usage or, alternatively, to more efficient context management through improved inter-agent information flow.
5. **To formalize RHI's implicit optimization objective:** Based on observed behaviors, the research seeks to propose an information-theoretic hypothesis for RHI's implicit optimization objective, suggesting it as a framework for continual learning within the model–harness co-evolution paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **Recursive Harness Self-Improvement (RHI)**, an iterative algorithm designed to optimize user-constructed harnesses.

#### 3.1. RHI Algorithm and Objective Function

RHI operates through an iterative process (Algorithm 1, Figure 2). At each iteration *i*:
1. A coding agent (A) utilizes the current harness (H⁽ⁱ⁾) to process a given task (x) and produce an output repository (y⁽ⁱ⁾).
2. An LLM evaluator (L_eval) compares the current output (y⁽ⁱ⁾) with the output from the immediately preceding iteration (y⁽ⁱ⁻¹⁾) using pairwise judgments, generating preference feedback (P.F.[*i*]).
3. This feedback is stored in a cumulative self-comparison history (D⁽ⁱ⁾ₓ).
4. An LLM harness optimizer (L_harness) receives the current harness (H⁽ⁱ⁾) and the accumulated history (D⁽ⁱ⁾ₓ) to generate the next harness revision (H⁽ⁱ⁺¹⁾ₓ). The evaluation prompt (x_eval) is used by L_eval to generate feedback but is not directly provided to L_harness.

This approach adopts a trajectory-local objective, comparing the current harness against its immediate predecessor (Equation 3). This is computationally lightweight compared to population-based search methods (Table 1), requiring only one new agent execution and one pairwise evaluation per iteration. The paper asserts that this noisy local ascent signal is sufficient for optimization, as maximizing the trajectory-local objective targets the same latent utility ordering as a broader population objective under standard pairwise preference models. The accumulation of preference feedback in D⁽ⁱ⁾ₓ is intended to enhance the reliability of harness optimization under a limited update budget.

#### 3.2. RHI Harness Definition

The harness is conceptualized as the agent loop itself and is represented as a prompt-level object, rather than executable code. It is decomposed into two primary components (Figure 3):
- **Agent Design:** Specifies the roles and instructions for candidate agents.
- **Agent Workflow:** Further broken down into:
 - **Contracts:** Define the information exchanged between subagents and the orchestrator.
 - **Hops:** Specify the interaction structure and overall workflow logic, including orchestrator-subagent workflow steps.
RHI prioritizes the optimization of agent workflow components (contracts and hops) through the L_harness prompt. The hypothesis is that task-specific contracts and hops improve performance and inference efficiency by enabling more effective context management, reducing redundant context propagation, and enhancing KV-cache efficiency. Auxiliary rules, such as acceptance gates and failure fallbacks, are also induced during RHI iterations.

#### 3.3. Benchmark and Evaluation

- **Tasks:** RHI is evaluated on 30 synthetic, open-ended machine learning research tasks across quantitative finance, robotics, and pharmaceutical ML (10 tasks per domain). These tasks, generated from industry job postings, require agents to produce a complete code repository with standardized deliverables (e.g., research report, plots, metrics JSON).
- **Evaluation Protocol:** A pairwise LLM-as-a-judge protocol is used, where an LLM (L_eval) compares two generated repositories based on multiple criteria: deliverable coverage, numerical and empirical rigor, reproducibility, presentation, engineering quality, and task alignment. Two LLM judges (GPT-5.5-max, Opus-4.7/4.8-xhigh) and three random seeds are used to assess robustness.
- **Experiment Settings:** Coding agents are instantiated with three base language models: Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.7, and Claude Opus 4.8, all initially set to 'high' reasoning effort. RHI-improved agents (e.g., A_L_b-high(H⁽ⁱ⁾ₓ, x)) are compared against corresponding test-time scaling baselines (e.g., A_L_b-xhigh/ultracode/max(x)) without RHI-generated harnesses.
- **Metrics:** Performance is measured by pairwise win/loss/tie counts. Resource usage is quantified by normalized cost, output token count, and cache read/write usage, all normalized against the respective base agent.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Recursive Harness Self-Improvement (RHI) yields several findings regarding its performance, cost efficiency, and underlying mechanisms.

#### 4.1. Performance Improvement: Few-Shot RHI Lifts Performance Ceiling

RHI demonstrates the ability to elevate the performance ceiling of test-time scaling baselines (Figures 5a, 6a-b, 7a-d).
- For Sonnet-4.6, two RHI iterations (sonnet-4.6-high+H) surpass the sonnet-4.6-max baseline, winning 20 out of 30 pairwise comparisons. Subsequent iterations continue to outperform the baseline.
- For Opus-4.7, a single RHI iteration (opus-4.7-high+H) is sufficient to outperform both opus-4.7-xhigh and opus-4.7-max baselines, despite opus-4.7-high+H initially underperforming.
- For Opus-4.8, opus-4.8-high+H performs comparably to opus-4.8-xhigh. After two RHI iterations, opus-4.8-high+H outperforms all test-time scaling baselines, including opus-4.8-xhigh, opus-4.8-ultracode, and opus-4.8-max.

A notable result is that opus-4.8-high+H outperforms opus-4.8-ultracode. The latter utilizes 'xhigh' reasoning and a built-in dynamic workflow capable of spawning multiple subagents. This outcome suggests that a task-specific, user-constructed harness provided at the prompt level can be more effective than a fixed, system-level provider-built harness, particularly on the evaluated benchmark.

#### 4.2. Performance Gains Not Primarily Due to Increased Output-Token Usage

The performance gains achieved by RHI are not consistently explained by an increase in output token usage, which distinguishes RHI from typical test-time scaling methods (Figures 5b, 7e).
- For Sonnet-4.6, performance improves across RHI iterations while the normalized output token usage remains approximately constant (1.71 to 1.86).
- Similarly, for Opus-4.8, performance increases with RHI iterations, whereas normalized output token usage remains largely flat (1.42 to 1.81).
- For Opus-4.7, the evidence is inconclusive, as both performance and output token usage increase across the limited two iterations analyzed.
This suggests that for two of the three models, RHI's improvements are not primarily driven by longer generated outputs.

#### 4.3. Improved Cost Efficiency Through Reduced Cache Read/Write Usage

RHI consistently improves cost efficiency across all tested base models, primarily by reducing cache read/write usage (Figures 5b, 6c, 7e).
- For Sonnet-4.6, opus-4.6-high+H shows a 7% lower normalized cost and a 33% reduction in cache read/write usage compared to sonnet-4.6-max.
- For Opus-4.7, opus-4.7-high+H demonstrates an 18% lower normalized cost and a 37% reduction in cache read/write usage compared to opus-4.7-max.
- For Opus-4.8, opus-4.8-high+H achieves a 23% lower normalized cost relative to opus-4.8-max and a 60% reduction relative to opus-4.8-ultracode. The corresponding cache read/write usage decreases by 32% and 64%, respectively.

These cost savings are attributed to RHI's ability to learn task-specific communication contracts and workflow hops, which facilitate more efficient context management. By restricting information flow to task-relevant data, RHI reduces redundant context propagation, leading to better KV-cache efficiency and lower inference costs, analogous to sparse attention mechanisms.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Complementary to Train-Time Scaling:** RHI improves the performance of a fixed model and raises the ceiling of same-family test-time scaling, but it does not consistently close the performance gap with stronger base models (Figure 8). This suggests RHI is complementary to, rather than a replacement for, train-time scaling.
- **Harness Factors Driving Gains:**
 - **Full-Harness Evolution:** Analysis of full harness embeddings shows a clear semantic separation between the initial harness (H) and RHI-improved harnesses (H), indicating significant adaptation. Consecutive harness similarity suggests the largest semantic change occurs in the first RHI update, with subsequent changes being more incremental (Figure 9c). Cumulative similarity decreases from H, showing divergence, while within-domain across-task similarity increases after the first iteration, indicating common domain-level structure.
 - **Component-Level Evolution:** Decomposing the harness into roles, instructions, contracts, and hops reveals that contracts exhibit the clearest task-dependent clustering and stabilize earliest (Figures 10, 11a). Hops and instructions also show task-dependent clustering, though less distinctly. Roles remain less separable. This points to task-specific coordination, particularly through communication contracts, as a primary driver of RHI's performance.
- **Implicit Optimization Objective:** The paper proposes an information-theoretic hypothesis for RHI's implicit objective (Equation 6), suggesting it increases task-relevant information in externally emphasized components (*f_ext*) while reducing task-conditional redundancy among all components (*f_int*).
 - **Evidence for increasing *f_ext***: Mutual information between contracts and hops (emphasized components) and the task increases monotonically across RHI iterations (Table 2).
 - **Evidence for decreasing *f_int***: Task-conditional total correlation among harness components decreases monotonically, indicating reduced redundancy and functional specialization (Table 3).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and advancement of AI systems by addressing a critical aspect of "model–harness co-evolution." The introduction of Recursive Harness Self-Improvement (RHI) offers a practical and computationally lightweight approach to optimizing user-constructed harnesses, which can generate higher-quality execution traces. These traces are posited as foundational data for training future foundation models, thereby accelerating the "data flywheel" in AI development.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
1. **Practical Framework for Users:** RHI provides a framework that allows users to continually specialize coding agents for diverse, open-ended tasks. This capability is particularly relevant in scenarios where provider-built general-purpose harnesses may not offer optimal performance or cost efficiency for specific applications.
2. **Performance and Cost Efficiency Gains:** The demonstration that RHI can raise the performance ceiling of low-reasoning-effort agents, even exceeding stronger test-time scaling baselines, while simultaneously reducing inference costs by up to 60%, suggests a viable alternative to simply increasing computational resources. This is particularly impactful as scaling compute on already powerful models often yields diminishing returns.
3. **Insights into Agentic System Design:** The findings underscore that performance gains are not solely from increased output length or raw computational power, but primarily from improved task-specific context management and effective inter-agent information flow. The emphasis on optimizing communication contracts and workflow hops highlights the importance of thoughtful multi-agent coordination design at the prompt level.
4. **Information-Theoretic Understanding of Optimization:** By proposing an information-theoretic hypothesis for RHI's implicit objective, the research provides a testable framework for understanding how harness updates lead to improved performance. This could guide the development of more principled and efficient harness optimization methods in the future.
5. **Advancement of Model–Harness Co-evolution:** This work primarily focuses on optimizing the harness for a *fixed* foundation model. The explicit articulation of the "second half of the loop"—how the generated high-quality execution traces can be effectively internalized into future foundation models—lays the groundwork for future research. Successfully closing this loop could lead to a self-improving AI ecosystem where models and their operational scaffolding recursively enhance each other.

In summary, RHI represents a step toward more adaptable and efficient agentic AI systems, particularly through its focus on localized, cost-effective optimization of prompt-level harnesses, and its emphasis on structured information flow in multi-agent environments.
