# Hyperbolic Vision Transformers: Combining Improvements in Metric Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces Hyperbolic Vision Transformers (HVT), a deep metric learning framework that combines Vision Transformers with hyperbolic space embeddings, optimized using a pairwise cross-entropy loss. The approach achieved state-of-the-art results on several image retrieval benchmarks, with hyperbolic embeddings consistently demonstrating improved performance over spherical counterparts.

## Problem

- Traditional metric learning methods often embed visual data into Euclidean space, potentially distorting inherent hierarchical relationships present in complex image datasets.
- Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), while effective, may not extract features that generalize as well as newer architectures for fine-grained image retrieval tasks.
- There is a need to investigate the synergistic benefits of integrating advanced encoder architectures, geometrically appropriate embedding spaces, and robust loss functions for enhanced metric learning performance.

## Method

- The method projects output embeddings into the ==Poincaré ball model== of hyperbolic space, leveraging its exponential volume growth to represent hierarchical data structures more effectively.
- Vision Transformers (**ViT-S**) are utilized as the encoder backbone, with various pretraining schemes (e.g., ImageNet-21k, DeiT-S, DINO) explored to generate discriminative image features.
- A modified ==pairwise cross-entropy loss== directly incorporates ==hyperbolic distances== to optimize embeddings, encouraging semantically similar items to cluster and dissimilar items to separate in the hyperbolic manifold.

## Results

- The proposed HVT method achieved new state-of-the-art results on multiple image retrieval datasets, including a **9%** improvement in Recall@1 on CUB-200-2011 (Hyp-ViT) over the previous best method IRTR_RR.
- Hyperbolic space embeddings consistently demonstrated improved performance over spherical (Euclidean-normalized) embeddings across all experiments, underscoring the benefits of hyperbolic geometry.
- The method showed robustness to the manifold curvature parameter 'c' within the range of **(0.01, 0.3)** and maintained effective performance with batch sizes greater than or equal to **400**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hyperbolic embeddings consistently outperform spherical embeddings across image retrieval benchmarks, indicating that visual data possesses intrinsic hierarchical structures better captured by hyperbolic geometry.
> - Vision Transformers, especially when effectively pretrained (e.g., on large-scale supervised datasets or via self-supervised DINO), prove to be superior feature extractors for metric learning compared to many CNN-based alternatives.
> - The combined application of Vision Transformers, hyperbolic embeddings, and pairwise cross-entropy loss creates a synergistic effect, and utilizing smaller patch sizes (8x8) for the ViT encoder yields substantial further performance improvements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Metric learning is a foundational task in machine learning, aiming to learn data embeddings where distances between representations reflect semantic similarity. This principle underpins numerous computer vision applications, including content-based image retrieval, near-duplicate detection, face recognition, person re-identification, and various forms of few-shot and zero-shot learning. Modern approaches to image retrieval typically consist of two primary components: an encoder responsible for transforming raw image data into compact representations (embeddings), and a loss function that guides the training process by optimizing the distances between these embeddings.

Historically, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have served as the dominant backbone for encoders in computer vision. More recently, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a competitive alternative. While ViTs may initially require more data to generalize effectively due to a lack of certain inductive biases inherent in CNNs (e.g., translation equivariance), they are capable of producing more general features. This property is considered advantageous for tasks like image retrieval, which often necessitate generalization to novel or unseen classes of images. Strategies to mitigate the data requirements of ViTs include training on large datasets, employing extensive data augmentation, utilizing distillation techniques, or applying self-supervised learning paradigms.

The choice of embedding space is another critical factor, as it dictates the metrics used for comparing representations. The Euclidean space is commonly used, often with embeddings normalized to a hypersphere, leading to the use of cosine similarity as a distance measure. However, there has been increasing interest in hyperbolic spaces for learning data embeddings. Hyperbolic geometry is distinguished by its exponential volume growth with respect to radius, contrasting with the polynomial growth observed in Euclidean spaces. This characteristic makes hyperbolic spaces particularly well-suited for embedding data with intrinsic hierarchical or tree-like structures, as shown by embedding theorems for trees and complex networks. Many natural datasets are considered to possess such hierarchical properties, suggesting that hyperbolic embeddings could offer increased representational power in lower dimensions compared to their Euclidean counterparts.

Loss functions in metric learning are designed to cluster similar objects while separating dissimilar ones in the embedding space. These functions can broadly be categorized into proxy-based losses, which train auxiliary representatives for data subsets, and pair-based losses, which operate directly on individual sample embeddings. Pair-based losses are often preferred for their ability to capture fine-grained interactions between samples and their flexibility to operate without explicit class labels in scenarios like self-supervised learning, where supervision is derived from pretext tasks that define positive and negative pairs. The pairwise cross-entropy loss has demonstrated efficacy in these contexts.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these advancements, aiming to investigate the synergistic benefits of combining Vision Transformers as powerful encoders, hyperbolic spaces for embedding hierarchical data, and robust pairwise cross-entropy loss functions. The work contributes to the ongoing exploration of more effective and geometrically appropriate representation learning techniques for complex visual data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose and evaluate a novel hyperbolic-based model for metric learning. This model integrates recent advancements in neural network architectures and embedding spaces to achieve improved performance in image retrieval tasks.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Leveraging Hyperbolic Geometry:** The central objective is to project output embeddings into hyperbolic space, specifically the Poincaré ball model, and to utilize hyperbolic distances directly within the loss function. The motivation for this stems from the inherent properties of hyperbolic spaces. Natural data, particularly visual data used in metric learning, often exhibits an underlying hierarchical structure. Hyperbolic spaces are characterized by their exponential volume growth, which makes them highly efficient for representing such hierarchies with low distortion, even in low-dimensional manifolds. This contrasts with Euclidean spaces, where volume grows polynomially, potentially limiting their ability to model complex hierarchical relationships effectively without requiring higher dimensions or introducing distortion. The authors aim to empirically demonstrate that this geometric choice can lead to more discriminative and accurate embeddings.

- **Incorporating Vision Transformers:** Another key objective is to employ Vision Transformers (ViTs) as the encoder backbone. The motivation here is twofold: First, ViTs have demonstrated strong performance and generalization capabilities in various computer vision tasks, positioning them as a robust alternative to traditional CNNs. For image retrieval, which often involves generalizing to unseen categories, the ability of ViTs to learn general features is considered particularly beneficial. Second, the authors aim to explore how ViTs, when paired with appropriate pretraining strategies (e.g., on large datasets like ImageNet-21k, via distillation like DeiT-S, or through self-supervised learning like DINO), can enhance the quality of embeddings learned for metric learning. The paper specifically aims to compare ViTs with existing CNN-based models and assess the impact of different ViT pretraining schemes within their proposed framework.

- **Utilizing Pairwise Cross-Entropy Loss:** The research aims to optimize the hyperbolic embeddings using a modified pairwise cross-entropy loss. The motivation for this choice lies in the effectiveness and generality of this loss function. Pairwise cross-entropy loss directly considers the relationships between individual samples, encouraging positive pairs (similar items) to be closer and negative pairs (dissimilar items) to be pushed apart. This fine-grained optimization is crucial for metric learning. Furthermore, this loss has shown significant success in self-supervised learning scenarios, which often align with the objectives of metric learning by training encoders to produce semantically consistent representations. The authors seek to demonstrate that adapting this loss to operate with hyperbolic distances can further improve performance compared to its Euclidean counterpart.

- **Empirical Validation and Synergy:** A comprehensive objective is to empirically verify that the joint usage of Vision Transformers, hyperbolic embeddings, and pairwise cross-entropy loss provides a superior performance for the image retrieval task. The authors explicitly state their aim to compare various formulations of their method (different ViT pretraining schemes, spherical vs. hyperbolic embeddings) under controlled conditions to isolate the contribution of each component. This allows them to ascertain the combined effectiveness and determine if a synergy exists between these elements, leading to state-of-the-art results. The calculation of δ-hyperbolicity is also motivated by the desire to quantify the intrinsic hyperbolicity of image embeddings and inform the choice of manifold curvature.

In summary, the motivation is to synthesize state-of-the-art encoder architectures (ViTs), geometrically appropriate embedding spaces (hyperbolic), and effective loss functions (pairwise cross-entropy) to establish a new paradigm for deep metric learning that can achieve enhanced performance and better capture the underlying structure of visual data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed Hyperbolic Vision Transformers (HVT), integrates a vision transformer encoder, a projection into hyperbolic space, and a pairwise cross-entropy loss function. The overall schematic involves processing images with a ViT, reducing dimensionality with a fully connected layer, and then mapping the resulting features to a hyperbolic space for optimization.

#### 3.1. Hyperbolic Embeddings

The model utilizes the $n$-dimensional **Poincaré ball model** ($D^n_c$, $g_D$) of hyperbolic space, characterized by a constant negative curvature parameter $c$. In this model, the space is an open ball where distances locally scale by a conformal factor $\lambda_c = \frac{2}{1-c\|x\|^2}$, causing distances to approach infinity near the boundary. This property is fundamental to the exponential volume growth, which makes hyperbolic spaces suitable for embedding hierarchical data structures without significant distortion.

To perform operations within this non-Euclidean space, the authors define:
- **Gyrovector Addition ($x \oplus_c y$):** A non-associative operation that generalizes Euclidean vector addition to hyperbolic space.
- **Hyperbolic Distance ($D_{hyp}(x, y)$):** Defined using the gyrovector addition and the `arctanh` function, which quantifies the geodesic distance between two points in the Poincaré ball. As $c \rightarrow 0$, this distance converges to the Euclidean distance.
- **Exponential Map ($\exp_c_x(v)$):** A bijection that maps a vector from a tangent space in Euclidean space to a point in the Poincaré ball. For practical purposes, the base point $x$ is often set to the origin (0). In this method, after the ViT encoder and a linear projection, the resulting Euclidean features are mapped to the hyperbolic space using this exponential map. These hyperbolic embeddings are then directly used in the loss function without being mapped back to Euclidean space.

#### 3.2. Pairwise Cross-Entropy Loss

The training process employs a modified pairwise cross-entropy loss designed to operate with hyperbolic distances. For each training iteration, a batch consists of $N$ distinct categories, with two samples per category, totaling $K=2N$ samples. The loss function for a positive pair ($i, j$) is formulated as:
$l_{i,j} = - \log \frac{\exp (-D(z_i, z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1, k \neq i}^K \exp (-D(z_i, z_k)/\tau)}$
Here, $D$ represents either the hyperbolic distance ($D_{hyp}$) or, for comparison, a cosine similarity-based distance ($D_{cos}$) for spherical embeddings. $\tau$ is a temperature hyperparameter. The total loss is accumulated over all positive pairs in the batch. The method also accommodates scenarios with more than two samples per category by dividing the batch into subsets and summing loss values across subset pairs.

#### 3.3. δ-Hyperbolicity Estimation

To estimate the intrinsic hyperbolicity of different datasets, the authors utilize the Gromov $\delta$ measure. This involves calculating the Gromov product for triplets of points and then deriving $\delta$ from a min-max matrix product of these values. A $\delta$ value closer to 0 indicates a higher degree of intrinsic data hyperbolicity, and it can be used to estimate an optimal curvature $c(X) = (0.144/\delta)^2$ for the Poincaré ball. This estimation informs the choice of the curvature parameter $c$ for the hyperbolic space.

#### 3.4. Feature Clipping

To address potential vanishing gradients and numerical instability that can arise when embeddings approach the boundary of the Poincaré ball, the method incorporates feature clipping. This technique, defined as $x_E_C = \min \{1, r/\|x_E\|\} \cdot x_E$, effectively restricts embeddings within a smaller, effective radius $r$ of the hyperbolic ball. This helps to keep embeddings away from the boundary where gradients can become infinitesimally small.

#### 3.5. Vision Transformers as Encoders

The core encoder architecture is the Vision Transformer (ViT-S), a smaller variant of the ViT model. Images are divided into 16x16 pixel patches, flattened, linearly projected, and combined with position embeddings and a "[class]" token. This sequence is then fed into a standard Transformer encoder. The output corresponding to the "[class]" token is used as the image's final representation.
The authors evaluate three pretraining schemes for the ViT-S encoder:
- **ViT-S:** Pretrained on the large ImageNet-21k dataset (14M images, 21K categories).
- **DeiT-S:** Trained on the smaller ImageNet-1k dataset (1.3M images, 1K categories) using a teacher-student distillation approach with a CNN-based teacher.
- **DINO:** Self-supervised training on ImageNet-1k, where the model learns to produce consistent outputs for augmented views of the same image without labels. This scheme aligns with metric learning objectives as it encourages similar representations for semantically similar inputs.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** CUB-200-2011, Cars-196, Stanford Online Products (SOP), and In-Shop Clothes Retrieval. These are standard benchmarks for category-level image retrieval.
- **Implementation Details:** The ViT-S encoder outputs 384-dimensional features. A linear head further projects these features to 128 dimensions. For hyperbolic versions ("Hyp-"), $c=0.1$, $\tau=0.2$, and clipping radius $r=2.3$ are used. For spherical versions ("Sph-"), $\tau=0.1$. The performance metric is Recall@K. Images are resized to 224x224 (or 256x256 for CUB then 224x224 crop).
- **Training:** AdamW optimizer is used with specific learning rates (1e-5 for DINO, 3e-5 for ViT-S/DeiT-S), weight decay of 0.01, and a batch size of 900. Gradient clipping and common data augmentations (random crop, horizontal flip) are applied.
- **Hyperparameter Investigation:** The study includes an analysis of the impact of encoder patch size (16x16 vs. 8x8), manifold curvature ($c$), embedding dimensionality, and batch size on model performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents several findings regarding the performance of Hyperbolic Vision Transformers (HVT) for metric learning across four benchmark datasets.

#### 4.1. Intrinsic Data Hyperbolicity

The calculated $\delta$-hyperbolicity values (Table 1) for embeddings obtained from different encoders (ViT-S, DeiT-S, DINO) on the four datasets exhibit consistency. Lower $\delta$ values, indicating a higher degree of intrinsic data hyperbolicity, support the suitability of hyperbolic spaces for representing these image datasets. The estimated optimal $c$ values derived from $\delta$ are close to 0.2, aligning with the choice of the curvature parameter in the hyperbolic model.

#### 4.2. Overall Performance Improvement

- **Hyperbolic vs. Spherical Embeddings:** A consistent finding across all experiments is that hyperbolic space embeddings ("Hyp-" versions) demonstrate improved performance compared to their spherical counterparts ("Sph-" versions) (Table 2 and 3). This indicates that the use of hyperbolic geometry in conjunction with hyperbolic distances for the pairwise cross-entropy loss is beneficial for learning discriminative features.
- **Vision Transformers vs. CNNs:** The results generally confirm the superior performance of Vision Transformer-based encoders over traditional CNN-based architectures. The DeiT, DINO, and ViT variants consistently outperform most prior methods that rely on ResNet-50 or Inception backbones.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** The proposed method, particularly the Hyp-DINO and Hyp-ViT configurations, achieves new state-of-the-art results on several datasets. For instance, Hyp-ViT shows a notable 9% improvement on CUB-200-2011 Recall@1 over the previous best method IRT$_R$. Hyp-DINO outperforms Hyp-ViT on Cars-196, while Hyp-ViT performs slightly better on SOP and In-Shop. This indicates the effectiveness of the combined approach.
- **Impact of Pretraining:** The choice of pretraining scheme significantly influences performance. ViT-S pretrained on the large ImageNet-21k dataset shows strong results, especially on CUB, suggesting that large-scale supervised pretraining is highly effective. DINO, which uses self-supervised pretraining on ImageNet-1k, also demonstrates competitive performance, particularly on Cars-196, SOP, and In-Shop, highlighting the efficacy of self-supervised learning for producing features suitable for metric learning. DeiT-S, trained with distillation, performs better than CNN-based models but generally lags behind the other ViT pretraining schemes.

#### 4.3. Visualization of Embeddings

UMAP visualizations of Hyp-DINO embeddings on the Poincaré disk for the Cars-196 dataset (Figure 2) illustrate that training samples are effectively clustered by class and pushed towards the boundary of the disk, indicating good class separation. Test samples exhibit a more complex structure, with some forming distinct clusters and others intermixing towards the center, suggesting potential hierarchical relationships based on attributes like pose, color, or shape.

#### 4.4. Impact of Hyperparameters

- **Encoder Patch Size (Table 4):** Employing smaller 8x8 patches for the Vision Transformer encoder leads to a substantial performance gain. For example, on the Cars-196 dataset, using 8x8 patches with Hyp-DINO improved Recall@1 by 4.4% (for 128D head embeddings) and 3.6% (for 384D encoder embeddings) compared to the default 16x16 patches. This is attributed to the encoder's ability to learn more complex dependencies from finer-grained input.
- **Manifold Curvature (c) (Table 5):** The method exhibits robustness to the curvature value $c$ within the range of (0.01, 0.3). Values outside this range, particularly larger ones (e.g., $c=0.5, 1.0$), result in performance degradation. This suggests an optimal range for $c$ where the hyperbolic space best captures the data's geometry, with smaller values potentially offering better optimization stability.
- **Embedding Size and Batch Size (Table 5):** As expected, reducing the output embedding dimensionality from 128 to 16 or 32 leads to lower recall values, but the method still maintains reasonable representation power given the high data variability. Regarding batch size, the method is robust for sizes greater than or equal to 400, with only minor performance degradation observed at a batch size of 200. This implies that the method does not necessitate extremely large batch sizes or complex distributed training setups for effective performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Hyperbolic Vision Transformers: Combining Improvements in Metric Learning" presents a methodology that achieves enhanced performance in deep metric learning, suggesting several areas of significance and potential impact.

#### 5.1. Methodological Advancement in Metric Learning

The paper's primary significance lies in its demonstration that the synergistic combination of Vision Transformers (ViTs), hyperbolic space embeddings, and pairwise cross-entropy loss can yield improved results in metric learning. By systematically evaluating each component and their combined effect, the authors provide empirical evidence that this tripartite integration leads to state-of-the-art performance on several benchmark datasets. This work suggests a promising direction for future research in metric learning by advocating for the adoption of geometrically-aware embedding spaces and powerful transformer architectures.

#### 5.2. Validation of Hyperbolic Geometry for Visual Data

The consistent outperformance of hyperbolic embeddings over spherical (Euclidean-normalized) ones across diverse datasets reinforces the growing hypothesis that hyperbolic spaces are more naturally suited for representing intrinsically hierarchical or tree-like data structures. Given that natural images often contain complex, hierarchical semantic relationships, this work contributes to the empirical validation of hyperbolic geometry as a powerful tool for visual representation learning. This could lead to more efficient and less distorted embeddings, potentially reducing the dimensionality required to achieve a given level of accuracy.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Feature Learning with Vision Transformers

The study further supports the utility of Vision Transformers as robust feature extractors for metric learning tasks, surpassing many CNN-based methods. The specific analysis of different ViT pretraining strategies (large-scale supervised, distillation-based, and self-supervised) highlights that effective pretraining is crucial and that self-supervised methods like DINO can yield highly competitive features for downstream metric learning, even with minimal explicit supervision on the target dataset. This indicates that the benefits of general, context-aware features learned by transformers extend effectively to tasks requiring fine-grained similarity perception.

#### 5.4. Practical Implications for Image Retrieval Systems

The improved Recall@K metrics suggest that this approach could lead to more accurate and effective content-based image retrieval systems. The robustness of the method to various hyperparameters, particularly batch size (for values $\ge$ 400), implies that it does not necessitate prohibitively large computational resources, making it potentially more accessible for practical deployments compared to methods that require massive distributed training. The ability to achieve strong performance even with lower-dimensional embeddings could also lead to more memory-efficient storage and faster retrieval operations.

#### 5.5. Future Research Directions

The authors explicitly outline several future research avenues. Given that hyperbolic embeddings and transformers were initially developed for Natural Language Processing (NLP), extending this method to multimodal learning, combining visual and language domains, represents a logical next step. Exploring the applicability of a single transformer architecture across multiple domains to learn universal representations with a common semantic metric is also suggested. These directions could lead to more generalized and powerful AI systems capable of understanding and relating information across different data modalities.

#### 5.6. Broader Societal Impact Considerations

The paper acknowledges the broader societal implications, particularly concerning applications of metric learning such as face recognition and person re-identification. While these technologies offer potential benefits in public safety and quality of life, they also carry risks related to privacy infringement, surveillance, and the perpetuation of social biases. The authors highlight the need for further research into learned social biases within Vision Transformers, a topic less explored compared to NLP models. This responsible disclosure underscores the importance of ethical considerations in the development and deployment of advanced AI systems.
