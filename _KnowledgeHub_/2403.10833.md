# Deep Reinforcement Learning-based Large-scale Robot Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> A DRL-based reactive planner from NUS efficiently solves large-scale autonomous robot exploration problems by implicitly predicting unknown areas. This approach achieves 12% better distance efficiency and 60% faster planning times compared to state-of-the-art conventional planners like TARE in large simulated environments, and successfully explores real-world indoor spaces without additional training.

## Problem

- Existing conventional planners, even hierarchical ones, make myopic decisions based solely on partial maps, struggling to predict unknown environmental structures for long-term efficiency.
- Learning-based (DRL) exploration methods were largely confined to small-scale environments and often underperformed compared to advanced conventional planners in large settings.
- Explicitly modeling the stochastic transition of unknown areas in Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) formulations for traditional exploration is highly challenging and unstable.

## Method

- Frames the exploration problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) on a dense collision-free graph, enhanced with utility and guidepost features for informative input.
- Employs an attention-based neural network as the policy, trained with the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm, leveraging privileged learning where the critic network accesses ground truth information during training for stable learning.
- Introduces a graph rarefaction algorithm to generate sparse, informative graphs, enabling the model trained on small environments to scale and generalize to large-scale exploration tasks.

## Results

- Achieved **11%** better exploration efficiency (path length) in small-scale environments compared to TARE Local and Utility baselines, with privileged learning improving performance by **7%** over a vanilla DRL model.
- Outperformed state-of-the-art TARE in large-scale Gazebo simulations, showing **12%** better distance efficiency and **6%** better time efficiency, while being **60%** faster in per-step planning time.
- Demonstrated successful and efficient real-world exploration in a cluttered indoor laboratory using a physical robot, without additional training, validating robust transferability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Providing the critic with ground truth information during DRL training (privileged learning) significantly stabilizes value estimation, leading to more robust and efficient policy learning for exploration.
> - Attention mechanisms within a DRL framework can enable implicit prediction of unknown environmental structures from partial observations, leading to more non-myopic exploration paths.
> - Intelligent graph sparsification (rarefaction) is effective for scaling DRL models, trained on smaller datasets, to vastly larger and more complex environments without retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robot exploration is a fundamental problem in robotics, aiming to efficiently map unknown environments. The field has evolved significantly, from basic frontier-based methods to sophisticated hierarchical and learning-based approaches. This paper situates itself at the forefront of this evolution, addressing critical limitations of current state-of-the-art techniques, particularly concerning scalability and proactive decision-making in large, complex environments.

Traditionally, autonomous exploration planners have relied on heuristic-based approaches, such as **frontier-based methods** and **sampling-based methods**. Frontier-based methods drive exploration by moving the robot towards the boundary between known and unknown areas (frontiers). While efficient in small-scale environments, they often suffer from "myopic" decisions in larger or more cluttered spaces, optimizing short-term objectives at the expense of overall exploration efficiency. Sampling-based planners, like those employing Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRTs), can generate non-myopic paths but become computationally intensive for very long planning horizons required in large-scale environments.

To address the scalability challenge, **hierarchical planning frameworks** have emerged as the state-of-the-art. These frameworks typically combine a global planner for high-level decision-making (e.g., selecting distant target regions) with a local planner for fine-grained path execution. TARE, for instance, represents a leading hierarchical approach capable of exploring environments at the hundred-meter scale while maintaining planning times under a second. Despite their advancements, these conventional planners share a fundamental limitation: they optimize paths solely based on the robot's *partial belief* (current map) of the environment. They struggle to implicitly predict or infer the structure of unknown areas, which is a crucial human capability for efficient exploration. This makes the exploration problem a **partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP)**, where the underlying transition model between the true environment and the robot's belief is highly stochastic and difficult to model explicitly.

In parallel, **learning-based approaches**, particularly Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), have gained traction in robotics due to their ability to learn complex policies from data and implicitly handle uncertainties. Previous DRL-based exploration planners often use Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to process visual map representations. However, these methods have largely been limited to small-scale environments, with performance often only on par with simple frontier-based planners. They also face challenges with robustness to noisy real-world sensor data and generalization to new environments. The authors' previous work, ARiADNE, addressed some of these limitations by employing attention-based Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) on graph representations of the environment, showing improvements over CNN-based DRL and basic frontier methods, but still struggling to scale to truly large environments compared to sophisticated conventional planners like TARE.

This current work directly tackles the aforementioned limitations. It aims to develop a DRL-based planner that not only matches or surpasses the efficiency of state-of-the-art conventional planners like TARE in large-scale environments but also incorporates the ability to implicitly predict unknown areas, a key missing component in traditional methods. By leveraging attention mechanisms and introducing novel training and scaling techniques, the paper pushes the boundaries of DRL in autonomous exploration, making it viable for practical, large-scale deployments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a **Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-based reactive planner capable of efficiently solving large-scale Lidar-based autonomous robot exploration problems in 2D action space**. The ultimate goal is to find the shortest total exploration path to completely map an unknown environment (classify it into free and occupied areas) while minimizing the overall exploration cost (e.g., path length or makespan).

The motivations for pursuing this DRL-based approach stem from several critical limitations of existing methods:

- **Myopic Decision-Making in Conventional Planners:** Current state-of-the-art conventional planners, even hierarchical ones like TARE, optimize paths based solely on the robot's *partial belief* (current map). While they can generate near-optimal paths on this partial map, these decisions can lead to suboptimal long-term behaviors as new measurements reveal unforeseen environmental structures. The core issue is that they cannot explicitly predict or infer unknown areas. For example, a robot cannot know if two seemingly separate corridors in its partial map are connected without fully exploring them, whereas a human might infer the connection.
- **Difficulty of Explicitly Modeling Unknown Areas:** The underlying transition model in autonomous exploration (how the true environment translates to the robot's observations) is highly stochastic and complex. Directly learning an explicit predictive model of unknown areas for conventional planners is challenging and often leads to instability or inaccuracy in practice.
- **Scalability Challenges for Existing DRL Planners:** While DRL offers the promise of learning adaptive, non-myopic policies, most prior DRL-based exploration planners have been confined to small-scale environments. They often struggle to generalize to larger, more complex settings due to input representation limitations (e.g., fixed-size CNN inputs) and the difficulty of learning long-term dependencies over vast areas. Their performance has typically been on par with basic frontier-based methods, falling short of advanced conventional planners for large-scale tasks.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Real-time replanning in large-scale environments requires very low per-step planning times. Although hierarchical planners like TARE have made strides, there is still room for improvement in computational speed, especially for real-life deployments.

The authors hypothesize that a model-free DRL approach, particularly one leveraging attention mechanisms, can elegantly address these challenges by **implicitly estimating the transition model and learning an adaptive policy that can reason about the entire belief at multiple spatial scales**. Their key motivation is to enable DRL to achieve superior long-term exploration efficiency by allowing the agent to make implicit predictions about unknown areas, leading to more intelligent, non-myopic decisions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a novel Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework for autonomous robot exploration, built upon an attention-based neural network. It features two key innovations: **privileged learning** for stable training and **graph rarefaction** for scalability to large environments.

**A. Problem Formulation as a POMDP on a Graph:**
The exploration problem is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The robot maintains a map `M` (its belief) and aims to minimize the cost of its exploration path `ψ` until the environment is fully mapped. At each step, the robot observes `o_i` (its partial map and path history) and selects an action `a_i` (next waypoint). The objective is to find an optimal policy `π*` that maximizes long-term discounted returns.

To simplify the problem for the DRL agent, the raw LiDAR point cloud data is first transformed into an occupancy grid. From this grid, a **dense collision-free graph G = (V, E)** is extracted. Nodes `V` uniformly cover the free areas `M_f`, and edges `E` connect k-nearest neighbors if they are collision-free. The robot's DRL planner decides which neighboring node to visit next.

**B. Informative Graph and Policy Network:**
The DRL planner uses an **informative graph G\* = (V\*, E)** as its input. This graph extends the collision-free graph by adding two properties to each node `v_i`:
- **Utility (u_i):** Quantifies observable frontiers (boundary between known free and unknown areas) within line of sight from `v_i`. This provides low-level, task-relevant information, reducing the need for the network to learn basic pattern recognition.
- **Guidepost (g_i):** A binary value indicating whether `v_i` has been visited. This empirically helps speed up training by encouraging the robot to explore unvisited areas.

The policy network itself is an **attention-based neural network**, similar to, comprising an encoder and a decoder:
- **Encoder:** Consists of six stacked attention layers. Each layer takes node features as input and allows each node to learn dependencies with its neighboring nodes. By stacking layers, the network can capture dependencies between non-neighboring nodes, modeling multi-scale relationships within the graph.
- **Decoder:** Takes the encoded node features. A global attention mechanism first integrates information from all nodes to refine the feature of the current robot position (`h_c`). Then, a pointer network is used, taking `h_c` as query and neighboring node features as keys, to directly output attention weights as the policy `π(a_i|o_t)`, defining probabilities for selecting each neighboring node as the next waypoint. This design naturally handles varying numbers of neighbors.

**C. Training with Ground Truth (Privileged Learning):**
The policy network is trained offline using the **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)** algorithm with discrete actions. SAC aims to maximize both returns and policy entropy for robust learning. The reward function for training includes a cost reward (negative path length), an exploration reward (number of new frontiers observed), and a fixed finishing reward upon completion.

A critical innovation is the use of **privileged learning** for the critic network. In the actor-critic framework, the critic estimates state-action values to guide the actor's learning. Typically, both actor and critic receive the same partial observation. However, in exploration, the underlying transition model is highly stochastic, making accurate value estimation from partial observations challenging for the critic. To address this, the authors allow the critic network to access **true states (ground truth information)** of the environment during training, effectively simplifying the critic's task from a POMDP to a more traditional MDP.
- The critic receives a **ground truth graph G' = (V', E')**. `V'` covers the entire (ground truth) free area of the environment, and each node `v'_i` includes `(x_i, y_i)`, its true utility `u_i`, and an `unexplored feature e_i` (binary, indicating if the node is in an already explored area).
- By providing the critic with full knowledge of the environment, its value estimations become significantly more accurate and stable (e.g., 10 times lower loss and gradient variance, leading to ~10% increased average per-step rewards compared to standard SAC). This precise feedback allows the policy network to learn more effectively to achieve long-term efficiency under partial observations. Crucially, the critic is only used during training, so access to ground truth does not affect real-time inference.

**D. Graph Rarefaction for Large-scale Exploration:**
To enable the model, trained on small-scale environments, to generalize and scale to much larger ones, a **graph rarefaction algorithm** is proposed. In large environments, the dense collision-free graph can become extremely large, with many zero-utility nodes separating distant frontiers. Directly feeding such a dense graph to the attention network would be computationally expensive and dilute the important information.
The rarefaction algorithm creates a **sparser information graph G_s = (V_s, E_s)** by:
1. Identifying all non-zero utility nodes (frontiers).
2. Grouping nearby frontiers.
3. Finding shortest paths from the robot's current position to these frontier groups.
4. Selecting a minimal set of intermediate nodes along these paths and frontier nodes that represent all relevant information in the map, ensuring connectivity and line-of-sight.
This sparser graph, with fewer but more informative nodes, is then fed to the policy network. This allows the attention mechanism to capture longer-term dependencies more efficiently (e.g., connecting distant frontiers with fewer "hops" in the graph), thereby extending the model's applicability to large-scale scenarios without additional training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments in simulated environments of varying scales and a real-world setting to validate their DRL-based planner.

**A. Validation in Small-scale Environments:**
- **Setup:** Tested on a benchmark set of 100 previously unseen small-scale simplified simulation environments (640x480 grid world).
- **Baselines:** Compared against Nearest, Utility, NBVP, TARE Local (local part of TARE), CNN, and ARiADNE (ablation variant of their model trained without ground truth).
- **Results:** The proposed DRL planner achieved **11% better exploration efficiency in terms of average path length** compared to TARE Local and Utility, and 15% better than CNN and NBVP.
- **Impact of Privileged Learning:** The model trained with ground truth (Ours) performed 7% better than ARiADNE (vanilla model without ground truth). This validates that providing the critic with ground truth significantly improves policy learning by enabling more precise long-term value estimations.
- **Implicit Prediction:** The authors observed through attention weight visualization that the planner learns to focus on areas where connections might be missing, suggesting it implicitly predicts the structure of unknown areas to make non-myopic decisions and avoid redundant movements.

**B. Validation in Large-scale Environments:**
- **Setup:** High-fidelity 3D Gazebo simulations of a 130m × 100m indoor office and a 150m × 150m outdoor forest. These simulations included realistic sensor models (Velodyne 16-line 3D LiDAR) and robot kinematics. The model used was the same as the small-scale tests, but with the graph rarefaction algorithm applied.
- **Baselines:** Compared against TARE (state-of-the-art large-scale planner), DSVP, and an "Optimal Human Practice" baseline (manual path with full prior knowledge).
- **Indoor Office Results (Table II, Figure 4, Figure 6):**
 - **Exploration Efficiency:** The DRL planner outperformed TARE by **12% in distance efficiency** and **6% in time (makespan) efficiency**. The average path length was 1020m (Ours) vs. 1158m (TARE) vs. 1462m (DSVP), approaching the human baseline of 879m.
 - **Planning Time:** The DRL planner exhibited significantly lower per-step planning time, being **60% faster than TARE** (0.15s vs. 0.24s), even though their implementation is in Python and TARE's in C++.
 - The planned paths showed fewer redundant movements, demonstrating the benefit of implicit prediction.
- **Outdoor Forest Results (Table III, Figure 5):**
 - Despite being trained only on indoor environments, the DRL planner achieved time efficiency on par with TARE and better distance efficiency in a cluttered outdoor forest environment where typical indoor structures (corridors, junctions) were absent. This suggests a degree of robustness and generalization, even if specific implicit predictions were less accurate.

**C. Validation in Real World (Figure 1, Figure 7):**
- **Setup:** A wheeled robot (customized Turtlebot3 with Leishen C16 LiDAR) explored an 80m × 10m indoor laboratory with cluttered furniture and moving pedestrians. LOAM was used for LiDAR odometry and mapping.
- **Results:** The robot successfully and efficiently explored the lab in 12 minutes without any additional training on real-world data. This highlights the robust applicability and transferability of the learned policy and the graph representation approach in a challenging real-life scenario. The use of an informative graph input, instead of raw images, likely contributes to this robustness against real-world noise and visual differences.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a significant advancement in the field of autonomous robot exploration, particularly by demonstrating the viability and superior performance of Deep Reinforcement Learning in large-scale, complex environments.

**Key Significance Points:**

- **First DRL Planner for Large-scale Exploration:** The paper successfully addresses the long-standing challenge of scaling DRL-based exploration to large, real-world relevant environments. This is a critical milestone, as previous DRL methods were largely confined to small-scale scenarios or struggled to outperform conventional approaches.
- **Implicit Prediction for Non-Myopic Decisions:** By leveraging attention mechanisms and enabling implicit prediction of unknown areas from partial observations, the DRL planner overcomes a fundamental limitation of traditional methods: their myopic decision-making. This allows the robot to make more intelligent, long-term optimal exploration choices, leading to significantly higher efficiency.
- **Effectiveness of Privileged Learning:** The innovative use of ground truth information during critic training (privileged learning) is a crucial methodological contribution. It stabilizes the DRL training process, enabling the policy to learn more precise state-action values despite the stochastic nature of exploration, and ultimately boosts overall performance. This technique has potential applications beyond exploration in other complex POMDPs.
- **Scalability via Graph Rarefaction:** The proposed graph rarefaction algorithm provides an elegant solution for generalizing models trained in smaller environments to much larger ones. By intelligently compressing the informative graph, it maintains computational feasibility while preserving critical information, making the DRL approach practical for extensive mapping tasks.
- **Superior Performance:** The DRL planner consistently outperforms state-of-the-art conventional planners like TARE in terms of exploration efficiency (path length, makespan) and, remarkably, planning time. The significant reduction in planning time (60% faster than TARE) makes this approach highly suitable for real-time applications requiring rapid replanning.
- **Robust Real-World Applicability:** The successful validation on a physical robot in a cluttered, dynamic indoor laboratory without additional training highlights the robustness and practical utility of the proposed framework. This demonstrates its potential for direct deployment in real-life scenarios, where traditional DRL approaches often struggle due to simulation-to-reality gaps.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Mapping:** The increased exploration efficiency and reduced planning time can significantly accelerate the mapping of large-scale environments, benefiting applications such as disaster response (rapid assessment of damaged buildings), industrial facility inspection, large-scale construction site monitoring, and underground exploration.
- **More Autonomous Robots:** By enabling robots to make more intelligent, human-like decisions about exploration, this research contributes to the development of truly autonomous systems capable of operating effectively in unstructured and unknown environments with minimal human intervention.
- **Advancement in DRL for Robotics:** The methodologies introduced, particularly privileged learning and graph rarefaction for DRL generalization, provide valuable insights and tools for future research in applying DRL to other complex robotic tasks involving large state/action spaces and partial observability.
- **Foundation for Multi-Robot Systems:** The paper lays the groundwork for future advancements, explicitly mentioning multi-robot exploration as a future focus. Efficient single-robot exploration is a prerequisite for effective multi-robot coordination and distributed mapping.

In conclusion, this paper presents a compelling DRL-based solution that pushes the boundaries of autonomous robot exploration. By cleverly combining attention mechanisms, a novel training paradigm, and an efficient scaling algorithm, the authors have developed a planner that is not only highly efficient and computationally lightweight but also demonstrates a crucial ability to implicitly reason about unexplored areas, bringing DRL exploration closer to widespread real-world deployment.
