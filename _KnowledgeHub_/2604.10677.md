# LIDEA: Human-to-Robot Imitation Learning via Implicit Feature Distillation and Explicit Geometry Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> The LIDEA framework presents a human-to-robot imitation learning approach that leverages implicit feature distillation and explicit geometry alignment to bridge the embodiment gap. This method enables data-efficient learning by substituting up to 80% of robot demonstrations with human data and enhances out-of-distribution generalization in real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- High cost and labor associated with acquiring sufficient robotic demonstrations create a bottleneck for scalable and robust robot learning.
- The significant "embodiment gap" between human hands and robot grippers (visual appearance, 3D geometry, action semantics) hinders effective transfer of skills from abundant human video data.
- Existing cross-embodiment transfer methods often introduce visual artifacts, struggle with kinematic mismatch, or rely on brittle explicit state estimation, limiting their generalizability and efficiency.

## Method

- **LIDEA** introduces a ==dual-stage transitive 2D feature distillation== pipeline to align visual representations of human, pseudo-robot, and real-robot observations in a shared latent space, initialized from ==DINOv3== and using a novel ==HPP-5M== dataset.
- An ==explicit 3D geometric alignment== module creates a ==canonical, embodiment-agnostic 3D observation space== by filtering out native agent geometry and injecting a virtual gripper point cloud.
- The aligned 2D features and canonical 3D observations are integrated into a ==3D diffusion-based policy== model (**RISE-2**) for robust action prediction in a mixed human and robot data training paradigm.

## Results

- LIDEA significantly improved data efficiency, demonstrating that human demonstrations can substitute up to **80%** of costly robot demonstrations across various manipulation tasks.
- The framework achieved superior out-of-distribution generalization, increasing first-corner success rate from **36%** (robot-only) to **63%** (robot + human data) in an OOD "Fold Towel" scenario.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of both implicit 2D feature distillation and explicit 3D geometric alignment, as removing either component resulted in substantial performance degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transitive 2D feature distillation, leveraging a large-scale pseudo-robot dataset (HPP-5M) as an intermediary, effectively bridges the semantic gap between human and real-robot visual observations.
> - Decoupling interaction structure from agent-specific geometry by creating a canonical 3D observation space is critical for consistent depth-aware policy learning across human and robot embodiments.
> - Robust cross-embodiment transfer requires both implicit semantic alignment of visual features and explicit geometric canonicalization of the 3D interaction space.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is continuously seeking to develop policies that are robust and generalizable. A primary obstacle to achieving this is the substantial cost and labor required to acquire large-scale robotic demonstrations. In contrast, human video demonstrations represent an extensive and readily available source of interaction data that could potentially alleviate this data scarcity.

Prior research has explored various strategies to leverage human data for robot learning, broadly categorized into:
- **Visual Editing:** Methods that attempt to translate human videos into "robot videos" by replacing human hands with robot grippers at the pixel level. While these can be effective, they often introduce visual artifacts due to discrepancies in appearance and 3D geometry and are computationally intensive, typically operating in 2D without consistent 3D geometric information.
- **Unified Representation Learning:** Approaches that aim to train policies directly on human data through co-training or unified tokenization. These methods face challenges due to the significant kinematic mismatch between human hands and robot end-effectors, which limits transfer efficiency and often necessitates extensive fine-tuning or indirect mappings.
- **Object-Centric Learning:** Strategies that bypass the embodiment gap by focusing on extracting affordances or retargeting object trajectories. However, these depend on explicit state estimation, which can be ambiguous for general object categories, particularly deformable or articulated objects, thereby limiting their applicability in complex environments.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing LIDEA, a framework that aims to bridge the embodiment gap without relying on pixel-level editing, addressing kinematic inconsistencies, or depending on explicit and potentially brittle object state estimation. LIDEA positions itself as an advancement in cross-embodiment transfer by focusing on establishing robust semantic and geometric alignment between human and robot observations, allowing for more effective and efficient utilization of human demonstrations for data-efficient and generalizable robot policy learning. It builds upon the capabilities of visual foundation models like DINOv3 for representation learning but adapts their distillation mechanisms for cross-embodiment feature alignment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable data-efficient learning of robust and generalizable 3D visuomotor policies for robotic manipulation by effectively leveraging human video demonstrations. This objective is motivated by several critical challenges in the field of robot learning:

1. **Data Scarcity in Robotics:** Acquiring a sufficient volume of high-quality robotic demonstrations is a labor-intensive and costly process, forming a bottleneck for scaling up robot learning. This limits the diversity and robustness of learned policies.
2. **Abundance of Human Interaction Data:** Human videos, in contrast, offer an almost unlimited and diverse source of real-world interaction data. Tapping into this resource could significantly accelerate progress toward general-purpose embodied AI.
3. **The Embodiment Gap:** A fundamental challenge in transferring skills from human demonstrations to robots is the inherent "embodiment gap." This gap manifests in three key areas:
 - **Visual Appearance:** Human hands and robot grippers look distinctly different.
 - **3D Geometry:** The physical structure and kinematic properties of human hands differ significantly from robot end-effectors.
 - **Action Semantics:** The way humans interact with objects, even for semantically equivalent tasks, can differ from how a robot gripper would perform the same action.
4. **Limitations of Existing Transfer Strategies:** Current methods for cross-embodiment transfer often fall short:
 - Visual editing techniques can introduce artifacts and are typically 2D, failing to maintain consistent 3D geometry essential for depth-aware policies.
 - Unified representation learning struggles with the kinematic mismatch, leading to inefficient transfer and requiring extensive fine-tuning.
 - Object-centric methods rely on explicit state estimation, which can be ambiguous or fragile, especially for complex objects or environments.

To address these motivations, LIDEA aims to achieve the following specific objectives:
- **Bridge the Embodiment Gap:** Develop a novel human-to-robot imitation learning paradigm that effectively mitigates the discrepancies between human and robot embodiments.
- **Enable Effective Knowledge Transfer:** Establish a shared feature space that preserves semantically equivalent interactions across human and robot observations, allowing policies to benefit from human demonstrations.
- **Achieve Embodiment-Agnostic Geometric Perception:** Create a consistent 3D observation space by decoupling the interaction structure from the acting agent's specific geometry, thereby providing reliable 3D-aware representations for policy transfer.
- **Improve Data Efficiency:** Reduce the reliance on expensive robot demonstrations by demonstrating that human data can effectively substitute a significant portion of required robot supervision.
- **Enhance Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization:** Enable policies to transfer unseen patterns and robustness from diverse human videos to novel robotic execution scenarios, including those with novel objects or visual distractors.
- **Introduce a Large-Scale Dataset:** Create the HPP-5M dataset to facilitate scalable semantic alignment between human and robot observations through strictly paired, interaction-equivalent frames.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The LIDEA framework is designed to bridge the human-to-robot embodiment gap through two complementary mechanisms: implicit 2D feature distillation and explicit 3D geometric alignment. These mechanisms are integrated into a mixed-data policy learning pipeline.

#### 3.1. A. Implicit 2D Feature Distillation

This component aims to align human and robot visual representations in a shared latent space using a dual-stage transitive distillation pipeline, mitigating the scarcity of direct human-robot paired data.

1. **Stage 1: Human-to-Pseudo-Robot Distillation:**
 - **Objective:** To establish semantic equivalence between human hands and robotic grippers within a "pseudo-robot" domain. Direct alignment between human and real robot is difficult due to precise geometric correspondence issues and data scarcity.
 - **Data Generation (HPP-5M Dataset):** A large-scale dataset, HPP-5M (Human-to-Pseudo-Robot Pair), is constructed. This dataset comprises approximately 5 million strictly paired, interaction-equivalent human and pseudo-robot frames. Human demonstration frames ($I_H$) are sourced from existing 3D hand-object interaction datasets (DexYCB, TACO, OakInk, OakInk2), which provide parametric 3D hand poses. These human hand motions are converted into equivalent gripper actions by fitting the gripper wrist pose and opening state using fingertip 3D positions, solving arm configurations via inverse kinematics (IK). The human is then removed from the original image using generative inpainting (Propainter), and a robot proxy (pseudo-robot) is rendered into the scene using a path-traced renderer to create pseudo-robot counterparts ($I_P$). This process ensures semantic equivalence.
 - **Distillation Process:** Two visual encoders, $E_H$ and $E_P$, are initialized from pre-trained DINOv3 weights. $E_H$ (human encoder) is frozen as the teacher, while $E_P$ (pseudo-robot encoder) is trained to match $E_H$'s representations.
 - **Region-of-Interaction (RoInt) Cropping:** To emphasize task-relevant interaction semantics over background correlations, standard random local crops are replaced with RoInt cropping. This strategy restricts local views to regions centered around hand/gripper-object contact, ensuring that the student encoder captures embodiment-agnostic interaction semantics.

2. **Stage 2: Robot Pseudo-to-Real Distillation:**
 - **Objective:** To transfer the aligned representations from the pseudo-robot domain to real-robot observations, completing the transitive bridge for real-world deployment.
 - **Data Generation:** Real-world robot joint trajectories are recorded, and a pseudo-robot mesh is synthesized under identical kinematic configurations. This pseudo-robot mesh is then rendered onto the native real-robot image ($I_R$) to create its pseudo-robot counterpart ($I_{P'}$), forming geometry-identical pairs ($I_{P'}, I_R$). This stage primarily bridges the photometric gap between rendered and real images.
 - **Distillation Process:** $E_P$ from Stage 1 serves as the frozen teacher, and $E_R$ (real-robot encoder) is trained as the student. Both encoders are initialized with the optimized Stage 1 weights.
 - **Augmentation:** Standard DINO-style global and local multi-crop augmentation is used, as the primary gap here is photometric, not embodiment-specific geometry.

3. **Distillation Objectives:** The student networks ($E_P$ in Stage 1 and $E_R$ in Stage 2) are optimized using a combined self-supervised learning objective based on DINOv3. The total loss ($L_{total}$) consists of:
 - $L_{DINO}$: A self-distillation loss on global CLS tokens, enforcing semantic consistency between teacher and student.
 - $L_{iBOT}$: A masked patch prediction objective that trains the student to reconstruct teacher patch features, providing dense local supervision.
 - $L_{KoLeo}$: A differential entropy regularizer that promotes a uniform feature distribution across the batch. The Gram loss, typically used in DINOv3, is omitted.

#### 3.2. B. Explicit 3D Geometric Alignment

This component addresses geometric mismatches by constructing a canonical 3D observation space, crucial for depth-aware 3D policies.

1. **Embodiment-Specified Filtering:**
 - **Objective:** To obtain an agent-independent scene representation by removing embodiment-specific geometry from point clouds.
 - **Human Demonstrations:** For human data, a foundation segmentation model (Grounded-SAM2) extracts hand-arm masks from RGB images. These masks are applied during depth unprojection to remove all human-associated points, yielding a clean, human-free background point cloud ($P_{bg}^H$).
 - **Robot Teleoperation Data:** For robot data, proprioceptive measurements (joint angles and URDF model) are used to determine the spatial configuration of robot links. Robot occupancy volume is computed, and points within this volume are removed from the observed point cloud, resulting in an arm-free background point cloud ($P_{bg}^R$).

2. **Canonical Gripper Filling:**
 - **Objective:** To reintroduce end-effector interaction grounding without reintroducing embodiment-specific artifacts.
 - **Virtual Gripper:** A canonical virtual floating gripper point-cloud template, parameterized by its opening state ($P_g(s)$), is used.
 - **Pose Estimation:** For robot data, the Tool Center Point (TCP) pose ($T_R$) and gripper opening state ($s_R$) are obtained from proprioception. For human data, a multi-view hand pose estimator (POEM) estimates 3D hand joints. Fingertips are then aligned to the virtual gripper tips via least-squares to optimize an equivalent TCP pose ($T_H$) and opening state ($s_H$).
 - **Hybrid Observations:** The canonical gripper point cloud, transformed to the camera frame using the estimated pose, is then "filled" into the decoupled background scenes, yielding the final hybrid 3D observations for both domains: $P_{hyb}^H = P_{bg}^H \cup (T_H \cdot P_g(s_H))$ and $P_{hyb}^R = P_{bg}^R \cup (T_R \cdot P_g(s_R))$. This ensures the 3D policy observes an identical geometric representation across human and robot data.

#### 3.3. C. Policy Learning and Deployment

The aligned visual semantics (2D features) and 3D geometries (point clouds) are integrated into a 3D diffusion-based policy model, RISE-2.

1. **Training:**
 - A mixed-data training paradigm is used, combining human demonstrations and a portion of robot demonstrations.
 - For human data, the raw RGB image ($I_H$) is processed by the frozen, pre-trained human encoder ($E_H$). The hybrid 3D observation ($P_{hyb}^H$) is processed by RISE-2's sparse 3D encoder.
 - For robot data, the raw image ($I_R$) is fed into the frozen real-robot encoder ($E_R$). The hybrid 3D observation ($P_{hyb}^R$) is processed by the shared sparse 3D encoder.
 - The extracted 2D dense features and 3D sparse tokens are fused and passed through RISE-2's transformer layers to predict denoised action trajectories.

2. **Deployment:**
 - The policy operates solely on the robot’s real-time sensor streams.
 - The raw RGB image ($I_R$) is processed by $E_R$ for 2D features.
 - The robot’s spatial occupancy is filtered from the unprojected 3D point cloud to obtain $P_{bg}^R$.
 - The canonical virtual gripper is injected using real-time TCP pose and gripper state to form $P_{hyb}^R$.
 - The policy fuses these aligned features to predict continuous actions for receding horizon control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

LIDEA was empirically validated across multiple real-world manipulation tasks, focusing on data efficiency and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization.

#### 4.1. A. Setup

- **Hardware:** Flexiv Rizon 4 robotic arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and an Intel RealSense D415 RGB-D camera.
- **Tasks:** Four tasks were designed: Close Laptop (articulated-object), Stack (6 DoF pick-and-place), Fold Towel (deformable-object), and Prepare Bread (long-horizon).
- **Baseline:** A "Pseudo-Robot" baseline was established, representing explicit visual editing methods where human images are translated into pseudo-robot images to train the same 3D visuomotor policy.

#### 4.2. B. Data Efficiency

Experiments demonstrated that LIDEA significantly improves data efficiency by leveraging human demonstrations.

- **Substitution of Robot Data:** Human data was shown to substitute up to 80% of costly robot demonstrations, enabling effective policy learning with minimal robot supervision.
- **Performance across Tasks:**
 - In simpler tasks like "Close Laptop," LIDEA efficiently extracted motion patterns from human demonstrations.
 - For 6 DoF pick-and-place tasks such as "Stack," LIDEA effectively transferred spatial coordination and grasp timing, leading to accurate object alignment.
 - Compared to explicit visual-editing baselines, LIDEA showed superior performance, particularly in tasks requiring precise 3D localization (e.g., "Fold Towel"). Visual-editing methods introduced noise and artifacts, corrupting fine-grained spatial perception, whereas LIDEA's geometric alignment provided consistent human-robot perception.
 - In the challenging, long-horizon "Prepare Bread" task, a policy trained on 8 robot demonstrations alone struggled to generalize. Augmenting this with human data (8 Robot + 48 Human) improved success rates significantly across three stages (80%, 53%, and 46%). In contrast, pixel-level visual editing for human data (Pseudo-Robot baseline) resulted in poor performance (33%, 0%, and 0%), indicating that artifacts accumulate and disrupt stable 3D perception over long horizons.

#### 4.3. C. Out-of-Distribution Generalization

LIDEA demonstrated the ability to transfer OOD robustness from human videos to robotic execution.

- **Fold Towel OOD Scenario:** The task involved training on in-domain robot data (blue towel) but testing on an OOD configuration (novel pink towel as target, folded blue towel as distractor).
- **Performance Improvement:** Policies trained on 40 robot demonstrations alone achieved only 18% final success in the OOD setting, with the distractor severely affecting the initial corner-grasping stage (36% success). However, augmenting training with OOD human demonstrations (40 Robot + 40 Human) substantially improved robustness, increasing first-corner success to 63% and final completion to 27%. This suggests LIDEA effectively learns appearance-robust interaction cues from diverse human videos, allowing the policy to identify functional targets and ignore distractors without requiring extensive OOD robot data.

#### 4.4. D. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies on the "Stack" task (using 20 Robot + 72 Human data) investigated the contribution of LIDEA's components.

- **Implicit 2D Distillation:**
 - Using DINOv3 without cross-embodiment distillation resulted in near-total failure (20% success), confirming that general visual representations encode human and robot as distinct entities, preventing effective transfer without latent alignment.
 - Relying solely on Stage 1 distillation led to a noticeable performance drop (19-20%), indicating the necessity of Stage 2 to bridge the Pseudo-to-Real gap.
 - Removing large-scale internet pre-training data reduced performance by 13%, while removing target-domain free-motion pre-training data caused a significant collapse to 33% success, highlighting the importance of both for representation diversity and scene-specific priors.
- **Explicit 3D Alignment:**
 - When neither human nor robot point clouds were filtered (w/o Filter Both), success rates dropped substantially (40-46%), performing worse than the pure robot baseline and indicating negative transfer.
 - Asymmetric filtering (e.g., retaining only robot or human point clouds) also led to performance degradation (20-33% decrease), due to training-deployment mismatches and out-of-distribution geometric noise. This confirms that creating a strictly embodiment-agnostic 3D space via filtering native geometries and filling a unified virtual gripper is crucial.

#### 4.5. E. Analysis of 2D Feature Distillation

Quantitative and qualitative analysis confirmed the effectiveness of 2D feature distillation.

- **Sequence-level Similarity:** Cosine similarity analysis of global features showed that after distillation, cross-domain human-to-pseudo-robot feature similarity closely followed the in-sequence pseudo-robot-to-pseudo-robot temporal consistency, indicating a substantial narrowing of the human-robot feature gap.
- **PCA Visualization and Attention Heatmaps:** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of human and robot features revealed that the aligned encoder learns geometry-consistent representations, assimilating the robot end-effector into a human-like semantic space. Self-attention heatmaps demonstrated that the aligned robot encoder focused more strongly on the "Region-of-Interaction," indicating improved attention to task-relevant execution areas.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LIDEA represents a notable advancement in human-to-robot imitation learning, addressing fundamental challenges in data scarcity and cross-embodiment transfer. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** By demonstrating that human demonstrations can substitute a substantial portion (up to 80%) of costly robot demonstrations, LIDEA offers a scalable pathway to acquiring diverse training data for robot manipulation. This could significantly reduce the financial and labor burden associated with developing robotic skills.
- **Robust Generalization:** The framework's ability to transfer out-of-distribution robustness from human videos to robotic execution, even in scenarios with novel objects and distractors, suggests a path toward more adaptable and reliable robot policies in unstructured environments. This is critical for deploying robots in real-world settings where variability is high.
- **Novel Embodiment Gap Bridging:** LIDEA's dual approach of implicit 2D feature distillation and explicit 3D geometric alignment provides a comprehensive solution to the embodiment gap. This contrasts with previous methods that often introduce artifacts or struggle with kinematic inconsistencies, offering a more robust and principled approach.
- **Foundation for Future Dexterous Manipulation:** The work's current limitation in mapping highly articulated human hands to parallel grippers presents a clear direction for future research, particularly in extending the framework to support multi-fingered dexterous robotic hands. This could unlock more complex and human-like manipulation capabilities for robots.
- **Scalable Dataset for Alignment:** The introduction of HPP-5M, a large-scale (5 million frames) dataset of interaction-equivalent human-to-pseudo-robot frame pairs, is a valuable resource for the research community. It provides critical supervision for semantic alignment and can accelerate further development in cross-embodiment learning.
- **Integration with Advanced Policy Models:** By integrating its aligned representations with a state-of-the-art 3D diffusion-based policy model (RISE-2), LIDEA demonstrates how improved data utilization and representation learning can synergize with powerful modern control architectures.
- **Reduced Reliance on Generative Visual Editing:** The methodology moves away from pixel-level visual editing for deployment, which often introduces artifacts and computational overhead, by focusing on robust representation alignment and a canonical 3D observation space.

In the long term, LIDEA's principles could contribute to the development of general-purpose embodied AI by enabling robots to learn a vast array of manipulation skills from the rich and abundant source of human interaction data available globally. Future work could also extend the aligned visual encoder to train broader vision-language-action (VLA) or video-action models from human demonstrations, further expanding the scope of robotic capabilities learned from readily available human experience.
