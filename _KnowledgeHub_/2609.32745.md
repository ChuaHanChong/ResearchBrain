# MORPH: Self-Organising Multi-Robot Task Allocation via Neuroplasticity-Inspired Adaptive Topology

> [!summary] Summary
> MORPH is a training-free framework for multi-robot task allocation. It learns which robots should coordinate by keeping a directed pairwise preference matrix that four brain-inspired local rules update online: reward-gated Hebbian, homeostatic, structural and metaplastic. It needs no task model, bids, position sensors or offline training. On TA-RWARE it reaches 110% of all-to-all throughput with 21% of links, and it degrades 3x less than proximity under demand shift.

## Problem

- Auction, market and optimisation allocators need a utility function or cost matrix fixed before deployment, so they cannot operate when useful role pairings only appear at runtime.
- Learning-based allocators such as PRIMAL, RODE and CommFormer train over millions of steps on one fixed distribution and do not adapt once the task distribution shifts.
- Proximity heuristics need position sensors and per-environment radius calibration, while stigmergy and threshold models keep only scalar or environmental signals, not directed pairwise preferences in agent memory.

## Method

- ==R1==, a ==reward-gated Hebbian rule==, strengthens directed preference weights in proportion to the ==Jaccard overlap== of two robots' tasks, multiplied by delivery reward and applied only on active links.
- **R2** ==homeostatic scaling== pushes each agent toward a target degree fraction, and **R3** ==structural plasticity== forms links from ==mutual information== plus a predictive hint and prunes weak links after a 20-step grace period.
- **R4** ==metaplasticity== combines a ==BCM-style sliding threshold==, updated before the Hebbian step, with a ==global arousal signal== from delivery rate, switching between consolidation and exploration on two timescales.

## Results

- At N=24 MORPH makes **107.4** deliveries versus **98.0** for the full graph (**110%**) while using **57 of 276 links**. Link usage falls from **97%** at N=8 to **21%** at N=24.
- Over six alternating left/right demand phases at N=18, MORPH loses **7.4%** from Phase 3 to Phase 6, versus **25.4%** for proximity, **14.6%** for task-similarity gating and **15.6%** when weights are reset.
- A calibrated proximity baseline still leads on raw throughput at larger scales (**113.2** vs **107.4** at N=24). Learned preferences show no correlation with distance (Spearman **-0.111**, p=0.174), and removing neuromodulation degrades performance most consistently.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sparse, learned coordination topology beat all-to-all broadcast at every tested scale, so dense communication adds coordination noise even when bandwidth costs nothing.
> - Keeping learned preferences across repeated regime shifts beats resetting them. Slow, persistent connectivity updates compound into retention, while resets only help short-term relearning.
> - A correct spatial prior wins on a fixed distribution, while history-based preferences win under shift. Which one to use depends on whether spatial structure stays stable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In a simulated warehouse, Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) move shelves and Picker agents assist at pickup points. Demand switches left, right, and back again, so the team must keep allocating work as familiar patterns return. MORPH does not fix every connection or assign work by distance alone. Instead, pairs of agents build and revise preferences as the team works, without an allocation model or a policy trained in advance. In this simulation, the team that kept its learned preferences lost less delivery performance over repeated shifts than proximity or the same rules with memory cleared at every shift.

Shift experiment at medium scale (N=18, 20 seeds), with six alternating left/right phases of 400 steps each. Upper: rolling 50-step delivery rate for Proximity, TSG, adaptive MORPH, and reset-MORPH. Lower: recovery time in steps for return phases P3–P6 (lower is faster). Phase 3 to 6 relative degradation is −7.4%, −25.4%, −14.6%, and −15.6% for MORPH, proximity, TSG, and reset-MORPH respectively.

### 2. How preferences change

Each agent holds directed preferences toward the others. An entry records how strongly one agent prefers to request a response from another, and the matrix begins at zero each episode. The policy retains a 0.25 response-probability floor before preferences are established; weights then scale that probability.

Four interacting local rules shape the matrix:

- Reward-gated Hebbian strengthening uses overlap in assigned tasks and successful-delivery feedback.
- Homeostasis biases link formation toward agents below a target degree.
- Structural plasticity forms candidate links from task-history similarity (with a predictive hint) and prunes weak, mature links. New links have a 20-step grace period before pruning.
- Metaplasticity uses a sliding weight-history threshold to help stale links decay. It also uses a performance signal to shift between consolidation and exploration.

Weight updates are fast, while structural and degree updates run every three steps. This layer sits over a Closest-Target Assignment controller using nearest-shelf assignment and A*; it does not replace that underlying controller.

### 3. What retained memory buys under shifts

The shift test runs at medium scale (N=18, 20 seeds) through six 400-step left/right demand phases (L–R–L–R–L–R). MORPH carries its preferences forward. MORPH-reset uses the same rules but clears learned weights at each phase boundary, and proximity recomputes spatial links. Recovery time is the number of steps needed to recover to 90% of the corresponding baseline delivery rate after a shift.

From Phase 3 to Phase 6, mean deliveries fell as follows:

- MORPH: 47.5 to 44.0 (−7.4% relative).
- Proximity: 45.7 to 34.1 (−25.4%).
- MORPH-reset: 48.2 to 40.8 (−15.6%).

This supports the interpretation that carried-forward coordination memory, and not just online updating, helps retain performance. The advantage appears in the later phases. Reset is ahead in mean deliveries in Phases 3 and 4 and has shorter recovery times at r3 and r4. MORPH's recovery times for r3–r6 are 36.9, 23.1, 35.7, and 17.7 steps. The endpoints improve, but the sequence is not monotonic, and the paper warns that the variance is large.

### 4. Sparse links, useful throughput

In the fixed-demand scale study, throughput is mean deliveries per 800-step episode across five seeds. At 24 agents, MORPH averages 107.4 ± 3.4 deliveries versus 98.0 ± 7.7 for Full Graph, the all-to-all baseline. It does so while using 57 of 276 possible links (21%). MORPH's mean deliveries exceed Full Graph's at all four scales, while its active-link share falls from 97% at N=8 to 21% at N=24. These are mean-throughput comparisons. They are not evidence of statistically significant superiority over all-to-all, or of measured savings in bandwidth, runtime, or computation.

TSG (Task-Similarity Gate) uses current co-assignment information but does not retain preference history. At 24 agents it averages 97.6 deliveries versus MORPH's 107.4, with 7.8 versus 57.0 active links. This supports the value of accumulated structure over this memoryless gate in the large-scale setting. It does not isolate which individual plasticity rule caused the gap.

Proximity is competitive. It ties MORPH at N=8 and has higher reported throughput at N=12, 18, and 24, with Welch-test p-values of 0.008 at N=18 and 0.038 at N=24. Its radius was calibrated per scale to match MORPH's converged link count, a favorable condition unavailable at deployment.

The shift comparison suggests that the useful property is not simply fast relearning. It is retaining preferences that can be reused when a demand pattern returns, while other links remain revisable. The evidence comes from one simulated warehouse benchmark with hand-set parameters, and physical deployment has not been tested.
