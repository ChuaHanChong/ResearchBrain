# A Few Words Go a Long Way: Language Guided Robot Policy Synthesis

> [!summary] Summary
> ARCHITECT, a framework for robot policy acquisition, integrates interactive program synthesis guided by natural language and human feedback with a persistent skill library. This approach enables more adaptable and data-efficient robot control, outperforming state-of-the-art VLA baselines and significantly reducing human intervention in new tasks by amortizing human effort.

## Problem

- Black-box Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are opaque, making them difficult to interpret, adapt, or correct when they inevitably fail in real-world deployments.
- Adapting VLA models requires extensive datasets and computational resources, rendering policy refinement expensive and inaccessible for non-expert end-users.
- Modular robot control systems often suffer from a 'specification gap,' where initial natural language instructions lack the precision to capture intricate geometric and physical task nuances.

## Method

- Developed **ARCHITECT**, an agentic framework that treats robot policy acquisition as an interactive ==program synthesis== task, orchestrated by a pre-trained ==LLM== (Claude Opus 4.6).
- Implemented a ==human-in-the-loop== refinement process where human supervisors provide natural language corrections to guide the LLM in modifying robot programs based on execution traces.
- Integrated a persistent '==skill library==' to distill successful human corrections into reusable code patterns or natural language rules, enabling long-term learning and knowledge amortization.

## Results

- ARCHITECT consistently outperformed state-of-the-art VLA baselines (π0, π0.5) and program synthesis baselines (Code As Policies) across **8** diverse manipulation tasks requiring multi-step reasoning and interaction with complex objects.
- Human evaluation demonstrated that the skill library significantly amortized human effort, reducing the mean number of human queries per trial for a new task from **4.67** to **0.83** (p = **0.036**) and enabling successful transfer to novel scenes for **4 out of 6** participants.
- The framework proved robust to natural language variations, successfully synthesizing precise policy updates from non-jargon human instructions and showing less sensitivity to prompt perturbations compared to leading VLA models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Interactive program synthesis, when guided by iterative human language corrections, can effectively bridge the 'specification gap' in modular robotics, allowing for granular policy refinement.
> - A persistent skill library enables long-term learning and knowledge transfer, significantly reducing human intervention for new, related tasks by accumulating reusable behaviors.
> - Human corrections are superior to VLM-generated feedback for diagnosing subtle physical and perceptual failures that current automated vision models struggle to detect or infer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements, particularly with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models have demonstrated capabilities in zero-shot manipulation, allowing robots to perform tasks without explicit prior training for every specific scenario. However, a significant challenge within this landscape is the inherent "black-box" nature of these end-to-end VLA policies. Their opaqueness makes them difficult to interpret, adapt, or correct when they inevitably fail in real-world deployments.

Current VLA models often struggle with adaptability due to several factors: they require extensive datasets to rectify even minor behavioral errors, exhibit unpredictable cascading failures in response to slight distribution shifts (e.g., a new object or minor scene perturbation), and lack the structural priors necessary for complex, multi-stage reasoning. Adapting these models, even with parameter-efficient methods, demands significant computational resources and time, rendering the process prohibitively expensive and inaccessible for most end-users who lack the means to "steer" the policy directly through retraining.

In contrast, modular approaches offer an alternative by decoupling high-level reasoning from low-level execution. This paradigm has shown promise for achieving steerable autonomy without requiring extensive expert demonstrations or robot-specific fine-tuning. Recent work has explored the use of large-scale foundation models as orchestrators to compose specialized perception and control tools. However, existing modular systems frequently encounter a "specification gap"; initial natural language instructions often lack the precision to capture the intricate geometric and physical nuances of a task, leading to failures that the system cannot autonomously resolve.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a framework that leverages the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) for interactive program synthesis, combined with human-in-the-loop feedback and a mechanism for persistent learning. It aims to bridge the gap between the broad capabilities of VLAs and the need for interpretable, adaptable, and cost-effective robot control in real-world scenarios. The work positions itself as a development towards empowering non-expert users to correct robot behavior through natural language, mirroring human-to-human teaching, while building on the foundation of LLM-orchestrated modular robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework named ARCHITECT that enables the interactive synthesis and refinement of modular robot policies, thereby addressing the significant adaptability gap observed in existing black-box Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The motivation stems from several critical challenges within current robotic manipulation paradigms:

1. **Addressing the Adaptability Gap of Black-Box VLAs:** Traditional VLA models, despite their impressive zero-shot capabilities, are fundamentally opaque. This "black-box" nature renders them difficult to interpret, adapt, or correct when errors occur. When faced with minor distribution shifts in real-world environments, these models can experience unpredictable, cascading failures that are costly to rectify, often requiring extensive data collection and expensive fine-tuning. The researchers aim to provide an alternative that allows for more granular intervention and correction.

2. **Enabling Interpretability and Localized Feedback:** Current VLAs lack structural priors for complex, multi-stage reasoning. When they fail, it is challenging to identify the root cause, leading to an inability to localize feedback effectively. A key objective is to create a modular architecture where failures can be isolated, and feedback can be applied at the appropriate level of abstraction, thereby enhancing interpretability and facilitating targeted corrections.

3. **Empowering Non-Expert Users through Natural Language Interaction:** The high cost and specialized knowledge required for training and fine-tuning VLA models make them inaccessible for most end-users. This work is motivated by the desire to empower non-experts to "steer" robot policies using natural language, akin to how humans communicate and correct each other. This approach aims to provide an intuitive and accessible interface for robot policy acquisition and refinement.

4. **Overcoming the Specification Gap in Modular Approaches:** While modular approaches offer potential for steerable autonomy, they often suffer from a "specification gap" where initial natural language instructions are insufficient to capture the precise geometric and physical nuances required for task execution. The objective is to develop a system that can iteratively refine policies through human feedback, thereby bridging this gap and allowing for more robust task completion.

5. **Achieving Long-Term Learning and Skill Amortization:** Existing human-in-the-loop robot learning methods often treat corrections as transient, consuming them once and then discarding them. This necessitates re-correction for similar failures in subsequent sessions. A central motivation is to implement a mechanism for persistent learning through a "skill library." This library is designed to distill successful human corrections into reusable, interpretable behaviors, allowing the agent to accumulate knowledge and amortize human effort across diverse tasks and sessions.

6. **Demonstrating Superior Performance and Data Efficiency:** The research aims to empirically demonstrate that a framework incorporating interactive program synthesis with human language corrections and a skill library can outperform state-of-the-art VLA models and other program synthesis baselines on complex, long-horizon tasks. A specific objective is to show that this approach can achieve robust performance with decreasing human intervention over time, offering a data-efficient and steerable alternative to traditional black-box robot learning methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ARCHITECT (Agentic Robot Code with Human In ThE loop CorrecTions) is presented as a framework for robot policy acquisition, treating it as an interactive program synthesis task. The methodology integrates large language models (LLMs) with a human-in-the-loop refinement process and a persistent skill library.

**System Architecture:**
ARCHITECT employs a modular design where a pre-trained LLM (Claude Opus 4.6) acts as an orchestrator, synthesizing executable robot programs. These programs utilize a suite of perception and control tools, abstracting away the low-level complexities. The core loop involves the LLM generating code from a natural language instruction, the robot executing the policy, and a human supervisor providing natural language corrections in response to observed failures or suboptimal behavior. An execution trace from the robot aids the LLM in self-diagnosis and localizing code improvements.

**Language Guided Policy Synthesis:**
The process begins with a natural language instruction describing the desired task. This instruction is embedded into a system prompt that guides the LLM to generate policy code. The generated code is grounded in the current scene through calls to a comprehensive set of control, perception, and proprioception primitives and tools. These tools include capabilities for visual question answering (VQA), querying the robot's proprioceptive state, and command-line interface (CLI) commands to access available skills. The LLM generates functions autoregressively until a complete policy is synthesized.

**Human-in-the-Loop Refinement (Policy Steering via Language Corrections):**
A central aspect of ARCHITECT is its interactive refinement process. When a synthesized policy fails during execution or performs suboptimally, a human observer provides a natural language correction. This approach is designed to be accessible to non-experts, contrasting with direct code editing or manual reward-function tuning. Examples of corrections include precise spatial adjustments ("The grasp was too high, try picking it up from the rim") or task-specific guidance.

**Agentic Refinement and Orchestration:**
To effectively integrate human corrections into code updates, the LLM agent analyzes program execution traces. This trace provides detailed information on which line of code or tool call led to a failure, allowing the model to pinpoint the exact cause (e.g., "fail: drawer not open enough"). With this localized feedback, the LLM then re-synthesizes or modifies the program, incorporating the human's correction and leveraging its tool suite.

**Skill Library and Memory:**
To ensure that corrections are not transient, ARCHITECT introduces a "Skill Library." This library serves as a persistent, structured mechanism for accumulating and reusing knowledge. When a human provides a correction, ARCHITECT synthesizes it into a "skill"—a concise natural language rule or code pattern that encapsulates the adjustment. This skill is added to the library. In subsequent code generation steps, all skills from the library are loaded into the agent's context, alongside the current task instruction and execution trace. This mechanism acts as a form of long-term in-context learning, enabling the agent to "remember" prior corrections, avoid previously identified failure modes, and reuse high-level primitives for future tasks.

**Tool Suite Implementation:**
The tool suite comprises generic and simple primitives across three categories:
- **Control Tools:** These define the robot's action space and are parameterized by geometric information from perception output. They range from global trajectory planning to fine-grained adjustments. Implementation uses cuRobo for motion generation, handling reachability, inverse kinematics, and collision constraints. Examples include `set_gripper_width()`, `move_ee_to_pose()`, and `move_ee_guarded()` with guarded contact.
- **Perception Tools:** These bridge raw sensory data to actionable spatial information, often utilizing off-the-shelf vision models parameterized by natural language queries. Examples include `detect_objects()` (using Grounded SAM2 and AnyGrasp), `get_placement_pose()` (using AnyPlace), `get_vqa_response()` (using GPT-5.4), and `get_keypoints_trajectory()` (using DIFT keypoints and a ReKep-based planner).
- **Proprioception Tools:** These allow the system to query the robot's current state to inform subsequent actions, such as `get_current_ee_pose()`, `get_current_joints()`, and `get_gripper_width()`.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted on a Franka Panda manipulator with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, equipped with a wrist-mounted ZED-mini stereo camera and a ZED 2i scene camera (DROID setup). The LLM used for orchestration was Claude Opus 4.6.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
- **Benchmarking Evaluation:** ARCHITECT was evaluated against state-of-the-art baselines: Code As Policies (CaP), π0 and π0.5 VLA models from Physical Intelligence, and an ablation of ARCHITECT using VLM-generated corrections (ARCHITECT-VLM). Additional baselines like Inner Monologue, ProgPrompt, MolmoAct2, and GR00T N1.7 were also included in supplementary analysis.
- **Tasks:** 8 diverse manipulation tasks were used, encompassing multi-step reasoning, non-prehensile manipulation, and interaction with deformable or articulated objects (e.g., articulated object manipulation, cloth folding, pick-and-place with clutter, long-horizon tasks).
- **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR), Goal Condition Recall (GCR), and Normalized Task Progression (NTP) were used to quantify performance across N=10 episodes per task, with object position/orientation and language instruction phrasing perturbed to assess generalization.
- **Human Evaluation (N=6):** A within-subjects experiment involving two structurally similar tasks (placing a cup, placing a box) assessed the value of human language corrections, skill library transfer to new scenes, and human effort amortization. Skills from Task 1 were optionally carried forward to Task 2 to measure transfer effects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding ARCHITECT's performance, efficiency, and robustness compared to existing methods:

1. **Outperformance of Generalist Policies:**
 - ARCHITECT demonstrated capabilities over a range of manipulation tasks, significantly outperforming state-of-the-art VLA baselines (π0 and π0.5) and the program synthesis baseline Code As Policies (CaP).
 - This advantage was particularly evident in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, interaction with articulated objects (e.g., closing a drawer), deformable objects (e.g., folding cloth), and long-horizon planning (e.g., uncovering and then picking an object).
 - VLAs (π0 and π0.5) exhibited high variance in performance based on object type and sensitivity to the phrasing of language instructions, often struggling with objects not well-represented in their training data.
 - CaP, while also using LLMs for program generation, was found to be brittle to perceptual ambiguity and task underspecification, highlighting the necessity of ARCHITECT's iterative, feedback-driven synthesis.
 - Additional baselines (Inner Monologue, ProgPrompt, MolmoAct2, GR00T N1.7) also generally achieved lower success rates across the diverse task suite when compared to ARCHITECT.

2. **Amortization of Human Effort Across Tasks via Skill Library:**
 - Human evaluation demonstrated that the skill library significantly amortizes human effort. When the skill library was populated from a participant's previous task session, the mean number of human queries per trial for a new task decreased from 4.67 to 0.83 (p = 0.036), with 3 out of 6 participants requiring zero corrections.
 - This reduction in human intervention contrasts with VLA policies, which would require new demonstrations, and CaP, which resynthesizes code from scratch without leveraging prior corrections.

3. **Transfer to Novel Scenes and Objects:**
 - The skill library enabled knowledge transfer to novel scenes and objects within similar task domains. In the human evaluation, without a loaded skill library, no participant succeeded on the second task (SR = 0/6).
 - However, when the skill library accumulated from the first task was loaded (even without further human correction loops), the success rate for the second task rose to 4/6. Success correlated with the density of corrections provided in the preceding task, suggesting that a richer correction trace leads to a more generalizable skill library.

4. **Superiority of Human Corrections over VLM-Generated Feedback:**
 - An ablation study (ARCHITECT-VLM) using automatic VLM-generated corrections instead of human corrections showed consistently lower performance than ARCHITECT-HiTL (human-in-the-loop).
 - Human corrections were capable of addressing failure modes that were challenging for VLMs to detect, such as subtle depth estimation errors leading to incorrect release heights or encoding physical intuitions about object properties (e.g., "close the gripper slowly so the baseball doesn't slip") that are difficult to infer solely from visual observations.

5. **Robustness to Natural Language Input:**
 - Users were able to provide effective corrections using natural, non-jargon language (e.g., "grasp the cup a little bit more to the left," "keep moving down until the pasta box is touching the shelf"). The system successfully synthesized policy updates from these naive instructions.
 - An ablation study on language instruction perturbations revealed that ARCHITECT was robust to variations in phrasing, length, presence of distractors, and levels of specification, whereas a leading VLA (π0.5) showed sensitivity to such changes, particularly with underspecified or overly specified prompts.

The overall results indicate that ARCHITECT's interactive program synthesis, combined with human oversight and persistent skill learning, provides a robust, adaptable, and data-efficient method for robot policy acquisition, surpassing the capabilities of purely autonomous VLA and program synthesis baselines.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of ARCHITECT presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotics and human-robot interaction:

1. **Enabling Steerable and Interpretable Robot Autonomy:** ARCHITECT offers a critical advancement over black-box Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by providing a framework for steerable and interpretable robot control. This means that non-expert users can directly influence and correct robot behavior through natural language, fostering a more intuitive and collaborative human-robot relationship. The modular nature of the synthesized policies allows for clearer understanding of robot reasoning, enhancing trust and simplifying debugging.

2. **Data Efficiency and Reduced Cost of Robot Learning:** The framework significantly reduces the reliance on massive, costly datasets and extensive fine-tuning typically required by end-to-end VLA models. By leveraging sparse human language corrections and synthesizing these into a persistent skill library, ARCHITECT amortizes human effort over time. This makes robot policy acquisition and adaptation more accessible and economically viable for real-world deployments outside of research labs.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability to Novel Scenarios:** ARCHITECT’s human-in-the-loop refinement process allows robots to adapt to subtle distribution shifts and novel failure modes that are challenging for purely autonomous systems. Human intuition can diagnose complex physical and perceptual errors (e.g., noisy depth sensors, object properties) that current vision-language models struggle to ground from pixels alone. This adaptability leads to more robust performance in diverse and unstructured environments.

4. **Long-Term Learning and Skill Accumulation:** The persistent skill library is a key innovation for lifelong robot learning. It enables the system to accumulate a repertoire of reusable, interpretable behaviors from past corrections. This mechanism facilitates knowledge transfer to new, related tasks, reducing the need for repeated corrections and significantly improving sample efficiency over time. This capability is vital for the development of generalist robots that can continuously learn and improve.

5. **Bridging the Specification Gap:** ARCHITECT effectively addresses the "specification gap" inherent in language-guided robotics, where initial natural language instructions often lack the precision for successful execution. Through iterative human feedback and program execution traces, the system refines high-level human intent into precise, executable robot code, enabling the completion of complex, long-horizon tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, primarily related to the performance of underlying low-level primitives, such as grasp generation, depth estimation accuracy, and motion planning. The modular design, while a strength, means that ARCHITECT's overall performance is bounded by the capabilities of these individual modules. Future work is expected to benefit from advancements in these foundational areas as new models become available.

Another limitation is the dependency on the number and quality of human corrections for building a robust skill library. Insufficient or low-quality corrections on initial tasks may hinder the system's ability to generalize effectively to new scenarios. Future research could explore methods to quantify correction quality, optimize the number of corrections required, or integrate more advanced self-correction mechanisms to complement human input.

**Overall Impact:**
ARCHITECT demonstrates a practical and effective pathway towards more steerable, data-efficient, and interpretable robots. By proving that "a few words go a long way" in closing the specification gap and amortizing human supervision, this work offers a compelling alternative to black-box learning, paving the way for more capable and user-friendly robotic systems in real-world applications.
