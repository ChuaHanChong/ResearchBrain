# Turning Video Models into Generalist Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> A new architecture, VERA, enables generalist robot control by decoupling an action-free video generative model for visual planning from a specialized Jacobian Inverse Dynamics Model (J-IDM) for action execution. This approach facilitates zero-shot, language-conditioned manipulation across multiple robot embodiments and complex tasks, outperforming existing end-to-end robot foundation models in reasoning-heavy scenarios.

## Problem

- Developing robot systems that generalize to novel tasks, embodiments, and environments, similar to the capabilities observed in large language models.
- The lack of web-scale, paired robot action data severely limits the generalization capabilities of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World-Action Models (WAMs).
- Existing Inverse Dynamics Models (IDMs) struggle with generalization, data efficiency, and scalability to high-dimensional action spaces, failing to faithfully translate visual plans to actions.

## Method

- A decoupled architecture (VERA) separates visual planning using an action-free video generative model from action execution handled by an embodiment-specific ==Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM)==.
- A novel Jacobian-IDM (J-IDM) is introduced, leveraging an image-space Jacobian to translate predicted visual transitions (via optical flow) into robot actions, trained with a joint forward-inverse objective.
- VERA employs a closed-loop, receding-horizon control: the video planner generates future frames, J-IDM converts a prefix into actions, the robot executes, and the cycle repeats with updated observations.

## Results

- VERA demonstrated zero-shot, language-conditioned manipulation on a real 7-DoF Panda arm, showing robustness to environmental variations and successfully performing complex tasks requiring visual and linguistic reasoning, including those with occlusions.
- A single video planner, when post-trained on diverse data and paired with respective J-IDMs, generalized across different embodiments (7-DoF Panda arm and 16-DoF Allegro hand) for contact-rich manipulation tasks like dexterous cube reorientation.
- J-IDM showed superior scalability with increasing DoFs and approximately **2x** better data efficiency compared to direct IDM baselines, leading to improved action reconstruction accuracy and higher closed-loop task success rates on simulated environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the visual planner from action execution preserves the video model's strong visuospatial reasoning capabilities, which can be compromised in end-to-end architectures.
> - Integrating classical robotics principles, specifically the Jacobian, into a deep learning framework can create a data-efficient and scalable inverse dynamics model for faithful video-to-action translation.
> - The challenge of scarce action-labeled data can be mitigated by using abundant action-free video data for planning and more readily available, task-agnostic data (potentially self-play generated) for training an embodiment-specific action translator.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic intelligence faces the challenge of developing systems capable of controlling a diverse range of robot embodiments to execute novel tasks in unfamiliar environments. This objective requires significant generalization capabilities.

Current approaches often draw parallels with the success of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), aiming to transfer their generality to robotics through Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models typically extend pretrained vision-language backbones with action outputs, often through fine-tuning on action-labeled data. However, a key limitation for VLAs in robotics is the absence of web-scale, paired robot action data, unlike the abundant text and image data available for LLMs and VLMs. Consequently, achieving robust generalization across new tasks, embodiments, and environments has proven challenging for VLAs, as robot actions appear distinct and difficult to generalize from comparatively small datasets.

An alternative strategy involves using video generative models as a robotics backbone. These models can generate "visual plans," which are imagined videos depicting task completion, and are not inherently constrained by a specific robot's action space. Video data is also more abundant than action-labeled data. The challenge then shifts to translating these visual plans into executable robot actions. Prior work in this direction includes integrating actions directly into the video backbone through joint video-and-action prediction (World-Action Models, WAMs) or action-conditioned world models coupled with inference-time search. While these methods have shown results, they still require the scarce data type of paired video, action, and text. Other approaches involve direct Inverse Dynamics Models (IDMs) that regress actions from pairs of generated frames using unconstrained neural networks or hand-crafted methods. These direct IDMs have demonstrated limitations in generalization with limited action data and exhibit reduced accuracy as the complexity of the action space, i.e., the number of degrees of freedom (DoFs), increases.

This research paper positions itself within this landscape by exploring a decoupled approach. It proposes maintaining the video generative model as an action-free planner operating in observation space and introducing a separate, embodiment-specific IDM to translate the video plans into robot actions. This separation aims to address the data scarcity and generalization challenges faced by more tightly coupled VLA and WAM architectures, particularly by developing a more robust and scalable IDM.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to explore and validate a decoupled architecture for robot control, where an action-free video generative model serves as a general visual planner and a separate, embodiment-specific Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) translates these visual plans into robot actions. This approach is motivated by several considerations:

1. **Overcoming Data Scarcity in Action-Labeled Data**: Existing robot foundation models (VLAs, WAMs) often require large datasets of paired video, action, and text. Such datasets are expensive and difficult to collect at scale, limiting the models' ability to generalize. By decoupling, the video planner can leverage abundant, action-free video data, and the IDM can be trained with more readily available, task-agnostic video-action data, potentially generated via self-play.
2. **Achieving Embodiment Agnosticism for the Planner**: A key aim is for the video planner to remain embodiment-agnostic, capable of generating visual plans for various robots without requiring retraining. This modularity allows the same video model to be shared across multiple robot platforms simply by pairing it with different embodiment-specific IDMs.
3. **Facilitating Model Interchangeability and Improvement**: The decoupled design permits independent iteration and improvement of both the video planner and the IDM. For instance, stronger video generative models can be integrated as planners without necessitating retraining of the IDM, and vice-versa.
4. **Addressing Limitations of Prior Inverse Dynamics Models**: Previous IDMs, particularly those using direct regression with unconstrained neural networks, have shown weaknesses. They often generalize poorly with limited action data and their performance degrades significantly with increasing action space dimensionality (DoFs). A core motivation is to develop an IDM that is faithful in translation, data-efficient, and scales effectively to high-dimensional action spaces.
5. **Enabling Zero-Shot and Generalizable Robot Control**: Ultimately, the work aims to demonstrate that this decoupled architecture can enable generalizable robot control, including zero-shot performance on new tasks and environments, and applicability across different robot embodiments. This includes complex manipulation tasks with varying object properties, camera perspectives, and detailed language instructions.

To achieve these objectives, the researchers propose a carefully designed IDM, termed the Jacobian-IDM (J-IDM), which leverages classical robotics principles within a deep learning framework, and integrate it into a closed-loop policy called VERA (Video-to-Embodied Robot Action Model).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Video-to-Embodied Robot Action Model (VERA) integrates a video world model for visual planning with a specialized Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) for action translation, operating in a closed-loop fashion.

#### 3.1. The Video-to-Action Problem

The central challenge addressed is the translation of visual plans, generated in pixel space by a video world model (π_vid), into embodiment-specific robot actions (e.g., position, velocity, torque commands). A video model predicts a sequence of future observations (ô_t+1..., ô_t+M) given an observation history and a goal. The task is to infer the actions (â_t:t+M-1) required to execute this visual plan.
The desiderata for an effective IDM are faithfulness (actions reproduce predicted visual transitions), data-efficiency (training with limited, potentially self-play, data), and scalability with increasing Degrees of Freedom (DoFs). Existing IDMs, such as hand-crafted methods or Direct IDMs (D-IDMs) that directly regress actions from image pairs, were found to lack faithfulness under data constraints and when dealing with complex, high-DoF embodiments.

#### 3.2. The Jacobian Inverse Dynamics Model (J-IDM)

To address the limitations of prior IDMs, the paper introduces the Jacobian-IDM (J-IDM).
- **Embodiment Jacobian in Image Space**: Drawing inspiration from classical robotics, where the embodiment Jacobian J_i(o) relates infinitesimal action perturbations (δa) to 3D point motion (δx_i ≈ J_i(o) δa), the J-IDM learns an image-space Jacobian field. An image-conditioned transformer, J_θ, outputs a dense field J_θ(·, o) that assigns to every pixel p a 2×n matrix. This matrix linearizes how an action increment δa moves that pixel, resulting in a pixel motion δp = J_θ(p, o) δa. This formulation replaces direct 3D point observation with readily available 2D pixel-space information.
- **Optical Flow for Observation Change**: The per-pixel observation change (δp) is obtained in practice from an off-the-shelf optical-flow estimator (e.g., RAFT, AllTracker) applied to consecutive video frames (o_t, o_t+1). This optical flow, denoted as v_t, serves as the target for pixel motion.
- **Joint Forward-Inverse Training Objective**: J_θ is trained on (o_t, δa_t, o_t+1) tuples. The loss function is a combination of two terms:
 1. A "forward" (pixel-flow) term: The Charbonnier objective ρ(x) on the difference between the predicted pixel motion (J_θ(p, o_t) δa_t) and the estimated optical flow v_t(p).
 2. An "inverse" (action reconstruction) term: The squared L2 norm of the difference between the actual action δa_t and the action reconstructed from the optical flow using the λ-regularized pseudoinverse of the predicted Jacobian (J_†,λ_θ(p, o_t) v_t(p)).
 This joint objective enforces both that the predicted Jacobian accurately models the forward mapping from actions to pixel motion and that actions can be faithfully reconstructed from observed pixel motion.
- **Inference**: At inference time, given two consecutive predicted frames (ô_t, ô_t+1) from the video planner, their optical flow v_t is extracted. The corresponding action (ĉ_δa_t) is then recovered by inverting the Jacobian using the λ-regularized pseudoinverse: ĉ_δa_t = J_†,λ_θ(ô_t) v_t. This minimizes the error between predicted pixel motion from δa and the observed optical flow, with L2 regularization on δa.

#### 3.3. Video World Model as a Closed-Loop Robot Planner (VERA)

VERA operates as a receding-horizon controller, integrating the video world model with the J-IDM.
- **Action-Free Robot Post-Training**: The video planner is instantiated from a pretrained Large Video Planner (LVP) model, such as a 14B Wan-family video diffusion transformer. It is then lightly adapted (post-trained) on robot-specific video-only data. Crucially, this post-training objective is purely generative video prediction, with no action head, ensuring the planner remains action-free and can be generalized across embodiments. Modifications include video-to-video fine-tuning for autoregressive rollouts (N context frames in, M future frames out) and a multi-view variant that tiles camera inputs into a single canvas.
- **Executing on a Visual Chunk**: The planner generates M future frames. However, the controller commits to executing actions corresponding to only the first K predicted frames (the "committed prefix"). The J-IDM converts this visual prefix (o_t, ô_t+1..., ô_t+K_keep) into a chunk of robot actions (â_t:t+rK_keep-1) by applying the Jacobian inversion (Eq. 6) independently to each adjacent pair of frames within the chunk, using the current observation o_t as the anchor. This action chunking allows the planner to reason over a longer visual look-ahead while the controller remains grounded with frequent feedback.
- **Closed-Loop Replanning**: After executing the action chunk, the robot observes new frames. These new observations are appended to its history, and the planner is queried again to generate a new visual plan, repeating the cycle. The execution horizon K manages the trade-off between smooth local execution (longer K) and reduced drift (smaller K).

#### 3.4. Experiment Setup

VERA was evaluated in both simulated and real-world environments:
- **Simulation**: PushT-Sim (planar pushing, 2-DoF), MimicGen Panda (7-DoF arm manipulation), and Allegro-Sim (16-DoF dexterous cube reorientation).
- **Real-world**: Panda-Real (zero-shot language-conditioned manipulation, 7-DoF arm, post-trained on DROID data) and Allegro-Real (RGB-only 16-DoF in-hand cube reorientation).
The evaluation metrics included closed-loop success rates and task progress.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides several findings regarding the performance of VERA and the efficacy of the Jacobian Inverse Dynamics Model (J-IDM).

#### 4.1. Faithful Video-to-Action Translation

The J-IDM demonstrated the ability to faithfully translate video chunks predicted by the video world model into executable robot action chunks. Qualitative observations across various environments, including a real-world Panda arm and Allegro hand, showed that robot executions closely aligned with the generated visual plans (Figures 3, 4). This faithful translation is identified as a foundational component enabling the system's visuospatial reasoning, prompt-following, and generalization capabilities.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot, Prompt-Following Manipulation on a Panda Arm

VERA demonstrated zero-shot, language-conditioned manipulation on a real Panda arm. The system exhibited robustness to variations in lighting, camera configurations, and object orientations. It successfully performed manipulations guided by complex prompts, including those requiring visual and linguistic reasoning, such as pressing a hidden button visible only from one of three cameras (Figures 1, 3).

#### 4.3. Multi-Embodiment Generalization

A single video planner, finetuned across diverse datasets including DROID (Panda arm) and Allegro hand simulations and real-world data, was capable of generating effective visual plans for both embodiments. When paired with their respective embodiment-specific J-IDMs, VERA successfully controlled both a 7-DoF Panda arm and a 16-DoF Allegro hand for contact-rich tasks like in-hand cube reorientation (Figure 4). The cube reorientation task, involving multi-stage dexterous finger motions, was completed despite its complexity for a purely visual system.

#### 4.4. J-IDM Performance under Data and Complexity Constraints

Controlled experiments comparing J-IDM with unstructured Direct IDM (D-IDM) baselines, including a UniPi*-style model and a flow-conditioned D-IDM, highlighted the benefits of the Jacobian-based constraint:
- **Scalability with DoFs**: A study using a 2D "toy finger" environment showed that as the number of DoFs increased, J-IDM was the only model that maintained faithful action reconstruction. D-IDMs exhibited substantial degradation (Figure 5a,b).
- **Data Efficiency**: At a fixed DoF (5-joint fingers), J-IDM demonstrated approximately a 2x improvement in data efficiency compared to D-IDMs, requiring less training data to achieve comparable accuracy (Figure 5c).
- **Action Reconstruction Accuracy**: Across multiple simulation environments (Allegro-Sim, PushT-Sim, 5-joint fingers), J-IDM achieved the lowest action-reconstruction Mean Squared Error (MSE) on held-out visual transitions. While competitive, it was slightly higher than D-IDM+Flow on Panda-Sim (Table 2).
- **Closed-Loop Success**: The improved fidelity of J-IDM translated to higher downstream task success. With the video planner held constant, J-IDM consistently outperformed UniPi* on every closed-loop task in simulated environments (Table 1), indicating that accurate action translation is critical for policy performance.

#### 4.5. Comparison with Robotic Foundation Models (VLAs and WAMs)

VERA was compared against state-of-the-art VLA and WAM baselines (π_0.5, DreamZero) on an in-house Panda manipulation suite:
- **Basic Tasks**: On fundamental tasks like "push A" or "pick up B", DreamZero achieved 90% success, VERA 60%, and π_0.5 30%. VERA's failures were often attributed to the video-to-action translation step, where the generated visual plans were correct, but the IDM lacked sufficient fidelity. In contrast, π_0.5 frequently failed by disregarding instructions and acting on incorrect objects.
- **Challenging Tasks**: On reasoning-intensive tasks, including location-based prompts (e.g., "push the button on top of the paper") and semantic-based prompts (e.g., "push the button matching the wrench’s color"), VERA significantly outperformed both DreamZero and π_0.5 (Figure 6). This suggests that decoupling the planner from the IDM allows VERA to better preserve and leverage the reasoning capabilities of the underlying video model.
- **Reasoning through Occlusions**: In the "find the button" challenge, where a button was hidden behind a wall and visible from only one of three cameras, VERA produced coherent plans to locate and interact with the button (Figures 3, 7). DreamZero and π_0.5 struggled with navigation and localization, with DreamZero's own dreamed futures often failing to produce a viable plan. This supports the hypothesis that preserving the action-free video branch helps retain strong video-model reasoning abilities that might otherwise be compromised in end-to-end architectures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a viable and modular alternative route towards generalist robot control by decoupling visual planning from action execution. The proposed Video-to-Embodied Robot Action Model (VERA), built upon a large action-free video generative model and a novel Jacobian Inverse Dynamics Model (J-IDM), offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Modular and Scalable Architecture**: The decoupled design is a central contribution. By separating the visual planner from the embodiment-specific action translator, the system gains modularity. This allows the video planner to remain general and embodiment-agnostic, potentially using a single model across diverse robot morphologies, while specific robot control is handled by dedicated IDMs. This modularity can simplify development and deployment, as improvements to the video model do not necessitate retraining the IDM, and vice-versa.
2. **Enhanced Generalization and Zero-Shot Capability**: The results demonstrate VERA's ability to perform zero-shot, language-conditioned manipulation tasks in varied real-world environments and across different robot embodiments (e.g., a 7-DoF Panda arm and a 16-DoF Allegro hand). This indicates progress towards achieving the goal of generalizable robot control that can adapt to unseen tasks and configurations without extensive retraining.
3. **Data Efficiency and Scalability of Control**: The Jacobian-IDM (J-IDM) is shown to be data-efficient and to scale robustly with increasing action dimensionality (DoFs). This is a critical factor for practical robot learning, as collecting large, high-dimensional, action-labeled datasets is challenging. By requiring less data for faithful action translation, the J-IDM can make learning control policies for complex robots more feasible.
4. **Preservation of High-Level Reasoning**: A key finding is that decoupling the visual planner from the action execution allows the video model to retain its inherent visuospatial and reasoning capabilities more effectively than end-to-end VLA or WAMs. VERA's ability to excel at challenging, reasoning-heavy tasks, including those with occlusions and semantic instructions, suggests that the video model's "dreams" are better preserved when not constrained by an action head during planning. This implies a pathway to building more intelligent and context-aware robot policies.
5. **A New Paradigm for Robot Foundation Models**: The work validates that faithful video-to-action translation, when combined with a strong video planner, can be a competitive, and in some cases superior, approach compared to directly learning action outputs within large multimodal models. This presents a promising direction for future research in robot foundation models.

**Limitations and Future Directions**:
The paper acknowledges limitations, including the continued requirement for robot-specific video data for planner post-training, reliance on off-the-shelf optical-flow trackers for J-IDM supervision, and the inability to reason about force-based control from RGB input alone. Additionally, action recovery can degrade when pixel motion in the predicted transition is minimal.

Future work is outlined to address these limitations, including exploring sim-to-real transfer for J-IDMs to reduce the need for real-robot action data, co-training planners across human and robot demonstrations, and developing embodiment-conditioned Jacobians capable of serving multiple morphologies. These directions point towards further enhancing the generality and practical applicability of the VERA framework.
