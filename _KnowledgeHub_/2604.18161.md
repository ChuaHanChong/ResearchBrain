# Does "Do Differentiable Simulators Give Better Policy Gradients?'' Give Better Policy Gradients?

> [!summary] Summary
> A critical re-evaluation of composite gradient methods for reinforcement learning with differentiable simulators introduces two new approaches: Discontinuity Detection Composite Gradient (DDCG) and Stepwise Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW-H). DDCG robustly handles discontinuous dynamics with a unified hyperparameter, while IVW-H provides efficient variance control, collectively improving performance in empirical-bias settings and practical continuous control tasks respectively.

## Problem

- Policy gradient methods using differentiable simulators can leverage 1st-order gradients for lower variance, but discontinuities (e.g., contacts, friction) introduce bias.
- Existing composite methods like Alpha-order Batched Gradient (AoBG) suffer from empirical bias where 1st-order gradients appear low-variance but are inaccurate, and AoBG itself is prone to noise and hyperparameter sensitivity.
- There is a lack of clear understanding regarding whether empirical bias or variance is the primary practical obstacle in various reinforcement learning settings, leading to inconsistent performance of composite gradient strategies.

## Method

- Introduces ==Discontinuity Detection== Composite Gradient (**DDCG**), which uses a lightweight statistical test to detect regions of discontinuity and adaptively switch between 0th-order and 1st-order gradient estimators.
- Develops Stepwise Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW-H), a per-step, per-action dimension inverse-variance weighting scheme that efficiently controls variance by leveraging batched actors.
- The statistical test in DDCG is designed to be computationally more efficient (O(d) times) and robust to small sample sizes compared to prior methods like AoBG.

## Results

- DDCG robustly detected discontinuities and adaptively switched estimators in empirical-bias regimes (e.g., Sigmoid, Ball with Wall, Friction, Tennis tasks) with a unified hyperparameter (`c=**0.3**`), outperforming or matching AoBG, which required extensive task-specific tuning.
- IVW-H consistently achieved strong performance and often improved upon standard Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) and other baselines (e.g., GIPPO) in high-contact MuJoCo-style tasks (CartPole, Hopper, Ant).
- AoBG did not consistently outperform the IVW baseline in practical continuous control tasks even with extensive tuning, suggesting that empirical bias was not the dominant performance bottleneck in these specific environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Empirical bias, where 1st-order gradients have spuriously low empirical variance but high true error, is a significant issue in explicitly discontinuous environments and can be reliably detected and mitigated by DDCG with robust performance.
> - For practical continuous control tasks (e.g., MuJoCo environments with contact dynamics), effective variance control at the per-step level (as achieved by IVW-H) often proves to be a more dominant factor for performance than explicit bias detection.
> - Previous composite gradient methods, like AoBG, exhibited limitations such as hyperparameter sensitivity and conservative behavior with small samples, which were addressed by the more robust DDCG.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

In reinforcement learning, policy gradient methods are a category of algorithms that optimize a parameterized policy by estimating the gradient of the expected return. When the environment is treated as a black box, 0th-order estimators such as REINFORCE are commonly employed. While broadly applicable, these estimators often exhibit high variance and low sample efficiency. The availability of a differentiable simulator can enable the use of 1st-order gradient estimators (e.g., via the reparameterization trick), which typically reduce variance and accelerate learning.

However, real-world systems frequently involve dynamics that are nonsmooth, such as contacts or friction. These discontinuities can introduce bias into 1st-order gradient estimates, undermining their reliability for optimization. Prior research has explored combining 0th-order and 1st-order gradients to leverage the strengths of both. Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) is a principled method for mixing these estimators, aiming to reduce the overall variance. However, specific challenges arise in discontinuous environments where 1st-order gradients may exhibit large errors but spuriously low empirical variance, a phenomenon termed "empirical bias" by Suh et al. (2022). This can lead IVW to incorrectly overweight biased 1st-order estimates.

Suh et al. (2022) proposed Alpha-order Batched Gradient (AoBG) to address empirical bias. AoBG augments IVW with a discontinuity-detection rule based on confidence intervals derived from the REINFORCE estimator. However, the REINFORCE estimator is known for its high noise, which can result in broad confidence intervals, reduced sample efficiency, and a requirement for extensive task-specific hyperparameter tuning. Moreover, previous reports indicated limited robust success of AoBG on standard robotics control benchmarks.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by critically reassessing the efficacy of composite gradient methods, particularly AoBG, and by investigating whether empirical bias or variance is the primary obstacle to practical performance in different settings. It introduces two contributions: Discontinuity Detection Composite Gradient (DDCG) as a more robust alternative to AoBG in explicitly discontinuous regimes, and a Stepwise Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW-H) as an efficient variance control mechanism for practical continuous control tasks. The work therefore aims to refine the understanding and application of differentiable simulators in reinforcement learning, especially when dealing with discontinuous dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research is motivated by a critical evaluation of existing composite gradient methods, particularly in the context of policy learning with differentiable simulators that may exhibit discontinuities. The authors identify two primary goals:

First, to reassess existing work, specifically the claims and practical performance of Alpha-order Batched Gradient (AoBG) (Suh et al., 2022), in scenarios where empirical bias is known to arise. The paper aims to re-establish the existence of the finite-sample bias phenomenon, where 1st-order gradients may appear to have low variance yet be inaccurate. A key motivation here is to develop a method that can detect these problematic regions and adaptively switch between estimators more robustly and with less hyperparameter tuning than AoBG. This leads to the introduction of Discontinuity Detection Composite Gradient (DDCG), which employs a lightweight statistical test to determine when to rely on 1st-order information. The objective is to show that DDCG can achieve comparable or superior results to AoBG, with improved robustness to hyperparameters and reliable behavior in small-sample regimes.

Second, to investigate whether empirical bias, which DDCG is designed to address, is indeed the primary obstacle in practical robotics control settings. Prior studies reported limited performance or incomplete realizations of inverse-variance mixing strategies in these domains. The motivation is to isolate the role of variance control by providing a clear and efficient per-time-step implementation of inverse-variance weighting, termed IVW-H. The objective here is to demonstrate whether stabilizing variance at the step level can be sufficient for strong performance in standard robotics tasks, even without explicit discontinuity detection, thereby suggesting that variance control might often be the dominant practical concern over empirical bias handling in these environments.

Collectively, the paper aims to contribute to a clearer understanding of the challenges posed by discontinuous dynamics in differentiable simulators and to provide more effective and robust policy gradient estimation strategies for various application contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper develops and evaluates two main approaches: the Discontinuity Detection Composite Gradient (DDCG) and the Stepwise Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW-H). Both build upon the foundation of mixing 0th-order (REINFORCE) and 1st-order (reparameterization trick) gradient estimators.

**Background on Gradient Estimators and Mixing:**
The core idea is to combine ˆg₀ (unbiased but high variance) and ˆg₁ (low variance but potentially biased in discontinuous regions) into a composite estimator ˆg_α = αˆg₁ + (1 − α)ˆg₀. Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) theoretically minimizes variance by setting α = V[ˆg₀] / (V[ˆg₀] + V[ˆg₁]). However, empirical bias (where ˆg₁ has low empirical variance but large true error) can cause IVW to fail. AoBG, a prior method, attempted to mitigate this by modifying α based on a bias measure B and a confidence term ε around the REINFORCE estimator, but it suffered from noise and hyperparameter sensitivity.

#### 3.1. Discontinuity Detection Composite Gradient (DDCG)

DDCG maintains the IVW structure but introduces a statistical test to gate the use of the 1st-order term. This test checks two assumptions:
- **(A1) Reliable Variance:** The empirical variance of the 1st-order gradient is close to its true variance.
- **(A2) Local Smoothness:** The objective function f is locally well-behaved (e.g., near-quadratic), ensuring the 1st-order gradient is accurate and low-variance.

The statistical test combines these assumptions. It relies on a Lipschitz-like condition on gradient changes, ∥∇f(x) − ∇f(y)∥ ≈ L∥x − y∥, and compares the empirical gradient variance (ˆv) to the variance expected from a locally quadratic model of the function. The core inequality is:
ˆv + ε_v ≥ (2(1 − c)V[f(x)] / σ²) − 2∥∇f∥²
Here, ε_v accounts for estimation error in ˆv, V[f(x)] is the variance of the function values, σ² is the variance of the sampled parameters, and ∥∇f∥² is the squared mean gradient. The parameter `c` relaxes the requirement for a perfectly quadratic function; a smaller `c` implies stricter smoothness. All quantities in the inequality are computed from the same finite batch of samples.

**Adaptive Weighting:** If the test in the inequality holds, DDCG assumes (A1) and (A2) are met, and it applies the inverse-variance-optimal weight ˆα_opt. If the test fails, indicating potential heavy-tailed or discontinuous behavior making IVW unreliable, DDCG reverts to a purely 0th-order estimator by setting α = 0.

**Comparison with AoBG:** A key methodological difference is that DDCG's test leverages variances of function values and gradients, which is shown to be O(d) times more efficient than AoBG's approach of constructing confidence intervals using the noisy `d log p(τ;θ)/dθ` terms from the REINFORCE estimator, especially in high-dimensional settings (d).

#### 3.2. Stepwise Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW-H)

IVW-H implements a per-step, per-action dimension inverse-variance weighting scheme. For each time step `t`, actor `n`, and action dimension `a`, it estimates empirical variances ˆv₀,t,a and ˆv₁,t,a across parallel rollouts (actors). These variances are then used to compute a per-step, per-dimension IVW weight:
ˆα_t,a = ˆv₀,t,a / (ˆv₀,t,a + ˆv₁,t,a)
The combined gradient is then formed element-wise: ˆg_α,t,n,a = ˆα_t,a ˆg₁,t,n,a + (1 − ˆα_t,a)ˆg₀,t,n,a. This combined gradient is then backpropagated through the policy network. A crucial aspect is that IVW-H utilizes batched actors at each time step to obtain variance estimates without requiring additional simulator calls, making it computationally efficient. This contrasts with some parameter-space IVW implementations that require extra simulator evaluations.

**Experimental Setup:** The methodology includes two main experimental parts:
1. **Empirical-bias regimes:** Replicating and re-evaluating AoBG experiments (Ball with Wall, Momentum Transfer, Pushing, Friction, Tennis tasks) to validate DDCG's robustness in explicitly discontinuous settings.
2. **Practical continuous control:** Evaluating IVW-H on standard MuJoCo-style tasks (CartPole, Hopper, Ant) to assess the role of variance control in environments with contact dynamics, comparing it against 0th-order, 1st-order, AoBG, IVW, and GIPPO. For these tasks, contact stiffness was sometimes elevated to probe harder contact scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were divided into two primary categories to assess DDCG in empirical-bias settings and IVW-H in practical continuous control.

#### 4.1. Part I: Empirical-Bias Regimes (Re-evaluating AoBG and Validating DDCG)

- **Function Optimization Tasks (Sigmoid and Quadratic Functions):**
 - **Sigmoid Function:** With high sample sizes, IVW demonstrated bias near sharp transitions (small temperature T) due to overconfidence in 1st-order gradients. Both AoBG and DDCG detected these near-discontinuities and reduced the α weighting parameter towards the 0th-order estimator, thereby lowering estimation error. However, with small sample sizes, AoBG's fixed γ hyperparameter became overly conservative, causing α to drop to zero and underutilizing available 1st-order gradient information. DDCG, using a fixed `c=0.3`, maintained robust detection and performance across varying temperatures and sample sizes without requiring re-tuning.
 - **Quadratic Function:** DDCG also demonstrated robust performance on quadratic functions across different sample sizes, indicating its general applicability.

- **Differentiable Simulation Tasks (Landscape Analysis, Trajectory Optimization, Policy Optimization):**

 - **Landscape Analysis (Ball with Wall, Momentum Transfer):**
 - For larger sample sizes (N=1000), IVW exhibited bias near collision events. Both AoBG (with tuned γ) and DDCG (with `c=0.3`) detected these discontinuities, shifting α to rely more on the 0th-order component.
 - For smaller sample sizes (N=10), AoBG's fixed γ led to conservative behavior where α often dropped to zero, underutilizing 1st-order information. DDCG consistently detected discontinuities and adapted α robustly with the same parameters.
 - Analysis of error decomposition showed that near discontinuities, 1st-order gradients exhibited large bias, which dominated the total error. DDCG successfully mitigated this by switching estimators.

 - **Trajectory Optimization (Pushing, Friction):**
 - **Pushing:** Under soft collisions, both AoBG and DDCG leveraged 1st-order gradients. However, with low samples, AoBG became conservative, failing to utilize 1st-order information effectively. DDCG maintained performance. Under stiff collisions, both methods converged by selecting α values near IVW, suggesting variance rather than bias was the primary issue in this specific setup.
 - **Friction:** The 1st-order estimator and IVW stalled once friction thresholds were crossed. AoBG (with re-tuned γ) and DDCG detected these transitions and shifted weight to the 0th-order estimator, enabling continued optimization. DDCG demonstrated robustness against small-sample noise, whereas AoBG required re-tuning.

 - **Policy Optimization (Tennis):** In this task involving ball-paddle impacts, 1st-order and IVW methods stalled due to discontinuities. Both AoBG (with tuned γ) and DDCG (with `c=0.3`) successfully detected nonsmooth regions, reverted to 0th-order updates, and continued to improve performance, achieving similar final results.

#### 4.2. Part II: Practical Continuous Control (IVW-H)

- **MuJoCo-style Tasks (CartPole, Hopper, Ant) with Elevated Contact Stiffness:**
 - **CartPole:** 0th-order baseline underperformed, while 1st-order, AoBG, IVW, IVW-H, and GIPPO achieved comparable final rewards.
 - **Ant:** IVW-H attained the highest returns. AoBG performed similarly to IVW (not outperforming it even with tuning), and both were superior to 1st-order-only and 0th-order-only methods.
 - **Hopper:** 0th-order surpassed 1st-order-only. GIPPO demonstrated optimization failure. AoBG and IVW performed well, with IVW-H showing further improvement over IVW.
 - Overall, in these high-contact tasks, variance control via stepwise IVW-H appeared to be more critical than explicit bias detection. IVW-H consistently matched or improved upon standard IVW and the GIPPO baseline without explicit bias detection.
 - Sensitivity analysis on AoBG's γ parameter in high-contact Ant and Hopper tasks revealed that even with extensive tuning, AoBG did not consistently outperform the IVW baseline, suggesting that empirical bias was not the dominant performance bottleneck in these specific environments.

#### 4.3. Summary of Experimental Findings

- **Empirical-bias settings:** DDCG provided robust discontinuity detection and adaptive estimator switching with a unified hyperparameter (`c=0.3`), outperforming or matching AoBG, which required extensive task-specific tuning of γ (ranging from 5x10⁻³ to 10⁸) and showed conservative behavior with small samples.
- **Practical continuous control:** IVW-H consistently achieved strong performance on MuJoCo-style benchmarks, often improving upon standard IVW and outperforming or matching other baselines. This suggests that in these practical settings, effective variance control at the step level often dominates the need for explicit empirical-bias detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning with differentiable simulators by refining the understanding and application of composite gradient estimators, particularly in the presence of discontinuous dynamics.

The introduction of **Discontinuity Detection Composite Gradient (DDCG)** addresses a recognized limitation of prior work like AoBG. By demonstrating that DDCG can achieve robust performance with a unified hyperparameter across various explicitly discontinuous tasks, even with small sample sizes, the paper offers a more practical and reliable method for mitigating empirical bias. This is significant for developing more stable learning algorithms in simulators where exact continuity cannot be guaranteed, potentially reducing the arduous process of hyperparameter tuning for practitioners. The finding that DDCG's statistical test is computationally more efficient than AoBG's approach further enhances its practical appeal.

The development and strong performance of **Stepwise Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW-H)** in continuous control benchmarks is also noteworthy. The results suggest that for certain classes of robotics tasks, such as MuJoCo-style environments, effective variance control at the per-step, per-action dimension level can be a more critical factor for learning success than explicit bias-detection mechanisms. This finding has implications for the design of future policy gradient algorithms, redirecting focus towards robust variance stabilization strategies when contact dynamics are present but may not induce extreme empirical bias. IVW-H's computational efficiency, achieved by leveraging batched actors for variance estimation, makes it a viable approach for real-world applications.

Collectively, this work provides a clearer delineation of when bias-focused mechanisms (like DDCG) are necessary and when variance control (like IVW-H) might be sufficient. This distinction can guide researchers in selecting and developing appropriate gradient estimation techniques based on the specific characteristics and potential discontinuities of their simulation environments. The potential impact includes more stable and sample-efficient training of policies for complex robotic systems, where differentiable simulators are increasingly utilized. Future research could expand the task suite and diagnostics to further solidify these distinctions, contributing to more generalized and robust policy learning frameworks.
