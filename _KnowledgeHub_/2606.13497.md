# SPARC: Reliable Spatial Annotations from Robot Demonstrations at Scale

> [!summary] Summary
> SPARC is a risk-aware framework that automatically generates structured spatial annotations from robot demonstrations, assigning a continuous reliability score to each. The method achieves 80.2% accuracy for interacted object localization on the new IA-Bench benchmark and leads to significant improvements in downstream embodied spatial reasoning and real-world robot manipulation task success rates.

## Problem

- Automated pipelines for generating spatial annotations from robot demonstrations often lack reliable quality signals, leading to high-confidence but incorrect labels in cluttered scenes.
- The unreliability of existing methods forces a scale-quality tradeoff, where researchers must either accept noisy labels or discard large amounts of potentially useful data.
- Existing alternatives like human review are expensive, and simulation environments suffer from a reality gap, while prior automated methods underutilize rich spatio-temporal and proprioceptive cues.
- There is a lack of benchmarks capable of assessing interacted object identity and its spatio-temporal properties within dynamic robot demonstrations.

## Method

- **SPARC** introduces a three-stage pipeline that decomposes long demonstrations into ==subtasks== using gripper signals and language-based parsing via LLMs, then detects and tracks object candidates.
- It leverages multi-modal signals including phase-aware object motion, 3D gripper proximity, robot-overlap filtering, and appearance-based detector confidence to identify manipulated objects.
- The framework computes a continuous, physically-grounded reliability score by combining these cues, allowing for fine-grained control over annotation quality and enabling selective prediction.
- A new benchmark, IA-Bench, is introduced with 1,748 human-annotated start and target locations across diverse robot demonstrations to evaluate interaction-aware object localization.

## Results

- SPARC achieved an **80.2%** accuracy for interacted object localization on the IA-Bench benchmark, surpassing detection-only baselines by over 22 percentage points (**58.1%**).
- It demonstrated superior quality-coverage tradeoff, retaining **77.6%** of samples at **90%** precision and **58%** at **95%** precision, significantly outperforming baselines that retained only **20%** at **95%** precision.
- VLM models fine-tuned on SPARC-annotated data achieved state-of-the-art grounding performance on several object-grounding and pointing benchmarks, and showed improved scaling behavior over detection-only data.
- Real-world robot policies trained with SPARC annotations more than doubled the success rate (**31%** vs **12%**) compared to policies trained with detection-annotated data in challenging, cluttered tabletop manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing automatic spatio-temporal annotation as a selective-prediction problem, by combining diverse cues like object motion, gripper proximity, and robot overlap, provides a more robust reliability signal than appearance-based confidence alone.
> - A continuous, calibrated reliability score allows users to explicitly control the tradeoff between annotation quality (precision) and dataset scale (coverage) without manual review.
> - High-quality, spatio-temporally grounded annotations derived from robot interactions are crucial for significantly enhancing embodied spatial reasoning in Vision-Language Models and improving the robustness of real-world robot manipulation policies.
> - Evaluating interaction-aware object localization requires dynamic benchmarks that consider robot proprioception and spatio-temporal properties, which traditional static benchmarks cannot provide.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The reliable generation of structured spatial annotations from robot demonstrations is a recognized challenge in robotics research. These annotations, which include bounding boxes, object trajectories, and manipulation phase labels, are considered foundational for numerous applications. These applications span the training of grounded robot policies, the development of embodied foundation models, motion planning, and hierarchical task composition, particularly within the context of object-centric reasoning in Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs).

Existing automated pipelines for generating such annotations typically involve chaining object detectors with trackers. However, a limitation of these methods is their reliance on detector confidence as the primary signal for object identity. Detector confidence primarily measures recognition certainty, not whether the detected object is the one actually being manipulated. In complex real-world scenarios characterized by clutter, occlusion, and varied viewpoints, these pipelines can incorrectly identify a plausible but unmanipulated object with high confidence, propagating this error throughout a demonstration. This leads to a fundamental scale-quality tradeoff: either a high volume of noisy labels is accepted, or a significant portion of potentially useful data is discarded by applying stringent confidence thresholds.

Alternative approaches include expensive human review for annotation verification or generating ground-truth data in simulated environments. While human review offers high reliability, its cost limits scalability. Simulation, conversely, struggles with the reality gap, hindering transfer to real-world applications. Some prior work has explored using temporal models for tracking or proprioceptive data for language generation, but many frameworks still revert to human verification or simulation to mitigate tracking failures. A common constraint of previous methods is their reliance on single-source, per-frame detections, which often underutilizes the spatio-temporal and proprioceptive structures inherent in robot tasks. Approaches that use binary heuristics or motion magnitude filtering also present limitations, such as discarding static interactions or still depending on an initial object detector.

SPARC addresses these limitations by reframing automatic spatio-temporal annotation as a selective-prediction problem. It proposes to leverage the rich spatio-temporal and proprioceptive cues present in robot demonstrations, moving beyond appearance-based detector confidence alone. By integrating object detection, dense tracking, and robot proprioception, SPARC aims to provide a more robust and calibrated reliability signal, thereby reducing annotation noise and retaining a greater proportion of usable data for downstream applications. This approach positions SPARC as an effort to enhance the scalability and quality of robot learning datasets without recourse to human verification or domain-specific simulations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **Spatial Annotations from Robot Demonstrations with Reliability Calibration (SPARC)**, a risk-aware framework designed to automatically label robot demonstrations with structured spatial annotations and assign a continuous reliability score to each generated annotation.

The motivation for SPARC stems from several challenges in the existing landscape of robot learning:

- **Reliability Gap in Automated Annotation:** Current automated pipelines for generating spatial annotations from robot demonstrations, such as bounding boxes, object trajectories, and manipulation phase labels, often lack a reliable quality signal. Detector confidence, which is commonly used, measures recognition certainty rather than whether the correct object is actually being manipulated. In cluttered scenes, this can lead to high-confidence but incorrect labels being generated and propagated.
- **Scale-Quality Tradeoff:** The unreliability of existing automated annotations forces a difficult choice: either accept a high volume of noisy labels to achieve scale, or apply aggressive filtering, which discards many potentially useful but unverified samples, thus limiting dataset size. This tradeoff hinders the development of large-scale, high-quality datasets necessary for training advanced robot policies and embodied foundation models.
- **Cost and Limitations of Alternatives:** Human annotation and verification are reliable but prohibitively expensive for generating datasets at the scale required for modern deep learning. Simulation environments can provide ground truth, but these often suffer from a reality gap, limiting their direct applicability to real-world scenarios.
- **Underutilization of Interaction Cues:** Existing methods often rely solely on visual detection. The authors argue that robot demonstrations inherently contain rich spatio-temporal and physical interaction cues (e.g., robot motion, gripper proximity, phase-aligned movement) that are underutilized for identifying manipulated objects and assessing annotation quality.
- **Need for Controllable Quality:** There is a need for a mechanism that allows users to explicitly control the tradeoff between annotation quality and dataset scale (coverage) without manual review, enabling the generation of datasets tailored to specific precision requirements.

To address these motivations, SPARC aims to:
- **Leverage Spatio-Temporal Structure:** Integrate multi-modal signals such as phase-aware object motion, 3D gripper proximity, and robot-overlap filtering to accurately identify the interacted object and its spatio-temporal properties.
- **Generate a Calibrated Reliability Score:** Develop a continuous, physically-grounded reliability score that effectively captures the likelihood of an object being correctly manipulated, allowing for fine-grained control over annotation quality.
- **Improve Downstream Robot Learning:** Demonstrate that SPARC-generated annotations lead to enhanced performance in downstream tasks, including embodied spatial reasoning for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and real-world robot manipulation policies.

A secondary objective is the introduction of **Interaction-Aware Bench (IA-Bench)**. This benchmark is motivated by the inadequacy of existing static object-grounding benchmarks, which cannot assess interacted object identity or its spatio-temporal properties within dynamic robot demonstrations. IA-Bench provides a necessary evaluation tool to measure the accuracy of localizing interacted objects in robot demonstrations with human-annotated ground truth.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPARC operates as an automatic pipeline that processes robot demonstration videos, language instructions, and gripper proprioceptive signals to produce object-centric spatial annotations. Each annotation includes a subtask instruction, the manipulated object's name, its initial and target locations, a 3D object trajectory, gripper phase boundaries, and a continuous scalar reliability score. The pipeline consists of three main stages:

#### 3.1. Stage 1: Subtask Decomposition and Phase Detection

- **Interaction Segmentation:** A long-horizon robot demonstration (visual frames `x_t`, proprioceptive states `q_t`) is decomposed into a sequence of shorter, object-centric segments. Each segment captures a single interaction with an object `o_k`. For tool-use tasks, the tool is considered the primary interaction object, with the target of tool use also tracked.
- **Gripper Phase Extraction:** The normalized gripper signal `a_t` (where smaller values indicate a more closed gripper) is used to extract maximal closed-gripper intervals `[s_k, e_k]`. A dual-pass procedure with hysteresis (asymmetric closing/opening thresholds `τ_enter`, `τ_exit`) is applied to robustly differentiate true grasps from noise. Short closures are filtered using an adaptive duration threshold (`d_min`), retaining only valid interaction intervals. Adjacent open-gripper intervals are classified as approach and release phases, completing the grasp cycle. This process is applied independently for each arm in bimanual setups.
- **Language-based Subtask Parsing:** A large language model, Qwen3.6-30B, is conditioned on the original language instruction `ℓ` and the ordered list of detected grasp cycles. It provides a structured description for each subtask, including the manipulated object name (`ℓo_j`), action type, initial (`q_init_j`) and target (`q_target_j`) locations, and natural-language descriptions for the approach, interaction, and release phases (`P_j`). This step also aligns the symbolic phase descriptions back to the measured trajectory timeline.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Candidate Proposal and Tracking

- **Object Proposal at Interaction Keyframes:** For each subtask, a video clip is extracted, and a detection keyframe is selected near the start of the object interaction (typically halfway through the first grasp phase). LLMDet is used as a region proposal model with a low confidence threshold to generate a broad set of object candidates (5-25 proposals). Robot arm and gripper detections are also collected for later filtering.
- **Segmentation and Candidate Refinement:** Each detected object proposal is refined into an instance mask using SAM2. This yields a set of candidates `c_i = (b_i, m_i, ℓo_i, D_i)`, comprising a bounding box, segmentation mask, object label, and detector confidence.
- **Temporal Tracking and 3D Lifting:** AllTracker produces dense pixel tracks across the video clip. For each candidate, tracks originating from pixels within its object mask are selected and lifted to 3D using MoGe-2, which predicts a dense geometry map per frame. The resulting candidate representation `o_i` includes its initial detection, segmentation mask, and propagated 2D and 3D tracks (`Γ_2D_i`, `Γ_3D_i`).
- **Target Proposal:** Target boxes are proposed using the target-location phrase extracted in Stage 1. Candidates are queried from early and late frames of the subtask clip, merged, and suppressed. If no explicit target location is predicted, a fallback mechanism uses the object or tool name and detects on the last frame.

#### 3.3. Stage 3: Reliability Scoring

This stage ranks the tracked object candidates by combining appearance reliability, manipulation consistency, and embodiment consistency into a composite reliability score `R_i`.
- **Detector Confidence (`D_i`):** The language-grounded detector confidence from LLMDet, representing appearance-level compatibility.
- **Phase-aware Motion (`A_i`):** Measures whether a candidate moves predominantly during the interaction phase. The mean 2D displacement of tracked points during interaction intervals (`μ_int_i`) is compared against displacement outside these intervals (`μ_non_i`). A soft signal-to-noise ratio `A_i = (μ_int_i)^0.6 / (μ_non_i + 1.0)^0.2` is computed, favoring objects that move consistently when the gripper is engaged.
- **3D Gripper Proximity (`P_i`):** Assesses if the candidate occupies the 3D local neighborhood of the active gripper. For each frame, an adaptive 3D sphere `B(g_t, r_t)` is constructed around the gripper center `g_t`. `P_i` is the aggregated fraction of the candidate's 3D points within this sphere over visible frames.
- **Robot-Overlap Soft Penalty (`Π_i`):** A continuous quadratic penalty is applied to candidates that overlap with a RobotSeg mask. This penalty is attenuated by the 3D depth separation between the gripper tip and the candidate, distinguishing true contact from identity confusion with robot parts.
- **Composite Reliability Score (`R_i`):** The normalized `A_i` and `P_i` are combined with `D_i` and `Π_i`:
 `R_i = w_A * b_A_i + w_D(b_P_i) * D_i + w_P * b_P_i - Π_i`
 where `w_A = 0.5`, `w_P = 0.3`, and `w_D(b_P_i) = 0.75 - 0.15 * b_P_i` is an adaptive weight that reduces reliance on detector confidence when 3D gripper-proximity evidence is strong. The selected object is `i* = argmax_i R_i`.
- **Final Target Selection:** After the manipulated object is selected, its target position is resolved from the detected target candidates by measuring how many tracked points from `i*` terminate inside each target candidate, normalized by target-box size to favor compact targets.

#### 3.4. IA-Bench Benchmark

To evaluate SPARC, the authors introduce IA-Bench, a benchmark comprising 1,748 human-annotated start and target locations of manipulated objects. The data sources include AgiBotWorld, DROID, BridgeData, and Open X-Embodiment, covering 12 robot embodiments and diverse scenarios, including bimanual setups. Unlike static benchmarks, IA-Bench provides videos and proprioceptive states to enable evaluation of dynamic interaction grounding.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Protocol

Evaluation is framed as a selective prediction problem. An annotation is considered correct if the bounding box matches ground truth at an IoU (Intersection over Union) > 0.4. Performance is measured by accuracy and coverage, summarized by standard selective-classification metrics: Area Under the Risk-Coverage curve (AURC), Excess AURC (E-AURC), and Coverage at 90% precision (Cov@90).

#### 3.6. Downstream Utility Evaluation

- **Embodied Spatial Reasoning:** SPARC annotations are used to construct a VQA dataset of 511k samples for object grounding, vacant location pointing, and trajectory prediction. A Qwen3.5-4B model is fine-tuned on this dataset (co-trained with other VQA data for general instruction following) and evaluated on various benchmarks.
- **Real-World Policy Performance:** Policies (VLAs with a Qwen3.5-0.8B backbone and a flow-matching action head) are trained on SPARC-generated annotations in a cluttered tabletop setting with 10 different manipulation tasks. Their performance is compared against policies trained with detection-annotated data and a no-reasoning baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across annotation quality, downstream model performance, and real-world robot task success.

#### 4.1. IA-Bench Performance and Reliability Scoring

- **Superior Accuracy:** SPARC achieved an interacted object localization accuracy of 80.2% on the IA-Bench benchmark, which significantly surpassed a detection-only baseline that yielded 58.1% accuracy.
- **Improved Quality-Coverage Tradeoff:** SPARC demonstrated superior control over the quality-coverage tradeoff. At a high-precision operating point (90% precision), SPARC retained 77.6% of samples (Cov@90), and at 95% precision, it retained 58% of samples. In contrast, the strongest detection-only baseline could only retain 20% of samples at 95% precision. This indicates SPARC can produce substantially more usable high-quality data.
- **Lower Selective Prediction Error:** The framework exhibited lower selective prediction error, with an AURC of 0.056 and an E-AURC of 0.035, confirming the effectiveness of its reliability score in capturing annotation correctness.
- **Contribution of Interaction Cues:** Ablation studies confirmed that each component of SPARC's interaction-aware score contributes to performance improvement. Adding object motion provided the largest initial gain, while robot-aware filtering and phase-aware motion further enhanced accuracy and calibration.
- **Outperformance of External Models:** SPARC's accuracy (80.2%) exceeded that of large proprietary embodied models (e.g., Gemini Robotics ER 1.6, which achieved 71.7%), while also providing a reliability score for selective annotation.
- **Robustness:** The reliability score was found to be robust to variations in hyperparameters and IoU match thresholds, indicating that its performance is driven by the underlying structural design rather than fine-tuned parameters.

#### 4.2. Downstream Embodied Spatial Reasoning (VLM Training)

- **State-of-the-Art Grounding Performance:** Qwen3.5-4B models fine-tuned on SPARC-annotated data demonstrated stronger grounding performance compared to models trained on human-annotated datasets (e.g., EO-1.5M) and detection-only baselines. SPARC-trained models achieved state-of-the-art results among similarly-sized models on object-grounding and pointing benchmarks (e.g., Where2Place, VABench, RoboRefIt, RefSpatial, ShareRobot).
- **Effective Trajectory Prediction:** SPARC provided effective supervision for trajectory prediction tasks, even when only object trajectories (not explicit gripper trajectories) were used for training.
- **Focused Gains:** Gains were more pronounced on embodied grounding and spatial pointing tasks, with smaller improvements on language-only Multiple-Choice Question Answering (MCQA) benchmarks, suggesting that SPARC's supervision effectively targets embodied reasoning.
- **Scaling Behavior:** Performance on IA-Bench consistently improved with increased dataset scale when using SPARC-ranked annotations, rising from 0.716 at 50K samples to 0.785 at 838K samples. In contrast, detector confidence-ranked data showed performance degradation as more samples were added, indicating an accumulation of noisy supervision.

#### 4.3. Real-World Policy Performance

- **Enhanced Manipulation Success:** In a challenging real-world tabletop manipulation setting involving cluttered, visually ambiguous scenes, policies trained on SPARC-generated annotations (SPARC Reasoning) significantly outperformed baselines. SPARC Reasoning more than doubled the success rate of policies trained on detection-annotated data (Detection Reasoning: 31% vs. 12%) and tripled that of a no-reasoning baseline (9%).
- **Annotation Quality Drives Performance:** The consistent performance advantage of SPARC-trained policies across ten distinct tasks indicated that the higher quality of reasoning data derived from SPARC annotations, rather than solely policy capacity, was the primary driver for improved success in hard grounding tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SPARC addresses a critical bottleneck in robot learning: the generation of reliable, large-scale, structured spatial annotations from robot demonstrations. The ability to automatically produce high-quality, spatio-temporal labels with a calibrated reliability score represents a notable contribution to the field.

The potential impacts of this work are several:

- **Scalable and High-Quality Data Generation:** SPARC's core innovation is its risk-aware framework, which enables a controllable tradeoff between annotation quality and dataset scale without requiring expensive human review. This capability allows researchers and developers to generate vast quantities of high-quality training data tailored to specific precision requirements, which is crucial for the data-intensive nature of modern robot learning and embodied AI.
- **Advancement in Embodied Learning:** The demonstrated improvements in downstream tasks, particularly embodied spatial reasoning for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and real-world robot manipulation policies, suggest that SPARC can significantly accelerate progress in these areas. By providing more accurate grounding and spatio-temporal understanding, SPARC annotations can lead to more capable and robust robot behaviors. The fact that models trained on SPARC data can match or exceed those trained on human-annotated data highlights the potential for reducing reliance on costly manual labeling efforts.
- **Enhanced Policy Robustness:** The substantial increase in success rates for real-world manipulation tasks, especially in cluttered and visually ambiguous environments, indicates that SPARC-generated annotations can directly translate into more reliable and effective robot policies. This impact is particularly relevant for deploying robots in complex, unstructured real-world settings.
- **New Benchmarking Capabilities:** The introduction of IA-Bench provides a valuable tool for the research community. By focusing on interaction-aware object localization within dynamic robot demonstrations, IA-Bench fills a gap left by static object-grounding benchmarks, enabling more comprehensive evaluation of annotation pipelines and embodied reasoning models.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By making its code, data, and models publicly available, SPARC facilitates reproducibility and encourages further research into improving automated annotation, refining reliability scoring mechanisms, and exploring the full potential of physically-grounded cues in robot learning. The identified limitations, such as challenges with detector errors, tracker identity switches, 3D lifting for transparent surfaces, and assumptions about camera motion or distinct grasp phases, also open specific avenues for future work.

In summary, SPARC offers a practical and effective solution for a fundamental data challenge in robotics, with the potential to significantly improve the performance and scalability of robot learning systems.
