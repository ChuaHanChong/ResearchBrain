# Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> Here's a single-sentence breakdown of this paper followed by the requested format:

## Problem

- Existing computer vision models are limited by requiring task-specific training and labeled datasets
- Natural language supervision could enable more general and flexible visual models but has struggled to match supervised performance
- Current methods for learning from natural language supervision have been held back by scale

## Method

- Train a large model (==CLIP==) to predict accurate pairings of images and text from ==400M pairs== collected from the internet
- Use a ==contrastive learning== approach that is more efficient than other language-based training methods
- Enable ==zero-shot transfer== by having the model make predictions using natural language text descriptions

## Results

- Zero-shot CLIP matches or outperforms supervised baselines on many vision benchmarks
- Linear probes on CLIP features outperform supervised models on **21** of **27** datasets
- Significant improvements in robustness to distribution shifts (**75%** reduction in performance drop)
- Strong performance on diverse tasks including OCR, action recognition, and geo-location
- Effective zero-shot transfer across broad range of vision tasks

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language provides a more flexible and generalizable form of supervision than traditional labeled datasets
> - Training at large scale is critical for good performance - model capabilities improve smoothly with compute
> - Zero-shot CLIP models are more robust to distribution shifts than supervised models, suggesting more generalizable learning
> - Language supervision enables wider range of capabilities (OCR, geo-location, etc) in single model

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Computer vision has traditionally relied on supervised learning with manually labeled datasets. While this approach has led to significant advances, it comes with important limitations: models trained this way can only recognize a fixed set of visual concepts, and creating large labeled datasets is expensive and time-consuming.

Meanwhile, the field of natural language processing (NLP) has seen a revolution through pre-training models on vast amounts of raw text, enabling them to learn versatile representations that transfer to many downstream tasks. OpenAI's research team led by Alec Radford asked: Could we do the same for computer vision?

This question led to the development of CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training), a model that learns from 400 million image-text pairs collected from the internet, without requiring manually curated labels. CLIP represents a paradigm shift in how we train vision models, moving away from specialized datasets toward learning from the natural language supervision that's readily available online.

### 2. What is CLIP?

CLIP is a neural network that jointly trains an image encoder and a text encoder to predict which images go with which text descriptions. Rather than training a model to classify images into predefined categories, CLIP learns to connect images with their textual descriptions in a shared embedding space.

The key innovation is how CLIP enables "zero-shot" transfer learning. After training, CLIP can be applied to any visual classification task by simply providing textual descriptions of the visual concepts to be recognized. For example, to classify dog breeds, you could prompt CLIP with "a photo of a beagle," "a photo of a corgi," and so on. CLIP will then predict which text description best matches a given image.

This approach is fundamentally different from traditional computer vision models that require task-specific fine-tuning. CLIP can generalize to new tasks without additional training, similar to how humans can recognize objects they've never explicitly been trained to identify.

### 3. The Methodology Behind CLIP

CLIP's training process involves three main components:

1. **Dataset Creation**: The researchers created a new dataset called WebImageText (WIT), containing 400 million image-text pairs collected from the internet. The data collection process focused on gathering pairs that include a wide range of visual concepts mentioned in English Wikipedia.

2. **Contrastive Learning Objective**: Instead of predicting the exact words in a caption (as in image captioning), CLIP learns to identify which caption goes with which image in a batch. Given N image-text pairs, the model is trained to identify the correct N matches among the N² possible combinations.

3. **Zero-Shot Transfer**: After training, CLIP can perform classification by encoding both the image and a set of candidate text descriptions (e.g., "a photo of a {class}") and selecting the class whose text embedding has the highest similarity with the image embedding.

The contrastive learning approach proved to be far more efficient than alternative methods. As shown in Figure 2, CLIP achieves the same zero-shot performance as a bag-of-words prediction model while processing 4x fewer images, and it's 3x more efficient than a transformer language model approach.

### 4. Model Architecture

CLIP is not tied to a specific architecture but instead explores various combinations of image and text encoders:

**Image Encoders:**
- ResNet-50 and scaled variants (RN50, RN101, RN50x4, RN50x16, RN50x64)
- Vision Transformer (ViT) variants (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14)

**Text Encoder:**
- A Transformer-based model similar to GPT-2, which encodes text using a masked self-attention mechanism

The authors found that both ResNet and Vision Transformer architectures performed well, with the larger variants providing better performance at the cost of increased computation. The best performing model was ViT-L/14, which uses a Vision Transformer with 24 layers and patch size of 14x14 pixels.

One technical detail worth highlighting is that while the paper explores different image encoder architectures, the text encoder remains constant, suggesting that the text representation is not the bottleneck for performance.

### 5. Training Process

CLIP's training process involves several important techniques:

1. **Data Preprocessing**: Images are randomly cropped and resized to a standard resolution (typically 224×224 pixels). Text is tokenized using a byte pair encoding with a vocabulary of 49,152 tokens.

2. **Optimization**: CLIP is trained using Adam optimizer with decoupled weight decay regularization. A cosine learning rate schedule is used, starting from a peak learning rate determined by a linear warmup.

3. **Prompt Engineering**: To improve zero-shot performance, the authors use templates like "a photo of a {class}" rather than just the class name. This provides context for the classification task.

4. **Prompt Ensembling**: For even better results, multiple prompts (e.g., "a photo of a {class}", "a picture of a {class}", etc.) are used, and their predictions are averaged.

The model is trained on large batches (32,768 image-text pairs) using mixed precision and gradient checkpointing to manage memory usage. Even with these optimizations, training the largest models required significant computational resources.

The contrastive objective is formulated as:

$$L = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \log \frac{\exp(sim(I_i, T_i) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(sim(I_i, T_j) / \tau)} + \log \frac{\exp(sim(I_i, T_i) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(sim(I_j, T_i) / \tau)} \right]$$

Where $I_i$ and $T_i$ are the encoded image and text, $sim$ is the cosine similarity, and $\tau$ is a learnable temperature parameter.

### 6. Key Results and Findings

CLIP demonstrates impressive performance across a wide range of tasks:

1. **Zero-Shot Classification**: Without any task-specific training, CLIP matches the performance of the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet (76.2% accuracy), despite that model being directly trained on ImageNet for many epochs.

2. **Linear Probe Evaluation**: When using CLIP's representations with a linear classifier, it outperforms the best publicly available ImageNet models. As shown in Figure 3, CLIP models (red stars) perform better than other approaches when evaluated across datasets.

3. **Wide Task Coverage**: CLIP shows non-trivial performance on tasks ranging from OCR and action recognition to geo-localization and many specialized classification tasks.

4. **Data Efficiency**: CLIP requires fewer labeled examples to achieve the same performance as supervised models. The authors found that zero-shot CLIP often matches the performance of supervised models trained with 1 to 4 shots (examples) per class.

5. **Data Overlap Analysis**: The researchers conducted a thorough analysis to ensure CLIP's performance wasn't simply due to memorizing test images. They found that while some overlap exists between pre-training and evaluation datasets, it doesn't significantly impact overall performance.

### 7. Robustness to Distribution Shifts

One of CLIP's most impressive characteristics is its robustness to distribution shifts. When images deviate from the standard ImageNet distribution—for example, in sketches, renderings, or unusual viewpoints—supervised models typically suffer dramatic performance drops. CLIP, however, maintains much stronger performance.

As illustrated in Figure 5, zero-shot CLIP significantly outperforms supervised models on distribution-shifted datasets like ImageNet-R (renderings), ImageNet-Sketch, and ObjectNet. This suggests that learning from diverse internet data helps CLIP develop more robust representations that generalize better to out-of-distribution samples.

The researchers further demonstrated that CLIP can be made adaptive to specific distribution shifts, improving its robustness even more. When images from new distributions are encountered, CLIP can be adapted through zero-shot domain adaptation, few-shot learning, or by using specialized prompts.

### 8. Few-Shot Learning

While CLIP excels at zero-shot prediction, it can also be used in few-shot settings where a small number of labeled examples are available. Figure 7 shows how CLIP's performance increases with the number of examples per class.

With just 1 to 4 examples per class, CLIP already outperforms fully supervised models on many distribution shifts. This property makes CLIP particularly valuable in settings where collecting large amounts of labeled data is impractical.

### 9. Scaling Properties

CLIP's performance scales predictably with computational resources, similar to language models like GPT-3. The authors observed smooth scaling of zero-shot performance with model size, amount of training data, and compute.

This predictable scaling suggests that further improvements might be achieved simply by training larger models on more data, though diminishing returns will eventually set in.

### 10. Limitations and Bias Analysis

Despite its impressive capabilities, CLIP has several important limitations:

1. **Accuracy Limitations**: While CLIP performs well on common objects and categories, it struggles with fine-grained classification (e.g., specific subspecies), abstract tasks, and counting.

2. **Comparison with Humans**: CLIP still falls short of human performance in several areas. As shown in Figure 9, even zero-shot human performance (green) substantially outperforms CLIP (blue) on many tasks.

3. **Social Biases**: The authors conducted extensive analysis of CLIP's biases and found concerning patterns. For example, when examining gender-related predictions, CLIP shows stereotyped associations between gender and occupations, appearances, and other attributes.

4. **Harmful Applications**: The authors acknowledge the potential for CLIP to be used in surveillance applications, though their testing found CLIP's performance was not exceptional for these purposes compared to specialized models.

### 11. Significance and Future Directions

CLIP represents a significant step forward in computer vision by demonstrating that models can learn effectively from natural language supervision at scale, without requiring manually labeled datasets. This approach offers several important advantages:

1. **Flexibility**: By training on a vast range of visual concepts described in natural language, CLIP can generalize to tasks not explicitly seen during training.

2. **Accessibility**: CLIP makes computer vision more accessible by allowing users to define new visual categories using natural language, without needing to collect and label examples.

3. **Efficiency**: The contrastive approach used in CLIP proves to be much more computationally efficient than alternative methods for learning from image-text pairs.

4. **Foundation for Future Work**: CLIP establishes a foundation for future research in multimodal learning, zero-shot transfer, and more robust computer vision systems.

Future directions indicated by this work include:
- Scaling to even larger datasets and model sizes
- Improving the handling of fine-grained distinctions and abstract concepts
- Addressing the social biases present in the model
- Exploring applications beyond classification, such as image generation, segmentation, and detection

CLIP demonstrates that the "pre-train on internet-scale data, then transfer" paradigm that transformed NLP can also be applied successfully to computer vision. This approach may ultimately lead to more general-purpose vision systems that can adapt to new tasks without task-specific training data.
