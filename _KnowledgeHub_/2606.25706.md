# Learning Asynchronous Upper-body Task-space Trajectory Tracking Policy for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a learning framework for humanoid robots that effectively tracks asynchronous and structurally incomplete upper-body task-space trajectories. The framework achieves a 99.50% success rate for asynchronous tracking, reducing position errors to 6.90 cm and rotational errors to 6.83 degrees, and enables safer adaptation to out-of-distribution tasks on a Unitree G1 robot by maintaining natural lower-body motions and preventing joint-limit violations.

## Problem

- High-level planners provide sparse, low-frequency task-space references, creating temporal asynchrony and frame mismatch with the robot's high-frequency low-level controllers.
- Sparse task references are structurally incomplete, underspecifying whole-body motions and hindering robust post-training of pre-trained policies without dense teacher supervision.
- Existing methods for bridging the execution-interface gap often suffer from accumulating errors, geometric drift, or a lack of stability and safety during adaptation to novel tasks.

## Method

- An ==Asynchronous Time-indexed Conditioning== (**ASYNC-3PT**) policy implicitly aligns robot motion with asynchronous references using the entire future trajectory and a time index, combined with a sliding-window global reward for pre-training.
- A kinematic Model Predictive Control (MPC) module augments sparse upper-body references into dense, feasible floating-base and upper-body joint trajectories, providing necessary guidance for post-training.
- A self-guidance reinforcement learning mechanism, incorporating action-level and FK-level regularization from a pre-trained policy, is employed to prevent policy drift and maintain natural lower-body behaviors during adaptation.

## Results

- ASYNC-3PT achieved a **99.50%** success rate for asynchronous tracking, significantly reducing asynchronous interval errors to **6.90** cm (position) and **6.83** degrees (rotation) compared to synchronous baselines.
- MPC-completed guidance and self-guidance during post-training enabled robust and safe adaptation to out-of-distribution tasks, preventing joint-limit violations and preserving natural lower-body motions.
- The proposed framework demonstrated successful real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, generalizing to various complex loco-manipulation tasks such as waving, deep squatting, and forward walking based on sparse upper-body trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicitly handling temporal asynchrony through time-indexed conditioning and a sliding-window global reward enables stable and frame-consistent execution of low-frequency task commands by high-frequency controllers.
> - Augmenting sparse task references with MPC-generated dense whole-body trajectories provides crucial constraints for robust and safe policy adaptation during post-training, even in the absence of original dense teacher supervision.
> - Self-guidance regularization is essential for preserving pre-trained motion priors and preventing policy drift, ensuring natural and safe lower-body behaviors when adapting to novel, sparse upper-body tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot control is transitioning from low-level servo control to high-level task-space execution. This shift involves high-level planners generating commands that abstract away joint-level specifics, focusing instead on desired end-effector poses or task outcomes. This approach offers advantages in semantic planning and scalable data collection, moving away from labor-intensive fixed trajectories or direct human teleoperation. However, it introduces an "execution-interface gap" between the high-level planners and low-level whole-body controllers.

Current humanoid control research often addresses this gap in several ways:
- **Teacher-student distillation:** This common paradigm involves training a "teacher" policy with privileged information (e.g., whole-body datasets) and then distilling its knowledge into a "student" policy that operates with more constrained, realistic observations. This is effective when references are updated synchronously.
- **Buffering and local commands:** Some methods buffer future action chunks or convert sparse references into local incremental velocity commands. While these mitigate rate mismatches, they can lead to geometric drift due to accumulated errors or unresolved frame inconsistencies.
- **Explicit frame estimation/reconstruction:** Approaches like HiWET and HERO attempt to reconstruct or refresh tracking targets in a world frame or use learned odometry to maintain spatial consistency. These methods often rely on global localization or state estimation and may optimize estimators and controllers separately, potentially failing under scenarios where the robot's base is in motion.

This paper addresses two specific and persistent challenges within this landscape:
1. **Temporal asynchrony:** High-level planners typically operate at low frequencies (e.g., 1-10 Hz), while low-level controllers require much higher frequencies (e.g., ~50 Hz) for stability. This discrepancy means that the robot's base frame shifts significantly during the execution of a single planner command, creating a mismatch between the reference frame (defined at planning time) and the robot's current execution frame. Existing remedies either break end-to-end learning or suffer from accumulating errors.
2. **Structural incompleteness:** High-level planners often provide sparse references, focusing only on task-relevant end-effectors (e.g., hands, head) and not specifying whole-body motions. This leaves the control problem under-constrained for high-dimensional humanoid robots. Furthermore, adapting pre-trained policies to new tasks with only sparse upper-body trajectories is challenging, as the original dense teacher supervision is unavailable, and direct reinforcement learning (RL) with sparse rewards can lead to instability and policy drift.

The presented work aims to bridge this execution gap by proposing a learning framework that implicitly handles asynchronous updates and augments sparse references to enable robust whole-body control and effective post-training for humanoid robots, thus extending the capabilities of existing task-level planners.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to develop a robust and adaptable control framework for humanoid robots that can effectively track asynchronous and structurally incomplete upper-body task-space trajectories provided by high-level planners. This is motivated by the recognized challenges of the execution-interface gap in modern humanoid control.

The specific motivations and objectives are:

1. **Address Temporal Asynchrony:** To enable stable control despite the frequency mismatch between low-rate planners and high-rate controllers. The goal is to manage the "frame mismatch" where the planned reference remains tied to its planning-time frame while the robot's base frame continuously changes during execution. The objective is to achieve this without relying on explicit frame estimation, which can introduce errors and break end-to-end learning.
 - **Objective:** Develop a policy conditioned on the entire future trajectory segment and a time index to implicitly align the robot's motion with the reference.
 - **Objective:** Implement a sliding-window global reward mechanism to penalize accumulated error within each planning interval, thereby mitigating frame drift.

2. **Handle Structural Incompleteness and Enable Robust Post-Training:** To allow humanoids to execute tasks specified by sparse upper-body trajectories, which inherently under-constrain the full-body motion. Furthermore, the framework aims to facilitate effective adaptation (post-training) of pre-trained policies to novel, task-specific sparse references, a scenario where traditional teacher supervision is absent.
 - **Objective:** Utilize a Model Predictive Control (MPC) module to complete sparse upper-body references into full floating-base and upper-body joint trajectories, providing denser guidance.
 - **Objective:** Integrate these MPC-completed trajectories into the policy's observations and the reinforcement learning reward structure to stabilize RL post-training.
 - **Objective:** Introduce a self-guidance regularization mechanism that constrains lower-body behavior and prevents policy drift during adaptation by leveraging the motion prior from a pre-trained policy.

3. **Demonstrate Practical Efficacy:** To validate the proposed framework through both simulated environments and real-world hardware experiments on a humanoid robot, showcasing its ability to achieve improved tracking accuracy, stability, and safer adaptation to out-of-distribution motions compared to existing methods.

In summary, the research aims to create a learning framework that directly addresses the fundamental challenges of asynchrony and incompleteness in task-space humanoid control, leading to more capable, robust, and adaptable robot behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework operates in two distinct stages: Pre-training (ASYNC-3PT) and Post-training (ASYNC-CA). Both stages utilize teacher-student distillation and reinforcement learning principles, adapted to handle the challenges of asynchronous and incomplete references.

**A. Stage 1: ASYNC-3PT (Asynchronous Time-indexed Conditioning)**

This stage focuses on handling temporal asynchrony during pre-training when a whole-body motion dataset is available.

1. **Problem Formulation:**
 - **Low-frequency Planner Output:** At each planning time $t_k$, the planner provides a finite-horizon reference $T_k = \{ {}^{b_{t_k}}T^{ref}_{ee_{t_{k,i}}} \}_{i=1}^H$ for task-relevant end-effectors (e.g., head, dual hands). These references are expressed in the planning-time base frame $b_{t_k}$.
 - **High-frequency Controller Execution:** The low-level controller runs at a higher frequency, executing each $T_k$ over multiple control steps. During this interval, the robot's floating base moves, causing a mismatch between the reference frame $b_{t_k}$ and the current execution frame $b_t$. The relative transform ${}^{b_t}T^{b_{t_k}}$ is not directly available, making direct error measurement challenging.

2. **Policy Formulation and Conditioning:**
 - The policy receives an observation $o_t = [o^{prop}_t, h_t, \hat{i}, o^{task}_t]$ and outputs PD joint targets $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{29}$.
 - $o^{prop}_t$: Proprioceptive observations (joint positions $q_t$, velocities $\dot{q}_t$, base angular velocity $\omega_t$, projected gravity $g_t$).
 - $h_t$: History buffer of past proprioceptive observations and actions.
 - $\hat{i}$: A time index, $\hat{i} = \lfloor \frac{t - t_k}{\tau} \rfloor$, indicating the current execution step relative to the last planning step. This index is crucial for the policy to implicitly learn phase alignment without explicitly selecting waypoints or transforming frames.
 - $o^{task}_t$: Task observation, including the entire cached future trajectory $T_k$ and $\Delta T_k$, which are local SE(3) errors between the reference and the current end-effector/head poses at update time $t_k$.

3. **Frame Drift Estimator (Auxiliary):**
 - An auxiliary head is integrated into the policy to estimate the height and frame drift ${}^{b_{t_k}}\hat{T}^{b_t}$ between the planning-time base frame and the current base frame.
 - This estimator serves as an auxiliary supervision signal for regularization and its prediction is *not* used to transform the reference trajectory, maintaining end-to-end learning.

4. **Teacher-Student Distillation and Reinforcement Learning:**
 - The policy is trained using a combined loss: $L_{train} = L_{imit} + L_{PPO}$.
 - **Imitation Learning ($L_{imit}$):** Comprises action distillation ($L_{act} = \|a^{stu}_t - a^{tea}_t\|^2_2$) from a privileged teacher policy (trained with global observations) and auxiliary state estimation loss ($L_{se}$) for the frame drift estimator.
 - **Reinforcement Learning ($L_{PPO}$):** Uses a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) loss. A key component is the **sliding-window global tracking reward**. This reward evaluates tracking error in the latest planning frame $b_{t_k}$ by comparing the reference ${}^{b_{t_k}}T^{ref}_{ee_{t_k, \hat{i}}}$ and the executed end-effector pose ${}^{b_{t_k}}T^{ee_t}$. The error is computed using $\text{Log}( ({}^{b_{t_k}}T^{ref}_{ee_{t_k, \hat{i}}})^{-1} {}^{b_{t_k}}T^{ee_t} )$, penalizing accumulated drift within each planning window. This reward resets at each new planner command.

**B. Stage 2: ASYNC-CA (Completion-Augmented for Post-Training)**

This stage addresses structural incompleteness and enables robust post-training when only task-specific sparse upper-body trajectories are available, and the dense teacher supervision from pre-training is no longer applicable.

1. **MPC-based Trajectory Completion:**
 - A kinematic Model Predictive Control (MPC) module is employed to augment the sparse task-space references $T_k$ (head and two hands) into a complete, feasible, and consistent trajectory for the floating base and upper-body joints.
 - The MPC formulation minimizes a cost function that includes end-effector tracking error, control input regularization, smoothness, joint constraints, and self-collision penalties over a prediction horizon.
 - The MPC outputs a completed reference $G_k = \{ {}^{b_{t_k}}T^{*}_{b_{t_{k,i}}}, q^{*}_{up, t_{k,i}} \}_{i=1}^H$, providing dense guidance for the base and upper-body joints.
 - This MPC completion acts as a constraint-aware source of auxiliary dense rewards for post-training, rather than aiming for an optimal whole-body plan.

2. **Observation and Reward Augmentation:**
 - The policy's observations are augmented to include the MPC-completed floating-base and upper-body joint references.
 - The reinforcement learning rewards are augmented to include upper-body and asynchronous sliding-window rewards for base tracking, providing denser constraints for post-training.

3. **Self-guidance Reinforcement Learning:**
 - To prevent policy drift and preserve the pre-trained motion prior during RL adaptation, the pre-trained ASYNC-CA policy ($\pi_0$) serves as a pseudo-teacher for the adapted policy ($\pi_1$).
 - **Action-level self-guidance ($L^{sd}_{act}$):** Regularizes the adapted policy's actions to stay close to the pre-trained policy's actions: $L^{sd}_{act} = E[\|a^1_t - a^0_t\|^2_2]$.
 - **FK-level self-guidance ($L^{sd}_{fk}$):** Regularizes the adapted motion in Cartesian space by comparing selected body poses derived from forward kinematics (FK) of the adapted and pre-trained policies: $L^{sd}_{fk} = E[\|FK(q^{tar}(a^1_t)) - FK(q^{tar}(a^0_t))\|^2_2]$. Lower-body poses are typically upweighted in this loss as they are not directly constrained by sparse head-and-hand tracking.

This two-stage approach combines implicit handling of asynchrony with a mechanism for reference completion and self-guidance, aiming to provide a comprehensive solution for learning robust humanoid control policies under sparse, low-frequency task commands.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted on a 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid robot in both simulation and real-world scenarios. The controller runs at 50 Hz, receiving sparse future trajectories with a 20-frame horizon, typically updated at 1 Hz (1-second reference interval). Metrics included success rate (Succ.), asynchronous interval errors (Pos$_{1s}$, Rot$_{1s}$), local tracking errors (Pos$_{loc}$, Rot$_{loc}$), linear and angular velocity errors, and joint-limit safety margins.

**A. Effect of Asynchronous Sparse Trajectory Tracking**

- **Necessity of Asynchronous Modeling:** Training an asynchronous controller (ASYNC-3PT) from scratch was found to be challenging, resulting in large tracking errors. Directly deploying a synchronous tracker (Sync W.ASC) under asynchronous updates led to a significant degradation in success rate (75.48%) and increased asynchronous tracking errors (14.64 cm for Pos$_{1s}$, 15.15$^\circ$ for Rot$_{1s}$). In contrast, ASYNC-3PT achieved a 99.50% success rate and significantly reduced asynchronous interval errors (6.90 cm Pos$_{1s}$, 6.83$^\circ$ Rot$_{1s}$) while maintaining comparable local tracking. This indicated that explicit asynchronous modeling is important for frame-consistent execution.
- **Time-indexed Conditioning:** Ablation studies showed that removing the time index from ASYNC-3PT increased Pos$_{1s}$ from 8.6 cm to 14.6 cm and Rot$_{1s}$ from 12.8$^\circ$ to 26.6$^\circ$. This affirmed the role of the time index in resolving phase mismatch within asynchronous reference intervals. The time index also enabled inference-time speed modulation, allowing the same reference to be executed faster or slower by adjusting its progression rate.
- **Re-planning Frequency:** ASYNC-3PT demonstrated stability across various re-planning frequencies. Higher frequencies consistently resulted in lower position and rotation errors, attributed to shorter execution intervals alleviating frame mismatch and error accumulation. This trend was observed with both OmniH2O- and SONIC-based teachers, suggesting its independence from the specific teacher backbone.

**B. Comparison of Base-augmented Tracking**

- **Unified vs. Decoupled Control:** A Decoupled baseline, which separated base and upper-body tracking, achieved a lower local position error (2.61 cm Pos$_{loc}$) but resulted in a lower success rate (92.30%) and worse asynchronous tracking compared to ASYNC-CA. This indicated that a unified, end-to-end asynchronous modeling approach (ASYNC-CA) was more effective for stable whole-body tracking despite potentially higher local errors.
- **Effect of State Estimation:** Removing the auxiliary frame drift estimator from ASYNC-CA maintained a high success rate (99.17%) but degraded tracking accuracy, with Pos$_{1s}$ increasing from 6.00 cm to 7.98 cm and Rot$_{1s}$ from 6.43$^\circ$ to 9.53$^\circ$. This suggests the estimator contributes to a more accurate base-aware tracking representation for the policy.

**C. OOD Post-Training with MPC Completion**

- **Effect of MPC-completed Guidance:** Post-training with MPC-completed guidance (ASYNC-CA) consistently improved success rates on out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks compared to zero-shot deployment and synchronous baselines. Specifically, ASYNC-CA exhibited significantly lower interval rotation drift and higher position drift than ASYNC-3PT (without MPC completion). Critically, ASYNC-CA maintained a positive joint-limit margin, indicating safer joint configurations, whereas ASYNC-3PT without MPC completion resulted in negative margins (joint-limit violations). This confirmed that MPC-completed guidance provided essential denser constraints for robust and safe OOD adaptation.
- **Generality Across Teacher Backbones:** The benefits of MPC-guided post-training were observed with both OmniH2O- and SONIC-based teachers, indicating that the methodology is not specific to a particular pre-training backbone.

**D. Real-world Hardware Experiments**

- **Effect of Self-Guidance:** On the Unitree G1 robot, removing self-guidance during OOD post-training led to unnatural lower-body motions (e.g., foot eversion, knee valgus) and poor real-robot transfer, even if the sparse tracking objective was met. With self-guidance, the lower body remained natural and stable, enabling reliable transfer to hardware. This demonstrated the importance of self-guidance in constraining unobserved lower-body motion.
- **Effect of OOD Post-Training:** For challenging squat-like OOD motions, the zero-shot ASYNC-CA policy showed inaccurate tracking and unstable forward staggering on the real robot. The post-trained ASYNC-CA policy, however, followed the reference more closely and maintained a stable whole-body posture in both simulation and hardware, confirming the practical benefits of OOD post-training.
- **Generalization:** The post-trained ASYNC-CA policy demonstrated generalization to a variety of whole-body motions on the Unitree G1, including waving, object manipulation, deep squatting, and forward walking, all driven by sparse upper-body trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses fundamental challenges in modern humanoid robot control, namely the temporal asynchrony and structural incompleteness inherent in bridging high-level task planners with low-level whole-body controllers.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

1. **Enhanced Robustness to Asynchrony:** The introduction of time-indexed conditioning and sliding-window global rewards offers an end-to-end method for handling reference frame mismatch without relying on explicit, potentially error-prone, state estimation. This improves the robot's ability to maintain coherent motion despite intermittent and low-frequency planner updates, making humanoid control more resilient in dynamic environments.
2. **Improved Adaptability and Safety for Novel Tasks:** The MPC-based trajectory completion mechanism provides crucial dense guidance for otherwise under-constrained sparse task references. Combined with two-level self-guidance regularization, this significantly stabilizes reinforcement learning during post-training. This allows pre-trained humanoid policies to adapt to out-of-distribution tasks specified by only partial body commands, reducing the risk of unsafe joint configurations or unnatural behaviors. This capability is critical for deploying humanoids in diverse and unstructured real-world scenarios where precise, full-body planning might not always be feasible or available.
3. **Bridge Between Planning and Control:** The framework directly addresses the "execution-interface gap," facilitating a more seamless integration of high-level task planners (which often output sparse, high-level commands) with the precise, high-frequency control requirements of humanoid robots. This could accelerate the development and deployment of task-oriented humanoid applications, as planners can focus on abstract goals while the proposed controller manages the complex real-time execution.
4. **Hardware Validation:** The successful deployment and demonstration on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot in real-world scenarios provide empirical evidence for the practical viability and transferability of the proposed learning-based approach. This validation strengthens the argument for its utility beyond simulation.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens avenues for future research, such as investigating the end-to-end training of both planners and controllers. By providing a more robust and adaptable control layer, it could support the development of more sophisticated, general-purpose humanoid intelligence capable of complex loco-manipulation tasks under varied and uncertain conditions. The concept of implicit frame alignment and self-guidance could also be applicable to other complex robotic systems facing similar asynchronous or incomplete command challenges.
