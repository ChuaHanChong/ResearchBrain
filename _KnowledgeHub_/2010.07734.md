# Self-training for Few-shot Transfer Across Extreme Task Differences

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Cornell University developed STARTUP, a self-training strategy designed to adapt feature representations from a source domain to drastically different target domains using only unlabeled data. This method consistently improves few-shot transfer performance, achieving an average of 2.9 percentage points higher 1-shot classification accuracy on challenging cross-domain benchmarks.

## Problem

- Existing Few-Shot Learning (FSL) techniques largely fail when there is an extreme domain gap between the base (source) dataset and the novel target few-shot task.
- Naive transfer learning, while a common baseline, provides suboptimal performance when features learned from a source domain (e.g., ImageNet) are not discriminative for an extremely different target domain.
- Pure Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) often requires extremely large quantities (millions) of unlabeled data to be competitive, which is not always available in practical target domains.

## Method

- A ==teacher== model is initially pre-trained on a large, labeled source dataset to establish an initial representation of general visual features.
- This pre-trained teacher model then generates ==soft pseudo-labels== for an unlabeled dataset from the target domain, effectively inferring meaningful similarity structures among target images.
- A ==student== model is subsequently trained using a composite loss that combines standard ==cross-entropy== on the original source data, ==KL divergence== to match the teacher's soft pseudo-labels on target data, and an optional ==self-supervised loss (SimCLR)== on target data to refine the representation.

## Results

- STARTUP consistently outperformed state-of-the-art baselines on the BSCD-FSL benchmark, achieving an average of **2.9 percentage points** improvement in 1-shot classification accuracy, with gains up to nearly **6 points** on datasets like CropDisease.
- Analysis revealed that STARTUP processes lead to more separated and discriminative clusters in the feature space, confirming that the teacher's soft predictions provide a useful grouping signal on the target domain (e.g., Adjusted Mutual Information around **0.3**).
- Performance scales positively with the amount of unlabeled target-domain data provided, and integrating a strong self-supervised objective like SimCLR further enhances the adapted representation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Even when source-domain classes are irrelevant to the target task, a strong teacher model can induce meaningful latent similarity structures or 'groupings' within unseen target domain data.
> - Self-training can be effectively repurposed to adapt general feature representations across extreme domain shifts, rather than solely improving classification on the same task.
> - By jointly leveraging source-domain knowledge and moderate amounts of unlabeled target-domain data, the student model learns more discriminative representations than either pure transfer learning or standalone self-supervised learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of **Few-Shot Learning (FSL)**, **Transfer Learning**, **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)**, and **Self-Training**, specifically addressing the challenging problem of **Cross-Domain Few-Shot Learning (CD-FSL)** under extreme domain differences.

- **Few-Shot Learning (FSL):** Traditional FSL aims to train models that can generalize to new classes with very few labeled examples (e.g., 1-5 shots per class). Most FSL techniques, however, assume that the "base" dataset (used for pre-training or meta-learning) and the "novel" target classes come from the same or very similar domains. They often rely on shared modes of variation or class-distinctive features between base and novel classes. This paper highlights that these assumptions break down when the domain gap is extreme.

- **Cross-Domain Few-Shot Learning (CD-FSL):** This sub-field directly addresses the challenge where the base dataset (e.g., ImageNet) is drastically different from the target few-shot task domain (e.g., medical images, satellite data). Prior work, as cited in the paper (Guo et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019b), has shown that state-of-the-art FSL methods largely fail in such extreme scenarios, often underperforming even a naive transfer learning baseline. This paper directly tackles this critical gap, aiming to develop a method that *successfully* bridges extreme domain differences.

- **Transfer Learning:** The most common approach for CD-FSL is to use a pre-trained model from a large source domain (like ImageNet) and fine-tune or use its features for the target domain. While often effective for similar domains, the paper emphasizes its limitations when the domain gap is "extreme," as features learned from ImageNet (object classification in internet photos) may not be optimally discriminative for tasks like pneumonia detection in X-rays or crop type identification in satellite images.

- **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL):** The rise of SSL, particularly contrastive learning methods like SimCLR, has demonstrated great potential for learning robust representations from unlabeled data. This is particularly appealing for domains like medical imaging where labeled data is scarce but unlabeled data is abundant. However, the paper notes a crucial limitation: pure SSL often requires extremely large amounts of unlabeled data (millions of images) to compete with ImageNet pre-training. With more practical, smaller unlabeled datasets, SSL struggles to outperform naive ImageNet transfer (Wallace & Hariharan, 2020).

- **Self-Training:** This technique typically involves using a "teacher" model trained on labeled data to generate pseudo-labels for unlabeled data, which are then used to train a "student" model (often with the original labeled data). Traditional self-training primarily aims to improve classification performance on the *same* task and label space. This paper innovatively adapts self-training for **representation learning and domain adaptation**, where the goal is to improve the *feature representation* for a *different*, novel target task with a disjoint label space. It extends the utility of self-training beyond its conventional scope, positioning it as a powerful tool for bridging domain gaps in representation learning.

In summary, this work specifically targets the Achilles' heel of FSL and transfer learning – the extreme domain gap – by intelligently combining insights from self-training and self-supervised learning with readily available unlabeled target-domain data. It seeks a "sweet spot" between relying solely on strong source-domain inductive biases (which can fail) and learning tabula rasa via pure SSL (which needs massive data).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a simple yet effective method that allows feature representations, initially trained on a large labeled dataset from a *source* domain, to **adapt to extremely different *target* domains** using only a small amount of unlabeled data from the target domain. This adapted representation should then facilitate rapid learning of novel classes with very few labeled examples in the target domain (few-shot learning).

The core motivations for this work stem from critical limitations in current state-of-the-art visual recognition systems:

- **High Annotation Costs:** Training deep learning models for new classes or novel domains typically requires thousands of labeled examples, which is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming in many real-world applications (e.g., medical diagnostics, environmental monitoring with satellite imagery).
- **Failure of Few-Shot Learning in Extreme Domain Gaps:** While FSL addresses the "few-example" problem, most techniques assume a high degree of similarity between the base pre-training domain and the target few-shot domain. When this assumption is violated – for instance, pre-training on ImageNet and transferring to X-ray images – FSL methods often perform poorly, even worse than simple transfer learning.
- **Limitations of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL):** SSL is a promising approach for leveraging abundant unlabeled data. However, for it to effectively *outperform* naive transfer from ImageNet, it typically demands extremely large quantities of unlabeled data (on the order of millions). Many practical applications may only have access to moderately sized unlabeled datasets. This creates a conundrum: pure SSL might not be strong enough with practical amounts of unlabeled data, while ImageNet-based transfer might not be flexible enough for extreme domain shifts.
- **Abundance of Unlabeled Data:** Despite the scarcity of labeled data, many real-world domains (e.g., undiagnosed X-rays, unlabeled satellite images) have a wealth of unlabeled data. The motivation is to effectively harness this resource to improve cross-domain transfer.

The paper aims to find a robust solution that can bridge the "extreme domain gap" in few-shot classification, outperforming existing baselines that either ignore unlabeled target-domain data or use it inefficiently.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named **STARTUP (Self Training to Adapt Representations To Unseen Problems)**, is a self-training strategy designed to adapt source-domain feature representations to a novel target domain using unlabeled data. STARTUP operates in two main phases: a representation learning phase and an evaluation phase.

#### 3.1. Representation Learning Phase

This phase involves three sequential steps to train a "student" model:

1. **Teacher Model Pre-training:** A "teacher" model, denoted as $f_{\theta_0} = C \circ \varphi$, is initially trained on a large, labeled **base dataset** ($D_B$) from the source domain (e.g., miniImageNet or full ImageNet). $\varphi$ is the embedding function and $C$ is a classifier head. This step establishes an initial representation that has learned some general visual features from the source domain. The teacher model is trained by minimizing the standard cross-entropy loss on $D_B$.

2. **Soft Labeling of Unlabeled Target Data:** The pre-trained teacher model ($f_{\theta_0}$) is then used to generate **soft pseudo-labels** for an unlabeled dataset ($D_u$) from the target domain. For each unlabeled image $x_i \in D_u$, the teacher model outputs a probability distribution $\bar{y}_i = f_{\theta_0}(x_i)$. These soft labels represent the teacher's "belief" about the class distribution of target images, essentially inducing a grouping of target images based on the source-domain feature space. A crucial insight here is that while the *specific classes* of the teacher model are irrelevant to the novel target task, the *notions of similarity and dissimilarity* implied by these soft predictions can be highly informative.

3. **Student Model Training:** A new "student" model ($f_\theta = C \circ \varphi$) is then trained. The student's parameters ($\theta$) are optimized using a composite loss function, leveraging both the original labeled base dataset ($D_B$) and the softly-labeled target dataset ($D^*_u$), along with an optional self-supervised loss:
 - **Cross-Entropy Loss on $D_B$ ($l_{CE}$):** The student is trained on the original labeled source data to retain prior knowledge from the source domain. This prevents catastrophic forgetting and ensures the student maintains generalizable features.
 - **KL Divergence Loss on $D^*_u$ ($l_{KL}$):** This is the core self-training component. The student model's predictions on the unlabeled target data are encouraged to match the soft pseudo-labels generated by the teacher using KL divergence. This term compels the student to learn a representation that emphasizes the groupings and similarity structures identified by the teacher within the target domain.
 - **Self-Supervised Loss on $D_u$ ($l_{unlabeled}$):** An additional self-supervised loss is applied to the unlabeled target data. The paper primarily uses **SimCLR** (Chen et al., 2020), a contrastive learning objective that encourages different augmentations of the same image to have similar representations while being dissimilar from other images. This term helps the student extract further target-domain-specific knowledge, refining the representation by focusing on intrinsic visual properties of the target data.

The student model's embedding function ($\varphi$) is initialized either from scratch, from the teacher's embedding with a random classifier, or from the full teacher model. The paper's default choice is to initialize the student's embedding with the teacher's embedding and a randomly initialized classifier head, based on empirical findings.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Phase

Once the student model's representation ($\varphi$) is trained using STARTUP, it is **frozen**. For each few-shot task in the target domain, a simple linear classifier is then trained on the provided "support set" (a few labeled examples per class) and evaluated on the "query set" (unseen examples from the same novel classes). This standard evaluation protocol for few-shot learning ensures that the learned representation's quality is the primary factor under assessment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were primarily conducted on the challenging **BSCD-FSL benchmark** (Guo et al., 2020), which involves transferring from miniImageNet (object recognition) to four drastically different target domains: CropDiseases (plant diseases), EuroSAT (satellite land-use), ISIC2018 (melanoma detection), and ChestX (chest X-ray diagnosis).

#### 4.1. Performance on Extreme Cross-Domain Few-Shot Transfer

- **Significant Gains over Baselines:** STARTUP consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art few-shot learning techniques, naive transfer learning, and self-supervised baselines (SimCLR) in most scenarios on the BSCD-FSL benchmark.
- **Average 2.9 Points Improvement:** On average, STARTUP achieves a 2.9 percentage point improvement in 1-shot classification accuracy over the best performing baseline (naive Transfer) across the challenging datasets.
- **Strongest Gains on Specific Datasets:** The improvement is particularly notable on CropDisease, where STARTUP boosts 1-shot accuracy by almost 6 points. This suggests STARTUP effectively adapts to domains with significant visual differences from ImageNet.
- **Outperforming Combined Baselines:** STARTUP generally outperforms "Transfer + SimCLR," a baseline that combines ImageNet pre-training with additional SimCLR training on target unlabeled data, demonstrating that the self-training mechanism of STARTUP provides unique benefits beyond simple combination.
- **Benefit from Stronger Teachers:** When using a larger and richer source (full ILSVRC 2012 ImageNet with ResNet-18), STARTUP continues to provide substantial improvements over the transfer baseline, particularly on EuroSAT and CropDisease, indicating its robustness to teacher quality.

#### 4.2. Analysis of "Why STARTUP Works"

The paper investigates two main hypotheses to explain STARTUP's effectiveness:

- **Hypothesis 1: STARTUP adds noise, increasing robustness.** This hypothesis, sometimes applicable in traditional self-training, suggests that unlabeled data primarily adds regularization. However, experiments show that training STARTUP on unlabeled data from a *different* domain than the target task leads to significant performance degradation. This indicates that the unlabeled data must be **from the target domain** and is not merely acting as noise, but rather imparting specific structure.
- **Hypothesis 2: STARTUP enhances teacher-induced groupings.** This hypothesis posits that the teacher model's soft predictions, despite being from a different label space, induce a **meaningful grouping** or similarity structure on the target domain data.
 - **Meaningful Grouping:** The Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) between the teacher's induced groupings and the ground truth labels on EuroSAT and CropDisease shows a substantial agreement (around 0.3), particularly for the datasets where STARTUP shows the largest gains. This supports the idea that the teacher's "understanding" of similarities, even if not class-specific, is still relevant.
 - **Enhanced Discriminability:** t-SNE visualizations confirm that STARTUP processes result in **more separated and discriminative clusters** in the feature space compared to the initial representation, reinforcing the idea that it accentuates these latent similarity structures.

These findings strongly suggest that STARTUP functions by (a) leveraging a potentially meaningful, albeit implicit, grouping from the source-trained teacher on the target domain, and (b) training a new representation that explicitly emphasizes and strengthens these groupings, making them more discriminative for downstream few-shot tasks.

#### 4.3. Few-Shot Transfer Across Similar Domains

- **Mixed Results on In-Domain FSL:** On benchmarks with smaller domain gaps (miniImageNet and tieredImageNet), STARTUP's benefits are less pronounced or even mixed. For miniImageNet, it neither significantly helps nor hurts. For tieredImageNet, some STARTUP variants, particularly with specific initialization strategies (e.g., STARTUP-Rand (no SS) for `tieredImageNet-more`), can outperform naive Transfer. This indicates that while primarily designed for extreme gaps, STARTUP can sometimes be beneficial even in closer domain scenarios, but its efficacy might be more sensitive to hyper-parameters and initialization.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Impact of Unlabeled Data Amount:** STARTUP demonstrably benefits from **more unlabeled data** from the target domain, showing improved accuracy with increasing amounts of unlabeled examples.
- **Training without Base Dataset:** Removing the base dataset from student training (fine-tuning paradigm) can lead to performance degradation in some cases (e.g., ISIC 5-shot), suggesting that jointly training with the source data helps maintain generalization.
- **Choice of Self-Supervision:** Using SimCLR as the auxiliary self-supervised loss generally outperforms other pretext tasks like rotation prediction, highlighting the importance of choosing an effective self-supervised method within the STARTUP framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper "SELF-TRAINING FOR FEW-SHOT TRANSFER ACROSS EXTREME TASK DIFFERENCES" makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact:

- **Pioneering Solution for Extreme CD-FSL:** This work presents one of the first successful and consistently effective solutions for few-shot transfer across truly *extreme* domain differences, a problem that has largely stumped prior state-of-the-art FSL and transfer learning methods. By demonstrating consistent improvements on the challenging BSCD-FSL benchmark, STARTUP establishes a new baseline for this critical problem.
- **Innovative Adaptation of Self-Training:** It re-imagines self-training not just for improving classifier performance on the same task, but for **adapting general feature representations** to novel domains with entirely different label spaces. This novel perspective broadens the applicability and theoretical understanding of self-training.
- **Effective Utilization of Unlabeled Data:** STARTUP provides a practical and efficient way to leverage readily available unlabeled data in target domains. This is particularly impactful for real-world applications where obtaining large labeled datasets is costly or impossible (e.g., medical imaging, remote sensing, specialized industrial applications).
- **Bridging the Gap between Transfer Learning and SSL:** The method offers a "sweet spot" solution, combining the inductive biases from strong pre-trained source models with the domain-specific learning capabilities of self-supervised methods on unlabeled target data. It avoids the pitfalls of relying too heavily on either approach alone when faced with extreme domain shifts.
- **Insights into Representation Learning:** The analysis of "why STARTUP works" provides valuable insights into how representations adapt. The finding that source-trained teachers induce meaningful *groupings* (not just class predictions) in novel domains, and that strengthening these groupings improves transfer, is a fundamental contribution to our understanding of feature transferability.
- **Practical Applicability:** The simplicity and effectiveness of STARTUP make it highly practical for researchers and practitioners. The release of the code further enables reproducibility and encourages adoption.
- **Future Research Directions:** This work opens avenues for further research into:
 - Exploring other self-supervised pretext tasks within the STARTUP framework.
 - Developing more sophisticated methods for generating and leveraging pseudo-labels for cross-domain representation adaptation.
 - Investigating the theoretical underpinnings of why certain "teacher-induced groupings" are more meaningful than others across domains.
 - Applying STARTUP to other challenging cross-domain transfer tasks beyond image classification.

In essence, STARTUP offers a powerful and elegant solution to a previously intractable problem in deep learning, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in few-shot learning and paving the way for more robust and data-efficient AI systems in diverse real-world applications.
