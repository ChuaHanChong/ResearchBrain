# Generalized Agent Iteration: One Formal Framework for Iterative Policy Improvement and Recursive Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> Generalized Agent Iteration (GAI) is a formal framework that casts classical generalized policy iteration and recursive self-improvement as two settings of one cycle: agent evaluation, where critics score the agent against an evaluation base, then agent improvement, where a modifier proposes a new agent instance. Two dials, whether the modifier lies inside the agent and whether the standard stays externally grounded, place existing systems on shared axes and make four defects statable one condition at a time.

## Problem

- Recursive self-improvement is claimed at many scales, yet the label is used loosely for ambitions that differ substantially, with no shared criterion separating them.
- Generalized policy iteration covers only systems whose update principle and reward lie outside the agent, so it cannot describe agents that edit their own improvement mechanism.
- Prior formal work fixes a single design, such as the proof-gated Gödel machine, rather than giving coordinates that make competing self-improvement designs comparable to each other.

## Method

- System definition: five components, ==policy==, ==action critic==, ==modifier==, ==modification critic==, ==evaluation base==, with the agent defined as whichever subset of them is modifiable.
- The **GAI cycle** alternates ==agent evaluation==, critics scoring the agent part against base rho, with ==agent improvement==, the modifier sampling a new agent instance the system adopts.
- Two ==dials== fix polarity, anchored, goal drift, or fully self-referential, alongside a reference ideal of two ==self-consistency conditions== calibrating the modification critic and restricting the modifier.

## Results

- The system table places **six** families on the dials: policy iteration, PPO and fixed outer loops as anchored GPI; Gödel machine, STOP, SICA and Darwin Gödel Machine as anchored RSI.
- Only **one** co-evolving-evaluator system occupies the goal-drift row and only a closed-system position paper the fully self-referential row; **four** defects are catalogued, each violating a named GPI condition.
- Cited benchmark evidence shows non-monotone search: **58.33%** of settings improve on the first valid attempt, yet **78.26%** of searches continuing past their best score finish lower.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Ask where the standard sits, not how faithful it is: an external reward model counts as anchored, the same judge inside the agent is goal drift.
> - Loops that keep weights, a prompt template or a verifier fixed outside are bounded self-improvement; attainable quality is capped by whatever component never enters the loop.
> - Goal drift is the failure hardest to notice: the objective keeps its name while the instrument measuring progress toward it is quietly rewritten.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a conceptual and formal gap between classical reinforcement learning and emerging work on recursive self-improvement (RSI). In reinforcement learning, iterative policy improvement is commonly described through **generalized policy iteration (GPI)**, in which policy evaluation and policy improvement alternate. The reward function and the update mechanism are normally treated as external to the policy being optimized. Under standard assumptions, this structure supports well-established convergence results, including convergence to an optimal policy in finite Markov decision processes.

Recent AI systems, however, increasingly modify more than their immediate outputs or policies. They may revise prompts, memory structures, tools, training-data strategies, code, optimization routines, evaluators, or entire research workflows. Examples discussed in the paper include Self-Refine, Reflexion, STOP, Gödel agents, the Darwin Gödel Machine, AlphaEvolve, automated scientific research loops, and systems that co-evolve their evaluators. These systems are often described using terms such as self-improvement, self-evolution, or recursive self-improvement, although the terms refer to substantially different mechanisms.

The paper situates these developments along a continuum. It distinguishes bounded self-refinement—where a system improves its outputs within a fixed outer loop—from stronger forms of RSI, in which the mechanism responsible for improvement can itself be modified. It also separates systems that retain an external evaluation standard from systems whose evaluators or goals can change.

The central contribution is therefore a proposed unifying framework, **Generalized Agent Iteration (GAI)**. GAI extends the evaluation–improvement structure of GPI to systems in which the agent includes modifiable mechanisms for changing itself. Rather than treating RSI as a collection of unrelated examples, the paper provides two formal classification dimensions for comparing systems and identifying where classical guarantees cease to apply.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Establishing a common formal vocabulary

The primary objective is to define a common mathematical description for both ordinary policy improvement and recursive self-improvement. The authors argue that existing terminology does not adequately specify what part of a system is being improved, what mechanism performs the improvement, and what standard determines whether a change is beneficial.

GAI addresses this by representing an agent as a configuration of system components. These components may include a policy, critics, a modifier, and an evaluation base. The agent is not defined solely as the policy that acts in the world. Instead, it consists of whichever components are designated as modifiable.

#### 2.2. Clarifying what “recursive” means

The paper focuses on a specific meaning of recursion: the improvement mechanism is itself among the components that can be changed. Under this definition, a system is recursively self-improving when its modifier can rewrite the modifier that will conduct future improvement. This distinguishes RSI from systems that repeatedly improve outputs using a fixed retry or reflection loop.

For example, Self-Refine and Reflexion may improve generated outputs or maintain reflection memories, but their outer improvement procedure remains fixed. STOP and Gödel-agent-style systems are more recursive because the code or routine responsible for improvement can be altered.

#### 2.3. Analyzing the role of external standards

A second objective is to determine whether the standard used to evaluate improvement remains grounded outside the agent. The paper identifies three possible conditions:

1. **Anchored:** The evaluation standard is externally grounded and cannot be rewritten by the agent.
2. **Goal drift:** The standard has external origins but is itself part of the modifiable agent and can therefore change.
3. **Fully self-referential:** No external standard remains, so the system evaluates itself using internally generated criteria.

This distinction is motivated by concerns that a system may appear to improve while changing the basis on which improvement is measured.

#### 2.4. Identifying failures of classical guarantees

The paper also aims to state, in a condition-by-condition manner, why the guarantees associated with GPI do not automatically transfer to RSI. It identifies four defects associated with self-modifying systems: difficult search over candidate selves, self-evaluation, ungrounded evaluation standards, and goal drift.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Formal system representation

The authors define an external world as a Markov decision process,

\[
W=(S,A,p,r),
\]

where \(S\) is the state space, \(A\) is the action space, \(p\) is the transition law, and \(r\) is the reward function. They also introduce an external goal \(G\), which represents the objective the system is expected to serve.

A system is represented by a configuration

\[
\chi=(\pi,V,m,U,\rho),
\]

with five conceptual components:

- **Policy \(\pi\):** Selects actions in world states.
- **Action critic \(V\):** Evaluates how well the policy performs.
- **Modifier \(m\):** Proposes changes to the agent.
- **Modification critic \(U\):** Evaluates whether proposed changes are beneficial.
- **Evaluation base \(\rho\):** Defines the standard against which the critics assess performance.

The agent is represented by a subset of these components. If \(Ag\) denotes the set of modifiable components, then an agent instance \(\xi\) is an assignment of contents to those components. The complete system can be updated by replacing its current agent portion with a proposed instance:

\[
\chi \leftarrow \chi[\xi].
\]

This formulation allows different systems to define the agent boundary differently. In a classical policy-optimization method, the agent may contain only \(\pi\) and \(V\). In a recursive system, it may include \(\pi\), \(V\), \(m\), and \(U\), and potentially \(\rho\).

#### 3.2. The GAI cycle

GAI consists of two alternating operations:

1. **Agent evaluation:** A critic evaluates the current agent or a proposed modification against an evaluation base.
2. **Agent improvement:** The modifier proposes a new agent instance, which the system adopts.

The framework deliberately abstracts away from implementation. Evaluation could use Bellman backups, temporal-difference learning, Monte Carlo estimates, benchmark tests, empirical validation, or another scoring procedure. Improvement could be greedy policy improvement, stochastic search, code rewriting, evolutionary search, or another modification mechanism.

#### 3.3. The two formal “dials”

The first dial asks whether the modifier belongs to the agent:

- If \(m \notin Ag\), the improvement mechanism is fixed and external. This corresponds to GPI-like systems.
- If \(m \in Ag\), the modifier can be rewritten by the system. This closes the recursive loop and corresponds to RSI.

The second dial asks whether the evaluation base remains externally grounded:

- If the base is external and outside the agent, the system is anchored.
- If the base has external origins but is included among modifiable components, the system permits goal drift.
- If the base contains no external content or depends only on the system’s own judgments, the system is fully self-referential.

#### 3.4. Reduction of GPI to GAI

The paper shows how conventional policy iteration fits within GAI. In this case, the agent consists of \(\{\pi,V\}\), while the modifier is fixed externally. The modifier implements evaluation and improvement operators such as

\[
V \leftarrow E_m(V,\pi;\rho_V)
\]

and

\[
\pi \leftarrow I_m(V).
\]

When \(E_m\) is policy evaluation and \(I_m\) is greedy improvement, the process becomes ordinary policy or value iteration. The evaluation base is grounded in the environmental reward. Thus, GPI is presented as an anchored, nonrecursive special case of GAI.

#### 3.5. Formalization of RSI

In RSI, the modifier is included in the agent. The current modifier can produce a successor containing a new policy, critic, modifier, and possibly modification critic. The modifier can therefore change the procedure that will perform future updates.

The paper also specifies reference self-consistency conditions for an idealized self-improver. The modification critic should accurately estimate the value of proposed successor agents, and the modifier should propose only changes that maximize the critic’s estimate of future value. These are descriptive conditions rather than guaranteed properties of actual systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. A unified classification of existing systems

The authors place several classes of systems within the GAI framework.

- **Policy iteration, value iteration, and PPO:** These have fixed external improvement mechanisms and externally grounded rewards, so they are classified as anchored GPI.
- **Fixed outer-loop pipelines:** Model, prompt, or harness optimization occurs within a designer-specified proposal–training–selection loop. These remain nonrecursive and anchored.
- **Gödel machines:** The rewriting mechanism is part of the system, but rewrites are restricted by proofs of utility improvement. They are classified as anchored, guarded RSI.
- **STOP, SICA, Gödel Agent, Darwin Gödel Machine, Polaris, and Hyperagents:** These systems modify code, prompts, memory, tools, or scaffolds while using external benchmarks or validation sets. They are classified as anchored RSI, although some remain bounded because model weights or other mechanisms stay fixed.
- **Red Queen Gödel Machine:** The evaluator co-evolves with the agent. This places the system in the goal-drift category.
- **Fully closed self-improvement proposals:** Systems without an external evaluation signal are classified as fully self-referential RSI.

The classification makes explicit that “self-improvement” does not necessarily imply strong recursive self-improvement. A system can improve its outputs or scaffold while remaining constrained by a fixed outer mechanism.

#### 4.2. Four defects associated with RSI

The paper identifies four consequences of moving away from the anchored GPI setting.

##### 4.2.1. Search over candidate selves

When the modifier is part of the agent, improvement involves searching over a potentially very large space of possible policies, critics, codebases, or modifiers. Unlike standard policy improvement, there is no general reason to assume that this search is computationally inexpensive or monotonic. The paper cites evidence from a benchmark in which many continued searches ended with lower final performance than an earlier best result.

##### 4.2.2. Self-evaluation

If the modification critic is itself modifiable, then the system changes both the object being evaluated and the instrument performing the evaluation. A critic may therefore become inaccurate, permissive, or aligned with a faulty internal criterion. External proof gates, validation suites, or risk controls can constrain this problem, but the framework notes that such safeguards must be explicitly maintained.

##### 4.2.3. Ungrounded evaluation bases

If the evaluation base does not remain grounded in the external goal, self-consistency alone does not imply goal achievement. A modifier and critic can agree that a change is beneficial according to an internal standard while that standard becomes increasingly disconnected from the world objective.

##### 4.2.4. Goal drift

Goal drift is a specific case in which the evaluation base has external origins but is itself modifiable. The external goal may remain fixed in principle, while the proxy or evaluator used to measure progress changes. The paper uses a coding example: a fixed external test suite can anchor improvement, but if the agent can edit the tests, it may improve its score without improving the code.

#### 4.3. No empirical performance evaluation

The paper does not present a new implementation, benchmark, ablation study, or experimental comparison. Its results are formal and taxonomic. The claimed findings consist of definitions, system classifications, reductions, and structural consequences derived from the framework. The authors explicitly describe several mathematical questions as unresolved, including the reachability and computational cost of self-modifications and whether internally constructed monitors can reliably detect evaluator drift.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GAI provides a structured vocabulary for distinguishing several processes that are often grouped under self-improvement. Its main significance is analytical rather than empirical. By specifying the agent boundary and the location of the evaluation standard, it allows researchers to ask whether a proposed system is merely refining outputs, modifying an implementation, modifying its own improvement mechanism, or altering the criteria by which improvement is judged.

The framework may support the design of safer and more interpretable self-improvement systems. In particular, it emphasizes the importance of preserving external evaluation, separating evaluators from the objects they assess, and treating proof gates, benchmarks, and rollback mechanisms as explicit parts of the system architecture. It also provides a way to compare guarded self-modification with more open-ended or self-referential proposals.

The framework’s limitations constrain the strength of its conclusions. It does not establish that a self-improving system can reach proposed successor configurations, quantify the computational resources required, model different time scales, or prove that the identified defects occur in every practical implementation. Its treatment of external safeguards is also partly dependent on where the analyst draws the boundary between the agent and its environment.

Overall, the paper offers a formal organizing scheme for research on iterative improvement and RSI. Its central contribution is to show how classical GPI and recursive self-modification can be represented as variations of the same evaluation–improvement cycle, while making clear that recursion introduces additional search, verification, and alignment problems.
