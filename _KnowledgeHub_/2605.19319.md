# SWEET: Sparse World Modeling with Image Editing for Embodied Task Execution

> [!summary] Summary
> SWEET is a framework from the Show Lab at the National University of Singapore that employs image editing models to create sparse, task-relevant visual keyframes for robot manipulation, offering an efficient alternative to dense video generation. This approach, which includes a mixed-training strategy for the action predictor, achieves superior keyframe fidelity and up to 92% task success rates in simulation, while reducing planning inference time from over 400 seconds to approximately 10 seconds per task.

## Problem

- Dense video generation for embodied control is computationally expensive and often redundant for robot manipulation tasks, which can typically be summarized by a few critical visual states.
- Existing visual prediction methods for robot control often use generated images as single-step goals or auxiliary conditions, rather than as sequential, sparse, and task-level plans.
- A domain gap arises between the clean, real keyframe transitions used to train action policies and the synthetically generated, edited keyframes produced by visual planners during inference.

## Method

- **SWEET** proposes a ==one-shot sparse visual planning== framework that leverages a fine-tuned ==image-editing model== (FLUX-Kontext) to progressively generate a sequence of task-relevant keyframes.
- The image-editing planner is conditioned on natural language instructions and optional ==arrow-based spatial guidance== to direct the visual plan.
- A ==goal-conditioned diffusion action predictor== is developed to translate the imagined sparse keyframes into executable robot action chunks, and a ==mixed-training strategy== is introduced to bridge the real-to-edited domain gap by combining real and filtered synthetically generated keyframe transitions.

## Results

- Image editing with FLUX-Kontext significantly outperformed a representative video generation model (Wan2.2) in keyframe prediction, achieving lower LPIPS (**0.07832** vs **0.1303** on RoboMimic) and higher PSNR (**20.71** vs **19.00**), while reducing planning inference time from over **400 seconds** to approximately **10 seconds** for a task-level plan.
- SWEET successfully executed full robot manipulation tasks in simulation (RoboMimic), demonstrating up to a **92.0%** success rate on the Lift task by translating sparse visual plans into robot actions.
- Mixed training for the action predictor consistently improved robustness and performance across both real and generated target keyframe domains, achieving **92.0%** success with both real and generated targets on the Lift task, compared to **58.0%** for a model trained only on real targets when facing generated targets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Image editing models are more efficient and generate visually more accurate sparse world models for robotic manipulation compared to dense video generation, producing keyframes with higher fidelity and embodiment consistency.
> - Conditioning the visual planner with both language and simple 2D spatial cues (arrow-based) effectively guides the image editing process for complex manipulation tasks.
> - Explicitly addressing the domain gap between real and synthetically generated visual subgoals through a mixed-training strategy is critical for robust action policy execution when using generative visual planners.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the broader fields of embodied intelligence, robot manipulation, and generative AI for control. Recent advancements in visual prediction, particularly dense video generation, have been explored as a foundation for embodied control, enabling robots to anticipate future observations and translate them into actions. These models can serve as generative world models or world-action models for planning, inverse dynamics, and direct robot control.

The paper acknowledges the emergence of generated visual goals as useful intermediate representations for robot control, including image-based subgoal synthesis, goal-image-conditioned policy learning, and object-centric goal-state generation. These approaches highlight the potential of visual future states to provide an explicit and interpretable interface between high-level task intent and low-level robot execution.

However, the authors identify a gap: existing efforts often treat generated images as single-step goals, auxiliary policy conditions, or final-state representations. The sequential imagination of multiple task-relevant future states using modern in-context image-editing models, especially in direct comparison with dense video generation under robotic data settings, remains underexplored.

This research specifically addresses the computational expense and potential redundancy of dense video generation for many manipulation tasks, which can often be summarized by a small number of task-relevant keyframes (e.g., approach, contact, grasp, transport, release). By proposing sparse keyframe prediction as an alternative, the work seeks to develop a more efficient and targeted approach to visual world modeling for robotics, leveraging the capabilities of image editing models rather than traditional video generation. It contributes to the ongoing development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and video model-based robot policies by proposing a more computationally efficient paradigm for visual planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether image editing models can function as sparse visual world models for robot manipulation by predicting task-level future states without requiring dense video rollout. This is motivated by the observation that while visual prediction is a promising paradigm for embodied control, dense video generation is computationally expensive and often generates redundant intermediate frames for manipulation tasks whose progress can be effectively summarized by a limited set of key visual states.

The specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Efficiency in Visual Planning:** To explore whether dense video prediction, traditionally used for future observation generation, is truly necessary for robotic manipulation, or if a more efficient sparse keyframe prediction approach can achieve similar or superior task-level planning capabilities. The hypothesis is that many manipulation tasks are characterized by a few critical visual milestones, making dense video generation inefficient.
- **Controlled Comparison of Generative Models:** To conduct a controlled comparison between a representative video generation model (Wan2.2) and an image editing model (FLUX-Kontext) under identical robotic data settings. This comparison aims to quantitatively and qualitatively assess their performance in predicting task-level keyframes, focusing on visual fidelity, scene consistency, robot-arm preservation, and inference efficiency.
- **Development of a Sparse Visual Planning Framework:** To propose and develop SWEET, a one-shot sparse visual planning framework that progressively generates a sequence of task-relevant manipulation keyframes. This framework utilizes successive image editing conditioned on natural language instructions and optional arrow-based spatial guidance.
- **Integration with Robot Execution:** To connect the imagined sparse visual keyframes with executable robot actions. This involves training a goal-conditioned diffusion action predictor capable of translating adjacent imagined keyframes into actionable command chunks for the robot.
- **Addressing the Real-to-Edited Domain Gap:** To mitigate the mismatch that arises between real, clean keyframe transitions (used for training the action predictor) and synthetically generated, edited visual subgoals (received by the action predictor during inference). This objective leads to the development of a mixed-training strategy that exposes the action predictor to the visual distribution of planner-generated targets.
- **Validation on Robotic Benchmarks:** To experimentally validate the effectiveness of SWEET on both real-world (DROID) and simulated (RoboMimic) robotic manipulation tasks, assessing improvements in keyframe prediction accuracy across diverse scenes and the feasibility of executing full pipelines from sequential keyframe planning to robot actions.

In essence, the research aims to establish image editing as a practical, lightweight, and effective prior for robotic visual planning, offering a computationally more efficient and task-centric alternative to dense video prediction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SWEET framework operates by first generating a sparse sequence of visual keyframes through image editing and then executing robot actions to reach these planned subgoals using a goal-conditioned policy.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

A robot trajectory $\tau = \{(x_t, a_t)\}_{t=1}^T$ is represented by a sparse sequence of task-relevant keyframes $K = \{k_0, k_1, \ldots, k_M\}$, where $k_m = x_{t_m}$ are visual observations at critical timestamps. Each keyframe transition $k_m \to k_{m+1}$ is associated with a subtask condition $c_m = (l_m, r_m)$, comprising a language instruction $l_m$ and an optional arrow-based spatial cue $r_m$. At inference, given an initial observation $o_0$ and a sequence of subtask conditions $C = \{c_m\}_{m=0}^{M-1}$, SWEET aims to predict a sparse visual plan $\hat{K}$ and infer executable action chunks $\hat{A}_{t:t+H-1}$ to navigate through these planned subgoals.

#### 3.2. Keyframe Dataset Construction

Supervision data is constructed from trajectory videos by manually annotating task-critical keyframes (e.g., approach, contact, grasp, transport, place, release). Each pair of neighboring keyframes $(k_m, k_{m+1})$ defines a subtask-level transition. Low-level actions $A_m = (a_{t_m}, \ldots, a_{t_{m+1}-1})$ between these keyframes serve as action supervision.
Additionally, each keyframe transition is augmented with:
- **Subtask-level text description ($l_m$):** Summarizes semantic changes (e.g., "moving toward the object").
- **Lightweight 2D spatial cue ($r_m$):** Rendered as an arrow-overlaid image $k_{arr_m}$ on the source keyframe, indicating the gripper's coarse image-space motion direction. The arrow color encodes gripper state changes (open-to-close, close-to-open), and a circle is used for in-place gripper actions.
The annotated tuple $(k_m, k_{arr_m}, l_m, k_{m+1}, A_m)$ is used, where $(k_{arr_m}, l_m, k_{m+1})$ supervises the image-editing planner, and $A_m$ supervises the action predictor. Human annotation ensures data quality for keyframe selection and spatial cues, although VLM-based annotation for arrow cues was explored.

#### 3.3. Image Editing Planner

The visual planner is instantiated using a pretrained image-editing model, specifically FLUX Kontext, fine-tuned with LoRA.
- **Input:** Current keyframe $k_m$, subtask text description $l_m$, and optional arrow-based spatial cue $r_m$.
- **Output:** Predicted next task-relevant keyframe $\hat{k}_{m+1}$.
- **Training:** The model is fine-tuned using the native image-editing objective of FLUX Kontext, updating only the inserted LoRA parameters. To support diverse conditioning, the arrow condition is randomly dropped during training while the text description is always provided.
- **Inference:** SWEET starts with the initial observation $\hat{k}_0 = k_0$ and iteratively generates subsequent keyframes by applying the image-editing planner with the current planned keyframe and the corresponding subtask conditions. This process yields a compact sequence of visual subgoals.

#### 3.4. Goal-conditioned Diffusion Action Predictor

A goal-conditioned diffusion action predictor $\pi_\phi$ is trained to translate planned keyframes into executable actions.
- **Input:** Current observation $o_t$ and a target keyframe $\hat{k}_{m+1}$.
- **Output:** An action chunk $\hat{A}_t$ of a fixed horizon $H=16$.
- **Training:** The predictor is trained in a receding-horizon manner using action sequences from annotated trajectories. Each training target is an action chunk of length $H$, conditioned on the current observation and the target keyframe of the current subtask. The standard diffusion-policy denoising objective is used.
- **Execution:** The policy predicts an action chunk towards the current target keyframe, executes it, and receives an updated observation. This closed-loop, receding-horizon process continues until the subgoal is considered reached, even though the high-level keyframe plan is generated open-loop once.

#### 3.5. Mixed Training with Edited Transitions

To address the domain gap between the clean, real keyframes used during action predictor training and the generated, edited keyframes encountered during inference, a mixed-training strategy is introduced:
- **Synthetic Edited Transitions:** For each ground-truth transition, the visual planner generates an edited target keyframe $\tilde{k}_{m+1}$. This edited keyframe is paired with the original action supervision.
- **Filtering:** Low-quality edited samples (e.g., with background distortion, object disappearance, robot inconsistency) are filtered out to maintain data quality.
- **Mixed Training:** The filtered edited transitions are combined with the original clean transitions in a 1:1 ratio. The action predictor is then trained on this mixed dataset, exposing it to the visual distribution of planner-generated subgoals.

#### 3.6. Inference Procedure

The full inference procedure consists of two main stages:
1. **Keyframe Plan Generation:** Starting from an initial observation $o_0$, the image-editing planner successively generates a sequence of future keyframes $\hat{K} = \{\hat{k}_0, \hat{k}_1, \ldots, \hat{k}_M\}$ using Equation (5), where each step takes the previously generated keyframe, subtask text, and optional arrow guidance as input. This generates a one-shot sparse plan.
2. **Sequential Subgoal Execution:** The planned subgoals are executed sequentially. For each transition, the next planned keyframe $\hat{k}_{m+1}$ acts as the visual target. The goal-conditioned action predictor $\pi_\phi$ takes the current online observation $o_t$ and $\hat{k}_{m+1}$ to predict an executable action chunk (Equation 6), which is then executed in the environment. This process continues in a closed-loop, receding-horizon fashion for each subgoal until all planned keyframes are traversed.

#### 3.7. Experimental Setup

- **Planner Implementation:** FLUX.1-Kontext-dev, LoRA fine-tuning for 6K steps on NVIDIA H20 GPU. Editing horizons are task-specific (e.g., 2-3 steps).
- **Action Predictor Implementation:** Diffusion Policy architecture, taking current observation and target keyframe as conditions. Predicts action chunks of length 16. Trained with DDPM denoising objective.
- **Datasets:**
 - **DROID:** Real-robot pick-and-place, 700 samples, 2,100 subtasks across diverse environments. Used for cross-scene generalization (seen/unseen scenes).
 - **RoboMimic:** Simulation, 600 samples, 1,600 subtasks for Lift, Can, and Square tasks. Used for keyframe generation and full-pipeline execution.
- **Metrics:** Keyframe quality: MSE, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS. Action execution: Task success rate (RoboMimic), endpoint MSE (gripper position).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across keyframe prediction and full-pipeline robot execution.

#### 4.1. Image Editing vs. Video Generation as Visual Planners

A controlled comparison was conducted between the image-editing model FLUX-Kontext and the video generation model Wan2.2 (TI2V 5B) under identical robotic data settings.
- **Quantitative Performance:**
 - **RoboMimic:** FLUX-Kontext achieved lower MSE (0.01114 vs. 0.01777) and LPIPS (0.07832 vs. 0.1303), and higher PSNR (20.71 vs. 19.00) and SSIM (0.9119 vs. 0.8563) compared to Wan2.2.
 - **DROID (Seen Scene):** FLUX-Kontext demonstrated lower MSE (0.01896 vs. 0.02180) and LPIPS (0.1570 vs. 0.2032), and higher PSNR (18.14 vs. 17.35) and SSIM (0.8366 vs. 0.8030).
 - **DROID (Unseen Scene):** FLUX-Kontext maintained its advantage with lower MSE (0.02682 vs. 0.02844) and LPIPS (0.2343 vs. 0.2625), and higher PSNR (16.02 vs. 15.64) and SSIM (0.7608 vs. 0.7337).
 These metrics consistently indicate that image editing produces keyframes with better pixel-level fidelity, structural consistency, and perceptual similarity to ground-truth keyframes.

- **Qualitative Analysis:**
 - FLUX-Kontext showed superior performance in preserving embodiment consistency (stable robot arms, grippers, objects) and generating more physically plausible task states, particularly regarding grasping, object shape, and robot-arm structure.
 - Wan2.2 frequently exhibited critical planning-level failures such as implausible grasps, object deformation, arm deformation, and object teleportation. These issues are considered detrimental for downstream robotic control as predicted keyframes serve as target states.

- **Efficiency:** FLUX-Kontext demonstrated a significant inference efficiency advantage. It generated 3 sparse keyframes for a task-level visual plan in approximately 10 seconds on an NVIDIA H20 GPU. In contrast, Wan2.2 required over 400 seconds to produce an 81-frame dense rollout for the same task. This highlights the practical benefit of sparse keyframe prediction for robotic manipulation.

#### 4.2. Full-Pipeline Execution on RoboMimic

SWEET successfully translated generated keyframes into executable robot behaviors on the RoboMimic simulation benchmark. The full pipeline, involving image-editing-based keyframe planning and subsequent goal-conditioned action prediction, achieved task success rates on Lift, Can, and Square tasks. This demonstrates the framework's ability to close the loop from visual imagination to robotic control even with limited training data (a few hundred demonstrations per task).

#### 4.3. Ablation Study on Action Predictor Training (Mixed Training)

An ablation study on RoboMimic investigated the impact of different training strategies for the action predictor, specifically comparing training with only real keyframes (Real-trained), only generated keyframes (Gen-trained), and a 1:1 mixture of both (Mix-trained).
- **Domain Sensitivity:** The action predictor exhibited sensitivity to the domain of the target keyframes. For example, on the Lift task, the Real-trained model performed well with real keyframe targets (87.0% success) but degraded significantly when tested with generated targets (58.0%). Conversely, the Gen-trained model showed higher success with generated targets (91.5%) but lower success with real targets (78.5%).
- **Robustness of Mixed Training:** The Mix-trained variant consistently achieved strong performance across both real and generated target keyframes. On Lift, it achieved 92.0% success for both generated and real targets. Similar trends were observed for Can (81.0% and 79.0% respectively) and Square (29.5% and 31.5% respectively), despite Square being a more challenging task requiring higher precision.
- **Error Reduction:** Mixed training generally reduced the gripper-position error (endpoint MSE) compared to single-domain training, indicating more accurate action predictions.
These results suggest that augmenting action predictor training with filtered edited transitions effectively bridges the real-to-edited domain gap, leading to more robust execution of visual plans generated by the image-editing planner.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SWEET framework and its underlying findings present several areas of significance and potential impact within embodied AI and robotics:

- **Validation of Image Editing as a Sparse World Model:** The work provides evidence that image editing models can serve as effective and efficient visual planners for robotic manipulation. This challenges the common assumption that dense video prediction is necessary for future state imagination in embodied control, offering a viable alternative focused on task-relevant keyframes.
- **Improved Efficiency in Planning:** By demonstrating that sparse keyframe prediction through image editing is significantly faster (e.g., 10 seconds vs. 400 seconds for a task plan) and produces more reliable visual outputs than dense video generation, the research proposes a more computationally tractable approach for online robotic planning. This efficiency could be crucial for real-world deployment where low latency is often required.
- **Enhanced Visual Fidelity and Plausibility:** The qualitative and quantitative comparisons show that image editing models produce keyframes with higher visual fidelity, scene consistency, and physical plausibility for robotic tasks. This directly addresses critical issues like object deformation, arm distortion, and implausible grasps that can hinder the utility of generated visual subgoals for robot control.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real / Generative-to-Real Gap:** The introduction and validation of the mixed-training strategy for the action predictor effectively mitigates the domain gap between real and synthetically generated visual subgoals. This is a practical contribution that improves the robustness and reliability of deploying policies with generative visual planners, a common challenge in AI for robotics.
- **Foundation for Scalable Visual Planning:** While the current work relies on human keyframe annotation, the success of image editing as a planner suggests a foundation for future scalable visual planning. If automated or semi-automated keyframe extraction from large-scale robot video datasets becomes more reliable, this framework could leverage such data more effectively.
- **New Direction for Embodied Visual Prediction:** The research opens an underexplored direction by advocating for sparse image transformation as a core component of embodied visual prediction, moving beyond the prevalent dense video generation paradigm. This could inspire further research into other forms of sparse or symbolic visual representations for robot learning and control.
- **Potential for Interpretability and User Guidance:** The use of sparse keyframes, conditioned by natural language and optional spatial cues, offers a more interpretable planning interface. Users or higher-level VLMs can specify task progress at a semantic level, and the robot system can translate this into a compact visual plan, potentially enhancing human-robot collaboration and debugging.

Limitations identified, such as the scalability of keyframe datasets due to reliance on human annotation and the need for further action predictor improvements, also point towards fruitful future research directions, including the fine-tuning of video-language models for automatic keyframe detection and joint training of visual and action prediction models.
