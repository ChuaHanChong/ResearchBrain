# Benchmarking Vision-Language-Action Models on SO-101: Failure and Recovery Analysis

> [!summary] Summary
> A standardized real-world benchmark on the low-cost SO-101 tabletop arm evaluating pi0.5, SmolVLA, Wall-X, and ACT across four manipulation tasks, adding a structured failure taxonomy, semantic/execution failure decomposition, and a recovery-rate metric beyond binary success. Execution instability dominates failures; pi0.5 leads in success (56.25%) and recovery (30.77%).

## Problem

- Existing VLA evaluations run in simulation or on expensive research-grade platforms, obscuring robustness challenges under low-cost hardware's noisier embodiment conditions.
- Benchmarks report binary task success only, so policies with comparable success rates can hide substantially different underlying failure and recovery behaviors.
- Semantic-complexity-based task design does not reliably isolate the capability being tested, since visuomotor shortcuts can solve tasks without the intended semantic competence.

## Method

- Execution-centric task suite of four tasks (Pen Transfer, Selective Color Sorting, Multi-Object Packing, Precision Pen Placement) each probing a distinct execution property: control fidelity, grounding, temporal consistency, embodiment-noise robustness.
- Structured failure taxonomy with four primary modes (Grasp Instability, Repetition Loop, State Mismatch, Precision Misalignment), aggregated into semantic-level versus execution-level failure decomposition.
- Recovery Rate metric computed as successful recoveries over recovery opportunities, applied uniformly across pi0.5, SmolVLA, Wall-X, and ACT fine-tuned on 100 teleoperated demonstrations per task on physical SO-101 hardware.

## Results

- Across 320 real-world episodes, pi0.5 attains the highest average success rate (56.25%) versus Wall-X (51.25%), ACT (33.75%), SmolVLA (32.5%), with Color Sorting hardest (0-10%) and Pen Transfer easiest (70-95%).
- Recovery Rate varies sharply: pi0.5 30.77% and Wall-X 20.51% versus ACT 6.45% and SmolVLA 3.23%, despite some models having similar or more recovery opportunities.
- Failure mode analysis shows execution-related failures (grasp instability, repetition loop) dominate for all models (often 90-100% of failed trials) while state mismatch is highest for ACT (98.11%) and lowest for pi0.5 (45.71%).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task success rate alone under-characterizes embodied robustness; models with similar success can differ drastically in recovery capability and failure composition.
> - Low-cost, noisy hardware acts as a stress test that surfaces failure modes hidden in simulation or high-precision research platforms.
> - Recovery from execution failure should be treated as a first-class, explicitly trained capability rather than an incidental byproduct of policy competence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have significantly enhanced the generalization capabilities of robotic systems, particularly in manipulation tasks specified through natural language. These models often leverage extensive robot datasets, such as Open X-Embodiment, to achieve promising performance in various embodied manipulation scenarios. However, the existing evaluation landscape predominantly consists of either simulation environments or high-cost, research-grade robotic platforms. These controlled settings, characterized by stable dynamics and precise hardware, may not fully expose the challenges inherent in real-world deployment, especially concerning embodiment uncertainty.

The broader research context includes established benchmark suites like RLBench and RoboSuite, which have contributed to standardized evaluation in simulation but do not capture the complexities of physical robot interaction. Real-world evaluations, while crucial, have largely concentrated on expensive platforms, leaving a gap in understanding the robustness of VLA policies when deployed on accessible, low-cost robotic systems.

This work positions itself within this gap by arguing that low-cost robotic platforms, rather than merely being constrained environments, serve as valuable "stress-test" settings for embodied AI. Such platforms introduce inherent noise and variability—including actuation inaccuracies, unstable grasping, sensing limitations, and accumulated execution drift—that reveal characteristic failure patterns often overlooked in conventional benchmarks. By focusing on a low-cost platform, the research aims to provide an evaluation framework that moves beyond simulation and high-end hardware, systematically characterizing policy robustness under realistic deployment conditions. It seeks to contribute to the understanding of VLA model performance under less-than-ideal circumstances, which is critical for their practical applicability and widespread adoption.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated strong generalization in robotic manipulation, their robustness on affordable real-world robots remains largely unexplored. Existing evaluations are predominantly conducted in simulation or on expensive research platforms, which often mask the challenges posed by real-world embodiment uncertainty. These uncertainties, common in low-cost robotic systems, include limited actuator precision, reduced control stability, lower payload capacity, noisier motion execution, joint backlash, control latency, trajectory jitter, limited positional repeatability, and imperfect calibration between visual observations and executed actions. These factors can lead to accumulated execution errors, particularly in long-horizon or contact-rich tasks, and significantly influence policy behavior, revealing failure patterns not apparent in controlled settings.

To address this gap, the study sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Introduce a Standardized Real-World Benchmark on a Low-Cost Platform:** The research aims to establish a benchmark built on the low-cost, open-source SO-101 robotic platform. This benchmark is intended to provide an accessible and reproducible testbed for embodied AI research under realistic deployment conditions, moving beyond simulation-based assessments and high-cost hardware.
2. **Develop a Robustness-Oriented Evaluation Framework:** Beyond conventional task success rates, a detailed evaluation framework is proposed. This framework incorporates a structured failure taxonomy, semantic- and execution-level failure decomposition, and recovery-aware evaluation metrics. The goal is to offer a more comprehensive characterization of policy robustness by analyzing not just whether a task succeeds, but also how and why it fails, and the policy's ability to recover from errors.
3. **Conduct a Systematic Real-World Evaluation of Representative Policies:** The study intends to fine-tune and evaluate a selection of representative VLA policies (π0.5, SmolVLA, Wall-X) and a strong imitation learning baseline (ACT) directly on the physical SO-101 platform. This systematic comparison under identical low-cost deployment conditions aims to provide a direct assessment of their performance and robustness.
4. **Provide Empirical Insights into Robustness Limitations:** Through experimental results, the research seeks to empirically highlight the robustness limitations of current VLA policies under low-cost real-world deployment. This includes identifying dominant failure sources, characterizing the variability in recovery capabilities across architectures, and emphasizing the importance of failure and recovery analysis in addition to binary success metrics.

Ultimately, the motivation is to establish SO-101 as a practical benchmark for evaluating embodied AI systems, thereby fostering the development of more robust and reliable robotic manipulation policies suitable for real-world, accessible deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research follows a structured pipeline designed for real-world evaluation of robotic policies under low-cost deployment conditions. This pipeline encompasses execution-centric task design, real-world data collection, task-specific policy adaptation, and comprehensive evaluation using a set of specialized metrics.

#### 3.1. Robotic Platform

All experimental work, including data collection, policy adaptation, and real-world evaluations, was conducted on the SO-101 robotic platform. The SO-101 is an open-source, low-cost tabletop manipulator specifically designed for embodied AI research. The choice of SO-101 is motivated by its inherent embodiment constraints, such as limited actuator precision, reduced control stability, lower payload capacity, and noisier motion execution, which are more pronounced compared to research-grade systems like Franka Panda or WidowX. These characteristics introduce significant stochasticity into the perception-action loop through actuator noise, joint backlash, control latency, trajectory jitter, limited positional repeatability, and imperfect visual-to-action calibration. This environment is considered suitable for exposing and studying the robustness challenges of VLA policies in realistic, low-cost deployments, where small execution errors can accumulate over time.

#### 3.2. Task Design Rationale

The benchmark tasks were designed with an "execution-centric" evaluation framework, moving away from organizing tasks by increasing semantic complexity. This shift was motivated by preliminary experiments showing that successful task completion based on semantic complexity does not always reliably indicate the acquisition of intended semantic capabilities on low-cost platforms, where visuomotor correlations might suffice for specific conditions.

The benchmark comprises four representative manipulation tasks, each designed to probe distinct, directly observable execution properties:

- **Pen Transfer:** Evaluates low-level control fidelity and grasp execution.
- **Selective Color Sorting:** Assesses language-conditioned object grounding robustness under distractor interference.
- **Multi-Object Packing:** Evaluates long-horizon execution stability and error accumulation.
- **Precision Pen Placement:** Measures robustness under fine-grained positional and contact constraints, serving as a stricter variant of Pen Transfer.

These tasks balance experimental reproducibility, demonstration availability, and coverage of distinct execution challenges, requiring stable control, accurate grounding, long-horizon planning, and precise physical interaction.

#### 3.3. Data Collection

Demonstrations were collected directly on the physical SO-101 platform through human teleoperation using end-effector control. During collection, synchronized observation-action pairs, including RGB observations, natural language instructions, and low-level robot control commands, were recorded. This approach ensured that both policy adaptation and evaluation occurred under consistent real-world embodiment conditions, minimizing simulation-to-reality discrepancies.

For each of the four tasks, 100 teleoperated demonstrations were collected, totaling 400 demonstrations. Object poses and scene layouts were randomized within predefined workspace constraints to enhance generalization while maintaining comparability. The collected datasets have been publicly released.

#### 3.4. Evaluated Policies and Task Adaptation

Four representative manipulation policies were selected for evaluation, encompassing both VLA models and classical imitation learning:

- **VLA Models:** π0.5, SmolVLA, and Wall-X. These policies generate actions conditioned on visual observations and language instructions.
- **Imitation Learning Baseline:** ACT (Action-Chunking Transformer), which is trained via behavior cloning without vision-language pretraining.

For each benchmark task, all policies were adapted using the respective task-specific demonstration datasets. Fine-tuning followed the original training paradigm and implementation details of each method (e.g., flow-matching for diffusion policies, reconstruction/token-based objectives for imitation learning). Only minimal interface-level adaptations were made to align policies with the SO-101's observation format and action space, without modifying model architectures, optimization procedures, or learning objectives. This approach aimed to ensure fair comparison by attributing performance differences primarily to architectural and learning paradigm variations.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics

Policies were evaluated using complementary metrics derived from 20 independent real-world hardware trials per model-task pair (320 total evaluation episodes), conducted under identical conditions:

- **Success Rate:** The proportion of trials where the policy completed the task successfully, defined by predefined task completion criteria without human intervention.
- **Failure Taxonomy:** Failed executions were categorized into four modes, with each failed trial assigned a single primary mode:
 - **Grasp Instability:** Failure to establish or maintain a stable grasp.
 - **Repetition Loop:** Repeated, oscillatory, or redundant actions without progress.
 - **State Mismatch:** Inconsistency between the policy's inferred task state and the actual environment state.
 - **Precision Misalignment:** Failure to achieve required positioning, alignment, or insertion accuracy under strict spatial constraints.
- **Semantic- and Execution-Level Failure Decomposition:** The fine-grained failure taxonomy was aggregated:
 - **Semantic-related failures:** Primarily represented by State Mismatch, reflecting scene understanding or task grounding errors.
 - **Execution-related failures:** Defined as the average occurrence of Grasp Instability and Repetition Loop, reflecting low-level manipulation instability. Precision Misalignment was excluded from this aggregation.
- **Recovery Rate:** Measures the ability of a policy to recover from intermediate execution failures and continue task execution without external intervention. It is calculated as the number of successful recoveries divided by the total number of recovery opportunities. A successful recovery restores a valid task state and resumes progress.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of VLA and imitation learning policies on the SO-101 platform yielded several observations regarding task performance, failure patterns, and recovery capabilities under low-cost real-world deployment conditions.

#### 4.1. Success Rate

Across the four benchmark tasks, VLA-based policies generally demonstrated performance comparable to or superior to the ACT imitation learning baseline. π0.5 achieved the highest average success rate at 56.25%, with Wall-X closely following at 51.25%. ACT recorded an average of 33.75%, while SmolVLA had a slightly lower average of 32.5%. These results indicate that policy performance is notably task-dependent under conditions of embodiment noise and execution uncertainty characteristic of low-cost robotics. Selective Color Sorting proved to be the most challenging task, with all policies achieving very low success rates (0–10%). Conversely, Pen Transfer yielded consistently high success rates (70–95%), suggesting relative robustness for simple pick-and-place manipulations. Multi-object Packing exhibited the largest performance variation among policies, indicating its discriminative power for evaluating VLA capabilities in long-horizon sequential manipulation.

#### 4.2. Failure Mode Analysis

Analysis of failure modes revealed systematic patterns across policies. ACT exhibited high rates of Grasp Instability, Repetition Loops, and State Mismatch, suggesting limitations in both perception and action consistency. VLA-based models generally showed reduced State Mismatch rates but still contended with persistent Grasp Instability and Repetition Loops. π0.5 recorded the lowest State Mismatch rate, implying improved global state consistency. Wall-X showed no occurrences of Precision Misalignment, yet still experienced high levels of low-level execution failures. The presence of 0% entries for certain failure categories reflects their empirical non-occurrence within the finite evaluation samples rather than a categorical absence of capability.

#### 4.3. Semantic-Level and Execution-Level Failure Analysis

Aggregating failure modes into semantic-related (State Mismatch) and execution-related (Grasp Instability + Repetition Loop) categories provided a higher-level understanding. Execution-related failures remained consistently high across all evaluated methods, highlighting low-level manipulation robustness as a significant bottleneck for real-world deployment, even for policies with strong semantic understanding. Semantic-related failures showed greater variability. ACT displayed high failure rates in both categories. π0.5 achieved the lowest semantic failure rate but maintained relatively high execution failure, suggesting stronger semantic grounding but persistent challenges in control robustness. SmolVLA exhibited higher semantic failure, while Wall-X showed lower semantic failure but was still dominated by execution-related issues.

#### 4.4. Recovery Behavior

Evaluation of recovery rates, which measure a policy's ability to self-correct from intermediate failures, revealed distinct differences. π0.5 achieved the highest recovery rate at 30.77%, followed by Wall-X at 20.51%. In contrast, ACT (6.45%) and SmolVLA (3.23%) exhibited substantially lower recovery rates, indicating limited capacity for self-correction. These findings suggest that recovery capability is an important differentiator among manipulation policies beyond initial action accuracy, contributing to overall task progress under failure conditions.

#### 4.5. Episode-Level Summary of Task Performance

A fine-grained episode-level analysis confirmed that execution-related failures (grasp instability, repetition loops, state mismatches) occurred substantially more frequently than precision misalignment, reinforcing that low-level control and state tracking instability are key challenges. Failure modes also demonstrated a clear task-dependent structure; simpler tasks like Pen Transfer had lower failure counts, whereas more complex tasks such as Multi-object Packing and Color Sorting induced higher frequencies of repetition loops and state mismatches, indicating amplified reasoning and execution failures with increased task complexity. π0.5 and Wall-X displayed comparatively stronger recovery effectiveness, particularly in higher-complexity tasks, suggesting better utilization of intermediate task states. ACT and SmolVLA showed limited recovery success despite encountering similar numbers of recovery opportunities.

#### 4.6. Success and Failure Examples

Qualitative examples from the Multi-object Packing task illustrated these quantitative findings. Successful trajectories, often from higher-performing policies like π0.5, exhibited stable grasping, consistent object placement, and effective recovery from minor misalignments. Conversely, failure cases, often from lower-performing policies like ACT, were characterized by repeated grasp attempts, unstable object placement, and early-stage state misinterpretation, leading to compounding errors. These examples supported the conclusion that execution instability and state mismatch are dominant failure modes, and recovery capability is a distinguishing factor.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of embodied AI by providing a comprehensive analysis of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on a low-cost robotic platform, moving beyond traditional evaluation paradigms.

**Significance:**

- **Accessible Real-World Benchmark:** The introduction of a standardized benchmark on the low-cost, open-source SO-101 robotic platform offers an accessible and reproducible testbed. This lowers the barrier to entry for real-world embodied AI research, allowing a broader community to experiment with VLA models under realistic hardware constraints.
- **Robustness-Oriented Evaluation Framework:** The proposed evaluation framework, which extends beyond binary task success rates to include a structured failure taxonomy, semantic- and execution-level failure decomposition, and recovery-aware metrics, provides a more granular and informative assessment of policy robustness. This acknowledges that task completion alone does not fully characterize a policy's real-world utility.
- **Empirical Insights into VLA Limitations:** The systematic real-world evaluation of representative VLA policies (π0.5, SmolVLA, Wall-X) and an imitation learning baseline (ACT) offers empirical insights into their robustness limitations. The findings highlight execution instability as a dominant failure source across models and demonstrate that recovery capability varies significantly, providing a deeper understanding of current architectural shortcomings.
- **Identification of Key Performance Gaps:** The study reveals a gap between high task success rates and other indicators of embodied robustness, such as failure modes and recovery capability. This suggests that models achieving similar task success might have fundamentally different underlying behaviors and resilience to errors.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerating Robust VLA Development:** By pinpointing execution instability and limited recovery as critical challenges, this work can guide future research towards developing VLA models that are explicitly designed for robustness, error detection, and self-correction in noisy, real-world environments.
- **Informing Benchmark Design:** The research advocates for the integration of complementary evaluation metrics, such as failure analysis and recovery behavior, into future embodied AI benchmarks. This could lead to more holistic and diagnostic evaluation protocols that better reflect the demands of real-world deployment.
- **Promoting Low-Cost Robotics in Research:** By demonstrating the utility of low-cost platforms as "stress-test" environments, the study encourages their broader adoption in embodied AI research. This could make advanced robotics research more accessible and foster innovation relevant to practical, cost-sensitive applications.
- **Bridging Simulation-to-Reality Gap:** The focus on real-world evaluation on a low-cost platform helps to better characterize the sim-to-real gap, providing insights into which aspects of VLA performance degrade most significantly under genuine embodiment uncertainty.
- **Foundation for Recovery-Aware AI Systems:** The emphasis on recovery capability positions it as a first-class objective for future VLA systems, potentially leading to the development of recovery-aware policy learning, failure-conditioned planning, and adaptive replanning mechanisms.
