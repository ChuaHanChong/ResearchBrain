# Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper tests whether large language models can fix their own reasoning mistakes without outside feedback, which it calls intrinsic self-correction. It re-evaluates published self-correction methods on math, commonsense and multi-hop QA. Gains reported earlier come from oracle labels, weaker baselines or under-specified initial prompts. Without these, self-correction usually lowers accuracy.

## Problem

- Prior self-correction work such as RCI and Reflexion reports large reasoning gains, but relies on oracle ground-truth labels to decide when to stop, which are unavailable in real deployments.
- Multi-agent debate is credited as a form of critique and self-correction, yet it uses several responses and is compared against weak baselines rather than equal-cost self-consistency.
- Some self-correction gains, such as those in Self-Refine, come from a sub-optimal initial prompt that leaves task requirements to the feedback prompt, so the source of improvement is unclear.

## Method

- Defines ==intrinsic self-correction==, where a model revises its answer using only its own capabilities. Uses a three-step loop: initial answer, ==self-generated feedback==, then a revised answer, for up to two rounds.
- Runs a paired ==oracle versus no-oracle== comparison on GSM8K, CommonSenseQA and HotpotQA. Tests GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2-70b-chat, with several feedback prompts.
- Compares ==multi-agent debate== with ==self-consistency== at equal response counts. Rewrites the Constrained Generation initial prompt to state all requirements, testing whether feedback gains only compensate for a weak first prompt.

## Results

- With oracle labels, GPT-3.5 CommonSenseQA rises from **75.8** to **89.7**. Without them, it falls to **38.1** after one round and **41.8** after two. GPT-4 GSM8K drops from **95.5** to **89.0**.
- Llama-2 GSM8K falls from **62.0** to **36.5** after two rounds. GPT-3.5 turns correct answers into incorrect ones (**8.8%**) more often than the reverse (**7.6%**) on GSM8K.
- On GSM8K, self-consistency with 6 responses scores **85.3** against **83.2** for debate, and with 9 responses **88.2** against **83.0**. A better initial prompt gets **81.8** on CommonGen-Hard, above the earlier self-corrected **67.0**, and self-correction lowers it to **75.1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Models cannot reliably judge the correctness of their own reasoning. Self-generated feedback works like an extra prompt that can bias a correct answer away, so unaided self-correction is not a dependable fix.
> - Evaluate self-correction against equal-cost baselines such as self-consistency, and put equal effort into the initial prompt. Otherwise gains may come from extra samples or better instructions, not correction.
> - Self-correction helps when there is a reliable external verifier, such as code execution, tools or trained critics. Building such feedback is more promising than hoping the model self-diagnoses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid proliferation and increasing sophistication of Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing, demonstrating unprecedented capabilities in text generation, summarization, and understanding. Models like GPT-3, GPT-4, and PaLM have showcased remarkable fluency and emergent abilities, prompting widespread adoption across various applications. However, alongside their impressive performance, concerns about LLMs' factual accuracy, reasoning robustness, and susceptibility to generating inappropriate or incorrect content (often termed "hallucinations") have grown. This has led to an active research front focused on mitigating these issues.

One promising contemporary methodology proposed to address these limitations is "self-correction." The core idea is that LLMs, by refining their initial responses based on some form of feedback, can enhance accuracy and appropriateness. Numerous recent works (e.g., Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023) have reported significant improvements using self-correction, fostering optimism within the community. These methods often involve an iterative process where the LLM generates an initial answer, then receives feedback (either internal or external) on that answer, and subsequently attempts to revise it.

This paper, "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet," directly challenges the prevailing optimistic narrative surrounding self-correction, particularly in the context of reasoning tasks. It raises a fundamental, paradoxical question: "If an LLM possesses the ability to self-correct, why doesn’t it simply offer the correct answer in its initial attempt?" The authors argue that many reported improvements in self-correction literature might stem from specific experimental setups that do not reflect true intrinsic self-correction ability, such as reliance on oracle (ground-truth) labels, unfair comparisons in terms of computational cost, or sub-optimal initial prompt engineering. By rigorously re-evaluating these claims and defining "intrinsic self-correction" more strictly, this work aims to provide a more nuanced and realistic perspective on the current state of LLM self-correction capabilities, thereby guiding future research efforts towards more genuinely effective strategies. It acts as an important reality check, urging the community to distinguish between perceived self-correction and actual, robust error correction without external assistance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this paper is to critically examine the role and efficacy of self-correction within LLMs, specifically focusing on their ability to improve reasoning. The authors aim to shed light on the true potential and, more importantly, the current limitations of this widely discussed methodology.

The primary motivations driving this research are:

- **Addressing Ambiguity in "Self-Correction":** The paper highlights that the definition and application of "self-correction" vary significantly across existing literature, leading to potential misinterpretations of LLM capabilities. A key distinction is drawn between self-correction that leverages external feedback (e.g., human input, tool results, oracle labels) and "intrinsic self-correction," which relies solely on the LLM's inherent capabilities without external cues. The authors are primarily motivated to investigate the latter, as high-quality external feedback is often unavailable in real-world scenarios, and understanding intrinsic capabilities is crucial.
- **Challenging Optimistic Claims:** The authors observe a discrepancy between the reported effectiveness of self-correction in some prior works (e.g., Madaan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023) and an intuitive understanding of LLM limitations. They aim to investigate if these reported improvements genuinely reflect an intrinsic self-correction ability or are artifacts of experimental design.
- **Unveiling Underlying Mechanics and Limitations:** By rigorously testing LLMs' self-correction capabilities on reasoning tasks, the paper seeks to understand *why* and *where* LLMs struggle. This includes identifying specific pitfalls in existing evaluation setups, such as the use of oracle labels, unfair comparisons of inference costs, and issues related to prompt design (e.g., sub-optimal initial prompts).
- **Providing Insights for Future Research:** Ultimately, the motivation is to offer concrete insights and suggestions for the LLM research community, encouraging the development of future methods that can genuinely enhance reasoning and address the observed limitations of current self-correction approaches. The paper serves as a "call to action" for a more critical and discerning perspective on LLM self-correction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a rigorous empirical methodology to evaluate the intrinsic self-correction capabilities of LLMs on reasoning tasks. Their approach systematically addresses what they identify as issues in prior self-correction evaluations.

#### 3.1. Definition of Intrinsic Self-Correction

A core aspect of their methodology is the strict definition of "intrinsic self-correction." This refers to scenarios where an LLM attempts to rectify its initial responses based *solely on its inherent capabilities*, without any external feedback, human intervention, or oracle labels guiding the correction process. This contrasts with approaches that leverage external verifiers, execution results, or ground-truth correctness information.

#### 3.2. Benchmarks

The evaluation focuses on three widely recognized reasoning benchmarks where existing self-correction methods had previously claimed significant improvements:
- **GSM8K (Grade School Math):** A dataset of linguistically diverse math word problems (1,319 test examples).
- **CommonSenseQA:** A multi-choice question answering dataset testing commonsense reasoning (1,221 development set questions).
- **HotpotQA:** An open-domain multi-hop question answering dataset (100 questions from a closed-book setting).

#### 3.3. Test Models

The study evaluates both state-of-the-art proprietary and open-source LLMs to ensure broad applicability of findings:
- **GPT-3.5-Turbo (gpt-3.5-turbo-0613)**
- **GPT-4 (accessed on 2023/08/29)**
- **GPT-4-Turbo (gpt-4-1106-preview)**
- **Llama-2 (Llama-2-70b-chat)**
To manage computational costs, a random sample of 200 questions (100 for HotpotQA) was used for most models, while GPT-3.5-Turbo was evaluated on the full set. Different temperature settings (1 for GPT-3.5/GPT-4, 0 for GPT-4-Turbo/Llama-2) were used to cover various decoding algorithms.

#### 3.4. Prompting Strategy for Self-Correction

A three-step prompting strategy, similar to that used in previous self-correction works, was adopted:
1. **Initial Generation:** The model produces an initial response to the problem (this also serves as the "Standard Prompting" baseline).
2. **Feedback Generation:** The model is prompted to review its initial response and generate feedback (e.g., identifying problems, suggesting improvements). For intrinsic self-correction, this feedback is solely generated by the LLM itself. Several different feedback prompts were tested (e.g., "Assume that this answer could be either correct or incorrect. Review the answer carefully and report any serious problems you find.").
3. **Refined Generation:** The model is then prompted to re-answer the original question, incorporating the self-generated feedback.
The self-correction process was allowed for a maximum of two rounds.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Scenarios and Comparisons

The authors systematically conduct experiments to address specific issues identified in prior literature:

- **Intrinsic vs. Oracle-Guided Self-Correction (Section 3):**
 - They first replicate prior results of self-correction with "oracle labels" (where the ground truth correctness is used to guide the self-correction loop, preventing incorrect changes).
 - They then remove oracle labels to evaluate "intrinsic self-correction," where the LLM must autonomously judge correctness and decide whether to correct. Performance is compared against the initial "Standard Prompting."
 - An empirical analysis is conducted to categorize how answers change (e.g., "Correct ⇒ Incorrect," "Incorrect ⇒ Correct").

- **Multi-Agent Debate vs. Self-Consistency (Section 4):**
 - They replicate a "multi-agent debate" method, where multiple LLM instances critique each other, on GSM8K.
 - They compare its performance against "self-consistency," a simpler method that generates multiple responses and uses majority voting. Crucially, the comparison is made on an equivalent number of LLM responses (inference cost) to ensure fairness.

- **Prompt Design Issues (Section 5):**
 - They analyze a "Constrained Generation" task from previous work (Madaan et al., 2023) where self-correction improved performance.
 - They demonstrate that the improvement was primarily due to a sub-optimal initial prompt lacking explicit instructions. By simply integrating the "feedback" instructions into the *initial* prompt, they show that a stronger baseline can be achieved, making self-correction redundant or even detrimental.

This methodical deconstruction of self-correction claims, alongside controlled experiments on diverse LLMs and benchmarks, allows the authors to draw robust conclusions about the current limitations of LLMs in self-correcting their reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings that collectively challenge the broad efficacy of intrinsic self-correction in Large Language Models, especially for reasoning tasks.

#### 4.1. Intrinsic Self-Correction Leads to Performance Degradation

- **Oracle-Guided vs. Intrinsic:** When self-correction is guided by oracle (ground-truth) labels, the models show significant performance improvements, replicating results from previous works (e.g., GPT-3.5 improved from 75.9% to 84.3% on GSM8K with oracle guidance). However, the authors emphasize that this scenario is impractical, as ground truth is often unavailable.
- **Intrinsic Self-Correction Performance Drop:** In the crucial "intrinsic self-correction" setting (without oracle labels), the performance of all tested models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-2) *decreased* across all reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, CommonSenseQA, HotpotQA) after one or two rounds of self-correction. For example, GPT-3.5's accuracy on GSM8K dropped from 75.9% (Standard Prompting) to 75.1% (round 1) and 74.7% (round 2). Llama-2's performance saw even more significant drops, from 62.0% to 36.5% on GSM8K after two rounds.
- **Reason for Degradation:** Empirical analysis (Figure 1) reveals that LLMs struggle to properly judge the correctness of their own reasoning. For GSM8K, GPT-3.5 was more likely to change a correct answer to an incorrect one (8.8%) than to revise an incorrect answer to a correct one (7.6%). On CommonSenseQA, GPT-3.5 frequently altered answers, often converting correct ones to incorrect due to the distracting nature of plausible-but-wrong options. More advanced models like GPT-4 and GPT-4-Turbo showed a higher tendency to retain their initial answers, suggesting higher confidence or robustness, but still exhibited performance drops when changes occurred. The authors attribute this to the supplementary prompt for self-correction potentially biasing the model away from an optimal initial response.

#### 4.2. Multi-Agent Debate Does Not Outperform Self-Consistency for Equivalent Inference Cost

- The study evaluated multi-agent debate, a technique involving multiple LLM instances critiquing each other, on GSM8K. While both multi-agent debate and self-consistency (simple majority voting among multiple generations) improved over standard prompting, multi-agent debate only slightly outperformed self-consistency when comparing the *same number of agents* (e.g., 3 responses).
- However, when comparing multi-agent debate to self-consistency with an *equivalent total number of model responses* (inference calls), multi-agent debate significantly *underperformed* simple self-consistency using majority voting. For instance, self-consistency with 6 responses achieved 85.3% accuracy, while multi-agent debate (requiring 6 calls for its first round) achieved 83.2%. This indicates that the improvement observed in multi-agent debate is primarily due to the "consistency" effect of averaging or selecting among multiple generations, rather than a genuine "self-correction" through critique.

#### 4.3. Prompt Design Issues in Prior Self-Correction Evaluations

- The paper identifies that some claimed improvements from self-correction in prior work might stem from sub-optimal initial prompts. They illustrate this using the "Constrained Generation" task from Madaan et al. (2023).
- In that work, the initial prompt did not explicitly instruct the model to include all input concepts. Self-correction then improved performance by guiding the model to identify and incorporate missing concepts.
- The authors demonstrate that by simply **integrating the previously implicit constraints into the initial prompt** (e.g., adding "Write a reasonable paragraph that includes *ALL* of the above concepts"), the "Standard Prompting" baseline significantly outperforms the self-correction results reported by Madaan et al. (e.g., 81.8% vs. 67.0%). When their "stronger" standard prompt was used, applying the self-correction mechanism again led to a decrease in performance (from 81.8% to 75.1%). This highlights that "self-correction" was, in such cases, merely compensating for an inadequately designed initial prompt rather than reflecting an advanced self-correcting ability.

In summary, the main findings consistently suggest that current LLMs struggle to intrinsically self-correct their reasoning errors. Improvements attributed to self-correction in some prior works are often found to be due to external guidance (oracle labels), the inherent benefits of generating multiple responses (self-consistency), or sub-optimal initial prompt design.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet" carry significant implications for the ongoing development, evaluation, and application of LLMs.

#### 5.1. A Critical Reality Check for LLM Capabilities

The most profound impact of this work is its role as a reality check. It challenges the sometimes overly optimistic narrative surrounding LLMs' "self-correction" abilities, particularly concerning complex reasoning. By demonstrating that current LLMs often fail to intrinsically identify and rectify their reasoning mistakes—and can even degrade performance when attempting to do so—the paper underscores that genuine self-awareness and robust error correction are still nascent capabilities. This dispels the notion that LLMs can simply "think harder" to correct their own logical flaws without external assistance.

#### 5.2. Guiding Future Research Directions

The paper offers clear, actionable insights for future research, steering the community towards more fruitful avenues:
- **Leveraging External Feedback:** The study strongly advocates for exploring techniques that effectively leverage *external feedback*. This includes feedback from code execution results (as shown in self-debugging), interaction with external tools like search engines and calculators, or input from trained verifier/critique models. This suggests that LLMs' strengths lie in processing and integrating information, rather than purely introspective error identification for complex reasoning.
- **Fair Baselines and Inference Costs:** It emphasizes the crucial need for fair comparisons in self-correction evaluations. Researchers are urged to account for the additional LLM calls and associated inference costs, comparing new methods against strong baselines like self-consistency that also utilize multiple model responses. This promotes a more economically realistic assessment of performance gains.
- **Diligent Prompt Design:** The work highlights the critical importance of prompt engineering. It advises that equal effort should be invested in crafting comprehensive initial prompts that include all task descriptions, rather than implicitly relying on "feedback" prompts to introduce unstated constraints. This ensures that any observed improvements are truly from the self-correction mechanism itself, not from compensating for a sub-optimally specified initial task.

#### 5.3. Fostering Rigor in Evaluation

The paper serves as a "call to action," urging researchers to approach the domain of LLM self-correction with a more discerning and critical perspective. It highlights prevailing ambiguities in the literature, where crucial details about label usage or external feedback might be obscured. By clarifying these distinctions, the paper promotes greater transparency and rigor in experimental design and reporting, ultimately leading to more trustworthy and reproducible research.

#### 5.4. Understanding LLM Limitations

The detailed empirical analysis of how and why LLMs fail to self-correct (e.g., tending to change correct answers to incorrect ones) provides valuable insights into the fundamental limitations of current model architectures and training paradigms. It suggests that while LLMs are excellent at generating coherent text, their internal "judgment" or "metacognition" about the correctness of complex reasoning steps is still unreliable.

#### 5.5. Broader Impact and Ethical Considerations

While the paper primarily focuses on reasoning, its implications extend to the broader deployment of LLMs. If LLMs cannot reliably self-correct, systems built upon them must incorporate robust external verification or human-in-the-loop mechanisms, especially in high-stakes applications where accuracy is paramount (e.g., healthcare, legal advice). The findings also implicitly underscore the ongoing challenge of "alignment" and "truthfulness" in LLMs, as models that cannot accurately evaluate their own reasoning are more prone to generating false or misleading information. The paper also acknowledges its limitation to reasoning tasks, noting that self-correction might be more effective for aligning responses with preferences (e.g., style, safety) where the LLM might have a better internal "compass."

In conclusion, this paper is a significant contribution that re-calibrates expectations regarding LLM self-correction. It moves the conversation beyond simplistic notions of models fixing their own errors, instead advocating for a more sophisticated understanding that incorporates external scaffolding and rigorous evaluation, thereby charting a more realistic and impactful course for future LLM research.
