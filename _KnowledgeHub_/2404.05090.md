# How Bad is Training on Synthetic Data? A Statistical Analysis of Language Model Collapse

> [!summary] Summary
> A statistical theory of language model collapse. Next-token prediction is modeled as a Softmax classifier over one-hot contexts, reducing each context's learned conditional to a multinomial empirical mean, and recursive training becomes a random walk on the probability simplex. Training purely on synthetic data provably collapses to a Dirac mass at rate exponential in generations; mixing in real data bounds the drift, but only if synthetic samples stay exponentially fewer than real ones. GPT2-scale experiments confirm both regimes.

## Problem

- Prior collapse studies are mostly empirical, observing degeneracy and lost linguistic diversity across generations without quantifying rates, collapse times, or safe synthetic-data budgets.
- Existing theory uses single-dimensional Gaussian toys with no training step, or diffusion models over images, neither matching discrete next-token prediction over a vocabulary.
- No prior work estimates how much synthetic data can be mixed with real data before recursive training drifts away from the original distribution; related theory on scaling laws and linear regression captures degradation with generation number but not the distributional shift of generated token distributions.

## Method

- A statistical next-token language model: a linear ==Softmax classifier== on one-hot context embeddings, whose ==cross-entropy minimizer== gives each context's conditional as the ==empirical multinomial mean== over its samples.
- Two recursive training regimes formalized: **Fully Synthetic** (each generation trained only on the previous model's samples) and **Partially Synthetic** (fixed N real samples mixed with n fresh synthetic ones each round).
- A **Total Collapse** definition (convergence to a Dirac mass) tracked by the ==dispersion statistics== sup-norm, squared 2-norm and its expectation, plus 1-norm drift, analyzed as an ==absorbing Markov chain== on the simplex; theorems giving ==closed-form dispersion recursions==, collapse probability and expected collapse time bounds, and a generation-independent bound on drift from the first-generation model via empirical-distribution ==concentration inequalities==.

## Results

- In the Fully Synthetic case, expected squared-norm dispersion follows the closed form one minus the geometric factor raised to generation count, and expected collapse time is bounded between order n and order n squared.
- Simulations over **100** runs with vocabulary **600**, support **52** and initial dispersion **0.1** confirm collapse time scales roughly linearly in sample size, indicating the quadratic upper bound is loose.
- The mixture bound implies synthetic sample count must grow only logarithmically in the real-data-to-vocabulary ratio, so drift stays below a target epsilon; drift plateaus in simulation as synthetic count rises; a character-level GPT2-style model on tiny Shakespeare (vocabulary **65**, **8** layers, **2000** iterations) shows 1-norm drift diverging over **9** generations under pure synthetic training, while **50/50** and **20/80** real mixtures stay stable; validation loss overfits only in the pure case.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recursive self-training on purely generated data collapses regardless of architecture; the driver is finite-sample statistical error alone, before any expressivity limits enter.
> - Safe synthetic budgets are far tighter than intuition suggests: the tolerable synthetic fraction scales logarithmically, so generated data must be a small minority of the corpus.
> - Less diverse starting distributions collapse faster, and the surviving mode is sampled proportional to initial token probability, so tails vanish first and dominant tokens absorb everything.
> - Mixing data from all past generations, rather than only the most recent ones, delays or plateaus degradation, so retaining historical data pools is qualitatively protective.
> - Sample count per context matters more than corpus size: with many contexts, per-context samples shrink and collapse arrives quickly even for large datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid proliferation and adoption of large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT have brought to the forefront a critical and emerging challenge: the "pollution" of the internet with synthetic data generated by these very models. Since future generations of LLMs are trained on vast datasets primarily sourced from the web, this recursive "train-generate" loop raises serious concerns about the long-term health, performance, and diversity of these AI systems. This phenomenon, termed **model collapse** by Shumailov et al. (2023), describes a degradation in model performance and output quality, where models tend to produce limited, repetitive, or less diverse outputs, eventually "forgetting" the nuances and tails of the original, real data distribution.

Prior research has identified and empirically investigated model collapse and similar phenomena. Shumailov et al. (2023) introduced the concept and attributed its causes to two main approximation errors: *statistical approximation error* (due to finite training samples) and *functional approximation error* (due to insufficient model expressivity). Other works, such as Alemohammad et al. (2023) (coining "Model Autophagy Disorder" or MAD) and Briesch et al. (2023) (studying "Self-Consuming phenomenon"), have provided empirical evidence of performance degradation in recursive training loops, linking it to the amount of real data. Guo et al. (2023) specifically observed a decline in linguistic diversity when training on synthetic text.

While these empirical studies have sounded the alarm, theoretical understanding, especially for language models, has been relatively scarce. Existing theoretical explorations of model collapse have often relied on simplified models or different domains: Shumailov et al. (2023) used a basic recursive Gaussian distribution for intuition without considering training, Fu et al. (2024) studied diffusion models (often used for images), and Dohmatob et al. (2024a, 2024b) examined linear regression, which, while offering insights into scaling laws and test error, does not directly translate to the discrete and sequential nature of language.

This paper bridges this critical gap by providing a **rigorous theoretical framework specifically for language models**. By introducing a simplified statistical model for next-token prediction, the authors aim to precisely characterize the mechanisms of model collapse, particularly focusing on the *statistical approximation error*. This work is timely and crucial because practitioners are already incorporating synthetic data into training datasets (e.g., Ben Allal et al., 2024; Gunasekar et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024), making a robust theoretical understanding and quantitative guidelines for mitigation an urgent necessity for the sustainable development of generative AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the escalating concern about the quality and diversity of future Large Language Models (LLMs) as the internet becomes increasingly saturated with synthetic content generated by current-generation LLMs. The inevitable cycle where new models are trained on data, generate new data, and then subsequent models are trained on this contaminated data—the "train-generate loop"—poses a significant threat to the long-term performance and linguistic richness of AI systems. The phenomenon of model collapse, characterized by performance deterioration, repetitive outputs, and the loss of information about the original data distribution's "tails," is a direct consequence of this loop.

The key objectives of this paper are:

1. **Develop a Rigorous Theoretical Framework:** To establish a sound statistical model that allows for a precise, quantitative analysis of model collapse in language models, moving beyond intuitive explanations and simplified models from other domains. The paper specifically focuses on characterizing the impact of *statistical approximation error*, acknowledging that functional approximation error is also relevant but outside the scope of this initial theoretical investigation.
2. **Characterize Recursive Training Scenarios:** To analyze the behavior of model collapse under different recursive training conditions, specifically:
 - **Fully Synthetic:** Training exclusively on data generated by the immediately preceding model. This represents a worst-case scenario.
 - **Partially Synthetic:** Training on a mixture of real data from the original distribution and synthetic data from the immediately preceding model. This scenario is considered more realistic for future LLM development.
3. **Quantify Model Collapse and Distribution Shift:** For each scenario, the goal is to quantify key statistical metrics that measure the degree of collapse (e.g., convergence to Dirac masses, loss of diversity) and the shift in the data distribution across generations.
4. **Determine Mitigation Strategies:** Specifically, for the partially synthetic scenario, the objective is to estimate a maximal amount of synthetic data that can be safely incorporated into the training mixture without leading to unavoidable model collapse, thereby providing concrete guidelines for practitioners.
5. **Validate Theoretical Findings Empirically:** To support the theoretical conclusions with empirical validations, using both simulations of the proposed statistical model and experiments with more realistic transformer-based language models.

In essence, the authors are motivated to understand not just *that* model collapse happens, but *how* and *why* it happens in the context of language models, and crucially, *what can be done to prevent or mitigate it* through principled statistical analysis.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

To rigorously analyze model collapse in language models, the authors adopt a multi-pronged methodology combining a simplified statistical model for theoretical tractability, defined recursive training scenarios, specific quantitative metrics, and empirical validation.

#### 3.1. Statistical Language Model Setup

The core of the theoretical analysis is built upon a simplified statistical language model.
- **Next-Token Prediction:** The model focuses on predicting the next token `Y` given a context `X`.
- **Vocabulary and Context:** It considers a vocabulary of size `s` and a context length `ℓ`. The true underlying language data is assumed to be generated from unknown conditional probabilities `p_jk = P{Y=k | X=j}`, where `j` represents a context and `k` a token.
- **One-Hot Embeddings:** Crucially for theoretical tractability, contexts `x_l` are represented using one-hot embeddings, rather than the complex, high-dimensional representations typical of modern transformer models. This simplification allows for a more direct analysis of the statistical properties.
- **Softmax Classifier:** The conditional probabilities `p_jk` are estimated by minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss using a Softmax classifier. The estimated probabilities `p̂_jk` are shown to be empirical means, directly reflecting the counts of tokens given a context.
- **Fixed Context Simplification:** For further analysis, the authors simplify by fixing a single context `j` and focusing on the vector of probabilities `p = (p_1, ..., p_s)` for the next token. `p^(0)` denotes the ground-truth distribution, and `p^(1)` represents the first-generation model trained on `N` real samples.

#### 3.2. Recursive Training Scenarios

The paper defines two main recursive training scenarios to study the evolution of `p^(m)` (the model at generation `m`):

1. **Fully Synthetic:** In this worst-case scenario, each successive model `p^(m)` is trained *exclusively* on `n` samples of synthetic data generated by the immediately preceding model `p^(m-1)`. No original real data is re-introduced after the first generation.
2. **Partially Synthetic:** This more realistic scenario involves training `p^(m)` on a mixture of `N` samples of real data from the original distribution `p^(0)` and `n` samples of synthetic data generated by `p^(m-1)`. This setup allows for assessing the attenuating effect of real data.

#### 3.3. Quantitative Metrics for Model Collapse

To quantify the degree and rate of model collapse, several statistical quantities are defined:
- `||p^(m)||∞`: The maximum probability assigned to any single token in `p^(m)`. A value of 1 signifies total collapse to a Dirac mass.
- `σ_m = ||p^(m)||2^2`: The sum of squared probabilities. This metric also approaches 1 as the distribution becomes less diverse and concentrates on fewer tokens.
- `S_m = E[σ_m]`: The expected sum of squared probabilities, used for theoretical analysis.
- `ρ_m = P(||p^(m)||∞ = 1)`: The probability that total collapse (to a Dirac mass) has occurred by generation `m`.
- `T`: The random time (number of generations) until total collapse first occurs.
- `||μ - ν||1`: The 1-norm distance (twice the total variation distance) between two distributions `μ` and `ν`, used to quantify the distribution shift between generations.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Analysis and Proofs

The authors derive closed-form expressions and bounds for these metrics for both scenarios. This involves leveraging properties of multinomial distributions and, for deviation control, concentration inequalities from prior work (Mardia et al., 2020). The proofs are presented in the appendix, showcasing the rigorous mathematical foundation.

#### 3.5. Empirical Validation

The theoretical findings are supported by two types of empirical experiments:
- **Transformer-based Models:** Experiments with a GPT2-style decoder-only generative model on the tiny Shakespeare dataset (vocabulary size `s=65`). This allows for validating the theoretical insights in a more realistic setting, where the functional approximation error is minimized by using the same architecture and training parameters across generations, thus isolating the effect of statistical approximation error.
- **Statistical Model Simulations (Extended Scenarios):** Simulations with the proposed statistical model are conducted for additional, more complex recursive training scenarios beyond the two primary theoretical cases. These include:
 - **Most Recent Models:** Training on synthetic data from the `K` most recent past models.
 - **Randomly Sampled Data:** Training on a random mixture of synthetic data from *all* previous models, potentially including real data.
These extensions aim to demonstrate the generality of the core findings across various practical settings.

By systematically simplifying the problem to a statistically tractable level, rigorously defining the recursive process and metrics, and then validating with both simplified and more complex empirical setups, the authors provide a comprehensive analysis of model collapse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper delivers several key theoretical and empirical findings that shed light on the mechanisms and inevitability of model collapse under different recursive training paradigms.

#### 4.1. Fully Synthetic: Training on Synthetic Data

The theoretical analysis for the "Fully Synthetic" scenario, where models are trained solely on data generated by the previous generation, yields stark conclusions regarding the inevitability of model collapse.

- **Total Collapse is Unavoidable:** The paper rigorously demonstrates that total collapse, defined as `p^(m)` converging to a Dirac mass `δ_i` (where all probability is concentrated on a single token `i`), is bound to happen almost surely. This means the model loses all information about the original diverse distribution.
- **Rate of Collapse (Theorem 1):**
 - The expected sum of squared probabilities, `S_m`, converges to 1 (indicating collapse) exponentially fast with the number of generations `m`. Specifically, `S_m = 1 - (1 - 1/n)^m (1 - S_0)`, where `n` is the synthetic sample size and `S_0` is the initial distribution's diversity.
 - The probability of total collapse `ρ_m` also tends to 1 exponentially fast in `m`.
 - The expected time `E[T]` until total collapse occurs is bounded between `O(n)` and `O(n^2)`. Empirical results suggest it is closer to `O(n)`, implying that collapse can happen relatively quickly, especially when the number of samples per context (`n`) is small.
 - Smaller synthetic sample size `n` and less diverse initial distributions (larger `S_0`) accelerate collapse.
- **Limiting Distribution (Proposition 1):** When total collapse occurs, the resulting Dirac mass `δ_i` (the token the model converges to always predicting) is more likely to be one that had a higher initial probability `p_i` in the original distribution. This explains the "early model collapse" (loss of tails) and "late model collapse" (complete loss of randomness) phenomena observed in prior work.

#### 4.2. Partially Synthetic: Handling Model Collapse with Real Data

In the "Partially Synthetic" scenario, where a mixture of real and synthetic data is used for training, the findings offer a more optimistic outlook but with crucial caveats regarding the amount of synthetic data.

- **Model Variation (Theorem 2):** The analysis of `S_m` (expected sum of squared probabilities) shows that `S_m` remains relatively stable and close to `S_0` when the amount of synthetic data `n` is significantly smaller than the amount of real data `N`. This suggests that incorporating sufficient real data can indeed prevent the dramatic increase in concentration observed in the fully synthetic case.
- **Model Deviation (Theorem 3 & 4):** This is a critical finding for practical guidance. The paper quantifies the expected `L1`-norm deviation `E||p^(m) - p^(1)||1` (deviation from the first-generation model) and shows that:
 - This deviation is *independent of the generation number m*. This implies that if the initial deviation from `p^(1)` is controlled, it doesn't necessarily worsen with more generations in this mixed data setting.
 - To ensure that `p^(m)` remains `ϵ`-close to `p^(1)` in `L1` norm, the amount of synthetic data `n` must be *logarithmic* in the ratio `Nϵ/s` (real data amount, error tolerance, vocabulary size). This is a strong result, indicating that the synthetic data proportion needs to be *exponentially smaller* compared to the real data to maintain distribution fidelity.

#### 4.3. Empirical Validations

The theoretical results are strongly supported by empirical experiments:

- **Transformer-based Models (GPT2-style):**
 - In the Fully Synthetic setting, `||p^(m) - p^(1)||1` clearly diverges over generations, and the models exhibit an overfitting effect with increased validation loss, consistent with functional degradation.
 - In the Partially Synthetic settings (e.g., 50% synthetic-50% real, 20% synthetic-80% real), the `L1`-deviation remains stable, and validation loss behaves more consistently across generations, confirming that mixing with real data indeed stabilizes performance.
- **Statistical Model (Extended Scenarios):**
 - **Most Recent K Models:** Even when training on data from multiple recent generations, total collapse is only *delayed* but still eventually occurs, albeit slower than in the `K=1` (fully synthetic) case.
 - **Randomly Sampled Data:** When synthetic data is sampled uniformly from *all* previous models (including the original real data), the model deterioration occurs but the deviation `||p^(m) - p^(1)||1` plateaus very quickly. Total collapse to a single Dirac mass is significantly delayed, or effectively avoided over the observed generations, suggesting a qualitative difference from fixed-window or single-predecessor synthetic training.

These findings collectively underscore the perilous nature of recursive training on purely synthetic data and provide quantitative benchmarks for how much real data is necessary to maintain model stability and prevent collapse, particularly emphasizing that the synthetic data fraction must be kept extremely small relative to real data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper provides a fundamental and timely contribution to the understanding of model collapse in the context of Large Language Models, with significant implications for the design and training of future generative AI systems.

**Crucial Insight into Inevitable Collapse:** The most striking finding is the theoretical demonstration that model collapse is **inevitable** when training solely on synthetic data, regardless of the complexity of the underlying model. This robust conclusion, derived from a simplified statistical framework, serves as a powerful warning against relying exclusively on self-generated content for model evolution. It highlights a deep, systemic issue rather than a mere artifact of current model architectures.

**Quantitative Guidelines for Mitigation:** For the first time in the context of language models, the paper offers **quantitative guidelines** for mitigating model collapse. The finding that the amount of synthetic data must be *logarithmically small* compared to the real data (i.e., real data needs to be exponentially larger than synthetic data) to maintain distribution fidelity is profoundly significant. This provides a concrete, theoretically backed rule of thumb for dataset curators and AI developers, indicating that simply adding "some" real data might not be enough; the proportion is critical and skewed heavily towards real data.

**Foundational Theoretical Framework:** By introducing a tractable statistical model for language, the paper lays a crucial **groundwork for future theoretical investigations** into LLM dynamics. It moves beyond simplified Gaussian models or non-language-specific contexts, offering a stepping stone for incorporating more complex aspects like functional approximation error (e.g., using high-dimensional context embeddings) or the impact of in-context learning. This framework can catalyze further rigorous analysis of the long-term behavior of self-improving AI systems.

**Implications for Data Curation and AI Sustainability:** The work underscores the paramount importance of **maintaining a fresh stream of original, human-created data** for the sustainable development of generative AI. In a world increasingly populated by synthetic content, this research highlights the challenge of sourcing and preserving "real" data to prevent future models from becoming degenerate. It suggests that data diversity and quality may become an even more critical bottleneck than computational power or model size.

**Alert for AI Evolution:** The paper serves as a significant alert to the AI research community and industry about the **self-consuming nature of current generative AI development practices.** If unchecked, the recursive generation and training loop could lead to a progressive loss of linguistic diversity, creativity, and accuracy in future models, ultimately stifling their evolutionary potential. The observed phenomena (loss of tails, convergence to repetitive outputs, overfitting) represent a "catastrophe" that needs proactive strategies.

In summary, this research is not merely an academic exercise; it offers critical theoretical insights and practical guidance on a pressing challenge facing the rapidly evolving field of generative AI. Its impact will likely be felt in how future LLMs are trained, how training datasets are curated, and how the long-term sustainability and diversity of AI intelligence are envisioned and protected.
