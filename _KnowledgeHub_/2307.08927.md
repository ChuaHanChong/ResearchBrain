# Multi-Stage Cable Routing through Hierarchical Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Berkeley AI Research Lab developed a hierarchical imitation learning system for multi-stage cable routing using a Franka Panda robot, enabling robust manipulation of deformable cables and effective failure recovery by integrating learned low-level clip insertion primitives with a high-level action sequencing policy. The system achieved a 50% success rate for 3-clip routing and demonstrated rapid adaptation to novel conditions through interactive fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Complex, multi-stage robotic manipulation tasks are hindered by compounding errors, where individual primitive failures drastically reduce overall task success probability.
- Manipulating deformable objects like cables presents challenges due to their infinite state space, complex non-linear dynamics, and the critical need for continuous vision-based perception.
- Traditional flat end-to-end learning methods often fail for long-horizon tasks, particularly when requiring diverse failure recovery strategies in real-world settings.

## Method

- A hierarchical imitation learning system was developed, comprising a learned low-level policy for vision-based cable insertion into a single clip and a learned high-level policy for sequencing actions.
- The low-level policy was trained on human teleoperation data, including specific recovery trials, to enable self-correction for local mistakes, utilizing end-effector mounted cameras and extensive data augmentation.
- The high-level policy orchestrates a mix of learned and scripted primitives, dynamically selecting actions based on visual observations and a primitive history embedding to achieve multi-stage cable routing and compensate for imperfections.
- An interactive fine-tuning scheme allows rapid adaptation of the high-level policy to novel or out-of-distribution scenarios with minimal human demonstrations.

## Results

- The learned low-level single-clip insertion policy achieved a 46% success rate, outperforming a scripted baseline and demonstrating that recovery demonstrations improve performance from 18% to 46%.
- The full hierarchical system attained 79%, 58%, and 50% success rates for 1, 2, and 3-clip routing tasks respectively, significantly surpassing flat end-to-end learning (0% for 3 clips) and hand-designed state machines (5% for 3 clips).
- Interactive fine-tuning with only 10 demonstrations led to substantial performance improvements for out-of-distribution clip configurations (e.g., from 0% to 15/30 for 3-clip OOD) and enhanced reliability for in-distribution tasks (from 50% to 85%).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training low-level policies with explicit recovery demonstrations significantly enhances their robustness and ability to self-correct local errors during complex manipulation.
> - A learned high-level policy, capable of intelligent primitive sequencing and failure recovery, is crucial for mitigating compounding errors and achieving high success rates in long-horizon, multi-stage tasks.
> - View-invariant observations from end-effector mounted cameras, coupled with rigorous data augmentation, are essential for developing generalizable vision-based controllers for deformable object manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses challenges in complex, multi-stage robotic manipulation, particularly focusing on tasks involving deformable objects and visual perception in real-world settings. Robotic manipulation, especially for long-horizon tasks, frequently encounters issues of compounding errors. If individual task stages have a non-negligible probability of failure, the likelihood of overall task success decreases multiplicatively with each additional stage. This paper integrates concepts from several subfields:

- **Learning-based Robotic Control:** Advances in imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) have enabled robots to learn vision-based controllers for various manipulation tasks. This work builds upon this foundation by applying IL for acquiring specific robotic skills.
- **Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning and Imitation Learning:** To address long-horizon tasks, hierarchical approaches have been developed to compose primitive skills, improve sample efficiency, and facilitate generalization. This paper explicitly employs a hierarchical imitation learning framework, similar to prior works that use fixed primitive sets and train a high-level policy for skill selection.
- **Skill Composition for Multi-Stage Tasks:** Traditional methods for skill composition include behavior trees and Task and Motion Planning (TAMP). These often rely on predefined action modes, symbolic states, or accurate state estimators and models of the environment. For deformable objects like cables, building such components is challenging due to the infinite variety of shapes and complex contact patterns. This work contrasts with these by learning both high-level sequencing and low-level control policies.
- **Deformable Object Manipulation:** Manipulating deformable materials (e.g., cloth, rope) presents a significant challenge due to their complex, non-linear dynamics and infinite state space. Existing approaches often rely on extensive domain expertise, object-specific models, or multi-modal sensory inputs. This paper contributes to this area by developing a learning-based method for cable routing, which has practical relevance in industrial applications, without relying on explicit deformation models or full visibility assumptions common in some prior work.

The paper positions itself by noting that while learning-based methods are effective for individual primitives, directly applying them to complex multi-stage tasks often fails. It proposes that combining imperfect, learned primitives with an intelligent higher-level controller capable of failure recovery is crucial. This hierarchical approach, particularly in the context of deformable object manipulation like cable routing, aims to provide robustness that surpasses simple sequential execution or flat end-to-end learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and generalizable robotic system capable of performing complex, multi-stage cable routing tasks through a series of clips. This task is representative of scenarios found in manufacturing and maintenance, requiring delicate manipulation of deformable objects while integrating perception and control over an extended temporal horizon.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in such complex manipulation tasks:

1. **Compounding Failure Probabilities:** When a robot executes a sequence of primitive behaviors, the probability of overall task failure increases multiplicatively with each stage. Even if individual primitives have a high success rate (e.g., 90%), a task with many stages (e.g., 10 stages) can quickly lead to an impractically low overall success rate (e.g., 0.9^10 ≈ 35%). This necessitates controllers that can handle imperfections and recover from mistakes.
2. **Deformable Object Manipulation:** Cables are deformable, meaning they can assume an infinite variety of shapes, and their interaction with the gripper and clips is complex. This makes accurate modeling and control challenging. The robot must continuously perceive the cable's configuration to correctly guide it into clips, requiring tight integration of visual perception and control.
3. **Vision-Based Control Integration:** The task requires closing the loop on visual perception, as proprioceptive information alone is insufficient to track the cable's state. Learning-based methods offer a means to achieve this without extensive manual engineering of perception systems.
4. **Limitations of Flat End-to-End Learning:** Directly training an end-to-end policy for the entire multi-stage cable routing task from scratch is often impractical due to the long horizon, high dimensionality of observations and actions, and the need for diverse failure recovery strategies. The paper posits that such naive approaches typically fail even with idealized demonstration data.
5. **Need for Robustness and Recovery:** Instead of striving for perfect, never-failing robotic skills, the motivation is to build robustness by enabling both low-level and high-level controllers to correct and recover from mistakes. This involves smart sequencing of primitives, retrying failed actions, and taking corrective measures as needed.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a hierarchical imitation learning system where a higher-level policy orchestrates lower-level primitives, dynamically selecting actions to compensate for individual skill imperfections and failures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a hierarchical imitation learning system for multi-stage cable routing, structured with both low-level motor control policies and a high-level sequencing policy.

**A. Overall System Architecture**
The system comprises a Franka Panda robot, two Basler RGB cameras mounted on the end-effector (wrist cameras), and two Intel RealSense cameras (top-down and front-facing views). The core of the system is a hierarchical policy (Figure 2):
- **Low-Level Primitives:** A set of individual skills, including a learned policy for inserting a cable into a single clip, and several scripted primitives for other subtasks.
- **High-Level Primitive Selection Policy:** A learned policy that integrates these primitives, dynamically selecting which one to trigger based on visual observations and task context.

**B. Low-Level Clip Routing Policy**
This policy is designed to perform the critical task of inserting a grasped cable into a single clip.

- **Task:** Inserting a cable near the current grasp point into the closest clip.
- **Training Data Collection:** A dataset of 1442 trajectories was collected via human teleoperation at 5Hz.
 - **Successful Trials (approx. 800):** Demonstrators successfully inserted the cable.
 - **Recovery Trials (approx. 600):** Demonstrators intentionally induced failure states (e.g., partial insertion failure) and then demonstrated recovery actions. This explicitly trained the policy to handle and recover from small local mistakes (Figure 5).
- **Imitation Learning:** Standard behavioral cloning (maximum likelihood estimation) was used to train a vision-based policy $\pi_\theta(a|o)$.
- **Sensor Observation ($o$):** Images from two wrist cameras and the z-component of the end-effector TCP pose (found to be sufficient, as spatial information is inferable from wrist cameras).
- **Robot Action ($a$):** A 4-dimensional Cartesian twist (full translation components, rotation around the z-axis).
- **Network Architecture (Figure 2):**
 - Two ResNet18 networks (with group normalization) process the two wrist camera images to extract feature embeddings.
 - The z-coordinate is passed through a fully connected layer for embedding.
 - These embeddings are concatenated and fed into a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to output the mean and log-standard deviation of a Gaussian action distribution.
- **Generalization Techniques:**
 - **View-Invariant Representations:** The policy operates in the end-effector's frame of reference. Wrist-mounted cameras ensure observations are relative to the gripper, making the policy robust to varying clip positions and orientations. Actions are also defined relative to the end-effector.
 - **Data Augmentation:** RandAugment was applied during training (two transforms chosen randomly, strength of nine) to increase data diversity and improve robustness to lighting variations, camera perturbations, and scene object variability (Figure 6).

**C. High-Level Primitive Selection Policy**
This policy is responsible for sequencing the various primitives to complete the multi-stage cable routing task.

- **Task:** Orchestrating the sequence of low-level actions to route a cable through multiple clips.
- **Network Architecture (Figure 2):**
 - **Visual Input:** Images from the two wrist cameras and the side camera are processed by ResNet18 encoders. The ResNet18 parameters from the low-level policy are reused and frozen for sample efficiency.
 - **Primitive History:** The history of up to six previously executed primitives for the current clip is encoded using a learned word embedding layer. This provides context for corrective actions.
 - **End-effector Pose:** The z-component of the end-effector pose is also included.
 - These inputs are concatenated and fed into a 3-layer MLP, which outputs a categorical distribution (four-way softmax) over the available primitives.
- **Primitives:**
 - **Learned Primitive:** The "clip insertion" primitive described above.
 - **Scripted Primitives:**
 - **Pickup:** Detects a colored marker on the cable using the top camera (the only calibrated camera) to construct a pick-up pose, then grasps the cable (Figure 7).
 - **Perturb_cable:** Rearranges the cable's shape to resolve pathological configurations. It uses the same detection method as Pickup to grasp the cable and applies predefined stretching motions to reduce slack (Figure 8).
 - **Go_next:** Advances the task to the next clip. It releases the cable and moves the end-effector to a position near the next clip, applying a small random perturbation to match the initial state distribution of the single-clip policy.
- **Training:** A dataset of approximately 250 high-level trajectories was collected by a human expert triggering discrete primitives via keyboard (Figure 10). The dataset was augmented by relabeling adjacent frames with the same primitive choice to make the policy more robust to local variations. This policy is trained via a standard cross-entropy loss.

**D. Interactive Finetuning**
To adapt to novel conditions or improve reliability, an interactive finetuning scheme (similar to HG-Dagger) is employed.
- **Procedure:** The high-level policy attempts the task under human supervision. If the policy selects an incorrect primitive, the human operator intervenes by overriding the choice. This process continues until the task is completed.
- **Training:** Only the MLP weights of the high-level policy are finetuned on these newly collected interactive datasets, with a learning rate warm-up. This allows rapid adaptation with as few as ten additional trials.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation aimed to assess the necessity of hierarchical structures, the effectiveness of design choices, and the generalization capabilities of the system.

**A. Low-Level Single Clip Routing Policy Evaluation**
- **Success Rate:** The learned low-level policy achieved an overall success rate of 46% (23 out of 50 trials) for single-clip insertion across varying clip positions and cable shapes. This included 18/25 for "easier" cable shapes and 5/25 for "harder" shapes. The authors note this is comparable to human teleoperation success rates (approximately 60% during data collection).
- **Comparison to Scripted Policy:** The learned policy significantly outperformed a scripted baseline that used ground-truth clip information but lacked visual cable perception. The scripted policy achieved 10/50 success, demonstrating the value of vision-based control.
- **Ablation Studies (Table II):**
 - **No image augmentation:** Performance dropped drastically to 11/50, highlighting the importance of data augmentation for robustness.
 - **No shared ResNet (separate encoders for wrist cameras):** Showed marginal benefit (21/50), suggesting sufficient network capacity.
 - **Side camera view instead of wrist cameras:** Performance plummeted to 6/50, confirming the criticality of view-invariant, end-effector mounted cameras for local control.
 - **No correction data (recovery demonstrations excluded):** Performance noticeably decreased to 18/50, with observed failures in recovering from partial insertions, validating the explicit training for recovery.

**B. High-Level Policy for Cable Routing Evaluation**
- **Multi-Clip Performance (Table III):** The full hierarchical system was evaluated on tasks with 1, 2, and 3 clips (24 trials per configuration).
 - 1 Clip: 19/24 (79%) success.
 - 2 Clips: 14/24 (58%) success.
 - 3 Clips: 12/24 (50%) success.
 - Total: 45/72 (62.5%) success.
 The performance did not drop exponentially with an increased number of clips, supporting the hypothesis that intelligent sequencing can mitigate compounding errors.
- **Baseline Comparison (Table IV):** The hierarchical imitation learning method (12/24 success for three clips) significantly outperformed alternative approaches:
 - **State Machine:** A hand-designed state machine with programmed recovery attempts achieved 5/24 success, indicating that learned high-level policies derive more effective recovery strategies.
 - **Flat End-to-End BC:** Achieved 0/24 success, failing even on the first clip due to issues like not picking up the cable or moving the arm out of bounds. This supported the necessity of hierarchical decomposition.
 - **Behavior Transformer (BeT):** Also achieved 0/24 success, showing similar failure modes as flat BC, suggesting that multimodal demonstration handling alone was not the bottleneck.
 - **Action Chunking (ACT):** Achieved 0/24 success, failing to route the current clip or prematurely dropping the cable. This indicated that implicit hierarchical approaches like ACT still underperformed explicit hierarchical policies for this task.
- **Ablation of High-Level Policy History (Table V):** Omitting the primitive history embedding from the high-level policy resulted in 0/24 success. The memoryless policy repeatedly prompted `go_next` after routing the first clip but failed to initiate actions for subsequent clips, underscoring the importance of historical context for decision-making and recovery.
- **Qualitative Analysis:** The learned high-level policy exhibited emergent behaviors, such as using the `Perturb_cable` primitive multiple times sequentially to adjust the cable into a graspable position after an initial perturbation failed to yield a favorable shape (Figure 13).
- **Failure Mode Analysis:** Over 95% of failures occurred when the high-level policy predicted `go_next` even though the cable was not successfully routed (e.g., merely behind the clip), leading to an unrecoverable state. This suggests a potential visual ambiguity issue.

**C. Interactive Fine-Tuning**
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Finetuning (Figure 15, Table VI):** For challenging OOD clip configurations (clips outside training distribution ranges, or an additional fourth clip):
 - Zero-shot performance was low (e.g., 6/30 for 3-clip OOD, 0/10 for 4-clip OOD).
 - Finetuning with just 10 interactive demonstrations per scenario significantly improved success rates (e.g., 15/30 for 3-clip OOD, 2/10 for 4-clip OOD). This demonstrated rapid adaptation to novel situations.
- **In-Distribution Finetuning (Table VI):** For four new in-distribution configurations, finetuning with 10 additional demonstrations per configuration improved reliability from 50% to 85% success, showing the system's ability to boost performance for desired levels of reliability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by demonstrating a viable approach for learning complex, multi-stage tasks involving deformable objects. The key significance and potential impact include:

- **Addressing the Compounding Error Problem:** The work provides evidence that a hierarchical imitation learning framework, which integrates learned recovery mechanisms at both high and low levels, can effectively mitigate the problem of multiplicatively decreasing success rates in long-horizon tasks. This is a foundational challenge in deploying robots for complex real-world applications.
- **Robust Deformable Object Manipulation:** The successful application to cable routing, a task involving complex interactions with a deformable object and requiring vision-based closed-loop control, advances the state of the art in deformable object manipulation. The learned policies are shown to be more effective than scripted alternatives, even when the latter are given privileged information.
- **Practical Relevance to Industrial Automation:** Cable routing is a common and challenging task in manufacturing and maintenance. This research offers a data-driven solution that could lead to increased automation in these sectors, potentially reducing manual labor and improving efficiency.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Adaptability:** The system's ability to generalize to novel clip arrangements within the training distribution, and critically, to rapidly adapt to out-of-distribution scenarios and improve reliability with a small amount of interactive human feedback, is a key strength. This interactive finetuning approach offers a practical pathway for deploying learning-based robots in varied and evolving environments.
- **Modular System Design:** The hierarchical structure allows for a modular design, enabling the combination of learned and scripted primitives. This flexibility means that simpler subtasks can be handled by robust, hand-engineered solutions, while complex, perception-dependent tasks can leverage learning, thereby simplifying overall system development and maintenance.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust baseline and framework for further exploration of hierarchical learning in robotics. It opens avenues for future research into developing more diverse and robust primitives, integrating autonomous improvement through reinforcement learning, and applying similar methodologies to other complex manipulation tasks with deformable objects.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of datasets and CAD files for the clips promotes reproducibility and facilitates further research and development within the robotics community, allowing others to build upon this work.
