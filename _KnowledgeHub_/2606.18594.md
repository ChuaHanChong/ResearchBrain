# Benchmarking Action Spaces in Reinforcement Learning for Vision-based Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This study benchmarks four action spaces in reinforcement learning for vision-based robotic manipulation, revealing that joint velocity actions enable robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, achieving 100% success in object picking and the largest displacement in object pushing, alongside smoother motions on a physical robot. Cartesian action spaces, in contrast, often failed to transfer effectively from simulation to reality.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) for robotic manipulation faces challenges in real-world deployment concerning safety, motion smoothness, and reliable sim-to-real transfer.
- Prior research on action-space design in RL for robotics often overlooked vision-based observations and extensive real-world evaluation.
- Vision-based control introduces complexities like partial observability and perceptual noise, which can significantly alter learning dynamics and real-world transferability.

## Method

- Policies were trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in MuJoCo simulation with vision-based observations for a Franka Emika Panda robot.
- Four common ==action-space representations== (pose increment, pose velocity, joint position increment, and joint velocity) were systematically benchmarked.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capabilities were evaluated on a physical robot for two distinct vision-based manipulation tasks: object picking and object pushing.

## Results

- For the object picking task, joint velocity achieved a **100%** real-world success rate with low jerk, whereas Cartesian pose velocity failed in all real-world trials.
- In the object pushing task, joint velocity yielded the largest median aggregate block displacement (**0.405** m) on the physical robot.
- Simulation performance did not reliably predict real-world success for all action spaces, particularly for Cartesian actions which showed significant performance degradation during sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Joint velocity actions consistently produced smoother motions (lowest jerk) and demonstrated high robustness for real-world vision-based sim-to-real transfer.
> - Cartesian action spaces (pose increment, pose velocity) suffered from degraded real-world performance due to sensitivities to inverse kinematics (IK) discrepancies and real-world actuator limitations.
> - Position-actuated action spaces, while sometimes faster in simulation, tended to induce larger torque differences and higher jerk, potentially due to direct amplification of policy output fluctuations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a framework for developing control policies for robotic tasks. However, its practical deployment in real-world robotic manipulation faces challenges related to safety, motion smoothness, and reliable transfer from simulation to physical systems (sim-to-real). A fundamental design decision in RL formulations for robotics is the choice of action space, which has been observed to influence factors such as motion quality, sample efficiency, and the efficacy of sim-to-real transfer.

Prior research has investigated the impact of action-space design on robot learning. Studies by Aljalbout et al., Mart´ın-Mart´ın et al., and Varin et al. explored various action representations (e.g., joint-space, Cartesian-space, incremental, torque, velocity, position, impedance control) across different manipulation tasks, robot platforms, and learning algorithms. These works highlighted the influence of action space on learning dynamics, sample efficiency, and control performance. However, a notable gap in much of this prior work is the limited focus on vision-based observations and extensive evaluation in real-world settings through sim-to-real transfer. Many studies were conducted primarily in simulation or with state-based observations, which do not fully capture the complexities introduced by visual input, such as partial observability and perceptual noise.

Vision-based control is crucial for robots interacting with unstructured environments, providing rich sensory information. However, integrating vision introduces challenges that can significantly alter learning dynamics and real-world behavior, particularly when combined with sim-to-real transfer due to potential discrepancies in visual appearance and dynamics between simulated and real environments.

This paper extends the existing body of knowledge by specifically addressing the impact of action-space design in **vision-based robotic manipulation** with a strong emphasis on **sim-to-real transfer**. It systematically evaluates four common action-space representations—pose increment, pose velocity, joint position increment, and joint velocity—on a physical robot using policies trained in simulation with vision-based observations. By doing so, this research aims to provide insights into which action spaces are more robust and effective for vision-world deployment under visual sensing, thereby complementing and expanding upon simulation-focused or state-based observation studies. The work also builds on findings such as those by Aljalbout et al., who found joint-velocity control to be effective for sim-to-real without vision, by testing this hypothesis within a vision-based context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers a robust framework for robotic control, its deployment in real-world scenarios is significantly affected by the choice of action space. This design decision can influence motion smoothness, safety, and overall task performance. The paper highlights that previous investigations into action-space design have often overlooked scenarios involving vision-based observations and have frequently been limited to evaluations solely within simulation environments. Vision-based manipulation introduces complexities such as partial observability and perceptual noise, which can substantially alter learning dynamics and the transferability of policies to the real world. These challenges are particularly amplified during sim-to-real transfer, where even minor mismatches between simulation and reality in dynamics or sensing can expose sensitivities not apparent in idealized simulated settings.

Given these considerations, the key objectives of this study are:

1. **Evaluate the impact of action-space design in vision-based robotic manipulation:** To systematically investigate how different action space representations affect the learning and real-world performance of robotic manipulation policies that rely on visual sensory input.
2. **Benchmark common action-space representations:** To compare the performance of four specific and widely used action spaces: pose increment, pose velocity, joint position increment, and joint velocity.
3. **Assess sim-to-real transfer performance:** To train policies in simulation and deploy them on a physical robot to evaluate their zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capabilities across the selected action spaces.
4. **Analyze performance across diverse manipulation tasks:** To conduct evaluations on two distinct vision-based manipulation tasks—object picking (prehensile) and object pushing (non-prehensile)—to understand the task-dependent effectiveness of each action space.
5. **Provide practical guidance for RL practitioners:** To synthesize the findings into actionable recommendations for researchers and engineers when selecting action spaces for both simulated training and real-world deployment in vision-based robotic manipulation. The ultimate goal is to identify action spaces that contribute to smoother motions, enhanced safety, and improved final task performance in real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a sim-to-real transfer pipeline to evaluate four action-space representations on a physical Franka Emika Panda robot for two vision-based manipulation tasks.

**A. Reinforcement Learning Algorithm**
Policies are trained using **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, an on-policy deep RL method. PPO was selected due to its compatibility with vision-based actor-critic architectures, its widespread adoption and demonstrated effectiveness in prior sim-to-real robotic learning, and its computational efficiency afforded by leveraging a large number of parallel simulation environments. The specific PPO implementation used is based on a known framework.

**B. Dynamics-based Simulation**
The training of policies occurs in **MuJoCo**, a physics engine designed for model-based control that captures real-world dynamics. MuJoCo operates through a sequential process of actuator, dynamics, and integrator functions.
- **Actuator Models:** Two primary actuator models are considered:
 - **Position Actuator:** `f_ac_pos:= K_p(q' - q) - K_v v`, a PD-controller aiming for a target joint position `q'` (with target velocity `v'`=0).
 - **Velocity Actuator:** `f_ac_vel:= K_v(v' - v)`, a P-controller aiming for a target joint velocity `v'`.
 `K_p` and `K_v` are proportional and damping gains, respectively.
- **Dynamics Function:** Computes joint accelerations `a_t` from applied torque `τ` and other system forces (`a_t = M^-1(τ + J^Tf - c)`).
- **Integrator:** Advances the simulator state using the fast implicit-in-velocity integrator for a balance of stability and computational cost.

**C. Real Robot Control System**
A Franka Emika Panda robot is controlled via the **Franka ROS Interface** using its Python API. Two actuation interfaces are utilized:
- `set joint positions velocities`: Employs a joint impedance controller (`f_ac_imp:= τ_c + K_p(q' - q) + K_v(v' - v)`) which, when `v'`=0, approximates the PD position controller used in simulation.
- `set joint velocities`: Commands target joint velocities directly, inferred to correspond to a velocity P-control (`f_ac_vel:= τ_c + K_v(v' - v)`).

**D. Tasks**
Two vision-based manipulation tasks are evaluated (Figure 2):
- **PandaPickCuboid:** A prehensile task requiring the robot to reach, grasp, and lift a red cuboid using visual input from a wrist-mounted camera. It requires multi-stage execution.
- **PandaPushCuboid:** A non-prehensile task where the robot must push the same red cuboid across a white base to maximize displacement. This involves interactions with friction and mass.

**E. Reward Functions**
- **PandaPickCuboid:** A sparse reward function that incentivizes pose tracking, penalizes gripper-block distance, penalizes floor collisions, and provides a large reward for lift success (reaching a minimum height). A "max-sum" formulation ensures only improvements in cumulative reward are considered.
- **PandaPushCuboid:** Rewards planar displacement of the block within boundaries, penalizes distance between the block and end-effector, and penalizes end-effector contact with the floor.

**F. Observation Spaces**
The observation space for the RL agent comprises:
1. An RGB image (64 × 64 × 3).
2. A proprioception vector (scaled joint positions `q`, joint velocities `v`, and grasp status).
3. The end-effector height.
4. The previous action.
For `PandaPushCuboid`, grasp status is omitted.

**G. Action Spaces**
Four action spaces are benchmarked, all bounded to (-1,1) with a cycle time of `δt` = 40ms. The Franka Panda has 7 joints (7-DOF); `PandaPickCuboid` adds an 8th dimension for gripper control.
- **Joint Space Actions:**
 - **Joint Velocities (v):** Policy outputs target joint velocities, mapped to torque via a velocity actuator (P-controller).
 - **Joint Position Increments (δq):** Policy outputs joint position increments; `q_target = q + c.δq`, then passed to a position actuator (PD-controller).
- **Cartesian Space Actions:**
 - **Pose Increment (δx):** Policy outputs increments for end-effector position (3 components) and rotation (3 components), plus the 7th joint rotation. `x_target = x ⊕ c.δx`. This `x_target` is converted to `q_target` using an analytical inverse kinematics (IK) solver, then passed to a position actuator.
 - **Pose Velocity (ẋ):** Similar to `δx`, but `x_target` is used to approximate joint velocities (`v_target ≈ (q_target - q) / δt`), which are then passed to a velocity actuator.
- **Gripper Control:** For `PandaPickCuboid`, the last action vector component controls gripper state (open/close) using step-wise thresholds. Thresholds are tuned for real-world trials.

**H. Experimental Setup**
- **Real-World Setup:** Franka Emika Panda robot with a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 camera, manipulating a 4x4x6 cm red block.
- **Neural Networks:** Vision-based actor-critic architecture. A convolutional encoder processes RGB images, features are concatenated with vector observations. An MLP head for the critic outputs a scalar state-value, while the actor's MLP head parameterizes a Gaussian policy.
- **Domain Randomization:** Applied to improve sim-to-real transfer. Includes visual perturbations (camera pose, lighting, image properties, block pose, textures) and dynamic perturbations (Gaussian noise on joint readings, randomized stiffness and damping).
- **Actuator Tuning:** Stiffness and damping gains for the real robot's impedance control were reduced to minimize vibrations and prevent unstable motion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the four selected action spaces through simulation training and real-world zero-shot sim-to-real transfer experiments.

**A. PandaPickCuboid Task**
- **Simulation Training (Figure 3):** All action spaces demonstrated comparable final performance in simulation, achieving success rates in the range of 0.98 to 1.00. Pose increment (δx) generally achieved the shortest episode lengths (fastest completion), followed by joint position increment (δq).
- **Real-World Evaluation (Table III):**
 - **Joint Velocity (v)** achieved a 100% success rate with a median completion time of 3.58 s. It exhibited the lowest measured jerk and consistently avoided collisions.
 - **Joint Position Increments (δq)** also achieved a 100% success rate and the fastest median completion time of 2.12 s. However, stable deployment in the real world required careful tuning of impedance parameters.
 - **Pose Increment (δx)** showed degraded real-world performance with a 41.67% success rate. The policy frequently dragged the cuboid and required an imposed lower bound on end-effector position to mitigate this behavior.
 - **Pose Velocity (ẋ)** performed the worst in real-world trials, failing to complete the task in any trial (0% success rate) due to unstable configurations or exiting the workspace.
- **Discrepancy Analysis:** The simulation results did not fully translate to the real world for Cartesian action spaces. The authors attribute this to the reliance of Cartesian actions on an inverse kinematics (IK) mapping. Discrepancies in robot link parameters between simulation and reality propagate through the IK, leading to inaccurate joint targets. Additionally, IK-generated trajectories can be demanding or abrupt, which idealized simulated actuators can execute but real-world actuators, constrained by friction, latency, and imperfect gains, may struggle to track accurately, amplifying errors.

**B. PandaPushCuboid Task**
- **Simulation Training (Figure 5):** Joint position increment (δq) and pose increment (δx) achieved higher median episodic returns in simulation compared to joint velocity (v) and pose velocity (ẋ), potentially due to generating faster trajectories that result in greater block displacement. δx and ẋ exhibited some failed runs, possibly due to IK singularities.
- **Real-World Evaluation (Table III):**
 - **Joint Velocity (v)** achieved the largest median aggregate block displacement of 0.405 m. While producing the lowest jerk, it was observed to cause multiple collisions, primarily due to forceful contact with cuboid edges.
 - **Joint Position Increments (δq)** achieved the second-highest displacement (median 0.104 m).
 - **Pose Increment (δx)** and **Pose Velocity (ẋ)** performed poorly in terms of block displacement.
 - **Compliance:** δx and δq, which operate through the robot's tuned impedance controller, exhibited more compliant behavior and reduced collision severity during interaction. This compliance was attributed to the impedance controller's reduced stiffness and attenuation of excessive forces, rather than explicit contact avoidance by the policy.

**C. Training Results on Jerk and Collision (Figure 6)**
- During simulation training for both tasks, **joint velocity (v)** consistently yielded the lowest floor-collision counts at convergence and exhibited the smallest jerk.
- Jerk, approximated as the finite difference of acceleration, reflects step-to-step changes in torque. The lower jerk for joint velocity is plausibly explained by the fact that under position actuation, torque variation is sensitive to changes in position error scaled by proportional gains (`K_p`). Since `K_p` values were generally larger than `K_v` (velocity gains), position control could induce larger torque differences. Furthermore, position actuation directly exposes policy output fluctuations to the position error, which are then amplified. In contrast, velocity actuation produces target velocities, and joint positions evolve via integration (`q_t+1 = q_t + v'_t δt`), acting as a low-pass filter that attenuates high-frequency components and introduces temporal smoothing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation by systematically benchmarking action spaces specifically in the context of vision-based control and real-world deployment via sim-to-real transfer. This area has been less extensively explored than simulation-only or state-based observation studies, addressing a critical gap in understanding how action-space design impacts practical robotic applications.

The **key significance** lies in the provision of empirical evidence and practical guidance for RL practitioners. The finding that action-space representation significantly affects real-world sim-to-real performance for vision-based tasks underscores that choices made during the initial RL formulation directly impact the robustness, safety, and effectiveness of deployed robotic systems.

Specifically, the identification of **joint velocity (v) as a highly effective action space** for both vision-based picking and pushing tasks has practical implications. Its demonstrated performance in terms of 100% success rate for picking, highest displacement for pushing, and consistently low jerk (indicating smoother motion) positions it as a balanced and robust choice for real-world vision-based manipulation. This can guide researchers and engineers toward more reliable and performant control strategies, potentially reducing development time and improving the safety of real-world robot interactions.

The study also highlights the **challenges associated with Cartesian action spaces (pose increment and pose velocity)** in sim-to-real transfer. The observed sensitivity to inverse kinematics (IK) discrepancies and real-world actuator limitations provides valuable insights into why seemingly effective policies in simulation may fail on physical robots. This emphasizes the importance of carefully considering the entire control pipeline, including kinematic transformations and real-world hardware characteristics, when designing RL agents. The observation that joint-impedance controlled action spaces (like joint position increments and pose increments) offer more compliant behavior, despite other performance trade-offs, informs design choices where safety and interaction robustness are paramount.

The detailed analysis of jerk and collision metrics during training and real-world deployment provides a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics of different action spaces. The explanation for joint velocity's smoother trajectories (lower jerk) due to the implicit low-pass filtering effect of position integration is a valuable technical insight for system designers.

In the broader context, this work contributes to making RL more practical and reliable for complex robotic tasks. By offering clear recommendations on action-space selection, it can facilitate the development of more capable and adaptable robotic systems that can operate effectively in human-centric or unstructured environments. Future work extending these benchmarks to more diverse objects, complex manipulation scenarios (e.g., cluttered scenes, multi-object manipulation), and other robot embodiments (e.g., locomotion, humanoids) has the potential to further broaden the impact of these findings across the robotics community.
