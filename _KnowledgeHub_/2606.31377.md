# Stage-Transition Dense Reward Modeling for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Stage-Transition Dense Reward (STDR) framework extracts structured, dense reward signals from unstructured expert videos, enabling efficient training of reinforcement learning agents for long-horizon robotic manipulation. It consistently outperforms existing visual reward learning baselines across 14 simulation tasks and demonstrates successful real-world transfer, often matching or exceeding human-designed rewards.

## Problem

- Sparse and delayed reward signals hinder efficient exploration and training of RL agents in complex, long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks.
- Manual dense reward engineering is time-consuming, requires domain expertise, and leads to brittle, task-specific solutions.
- Existing demonstration-based visual reward learning methods struggle with the temporal logic of multi-stage tasks and are vulnerable to out-of-distribution (OOD) states, leading to reward hacking.

## Method

- The Stage-Transition Dense Reward (**STDR**) framework segments unstructured expert videos into semantic stages using a ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)== for pseudo-labeling.
- It employs a ==Stage Discriminator== (==LSTM== with ==VAE-based OOD detection== and grasping regulation) and an ==Intra-stage Progress Estimator== (==FiLM-modulated ResNet== with ==Mahalanobis-based OOD fusion==) to generate both coarse stage-transition and fine-grained within-stage rewards.
- The final reward combines these signals, incorporating robustness mechanisms to prevent reward hacking and ensure reliable feedback, even in out-of-distribution states or during critical interactions like grasping.

## Results

- STDR consistently achieved higher sample efficiency and final success rates across **14** robotic manipulation tasks in MetaWorld, ManiSkill, and Franka Kitchen simulations, outperforming Sparse, VIP, and LIV baselines.
- The framework matched or surpassed human-designed dense rewards in several high-difficulty tasks and demonstrated superior data utilization efficiency compared to offline imitation learning methods like Diffusion Policy.
- Real-world experiments with a Franka arm validated STDR's stability, increasing reward trend coupled with task progress, accurate failure detection, and robustness to environmental noise and interference.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing complex, long-horizon tasks into a sequence of semantically distinct stages, inferred from unstructured expert videos, provides a more effective and structured incentive landscape for RL agents.
> - Combining coarse stage-transition signals with fine-grained intra-stage progress estimates, dynamically conditioned on the current stage, is crucial for both global task progression and local efficient exploration.
> - Integrating explicit out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and task-specific physical state verification (e.g., grasping regulation) is vital for mitigating reward hacking and ensuring the reliability of learned reward functions in diverse or novel states.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in robotic manipulation; however, its application to complex, long-horizon tasks is often hindered by the challenge of sparse and delayed reward signals. In these scenarios, successful task completion may only provide a single positive reward at the very end, making efficient exploration difficult and requiring extensive interaction budgets.

A common approach to address sparse rewards is dense reward engineering, where researchers manually design intermediate feedback signals. However, this process is resource-intensive, task-specific, and lacks robustness to variations in environments or object configurations. This limitation has driven research into learning reward functions from expert demonstrations, aiming to reduce manual effort and provide a more scalable solution.

Current demonstration-based reward learning methods can be broadly categorized. Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) attempts to infer a reward function that rationalizes observed expert behavior. While conceptually powerful, IRL often faces challenges with high-dimensional and partially observable robotic states. More recent approaches, particularly in visual RL, focus on learning reward signals directly from visual expert observations. Examples include Value-Implicit Pre-training (VIP) and Language-Image Representations and Rewards (LIV), which often define rewards based on visual similarity to a goal state in a latent space. While effective for short-horizon tasks, these similarity-based methods may struggle with long-horizon manipulation where progress depends on a sequence of distinct subtasks and underlying temporal logic rather than purely geometric proximity.

Furthermore, a significant challenge across reward learning methods is vulnerability to out-of-distribution (OOD) states. When an agent deviates from the expert demonstration manifold, learned reward functions can exhibit spurious correlations, leading to "reward hacking" and unreliable policy optimization. Prior work has attempted to mitigate this through broadened supervision (e.g., sub-optimal trajectories) or constrained exploration (e.g., behavioral cloning). Despite these efforts, ensuring reliable reward evaluation under distribution shifts remains an active area of research.

This work, Stage-Transition Dense Reward (STDR), situates itself within this landscape by addressing two key limitations: the struggle of existing visual reward learning methods with the temporal logic of long-horizon tasks, and the need for robustness to OOD states and reward hacking. It aims to bridge the gap between unstructured expert video demonstrations and structured, dense reward signals, offering a scalable alternative to manual engineering while incorporating mechanisms for reliability beyond the expert data distribution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, vision-based framework that can automatically generate dense, structured reward signals for training Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents in long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. This framework aims to overcome the limitations of traditional sparse reward settings and the challenges associated with manual reward engineering.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations in robotic RL:

1. **Sparse and Delayed Rewards in Long-Horizon Tasks:** Many practical robotic manipulation problems, particularly those involving a sequence of distinct sub-goals (e.g., pick-place, assembly), provide only a terminal success signal. This "sparse reward" paradigm leads to inefficient exploration, especially in environments with high-dimensional observation and action spaces, often requiring an impractically large number of interactions for an agent to discover successful behaviors. Without intermediate feedback, learning frequently stalls or requires prohibitive computational resources.

2. **Cost and Brittleness of Manual Dense Reward Engineering:** A common solution to sparse rewards is to design dense reward functions that provide continuous guidance towards the goal. However, manually engineering these reward functions is notoriously time-consuming, requires extensive domain expertise, and results in highly task-specific solutions. Such handcrafted rewards are often brittle, meaning minor changes in environment parameters, object configurations, or task specifications can render them ineffective or require significant re-tuning. This manual bottleneck hinders the scalability of RL to a broader range of robotic tasks.

3. **Limitations of Existing Demonstration-Based Reward Learning:** While learning rewards from expert demonstrations offers a path to scalability, existing methods, particularly those leveraging visual observations, often fall short for long-horizon tasks. Many approaches rely on general similarity measures (e.g., embedding distance to a goal image) which may not capture the sequential, logical progression inherent in multi-stage manipulation. Moreover, readily available expert demonstrations, especially videos, are typically unstructured, lacking explicit subtask boundaries or progress annotations. Converting these raw, unstructured videos into a temporally coherent and reliable dense learning signal remains a significant challenge.

4. **Vulnerability to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) States and Reward Hacking:** Learned reward functions, like many data-driven models, are prone to vulnerabilities when the agent explores states significantly different from those seen in the expert demonstrations. This "distribution shift" can lead to a policy exploiting spurious correlations in the learned reward function, resulting in "reward hacking" where the agent receives high rewards without actually making meaningful progress or even completing the task. Ensuring that the learned reward remains meaningful and provides appropriate guidance even when the agent ventures into unseen states is crucial for robust training.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of STDR are:
- To leverage semantic understanding to infer a logical stage structure from unstructured expert video demonstrations.
- To deliver two complementary online learning signals: a coarse, goal-directed "stage-transition feedback" and a fine-grained, "within-stage progress feedback," thereby creating a temporally coherent and locally guiding incentive landscape.
- To integrate explicit robustness mechanisms, including an out-of-distribution (OOD) detection mechanism and a grasping regulation module, to prevent reward hacking and ensure reliable reward assignment when observations deviate from the expert distribution or involve critical interaction cues.

By achieving these objectives, STDR aims to provide a scalable, robust, and effective method for training RL agents in complex robotic manipulation scenarios, ultimately reducing the reliance on laborious manual reward design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Stage-Transition Dense Reward (STDR) framework is designed to distill structured, dense reward signals from unstructured expert videos for training reinforcement learning agents. It operates in two main phases: an offline pre-training phase to learn the reward model and an online phase where the pre-trained model guides RL agent training.

**A. Problem Formulation**

The robotic task is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) (S, A, P, R, γ), where S is the state space, A is the action space, P is the transition dynamics, R is the reward function, and γ is the discount factor. The objective is to find an optimal policy that maximizes the expected cumulative return. Given that environments typically provide a sparse reward R_sparse (1 for success, 0 otherwise), the STDR framework aims to learn a dense reward function from expert demonstrations to facilitate efficient policy optimization.

**B. Stage Segmentation**

To impose structure on long-horizon tasks, STDR first conceptualizes them as a sequence of semantically distinct stages.
- **VLM-based Pseudo-labeling:** A Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically VLM-Qwen3, is used to perform temporal segmentation on unstructured expert video demonstrations (D). Given a task description and an expert video, the VLM is prompted with sampled frames and outputs JSON-formatted segments, each indicating a stage number, start frame, and end frame.
- **Consistency and Robustness:** Monotonic, full-coverage boundaries are enforced, and ill-formed outputs are re-queried. A task-specific number of stages (K) is determined by aggregating VLM-predicted segment counts across multiple demonstrations (e.g., using mode or trimmed-mean) to mitigate sensitivity to individual segmentation errors.
- **Dataset Creation:** Each frame `o_t` in the expert videos is assigned a categorical pseudo-label `y_t ∈ {0, ..., K-1}` corresponding to its inferred stage, creating a labeled dataset `D_label = {(τ, y)}`.
- **Weak Supervision:** The VLM-based segmentation is treated as weak supervision for offline pre-training, not as an online oracle. The downstream models are trained to capture coarse, order-consistent logic under potentially noisy labels, with online inference relying on the learned reward model itself, augmented with OOD-aware gating and grasping verification.

**C. Reward Model Architecture**

The STDR reward model consists of two main components: a Stage Discriminator and an Intra-stage Progress Estimator.

- **a) Stage Discriminator:**
 - **Architecture:** An LSTM network processes a sliding window of observations `O_t = {o_{t-L+1}, ..., o_t}` to capture temporal dependencies, essential for detecting "transition points."
 - **Training:** Supervised by the `D_label` dataset, the discriminator learns to map each temporal window to a stage index `k_t ∈ {0, ..., K-1}`.
 - **Robustness Mechanisms:**
 - **OOD Detection (Auxiliary VAE):** A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is integrated to monitor the reconstruction error of `O_t`. To train the VAE for OOD detection, expert data is augmented with short random-policy rollouts as negative examples. If the reconstruction error exceeds a predefined threshold `δ`, the stage prediction `k_t` is conservatively reset to the initial stage (0), preventing spurious reward signals in unfamiliar states.
 - **Grasping Regulation Module (MLP-based):** For tasks involving grasping, an MLP acts as a visual verification gate. This module predicts stable grasping from visual features. A stage `k_t` is permitted to advance from a grasp-related stage to subsequent stages (e.g., moving or placing) only if this module predicts a stable grasp, aligning the reward with physical interaction logic.
 - **Stage Reward:** The stage discriminator outputs `R_stage = k_t / K`, providing discrete, monotonic progress signals across the task horizon.

- **b) Intra-stage Progress Estimator:**
 - **Purpose:** Provides fine-grained, dense guidance within each individual stage, estimating a normalized progress value `p_prog ∈`.
 - **Architecture (Progress Network Φ):** Consists of a ResNet backbone and a fusion MLP. It predicts `p_t = Φ(o_t, e_k)`, where `e_k` is an embedding of the current stage index.
 - **FiLM Modulation:** Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) dynamically conditions the visual backbone on `e_k`. This allows the single progress estimator to adaptively focus on stage-relevant visual cues across different stages.
 - **Training Objective (L_total):** The network Φ is trained with a composite loss over expert trajectories:
 - **Regression Loss (L_reg):** `L2` loss against an intra-stage local temporal target `˜p_t = t / (T-1)`, where `t` is the relative index within a sub-stage of length `T`.
 - **Pairwise Ranking Loss (L_rank):** Encourages `p_j > p_i` for any `i < j` within the same trajectory, enforcing temporal directionality.
 - **Isotonic Regularization (L_iso):** Penalizes `p_t > p_{t+1}` to encourage monotonicity in progress.
 - **Visual Consistency Loss (L_cons):** Uses dual-stream augmentation to enforce invariance to visual perturbations, comparing predictions from strongly (`o^s_t`) and weakly (`o^w_t`) augmented views of the same observation.
 - `L_total = λ_reg L_reg + λ_rank L_rank + λ_iso L_iso + λ_cons L_cons`, where `λ` are hyperparameters.
 - **OOD-aware Fusion:** To prevent reward hacking, a Mahalanobis distance-based OOD detector adaptively blends the regressed progress `p_reg` with a conservative retrieval-based estimate `p_near`.
 - `d_ood` is the Mahalanobis distance of the current observation's features to the expert manifold.
 - An adaptive mixing weight `g(d_ood) = σ(−κd_ood)` is used. When `d_ood` is low (near expert data), `g` is high, favoring `p_reg`. When `d_ood` is high (OOD), `g` is low, favoring `p_near`.
 - The fused estimate `ˆp_prog = g(d_ood) · p_reg + (1 − g(d_ood)) · p_near`.
 - If `d_ood > η` (a predefined distance threshold), `p_prog` is set to 0, enforcing an upper bound for significantly OOD states.
 - **Intra-stage Reward:** `R_prog = p_prog / K`. This scaling confines the dense signal within discrete stage points, ensuring stage transitions dominate the global incentive landscape.

- **Overall Reward:** The final synthesized reward `R_STDR` is `1.0` if the task is successful, and `R_stage + R_prog` otherwise. This structure aims to provide a globally monotonic incentive landscape.

**D. System Summary**

- **Reward Inference:** During online RL, the framework takes an observation sequence `O_t`. The LSTM-based Stage Discriminator infers `k_t`. This `k_t` generates a stage embedding `e_k` which modulates the FiLM layers of the Progress Estimator. The Estimator outputs `p_reg`, fused with `p_near` via the Mahalanobis OOD detector to get `p_prog`. Finally, `R_stage` and `R_prog` are combined to yield `R_STDR`. Unified hyperparameters for OOD detection (`η`, `κ`, `δ`) are used across tasks.
- **Training:** The STDR pre-training phase learns the structured reward function from expert videos. The second phase uses this pre-trained model to train an RL agent from scratch via online interaction, mapping visual progress into a dense, continuous feedback signal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the STDR framework through extensive experiments across 14 manipulation tasks on three simulation platforms (MetaWorld, ManiSkill, Franka Kitchen) and validates its transferability to a real-world robotic setup.

**A. Simulation Experiments**

The evaluation benchmarked STDR against Sparse, Human-Dense, VIP, and LIV reward baselines.

- **Overall Performance:** STDR consistently demonstrated improved sample efficiency and higher final success rates across all 14 tasks compared to the Sparse, VIP, and LIV baselines under identical visual-only observation settings.
- **Comparison to Human-Dense Rewards:** STDR matched or surpassed the performance of handcrafted Human-Dense rewards in several high-difficulty tasks, such as Pick-Place and Stick-Push in MetaWorld. This suggests that STDR can provide effective guidance without requiring expert domain knowledge for tuning.
- **Platform-Specific Analysis:**
 - **MetaWorld (8 tasks):** STDR exhibited rapid convergence, frequently achieving a 1.0 success rate within 1M to 2M steps for simpler tasks.
 - **ManiSkill (3 tasks):** In this high-precision environment, baseline methods like LIV and VIP showed negligible progress. STDR, however, maintained stable and superior performance, attributed to its temporal stage decomposition mechanism, indicating its reliability for fine-grained manipulation.
 - **Franka Kitchen (3 tasks):** STDR remained effective in complex scenes with cluttered backgrounds and diverse joint constraints, outperforming all baselines by successfully identifying key task transitions.
- **Data Efficiency and Performance Analysis (ManiSkill):** A specialized evaluation in ManiSkill compared STDR against an offline imitation learning method, Diffusion Policy (DP), using the same set of expert demonstrations. STDR achieved significantly higher success rates (e.g., 0.959 for PullCube, 0.943 for PushCube, 0.632 for PickCube) compared to DP (e.g., 0.120 for PullCube), LIV (e.g., 0.051 for PullCube), and VIP (0.000 for all), indicating better data utilization efficiency and generalization when combined with online RL. Human-Dense rewards achieved comparable or slightly higher performance in ManiSkill (e.g., 0.961 for PickCube).

**B. Real-World Evaluation**

Experiments on a physical Franka arm with an Intel RealSense camera were conducted using a limited set of real-world demonstrations.

- **Successful Executions:** Rewards assigned by STDR on successful task videos exhibited significant stability and an increasing trend that was closely coupled with task progress. In contrast, VIP and LIV showed signal plateaus in intermediate stages, lacking the distinct gradients needed for guiding action sequences.
- **Failure Detection:** On failure videos (e.g., object dropping), baseline methods often continued to provide erroneous positive feedback. STDR, however, accurately identified failure states and produced appropriately low reward signals, indicating alignment with physical execution logic.
- **Robustness:** These findings suggest STDR's robustness to background interference and sensor noise in real-world environments, stemming from its explicit temporal stage modeling.

**C. Ablation Study**

Ablation experiments investigated the contributions of STDR's core components.

- **Component-wise Ablation (Window-Open, Button-Press tasks):**
 - **Stage-Only (w/o Progress Estimator):** This variant, relying solely on discrete stage transitions, showed significant learning delay and higher variance. While it could eventually reach high success rates, its progress was slower, highlighting the necessity of continuous intra-stage guidance for efficient exploration.
 - **Progress-Only (w/o Stage Discriminator):** This variant, which eliminated explicit stage partitioning and relied on global progress estimation, experienced a noticeable decrease in the final success rate. This indicates that without a logical stage structure, the agent struggled to maintain the correct execution sequence in long-horizon tasks. The full STDR model achieved faster convergence and higher final success rates.
- **Impact of Grasping Regulation (Pick-Place, Coffee-Push tasks):**
 - **No Grasping (w/o Grasping Regulation):** Removing the MLP-based grasping discriminator led to "stage leakage," where the reward signal prematurely transitioned to the next stage before the object was securely grasped. This resulted in the agent often moving with an empty gripper or dropping the object mid-air. This validated the importance of the physical state discriminator for aligning the incentive landscape with actual task logic.

In summary, the results indicate that STDR provides an effective and robust solution for dense reward learning in long-horizon robotic manipulation, outperforming representation-based baselines and, in several cases, matching or exceeding human-engineered rewards, with strong validation in both simulated and real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Stage-Transition Dense Reward (STDR) framework represents a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation, addressing several fundamental challenges and offering a pathway toward more autonomous and scalable robot learning.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Addresses the Sparse Reward Bottleneck:** STDR offers a principled and automated solution to the pervasive problem of sparse and delayed rewards in long-horizon robotic tasks. By distilling structured, dense feedback from unstructured expert videos, it reduces the need for extensive environment interactions, thereby improving sample efficiency and making complex tasks more tractable for RL agents.
2. **Reduces Manual Reward Engineering:** The framework largely eliminates the laborious, time-consuming, and brittle process of manually designing dense reward functions. This represents a substantial practical advantage, as handcrafted rewards are notoriously task-specific and require deep domain expertise, limiting the scalability of RL applications. STDR's ability to match or surpass human-designed rewards in several challenging tasks highlights its potential to democratize dense reward provision.
3. **Introduces Structured Guidance for Long-Horizon Tasks:** By conceptualizing complex tasks as a sequence of functional stages and providing both stage-transition and within-stage progress feedback, STDR offers a more temporally coherent and fine-grained incentive landscape. This hierarchical reward structure guides the agent through the task's logical progression, which is crucial for multi-step manipulation problems where simple similarity-based rewards may fail.
4. **Enhances Robustness and Reliability:** The integration of OOD detection mechanisms (VAE and Mahalanobis distance) and a task-specific grasping regulation module significantly improves the robustness of the learned reward function. These components prevent reward hacking in unfamiliar states and ensure that reward signals align with actual physical interaction logic, particularly for critical actions like grasping. This robustness is vital for deploying RL agents in dynamic and potentially uncertain real-world environments.
5. **Demonstrated Real-World Transferability:** Successful validation on a physical robot platform indicates STDR's practical feasibility and robustness to real-world visual complexities, such as sensor noise and background interference. This sim-to-real transferability is a critical step towards deploying learned policies on physical hardware.
6. **Leverages Vision-Language Models for Automation:** The use of VLMs for initial task segmentation from unstructured videos demonstrates an effective way to leverage recent advances in foundation models for automating early stages of RL pipeline development, further reducing manual intervention.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robot Skill Acquisition:** By providing robust and dense reward signals, STDR could significantly accelerate the development and learning of new manipulation skills for robots, reducing the time and resources required for training.
- **Broader Application of RL in Robotics:** The framework's ability to handle long-horizon tasks and unstructured video demonstrations makes RL more accessible for a wider range of industrial, service, and research robotics applications that currently face reward design challenges.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** STDR's hierarchical reward structure and robustness mechanisms could serve as a foundation for further research into more complex task structures, learning from imperfect demonstrations, and integrating multi-modal sensing for even richer reward signals.
- **Reduced Human Supervision:** By automating the reward learning process, STDR moves towards autonomous robotic learning with minimal human intervention, aligning with the long-term goal of general-purpose intelligent robots.

Despite its achievements, the authors acknowledge limitations, such as the current reliance on visual observations only, the assumption of consistent stage sequences, and the need for further investigation into direct physical robot training. Addressing these could further amplify STDR's impact and broaden its applicability.
