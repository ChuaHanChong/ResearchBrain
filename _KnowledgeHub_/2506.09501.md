# Understanding and Mitigating Numerical Sources of Nondeterminism in LLM Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Rice University, University of Minnesota, and Adobe Inc. systematically investigated numerical precision as a source of non-determinism in Large Language Model (LLM) inference. They demonstrated that 16-bit precision (BF16) leads to substantial variability in outputs and performance, even under greedy decoding, and proposed LayerCast, a hybrid precision inference pipeline that achieves FP32-level reproducibility with reduced memory usage.

## Problem

- LLM inference, even in supposedly deterministic settings like greedy decoding with fixed random seeds, often produces inconsistent outputs across different hardware and system configurations.
- Existing LLM evaluation practices frequently overlook the impact of numerical precision, leading to unreliable benchmark scores and difficulties in distinguishing true model improvements from random variations.
- Inconsistent LLM outputs due to subtle numerical variations pose risks to the reliability, safety, and fairness of LLMs deployed in critical real-world applications.

## Method

- Conducted a comprehensive empirical study across various LLMs, benchmarks, ==hardware configurations== (GPU types, counts, batch sizes), and precision settings (BF16, FP16, FP32).
- Analyzed the underlying numerical mechanisms, identifying the non-associative nature of floating-point arithmetic and minimal logit gaps in reasoning models as key drivers of non-determinism.
- Developed LayerCast, a lightweight hybrid precision inference pipeline that stores model weights in BF16 but performs all computations in FP32, aiming for numerical stability with memory efficiency.

## Results

- BF16 precision introduced up to **9%** variation in accuracy and up to 9,000 tokens difference in response length for reasoning models across different system configurations, while FP32 consistently yielded near-perfect reproducibility.
- The proposed LayerCast method achieved reproducibility levels nearly identical to full FP32 inference (e.g., zero standard deviation in accuracy) while reducing memory usage by approximately **34%** compared to FP32.
- The observed reproducibility challenges generalized across larger models (Qwen3-**32B**), diverse reasoning tasks (GPQA Diamond), and different inference backends (vLLM and HuggingFace Transformers), confirming the broad applicability of the findings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Greedy decoding with 16-bit precision (BF16) is not deterministic, leading to notable variance in LLM outputs and performance, particularly for reasoning models where top-1 and top-2 token probabilities are very close.
> - Numerical precision, specifically the non-associativity of floating-point operations in parallel computing, is a fundamental and often overlooked source of non-reproducibility in LLM inference.
> - Runtime configurations such as GPU count, batch size, and specific GPU architecture can significantly amplify numerical non-determinism, especially when using lower precision formats like BF16.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become ubiquitous, driving significant advancements across diverse applications. However, the rapid pace of development has outstripped the maturity of evaluation and benchmarking practices. A core assumption underlying much of LLM research is that benchmark scores are accurate and reproducible, especially when using "deterministic" settings like greedy decoding with a fixed random seed. This paper critically challenges this assumption by demonstrating that LLM inference, even under these supposedly deterministic conditions, is highly sensitive to subtle changes in hardware and system configurations.

Prior work has acknowledged various sources of non-determinism in LLM inference, including prompt formatting, decoding parameters (temperature, top-p), and random seed settings. Some studies have also touched upon hardware and software configurations, and even the non-determinism of deep learning training in general. Notably, recent research has started to investigate the impact of numerical precision on specific aspects like mathematical reasoning. However, this paper distinguishes itself by being the "first systematic investigation into how numerical precision affects reproducibility in LLM inference." It provides a comprehensive, quantitative analysis of this overlooked factor across a wide range of hardware, software, and precision settings.

The problem highlighted by this paper—that even identical LLM inferences can yield different results due to numerical precision—undermines the very foundation of reliable LLM evaluation. It directly impacts the trustworthiness of reported benchmark scores, complicates distinguishing genuine model improvements from mere statistical noise, and poses significant challenges for ensuring reliability, safety, and fairness in deployed LLM applications. The paper thus fills a critical gap in the understanding of LLM stability, shifting the focus from higher-level architectural or algorithmic factors to the fundamental numerical computations underlying LLM inference. It builds upon previous acknowledgments of non-determinism (e.g., from PyTorch and HuggingFace documentation) by providing a deep dive into its root cause in floating-point arithmetic.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate and quantify the impact of numerical precision on the reproducibility of Large Language Model (LLM) inference. The authors aim to:

- **Demonstrate and Quantify Reproducibility Fragility:** Show that LLM performance and outputs are surprisingly fragile, varying significantly with system configurations like evaluation batch size, GPU count, and GPU version, even under "deterministic" greedy decoding.
- **Trace the Root Cause:** Identify the fundamental reason for this variability, which they pinpoint to the non-associative nature of floating-point arithmetic under limited numerical precision.
- **Systematically Investigate Numerical Precision:** Conduct controlled experiments across diverse hardware, software, and precision settings (BF16, FP16, FP32) to quantify when and how model outputs diverge.
- **Highlight Neglected Factors:** Bring attention to floating-point precision as a critical, yet often overlooked, factor in current LLM evaluation practices.
- **Develop a Mitigating Solution:** Propose and validate a lightweight inference pipeline, LayerCast, designed to balance memory efficiency with numerical stability to improve reproducibility.
- **Provide Practical Suggestions:** Offer actionable recommendations to the LLM community for achieving more reproducible results in both greedy decoding and random sampling scenarios.

The motivation for this work stems from the increasing integration of LLMs into critical real-world applications. The impressive progress in LLM capabilities relies heavily on accurate and reproducible benchmark scores. If these scores are not reliably reproducible, distinguishing true advancements from random variations becomes impossible, hindering genuine progress. Furthermore, for applications demanding high reliability (e.g., healthcare, automated coding), inconsistent outputs from seemingly identical inputs due to numerical variations are unacceptable and pose significant risks. The authors recognize that current evaluation practices often neglect numerical precision, leading to potentially misleading conclusions and an underestimation of actual model uncertainty. Addressing these issues is crucial for fostering trust, ensuring fair comparisons, and enabling responsible deployment of LLM technologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopt a comprehensive empirical approach, combining extensive experimentation with detailed analysis of numerical phenomena and a practical solution.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Models:** Four recent LLMs were evaluated: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (reasoning models), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (non-reasoning models). Additional experiments included Qwen3-32B (larger model) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B on GPQA Diamond (diverse reasoning task) to verify generalizability.
- **Benchmarks:** Five commonly used LLM evaluation benchmarks were utilized: AIME’24, MATH500 (math reasoning), LiveCodeBench-Easy, LiveCodeBench-Medium, and LiveCodeBench-Hard (code generation). GPQA Diamond was used for generalizability.
- **Inference Backend:** Primarily vLLM, with verification experiments using HuggingFace Transformers to ensure findings are not backend-specific.
- **Hardware Configurations:** To simulate diverse deployment environments, experiments covered 12 different runtime configurations:
 - **GPU Types:** NVIDIA L40S and A100.
 - **GPU Counts (Tensor Parallel Size):** 2 and 4 GPUs.
 - **Evaluation Batch Sizes:** 8, 16, and 32.
- **Numerical Precision Settings:** BF16, FP16, and FP32.
- **Decoding Strategies:** Both greedy decoding (temperature=0, single run assumed deterministic) and random sampling (temperature=0.7, top-p=0.95, multiple runs for Pass@K).

**Metrics for Quantifying Reproducibility:**
- **For Greedy Decoding:**
 - **Std@Acc (Standard deviation of accuracy):** Measures the stability of accuracy across the 12 runtime configurations.
 - **Avg_Std@Output_Length (Average standard deviation of output length):** Quantifies variability in generated response length across configurations.
 - **Div_Index (Divergence index):** The token position at which outputs diverge between runs for the same question. A higher index means greater consistency.
 - **Avg_Std@Top1_Prob (Average standard deviation of top-1 token prediction probability):** Measures the numerical fluctuation in predicted probabilities for the most likely token before divergence.
- **For Random Sampling:**
 - **Pass@1 (Mean Accuracy):** The average accuracy over multiple independent runs (16 or 64 for AIME’24, 4 for MATH500).
 - **Standard Deviation of Pass@1:** Calculated across the 6 system configurations (3 batch sizes × 2 GPU counts) to assess reproducibility of the averaged metric itself.

**Analysis of Numerical Errors:**
- The paper delves into the specifics of floating-point arithmetic (FP32, FP16, BF16 formats, mantissa/exponent bits) and the non-associativity of addition.
- It illustrates how rounding errors accumulate and how different execution orders in parallel GPU computations (due to factors like continuous batching, operator implementations, collective operations, and tensor parallelism) can lead to different final results, especially when top-1 and top-2 token probabilities are very close.

**Proposed Solution: LayerCast:**
- **Hybrid Precision Approach:** LayerCast aims to combine the memory efficiency of 16-bit precision with the numerical stability of FP32.
- **Workflow:**
 1. Model parameters are initially loaded in FP32.
 2. Linear layer weights and biases are explicitly cast to BF16 for storage, reducing memory footprint.
 3. During inference, these weights are "just-in-time" upcasted back to FP32 for all computations (matrix multiplications, attention scores, norms).
- **Implementation:** Released as a patch to vLLM, requiring minimal code changes.

This methodology provides a robust framework for dissecting the problem, quantifying its extent, understanding its underlying mechanisms, and offering a practical, validated solution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several critical findings concerning the impact of numerical precision on LLM inference reproducibility:

#### 4.1. Greedy Decoding is Not Deterministic with BF16

- **Significant Variance in Accuracy and Length:** Contrary to common belief, greedy decoding with BF16 precision does not guarantee deterministic outputs. For reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, BF16 introduced up to 9% variation in accuracy and up to 9,000 tokens difference in response length across varying GPU counts, types, and batch sizes (Table 3, 4, Figure 1).
- **Precision Matters:** FP32 consistently achieved near-perfect reproducibility (negligible Std@Acc and Avg_Std@Output_Length), while FP16 showed moderate variability, and BF16 exhibited substantial instability across all benchmarks and models (Tables 3, 4, 6, 7).
- **Minimal Logit Gaps as Root Cause:** Analysis revealed that for reasoning models, the probability differences between the top-1 and top-2 competing tokens are often minimal (Figure 3). These small gaps make token selection highly sensitive to minute numerical fluctuations introduced by finite precision.
- **Higher Probability Variance in BF16:** BF16 showed significantly higher average standard deviation in top-1 token prediction probabilities (Avg_Std@Top1_Prob) compared to FP16 and FP32 (Figure 4). This increased variance, due to BF16's limited mantissa bits, raises the likelihood of token flips.
- **Earlier and More Frequent Divergence in BF16:** With BF16, divergence (Div_Index) frequently occurred early in the generation process, affecting over 90% of examples. FP32, in contrast, resulted in very few divergent examples (around 2.2%), and when divergence did occur, it was much later in the sequence (Figure 5, Table 9).

#### 4.2. Random Sampling Also Suffers from Reproducibility Issues

- **Additional Variance Source:** Even in random sampling, numerical precision introduced an additional source of variance beyond the intended stochasticity. Lower-precision formats like BF16 tended to produce higher standard deviations in Pass@1 performance across different system configurations (Table 5).
- **Increased Runs Needed for BF16:** This implies that researchers using BF16 for sampling-based evaluations may need substantially more runs to achieve the same statistical confidence as with higher-precision formats, incurring unacknowledged computational overhead.

#### 4.3. Impact of Runtime Configurations (Ablation Studies)

- **GPU Count:** Configurations with more GPUs (e.g., 4 vs. 2) tended to exhibit higher probability variation (Avg_Std@Top1_Prob), especially in BF16. This is attributed to increased parallel computation leading to more varied floating-point operation orderings and rounding errors (Figure 6a, Table 13).
- **Batch Size:** Counter-intuitively, smaller batch sizes often produced higher variance in token probabilities. Larger batches benefited from optimized CUDA kernels that might limit error accumulation (Figure 6b, Table 15).
- **GPU Architecture:** A100 GPUs generally showed slightly higher probability variance than L40S under identical configurations, likely due to differences in hardware-level floating-point implementations and memory hierarchies (Figure 6c, Table 16). All these effects were most pronounced under BF16.

#### 4.4. LayerCast Achieves Near-Perfect Determinism with Memory Efficiency

- **FP32-level Stability:** The proposed LayerCast method, which stores weights in BF16 but performs all computations in FP32, achieved reproducibility levels nearly identical to full FP32 inference (Std@Acc of 0, minimal Div_Percent and high Div_Index) (Figure 8, Tables 25, 26).
- **Memory Efficiency:** LayerCast significantly reduced memory usage (e.g., 34% reduction) compared to full FP32, making it a practical solution, especially for KV cache in long-context scenarios.

#### 4.5. Generalizability of Findings

- The reproducibility challenges were confirmed on a larger model (Qwen3-32B on AIME’24, Table 10) and a diverse reasoning task (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B on GPQA Diamond, Table 11).
- Verification using the HuggingFace Transformers backend (without vLLM) showed similar reproducibility issues, confirming that the phenomenon is fundamental to GPU hardware and floating-point arithmetic, not specific to an inference backend (Table 12).

In summary, the core finding is that numerical precision, especially with BF16, is a critical and often overlooked source of non-determinism in LLM inference, profoundly affecting the reproducibility of evaluation results. Reasoning models are particularly vulnerable due to minimal logit gaps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the entire Large Language Model ecosystem, from fundamental research to practical deployment:

- **Rethinking LLM Benchmarking and Evaluation:** The most immediate impact is a challenge to the perceived reliability of current LLM benchmarks. The finding that even greedy decoding is non-deterministic under common 16-bit precision (BF16) suggests that many reported performance scores might be unreliable or not truly comparable across different hardware setups. This necessitates a re-evaluation of current benchmarking practices, urging researchers to explicitly account for numerical precision and its effects.
- **Enhanced Reproducibility in Research:** For academic and industrial research, the findings highlight the critical importance of specifying and controlling numerical precision settings. It provides concrete evidence and guidance for why reproducibility flags (like `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms`) often fall short. The paper advocates for using FP32 in greedy decoding for robust results or increasing the number of runs significantly for random sampling, along with reporting standard deviations and error bars that truly reflect uncertainty.
- **Improved Trust and Reliability in LLM Deployment:** In real-world applications where LLMs are used for critical tasks (e.g., medical diagnostics, financial analysis, code generation), inconsistent outputs from identical inputs are unacceptable. The work underscores that numerical non-determinism can lead to unexpected behaviors, impacting safety, fairness, and overall trustworthiness. Solutions like LayerCast offer a pathway to deploy LLMs that are both performant and reliably deterministic.
- **Addressing the "Black Box" Nature of LLMs:** By tracing variability to fundamental numerical operations, the paper provides a more granular understanding of LLM behavior, moving beyond purely algorithmic or architectural explanations. This contributes to demystifying some aspects of LLM unpredictability.
- **Informing Hardware and Software Development:** The ablation studies revealing the impact of GPU count, batch size, and even specific GPU architectures provide valuable feedback for hardware designers and software framework developers. It suggests areas where further optimizations or standardization in floating-point operations and kernel implementations could improve determinism. Batch-invariant operations are acknowledged, but the paper shows their limitations beyond batch dimension variations.
- **Optimized Inference Solutions:** The LayerCast proposal is a practical and impactful contribution. It offers a crucial balance between memory efficiency (critical for deploying large models, especially with long contexts) and the absolute need for deterministic, reproducible results. Its release as a vLLM patch facilitates immediate adoption by the community, offering a concrete step towards more reliable LLM inference without prohibitive computational costs.
- **Specific Relevance for Reasoning Models:** The finding that reasoning models are particularly vulnerable due to minimal logit gaps between competing tokens is highly significant. This suggests that the complex, multi-step thought processes in these models amplify the effects of small numerical errors, leading to divergent "chains of thought" and ultimately affecting final accuracy. This has implications for how reasoning abilities are evaluated and improved.

In essence, this paper serves as a wake-up call, demonstrating that foundational numerical computing principles cannot be overlooked in the era of LLMs. It provides both a rigorous analysis of a significant problem and a practical path forward, paving the way for more robust, trustworthy, and truly reproducible LLM research and deployment.
