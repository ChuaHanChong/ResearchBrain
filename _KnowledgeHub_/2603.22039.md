# RAFL: Generalizable Sim-to-Real of Soft Robots with Residual Acceleration Field Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Duke University developed RAFL, a framework that augments differentiable soft robot simulators with a generalizable, element-level residual acceleration field to bridge the sim-to-real gap. The system consistently improves simulation accuracy across varying robot morphologies and mesh discretizations, outperforming traditional system identification and global residual learning methods in zero-shot generalization settings.

## Problem

- The 'sim-to-real gap' limits the accuracy of differentiable simulators for soft robots, particularly when robot morphology is a design variable for optimization.
- Traditional system identification methods often yield material parameters that do not generalize to new geometries, requiring costly recalibration for each new design.
- Existing global residual physics learning approaches are dependent on specific mesh topologies and global state representations, which restricts their transferability across different robot shapes and discretizations.

## Method

![[2603.22039-method.png]]
*The RAFL training and generalization pipeline. The model learns from sparse markers on a canonical beam and generalizes to unseen shapes like fish-tails and different mesh densities.*

- **RAFL** augments a base ==differentiable simulator== with a learned, ==element-level corrective dynamics field== that predicts a ==continuous residual acceleration== at Gaussian quadrature points.
- The framework uses a ==Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)== that takes ==physically structured local deformation and velocity gradients== as input, ensuring ==rotational equivariance== and a ==zero residual at rest==.
- The model is trained ==end-to-end through the differentiable simulator==, optimizing parameters to minimize the mean squared error between simulated and observed ==sparse marker trajectories==.

## Results

- In sim-to-sim experiments, **RAFL** consistently reduced simulation error across anisotropically scaled and non-scaled geometries, and different discretizations, outperforming a global Supervised Residual Learning (SRL) baseline.
- In sim-to-real transfer, RAFL showed consistent accuracy improvements over the original simulator in **every** scaled and non-scaled case, achieving the lowest error in most generalization settings while **system identification baselines often exhibited negative transfer**.
- RAFL supports **continual refinement**, allowing for substantial further improvements when fine-tuned on new morphologies after zero-shot transfer, demonstrating its adaptability and ability to accumulate accuracy across designs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the learned correction from global mesh structure by focusing on element-level residual acceleration enables transferability across diverse soft robot morphologies and discretizations.
> - Employing physically structured input features (e.g., principal stretches, co-rotated strain rates) and enforcing constraints like rotational equivariance and zero residual at rest enhances generalizability and physical plausibility.
> - The learned residual forces induced by RAFL are distributed more uniformly throughout the body, reducing sensitivity to specific vertex locations compared to localized forces from global-state residual models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of soft robotics increasingly leverages differentiable simulators for tasks such as optimizing control, material parameters, and morphology. These simulators facilitate gradient-based optimization, allowing for rapid iteration and design exploration without the need for physical fabrication. A significant challenge in this domain is the "sim-to-real gap," which refers to discrepancies between simulated and real-world robot behavior. This gap becomes particularly problematic when the geometry of the robot is a design variable, as simulations calibrated for one morphology may not accurately predict the behavior of another.

Existing approaches to bridge the sim-to-real gap include system identification and residual physics learning. System identification methods aim to calibrate simulators by fitting global material parameters to observed data from physical systems. However, these methods often encounter limitations: identified parameters may not generalize to new geometries, as they can absorb geometry-dependent effects due to constitutive model misspecification or sparse observations. This necessitates repeated recalibration for each new design, which is resource-intensive.

Another strategy involves using more expressive constitutive models, which can improve simulation accuracy but often at a substantial increase in computational cost, making them impractical for large-scale differentiable optimization. Furthermore, these models may not account for all sources of sim-to-real discrepancy, such as unmodeled internal damping.

Residual physics learning offers an alternative by augmenting a base simulator with learned corrective dynamics. While these methods have shown promise in improving accuracy, existing formulations typically operate on global state representations, making their learned corrections dependent on specific mesh topologies and fixed degrees of freedom. This inherent dependence limits their transferability across geometries and discretizations, meaning a residual model trained on one shape may not be applicable to another.

This research introduces a framework that positions itself by addressing the shortcomings of both system identification and existing global residual models. It aims to develop a generalizable, element-level corrective dynamics field that is agnostic to global mesh topology and discretization. By focusing on local features and maintaining the structure of the base simulator, this work seeks to provide a more robust and scalable solution for sim-to-real transfer, especially crucial for morphology optimization in soft robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the accuracy and generalizability of differentiable simulators for soft robots, specifically addressing the challenges posed by the "sim-to-real gap" when robot morphology is a design variable. The authors articulate several motivations and specific objectives to achieve this goal:

**Motivation for Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap in Morphology Optimization:**
- Differentiable simulators are instrumental for inverse problems in soft robotics, allowing gradient-based optimization of control, material parameters, and morphology. When geometry is a design variable, simulation enables rapid iteration and accelerates convergence toward optimized designs.
- However, the accuracy of these simulators in predicting real-world behavior is limited by discrepancies between the simulated and physical systems.
- This limitation is particularly pronounced in morphology optimization, where parameters identified for one geometry often fail to generalize to new designs, leading to degraded simulation accuracy.

**Limitations of Current Approaches Driving the Research:**
- **System Identification (SysID):** While common for calibration, SysID often results in identified parameters that absorb geometry-dependent effects rather than reflecting intrinsic material properties. This occurs when constitutive models are misspecified or observations are sparse, making the problem underdetermined. Consequently, parameters identified on one morphology do not reliably transfer to new geometries, requiring repetitive and costly re-tuning during morphology exploration.
- **Global Residual Physics Learning:** Existing residual learning formulations typically operate on global state representations, tying the learned correction to a specific mesh topology or fixed degrees of freedom. This structural dependence restricts their transferability across different geometries and discretizations, limiting their applicability for robust sim-to-real transfer that can generalize to various morphologies.
- **Expressive Constitutive Models:** Increasing the expressiveness of constitutive models to improve accuracy often leads to substantially higher computational costs, which can hinder their practicality within large-scale differentiable optimization loops.

**Specific Objectives of the RAFL Framework:**
- **Develop a Transferable Corrective Dynamics Field:** The core objective is to augment a base simulator with a learned corrective dynamics field that can transfer across different soft robot morphologies and mesh discretizations.
- **Decouple Correction from Mesh Structure:** The framework aims to achieve this transferability by shifting the level of learning from global state representations to shared, element-level features that are locally defined and agnostic to discretization. This ensures the learned correction is not bound to a particular mesh or global state dimension.
- **Enable End-to-End Differentiable Training:** The model is designed to be trained end-to-end through a differentiable simulator using sparse marker observations, allowing for direct optimization based on observed physical behavior.
- **Demonstrate Zero-Shot Generalization:** A key objective is to show that a model trained on a single canonical geometry can provide consistent improvements on unseen morphologies without further training or adaptation.
- **Support Continual Refinement:** The framework aims to allow for the continual refinement of the learned residual field as new morphologies are encountered, enabling simulation accuracy to accumulate across designs rather than requiring a reset.
- **Provide a Scalable Pathway:** Ultimately, the research seeks to offer a scalable pathway toward morphology-robust differentiable simulation, where candidate geometries can be rapidly evaluated using a reusable and adaptable correction model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Residual Acceleration Field Learning (RAFL) framework augments a base differentiable simulator with a learned, element-level corrective dynamics field. The methodology is designed to decouple this correction from mesh structure, allowing for generalization across various geometries and discretizations.

**Base Simulation Environment:**
- Soft bodies are simulated using a corotational linear elastic material model implemented in DiffPD, a differentiable projective dynamics simulator. This model provides a stable and efficiently differentiable base.
- Bodies are discretized as hexahedral meshes, with the state `s_t` at time `t` comprising vertex positions `q_t` and velocities `v_t`.
- The simulator advances the system by solving equations that incorporate internal elastic forces `f_int(q_t+1)` and external forces `f_ext_t` (e.g., gravity), using a mass matrix `M`.
- The material parameters for the base simulator were set to a density ρ = 1070 kg m⁻³, Young’s modulus E = 215 kPa, and Poisson’s ratio ν = 0.45, reflecting a Smooth-On Dragon Skin 10 silicone.

**Residual Model Formulation:**
- RAFL introduces a residual model `f̂_res(φ_t; Θ)` which is added to the external forces in the simulation update step: `s_t+1 = SIM(s_t, f_ext_t + f̂_res(φ_t; Θ))`.
- Instead of directly learning nodal forces (which are topology-dependent), RAFL predicts a continuous *residual acceleration field* `â_res(d_t,e; Θ)` at each 8-point Gaussian quadrature sample `e` within an element.
- This element-level acceleration is then scaled by the element mass `m_e` and accumulated via standard Finite Element Method (FEM) quadrature using shape functions `N_e` to produce the total residual force `f̂_res`.

**Feature Engineering and Network Architecture:**
- The neural network for `â_res` takes as input a 34-dimensional feature vector `d_t,e` for each quadrature sample. These features are derived from local deformation and velocity gradients.
- Key features include: principal stretches, the Right Cauchy Green Tensor (centered by identity matrix), determinant of the deformation gradient (centered by 1), strain rates (co-rotated to rest frame), volumetric and shear strain rates, spin rates, vorticity magnitude, and co-rotated external accelerations (e.g., gravity). These features are designed to be physically structured.
- The neural network is a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with 4 layers and 64 hidden units per layer.
- **Structural Constraints:**
 - **Rotational Equivariance:** The rotation component `R_e` is extracted from the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient `F_e`. Tensor-valued features are co-rotated into the local rest frame before being fed into the network, and the predicted residual acceleration is rotated back to the spatial frame using `R_e`.
 - **Zero Residual at Rest:** Bias terms are removed from the network, and all input features are centered such that they vanish in a stationary, undeformed configuration under zero external loading. This enforces a physically plausible behavior where no residual correction is applied if the system is at rest and perfectly modeled.

**Training Process:**
- RAFL is trained end-to-end through the differentiable simulator. The network parameters `Θ` are optimized to minimize the mean squared error between simulated and observed sparse marker trajectories over multiple trajectories and time steps.
- The loss function includes regularization terms for the magnitude of the predicted residual forces (`λ_1`) and L2 weight decay on network parameters (`λ_2`).
- Adam optimizer is used for training, typically for 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation trajectories.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
- **Supervised Residual Learning (SRL):** This baseline (from Gao et al.) models residual nodal forces directly from the global state using a single large neural network. Its training is a two-step process: first, per-step optimization to compute target residual forces, then training the network to predict these targets. It is topology-dependent, requiring a fixed mesh.
- **System Identification (SysID):** This baseline optimizes the Young’s modulus `E` and Poisson’s ratio `ν` of the base simulator to minimize trajectory reconstruction error. Variants include optimizing both parameters or only `E` with a fixed `ν`.

**Experimental Settings and Evaluation:**
- **Sim-to-Sim:** The framework was first evaluated by matching a "target" simulator with altered material parameters (E=264kPa, ν=0.499). Full vertex states were available. Generalization was tested across anisotropically scaled fixed-topology meshes (longer/shorter/thicker/thinner beams) and genuinely different shapes and discretizations (non-scaled beams, crude/finer fish-tail meshes). Two passive motion regimes were used: vibrating beams (released with tip weight) and twisted beams (released from initial twist angles). Error was measured as mean squared vertex position error (`E_q`).
- **Sim-to-Real:** Sparse physical markers (32 markers) tracked from real cantilever beam experiments (vibrating under tip weights) were used. The observation operator `x(s_t)` was based on interpolated marker positions. Error was measured as mean squared marker position error (`E_x`). This setting evaluated cross-shape generalization (trained on one morphology, tested on others) and included qualitative visualizations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RAFL was conducted in both sim-to-sim and sim-to-real settings, comparing its performance against a Supervised Residual Learning (SRL) baseline and traditional System Identification (SysID).

**A. Sim-to-Sim Transfer:**
- **Canonical Beam (Training Geometry):** Both RAFL and SRL significantly reduced simulation error compared to the original simulator. SRL achieved the lowest error on the training geometry, while RAFL demonstrated comparable performance despite its enforced locality constraints.
- **Scaled Geometries (Fixed Topology, Anisotropic Scaling):** RAFL exhibited robust performance, achieving the lowest error in most cases. In contrast, SRL occasionally showed strong degradations, with error increasing significantly in certain scenarios (e.g., on the thinner scaled vibrating beam, SRL error rose to 17.644 mm, while RAFL reduced it to 1.358 mm).
- **Non-Scaled Geometries and Different Discretizations (Varying Element Count, Different Shapes):** The SRL baseline was not applicable to these cases due to its dependence on a fixed global state dimension. RAFL, however, consistently reduced simulation error across all non-scaled beam geometries and the more complex fish-tail shapes (crude and finer discretizations). This indicated RAFL's ability to transfer learned local correction structures beyond the specific training morphology.
- **Adaptability and Continual Refinement:** Fine-tuning RAFL models on fish-tail trajectories, after initial zero-shot transfer, led to substantial further improvements. This suggests that the learned residual acceleration field provides a transferable initialization, requiring minimal adaptation for new morphologies.

**B. Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
- **Canonical Beam (Training Geometry):** All methods improved upon the original simulator. SRL achieved the lowest marker error on the training geometry, with RAFL showing competitive performance and outperforming both SysID variants.
- **Cross-Shape Transfer (Scaled and Non-Scaled Beams):** This setting revealed a significant distinction in generalization capabilities:
 - **System Identification (SysID):** Both variants (optimizing Young's Modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν, or E alone) frequently exhibited unstable performance and negative transfer. This means that parameters identified on the canonical beam often degraded simulation accuracy when applied to unseen geometries, sometimes performing worse than the original, untuned simulator. For instance, SysID degraded on shorter and thicker non-scaled beams.
 - **Supervised Residual Learning (SRL):** The SRL baseline also demonstrated instability and negative transfer in the scaled beam settings.
 - **RAFL:** Demonstrated consistent improvements over the original simulator in every scaled and non-scaled case. It achieved the lowest error in the majority of these generalization settings. For example, on the non-scaled thinner beam, RAFL reduced the error from 6.571 mm to 3.536 mm, outperforming both SysID baselines.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Visualizations of representative trajectories showed that RAFL more accurately reproduced oscillatory motion and achieved more precise long-horizon behavior across various non-scaled geometries. SysID simulations often exhibited exaggerated stiffness or deviations from the physical system's equilibrium configurations.
- **Expanded Cross-Shape Generalization Analysis:** A comprehensive evaluation across morphology pairs confirmed that SysID frequently failed to transfer (negative transfer), whereas RAFL consistently achieved zero-shot accuracy improvements between meshes in most cases.

**C. Residual Force Structure and Transfer Behavior:**
- An analysis of the spatial distribution of residual forces provided insight into the observed transfer behavior.
- **SRL:** The residual forces produced by the SRL baseline were spatially localized, concentrating primarily near surfaces and the free end of the beam. This pattern suggested a strong dependence on specific vertex positions and a tight coupling to the global mesh geometry, explaining its poor generalization.
- **RAFL (Implicitly Derived):** In contrast, the residual forces induced by RAFL were more uniformly distributed throughout the beam's interior. This indicated a reduced sensitivity to specific vertex locations and weaker coupling to global geometric structure, which is consistent with its improved zero-shot transfer performance across varying geometries and discretizations.

Overall, the findings indicate that learning transferable local residual accelerations, as implemented in RAFL, enables more reliable sim-to-real generalization compared to globally optimized material parameters or global-state residual force models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RAFL framework presents a notable advancement in the field of soft robotics, particularly concerning the integration of differentiable simulation with real-world applications. Its significance and potential impact can be understood in several key areas:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Morphology Optimization:** The core contribution of RAFL is its ability to reduce the sim-to-real gap, especially when the geometry of soft robots is subject to change. By providing a generalizable and transferable corrective dynamics model, RAFL addresses a fundamental challenge that currently limits the efficacy of differentiable simulators in iterative design processes for soft robots. This directly impacts the ability to optimize robot shapes and structures in simulation with a higher confidence that the resulting physical prototypes will behave as predicted.

- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** Unlike traditional system identification methods or global-state residual learning, RAFL demonstrates consistent zero-shot generalization across unseen morphologies and discretizations. This robustness means that a single model trained on a canonical geometry can improve simulation accuracy for a wide range of related designs. This capability is crucial for exploration in morphology optimization, where testing numerous candidate designs would otherwise require prohibitive recalibration efforts.

- **Reduced Calibration Overhead and Scalability:** The framework's ability to support continual refinement allows simulation accuracy to accumulate across designs. This eliminates the need to reset calibration for each new morphology, which is a significant departure from methods requiring iterative tuning or re-identification of parameters. This efficiency gain contributes to a more scalable pathway for developing and optimizing soft robots, reducing the time and data required for each design iteration.

- **Methodological Advancement in Residual Physics Learning:** By reformulating residual learning from global nodal forces to element-level acceleration fields, RAFL introduces a novel approach that decouples corrective dynamics from mesh structure. This element-level, local feature-based approach mitigates the entanglement between geometry and material properties often observed in system identification, offering a more robust and physically grounded way to learn corrections. The structural constraints (rotational equivariance, zero residual at rest) further enhance its physical plausibility and data efficiency.

- **Facilitating Future Research and Development:** The success of RAFL opens avenues for further research. Its foundational principles can be extended to more complex scenarios in soft robotics, such as actuated robots, contact-rich environments, and fluid-structure interactions, by incorporating additional inputs into the residual formulation. Furthermore, the integration of advanced physical priors (e.g., stability, passivity, energy-consistency) within the neural architecture could further enhance its robustness and reliability. The vision of co-evolving morphology optimization with sim-to-real calibration based on RAFL suggests a promising direction for achieving highly optimized and reliable soft robot designs.

In summary, RAFL offers a practical and scalable solution for improving the fidelity of soft robot simulations, especially in the context of design space exploration. Its generalizable nature and efficient training mechanism have the potential to accelerate the development cycle of soft robotic systems by making differentiable simulation a more reliable tool for real-world applications.
