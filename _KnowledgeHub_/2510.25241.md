# One-shot Adaptation of Humanoid Whole-body Motion with Walking Priors

> [!summary] Summary
> A method for one-shot adaptation of humanoid whole-body motion policies enables robots to learn new, non-walking behaviors from a single demonstration clip. It leverages a pre-trained walking model and synthetically generated intermediate motion data, consistently outperforming baselines and achieving stable execution where direct single-shot training fails.

## Problem

- Training expressive humanoid whole-body motion policies requires extensive, costly, and labor-intensive motion capture datasets.
- Acquiring sufficient data for complex, rare, or highly specific non-walking motion patterns is challenging and inefficient.
- Directly training a motion policy from a single non-walking demonstration often leads to failure due to insufficient data for stability and generalization.

## Method

- A two-phase training scheme first trains a base model on abundant walking motion data, then fine-tunes it for new tasks.
- ==Leverages Order-Preserving Optimal Transport (OPOT)== to align a single non-walking target motion with relevant walking motions from the base dataset.
- Generates a diverse set of intermediate, collision-free pose skeletons by interpolating along geodesic paths between aligned walking and target poses, which are then used for policy fine-tuning.

## Results

- The proposed "Geo. FT Base Model" consistently achieved the strongest performance across 17 out of 20 metric-motion combinations compared to five baselines.
- The method enabled stable execution, achieving high mean episode lengths for motions like Jump, Punch, and Wash, where direct single-shot training often resulted in robot falls.
- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of Order-Preserving Optimal Transport for temporal coherence and demonstrated performance improvements with increased geodesic sampling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly learning complex humanoid whole-body motions from a single demonstration is often unstable and ineffective without prior knowledge.
> - Leveraging a pre-trained walking motion policy provides a robust prior that significantly improves stability and performance during adaptation to new tasks.
> - Generating intermediate, kinematically plausible motions by interpolating between a walking prior and a single target demonstration, via order-preserving optimal transport, efficiently bridges the data gap for policy fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid whole-body motion control has advanced significantly, transitioning from model-based control to data-driven reinforcement learning (RL) approaches. Contemporary RL pipelines for humanoid motion training typically rely on extensive datasets of diverse human motions, such as walking, running, and jumping. These datasets are often curated through motion capture systems, which record human subjects performing various actions. Examples include the CMU MoCap dataset and AMASS, which provide large repertoires of movements subsequently retargeted to humanoid robots for policy learning.

Despite progress in enabling humanoids to perform agile and expressive behaviors, a persistent limitation is the demanding nature of data collection. Motion capture is a resource-intensive process, requiring specialized equipment, controlled environments, and substantial labor. This challenge intensifies when attempting to acquire data for complex, rare, or highly specific motion patterns, often leading to inefficiencies or biases in policy learning.

Existing research has explored various aspects of humanoid control, including transformer-based policies for robust locomotion, world models for stability and perception, curriculum learning, and hierarchical strategies for broader task scope. Skill diversity and exploration, along with data efficiency in imitation learning, are also active areas. While one-shot learning has been investigated in robotic arm manipulation, where complex tasks are learned from a single human demonstration, its application to humanoid motion—which involves dynamic balance, multi-joint coordination, and prior locomotion competence—has been less explored.

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the data scarcity issue for humanoid whole-body motion. It proposes a method for adapting a humanoid motion policy to a *new* target motion from *only a single target clip*, while leveraging auxiliary walking data and a walking-trained base model. This approach aims to reduce the reliance on large, custom-collected datasets for each new motion, distinguishing itself from methods that require multiple training samples per motion category or those that primarily focus on generating motions without directly addressing policy adaptation under data constraints. Unlike some motion generation methods that require training neural networks, the proposed approach generates motion without such a requirement, which contributes to its light-weight nature.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a data-efficient adaptation approach for humanoid whole-body motion policies. Specifically, the authors aim to enable a humanoid robot to learn a new, non-walking motion from a single target demonstration clip, in conjunction with readily available walking motion data and a pre-trained walking-prior model.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several challenges inherent in current humanoid robotics training paradigms:

1. **High Cost and Labor Intensity of Data Collection:** Training expressive whole-body humanoid motion policies typically necessitates large and diverse datasets of human motions. Collecting such high-quality motion capture data is a labor-intensive, time-consuming, and expensive process. It requires specialized equipment and environments, and variations in environmental conditions or human performers can introduce inconsistencies.
2. **Difficulty in Scaling Datasets for Complex or Rare Motions:** The challenge of data collection becomes particularly pronounced when attempting to acquire multiple training samples for rare or complex motion patterns. This often requires extensive teleoperation or simulation, which can be inefficient and introduce biases into the policy learning process.
3. **Availability Disparity between Walking and Non-Walking Motions:** While walking motions are ubiquitous and easily obtainable (e.g., from internet videos), diverse non-walking motion videos are significantly harder to acquire in large quantities suitable for training.
4. **Limitations of Direct One-Shot Training:** Empirical observations indicate that directly training a motion policy on a single non-walking target clip often results in failure across various motion categories due to insufficient data for generalization and stability.
5. **Underexplored Area in Humanoid Robotics:** Although one-shot learning has found applications in robotic arm manipulation, its extension to complex humanoid whole-body motion, which involves challenges such as dynamic balance, prior locomotion competence, and multi-joint coordination, remains an area with significant research gaps.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a method that leverages a pre-trained walking prior and synthesizes intermediate motion data. This strategy is designed to bridge the gap between abundant walking data and scarce target-motion data, thereby facilitating smooth policy adaptation and reducing the reliance on exhaustive target-motion datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method for one-shot adaptation of humanoid whole-body motion is structured in a two-phase training scheme, preceded by motion data curation and a series of geometric and optimization steps to generate synthetic training samples.

**A. Human Motion Data Curation and Base Model Training:**
The process begins with curating human motion clips, primarily from the CMU MoCap dataset. Unlike methods that train models on large portions of diverse MoCap data, this approach formulates training in two phases. First, a **Base Model** is trained on a substantial collection of walking motion clips (approximately 130 clips), which are considered relatively easy to acquire. These clips are retargeted to the robot's kinematic structure (e.g., Unitree H1 with 19 DoFs) by mapping human joint rotations to humanoid joint angles. Behavior goals (upper-body joint positions, keypoints) and root movement goals (linear velocity, roll/pitch/yaw, height) are extracted. A goal-conditioned reinforcement learning policy is then trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in a simulated environment (Isaac Gym), where the upper body tracks reference motions and legs maintain balance.

**B. Geodesic Distance for Pose Skeletons:**
To quantify motion discrepancies, pose skeletons are mathematically represented. Each pose skeleton at a timestep `n` is defined as `(x_n, {q_n,1..., q_n,J})`, where `x_n` is the 3D root (pelvis) translation and `{q_n,j}` are unit quaternions for `J` joint rotations. The representation is invariant to skeleton scale. The geodesic distance between two poses combines:
- **Euclidean distance** for root translations: `d_t(x_1, x_2) = ||x_1 - x_2||_2`.
- **Sum of geodesic distances on SO(3)** for corresponding joint rotations: `d_r(q_1,j, q_2,j) = 2 arccos(|<q_1,j, q_2,j>|)`.
The total geodesic distance is a weighted sum of these components, with a weight `w=1` applied to the rotational component sum.

**C. Order-Preserving Optimal Transport (OPOT) for Skeleton Sequence Alignment:**
Given a walking skeleton sequence `S = {s_1..., s_N}` and a non-walking target sequence `T = {t_1..., t_M}`, the discrepancy between these sequences is measured using OPOT. This method extends the classical Wasserstein distance by incorporating temporal regularizations to preserve chronological structure, preventing non-monotonic mappings.
- **Cost Matrix:** `D(n, m)` is computed using the geodesic distance between individual pose skeletons `s_n` and `t_m`.
- **Regularizations:**
 - **Inverse Difference Moment Regularization:** Encourages local, homogeneous transport by favoring couplings near the diagonal of normalized indices.
 - **KL-Divergence Regularization with Gaussian Prior:** Aligns the transport plan with a prior distribution modeling expected couplings based on positional similarity.
- **Optimization:** The OPOT distance is formulated as a minimum cost problem with entropic regularization and solved using Sinkhorn iterations. The parameters `λ_1=50`, `λ_2=0.1`, `δ=1`, and 20 maximum iterations were used.
- **Output:** An optimal soft transport plan, which provides per-frame alignment between walking and non-walking sequences, and the shortest distance between them. The 10 walking motions with the smallest shortest distances to the target non-walking motion are selected for generating intermediate motions.

**D. Soft Transport Plans to Hard Assignments:**
The soft transport plan from OPOT, where a target pose might be associated with multiple source poses, is converted into a hard assignment matrix `γ`. This ensures each target pose skeleton is assigned to exactly one walking pose skeleton, while allowing walking skeletons to be reused. This is achieved by formulating a linear sum assignment problem and solving it using the Hungarian algorithm, maximizing the total assignment score. The method handles two cases:
- **Sufficient Walking Skeletons (N ≥ M):** Each target skeleton can be assigned a unique walking skeleton.
- **Insufficient Walking Skeletons (N < M):** Walking skeletons are reused by tiling the cost matrix to create virtual skeletons.

**E. Pose Skeletons Sampling along Geodesics:**
With hard assignments established, new pose skeletons are interpolated and sampled along the geodesic path in the product manifold `R^3 × SO(3)^J` between each matched walking pose `s_n` and target pose `t_m`.
- **Root Translation:** Linear interpolation: `x(τ) = (1 - τ)x_n + τx_m`.
- **Joint Rotations:** Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP) on `H_1` for each joint's rotation: `q_j(τ) = SLERP(q_n,j, q_m,j, τ)`.
For each of the Q=10 selected walking motions, six new pose skeletons are generated along these geodesics, resulting in intermediate motions.

**F. Optimization for Collision-Free Skeletal Poses:**
The generated poses may contain self-collisions. An optimization routine is applied to make them collision-free for robot retargeting.
- **Forward Kinematics:** Computes world-space joint transforms and positions.
- **Geometric Modeling:** Each joint is modeled as a sphere (radius proportional to bone length), and each bone as a capsule (cylinder capped by hemispheres, radius equal to parent's sphere radius).
- **Collision Detection:** Uses a fast batched segment-segment distance kernel.
- **Differentiable Energies:**
 - **Sphere Energy:** Penalizes interpenetration between non-adjacent joint spheres.
 - **Capsule Energy:** Penalizes overlaps between non-adjacent bone capsules, based on minimum line segment distance.
- **Optimization:** The combined sphere and capsule energies are minimized using Riemannian gradient descent on the product manifold `SO(3)^J` for joint rotations, with a learning rate of `lr`. This ensures descent along geodesic directions and produces physically plausible poses.

**G. Policy Adaptation (Fine-tuning):**
The newly generated, collision-free intermediate motion data (along with the single target clip) are then used to fine-tune the Base Model. This fine-tuning process employs the same PPO-based reinforcement learning strategy as the Base Model training, enabling the gradual adaptation of the walking-trained model to the new target motion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted on the CMU MoCap dataset, using a two-phase training scheme: a Base Model trained on walking motions, followed by fine-tuning with generated intermediate motions and a single target clip. Training and evaluation clips were performed by different actors to assess generalization. Five non-walking target motions (Basketball, Dance, Jump, Punch, Wash) were used, with only a single training clip per category.

**A. Baselines:**
The performance of the proposed method was compared against five baselines:
1. **Base Model:** Trained from scratch on 135 walking clips.
2. **Single Motion Model:** Trained from scratch using only one target motion clip.
3. **Single Finetune (FT) Base Model:** Fine-tuned the Base Model using only one target motion clip.
4. **Geodesic (Geo.) Motion Model:** Trained from scratch using only the motions sampled along geodesics.
5. **Geodesic (Geo.) Finetune (FT) Base Model (Proposed Method):** Fine-tuned the Base Model using the motions sampled along geodesics.

**B. Evaluation Metrics:**
Performance was assessed using four metrics:
- **Mean Episode Linear Velocity Tracking Reward (MELV):** Measures accuracy of linear velocity tracking.
- **Mean Episode Roll Pitch Tracking Reward (MERP):** Measures accuracy of roll and pitch tracking.
- **Mean Episode Key Body Tracking Reward (MEKB):** Measures accuracy of tracking key body parts.
- **Mean Episode Lengths (MEL):** Indicates policy stability and ability to complete episodes (maximum possible for all tasks is 1501.990).

**C. Key Observations and Results:**

- **Failure of Single-Sample Training:** The "Single Motion Model" often failed, leading to robot falls (indicated by '×') in three out of five tasks (Jump, Punch, Wash). Even when it did not fall, its performance across metrics was generally lower than fine-tuned approaches. This suggests that training a policy from scratch with only a single motion clip is insufficient for robust humanoid control for complex motions.
- **Benefits of Fine-tuning a Base Model:** Fine-tuning the Base Model with a single target clip ("Single FT Base Model") improved stability and performance significantly compared to training from scratch with a single clip. It often achieved competitive, second-tier results.
- **Effectiveness of Geodesic Motion Generation:**
 - The "Geo. Motion Model" (training from scratch on generated motions) demonstrated notable improvements. For Basketball, it achieved the best or second-best results across all metrics. It also showed solid improvements for Jump, Punch, and Wash. This indicates that the synthetically generated motions provide a valuable training signal even without a pre-trained base.
 - The proposed "Geo. FT Base Model" consistently yielded the strongest overall performance. Across the 20 metric-motion combinations, it achieved the top or second-best scores in 17 instances. Specifically, it demonstrated the highest MELV and MEKB values for four motions (Dance, Jump, Punch, Wash) and the highest MERP for Dance, Punch, and Wash. For Basketball and Jump, it also delivered competitive results, often the best. This consistent performance indicates that combining a walking-trained prior with geodesic-interpolated motions is effective for adaptation.
- **Robustness:** The "Geo. FT Base Model" achieved high Mean Episode Lengths (MEL) for most tasks, indicating greater stability and ability to maintain motion compared to baselines, particularly where the "Single Motion Model" failed.

**D. Ablation Studies:**

- **Effect of Transport Formulation:** Replacing the Order-Preserving Optimal Transport (OPOT) with classical, permutation-free optimal transport for the Jump motion resulted in consistent performance drops across all four metrics. This suggests that preserving temporal coherence and avoiding non-monotonic mappings in the alignment process is crucial for generating kinematically plausible intermediate motions that benefit policy learning.
- **Effect of Number of Geodesic Samples:** Varying the number of interpolated poses (1, 3, or 6) used for training showed a monotonic improvement in performance as the number of samples increased, with six samples achieving the best scores. This indicates that denser sampling along geodesics provides richer coverage of intermediate pose spaces and transition dynamics, which is beneficial for policy adaptation in data-scarce settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of data scarcity for whole-body motion learning. The proposed one-shot adaptation method, leveraging walking priors and synthetic motion generation, has several implications:

1. **Increased Data Efficiency:** The primary impact is enabling the adaptation of complex humanoid behaviors from a single target demonstration, substantially reducing the demand for extensive, labor-intensive, and costly motion capture datasets. This can accelerate the development and deployment of new skills for humanoid robots.
2. **Enhanced Versatility and Adaptability:** By demonstrating that a pre-trained walking model can be effectively adapted to diverse non-walking motions, the work enhances the versatility of humanoid robots. It suggests a more flexible paradigm for skill acquisition, where foundational locomotion capabilities can be extended to a broader range of tasks with minimal new data.
3. **Accessible Skill Acquisition:** The reliance on easily obtainable walking data and a single target demonstration makes the process of teaching new motions more accessible to researchers and practitioners who may not have access to large-scale motion capture facilities.
4. **Novel Motion Generation Strategy:** The integration of order-preserving optimal transport, geodesic interpolation, and collision-free optimization provides a lightweight and neural network-free approach for synthesizing kinematically plausible intermediate motions. This strategy is distinct from data-driven generative models that require extensive training.
5. **Robustness and Stability in Policy Learning:** The consistent outperformance of the proposed method against baselines, particularly its ability to ensure stable execution (high episode lengths) for motions where direct single-shot training fails, highlights its robustness for reinforcement learning in complex physical simulations.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens avenues for further research in data-efficient and curriculum-based learning for embodied AI. Future efforts could explore integrating this framework with perception, developing more sophisticated geodesic interpolation techniques, or extending it to real-world hardware transfer challenges.

In summary, this research contributes a practical and effective methodology for expanding the behavioral repertoire of humanoid robots under severe data constraints, offering a path toward more agile and cost-effective robot skill acquisition.
