# Learning Visuotactile Skills with Two Multifingered Hands

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley developed HATO, a low-cost teleoperation system for bimanual robots equipped with repurposed multifingered prosthetic hands featuring integrated touch sensors. They demonstrated learning complex dexterous manipulation skills from human demonstrations using visuotactile data, achieving high success rates on tasks like pouring and stacking while highlighting the critical role of both vision and touch.

## Problem

- Achieving human-level dexterity in robotic manipulation, particularly with bimanual, multifingered systems, is challenging due to hardware and control complexities.
- Existing bimanual robotic systems primarily rely on simpler parallel-jaw grippers, limiting the range of complex, adaptive tasks that can be performed.
- A lack of affordable, accessible teleoperation systems and multifingered hands with integrated touch sensing hinders the collection of high-quality human demonstration data for learning advanced dexterous skills.

## Method

- ==The HATO== (Hands-Arms Tele-Operation) system was developed, repurposing commercially available prosthetic hands (Psyonic Ability Hands with 60 touch sensors per hand) for bimanual robot arms.
- HATO utilizes off-the-shelf Meta Quest 2 VR hardware for intuitive control, mapping controller poses to arm end-effector positions and simplifying multifingered hand control.
- Visuotactile data collected via HATO trains Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) to learn complex, long-horizon bimanual manipulation skills.

## Results

- Learned policies achieved high success rates: 10/10 for Slippery Handover and Tower Block Stacking, and 9/10 for Wine Pouring, with **100%** success in object pickup for all tasks.
- Policies demonstrated high data efficiency, saturating performance with 75-200 human demonstrations depending on the task.
- Ablation studies showed that removing vision led to complete task failure (e.g., 0/10 for Steak Serving), while removing touch enabled object pickup but resulted in subsequent task failure (e.g., 0/10 for Steak Serving transfer), highlighting their distinct and crucial roles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Repurposing commercially available, touch-enabled prosthetic hands can significantly reduce the cost and complexity of hardware for advanced dexterous robotics research.
> - Simplified, intuitive teleoperation mappings using off-the-shelf VR can effectively enable non-experts to collect high-quality, multimodal datasets for complex bimanual tasks.
> - Both visual and tactile sensing are critical and complementary for robust dexterous manipulation, with touch being particularly indispensable for fine manipulation, object stability, and handling subtle force feedback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The long-term aspiration in robotic manipulation is to achieve human-level dexterity, enabling robots to perform complex tasks with adaptability and precision comparable to humans. This paper directly addresses this grand challenge by exploring a novel integration of several key elements: **bimanual systems, multifingered hands, visuotactile data, and learning from human demonstrations.**

Historically, bimanual robotic systems have largely relied on simpler parallel-jaw grippers as end-effectors due to their lower cost, maintenance, and relative ease of control. However, these grippers inherently limit the range and complexity of achievable motions, hindering capabilities such as adaptive grasping, intricate in-hand manipulation, dexterous object handovers, sophisticated tool use, and complex bilateral coordination—all tasks humans perform with ease. While advanced multifingered hands exist (e.g., Allegro Hand, Shadow Dexterous Hand), their high cost, limited availability, and challenges in integrating rich sensory feedback (especially touch) have restricted their widespread use in research, particularly in bimanual setups.

Current approaches to robotic control largely fall into three categories:
- **Classical control:** Often task-specific and requires extensive expert knowledge of system dynamics and task structures, making it less adaptable to diverse, real-world scenarios.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Can learn complex behaviors but typically suffers from high sample complexity, often requiring extensive simulation and confronting the "sim-to-real" gap when transferring policies to the physical world. While progress has been made for single and even dual-arm systems, directly applying RL to high-dimensional, multifingered, bimanual systems remains exceedingly challenging.
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** Learning from human demonstrations offers a promising pathway to acquire human-level dexterity by directly observing and mimicking human behavior. This approach bypasses some of the challenges of classical control and the sample inefficiency of RL, especially when combined with deep neural networks for robust representation learning.

This paper positions itself at a crucial intersection within this landscape. While imitation learning for bimanual manipulation has been studied, previous works primarily focused on parallel-jaw grippers. Research into visuotactile sensing has shown its benefits for grasping, in-hand manipulation, object recognition, and cloth handling, but these studies have largely been confined to single hands or systems lacking dexterous multifingered capabilities. The authors explicitly state that their work is the "first at the intersection of bimanual dexterous manipulation and imitation learning from visuotactile inputs," highlighting its novelty.

Furthermore, a significant bottleneck for imitation learning is the availability of diverse, high-quality demonstration data. Existing teleoperation systems, crucial for collecting such data, are mostly designed for parallel-jaw grippers or single-hand setups. Systems for dexterous multifingered hands often rely on complex retargeting, introducing latency and reducing intuitiveness. This work aims to bridge this gap by developing an accessible, low-cost teleoperation system specifically designed for bimanual, multifingered, visuotactile data collection. The paper also acknowledges concurrent work (Wang et al.) that demonstrates bimanual dexterous manipulation but distinguishes itself by emphasizing the critical role of tactile input, which it shows to be vital for robust task completion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to replicate human-like dexterity, perceptual experiences, and motion patterns in robotic systems. To achieve this, the authors are motivated to explore learning from human demonstrations using a bimanual system equipped with multifingered hands and rich visuotactile data.

The paper identifies two major challenges that hinder progress toward this goal and form the core motivations for their work:

1. **Lack of an affordable and accessible teleoperation system** suitable for dual-arm setups with multifingered hands. Traditional systems are often bespoke, expensive, and complex, limiting data collection efforts.
2. **Scarcity of multifingered hand hardware equipped with touch sensing.** While dexterous hands exist, integrating high-fidelity touch sensors in a practical and scalable manner, especially for research purposes, remains a significant hurdle.

Based on these challenges, the key objectives of the research are clearly outlined as follows:

- **Hardware Adaptation:** To introduce a novel hardware adaptation by repurposing commercially available prosthetic hands (Psyonic Ability Hands) that are already equipped with rich touch sensors, making them accessible for research.
- **Teleoperation System Development:** To develop HATO (Hands-Arms Tele-Operation), a low-cost, intuitive, and accessible teleoperation system. This system is designed to leverage off-the-shelf virtual reality (VR) electronics (Meta Quest 2) and provide novel, simplified mappings for controlling dual-arm, multifingered robots.
- **Comprehensive Software Suite:** To complement the hardware with a versatile software suite that enables efficient and reliable multimodal data collection, supports robust data processing, facilitates scalable policy learning, and ensures smooth policy deployment.
- **Skill Learning and Demonstration:** To utilize the collected visuotactile data to learn a variety of complex, long-horizon, and high-precision bimanual manipulation skills that explicitly require multifingered dexterity and touch feedback, demonstrating capabilities difficult to achieve with less advanced systems.
- **Empirical Investigation:** To conduct thorough ablation studies to empirically investigate the effects of dataset size, different sensing modalities (vision, touch, proprioception), and visual input preprocessing strategies on the performance, efficiency, and robustness of learned policies.
- **Open Science:** To open-source all developed hardware designs, software systems, and collected teleoperation datasets to foster further research, collaboration, and replication within the robotics community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology revolves around the development and application of **HATO (Hands-Arms Tele-Operation)**, a comprehensive system for data collection and policy learning in bimanual dexterous manipulation.

**A. Robot and Sensor Setup:**
- **Robot Arms:** Two UR5e 6-DoF robot arms are used, providing a stable and capable base for bimanual tasks.
- **Robot Hands:** Two Psyonic Ability Hands, originally designed as prosthetics, are repurposed for research. This innovative adaptation involves custom Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) to simplify electrical integration. Each hand features five fingers with 6 actuated Degrees of Freedom (DoFs) (one per non-thumb finger, two for the thumb). Crucially, each fingertip is equipped with six discrete touch sensors, providing a total of 60 continuous touch readings per hand.
- **Visual Sensing:** Three Intel RealSense RGB-D cameras provide visual input: two are wrist-mounted on each robot arm (offering egocentric views), and one is placed at a stationary "third-view" or "head-view" position.
- **Teleoperation Platform:** A Meta Quest 2 VR headset and its pair of controllers serve as the human interface, leveraging their spatial tracking (visual and IMU) and button inputs.

**B. Teleoperation Pipeline:**
HATO's teleoperation system is designed for intuitiveness and efficiency in data collection:
- **Arm Control:** The pose (position and orientation) of each Quest controller is directly mapped to the desired end-effector (EEF) pose of the corresponding UR5e robot arm. Inverse Kinematics (IK) solves for the arm's joint positions. A "pause-and-adjust" feature (using the trigger button) allows operators to reposition themselves, which is crucial for long-duration or physically demanding demonstrations.
- **Hand Control:** A simplified but effective mapping is employed for multifingered hands:
 - **Power Grasp:** The grip button on the Quest controller continuously controls the flexion/extension of the four non-thumb fingers, allowing for fine-grained control over grasping force.
 - **Thumb Control:** The 2-D position of the thumbstick controls the two DoFs of the thumb (flexion/extension and abduction/adduction).
 This mapping provides an intuitive user experience without complex retargeting, which can introduce latency.

**C. Data Collection and Preprocessing:**
- Multimodal data is collected at 10Hz, encompassing:
 - **Proprioception:** Joint positions of both arms, finger positions of both hands, and end-effector poses.
 - **Vision:** RGB and depth images (480x640 resolution, resized to 240x320) from all three cameras.
 - **Touch:** The 60 continuous touch sensor readings from each hand.
 - **Control Actions:** The desired joint positions for both arms and hands.
- All data is linearly normalized to a range of [-1, 1] (except raw image values) to prepare it for neural network processing.

**D. Policy Learning:**
- **Imitation Learning with Diffusion Models:** The system learns bimanual dexterous skills by treating action prediction as a conditional generation problem using a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM), following the Diffusion Policy framework.
- **Input Observations:** At each timestep, observations from multiple modalities are concatenated:
 - **Proprioception:** End-effector poses (chosen over arm joint positions to avoid issues near singularities). Processed by a two-layer ReLU network.
 - **Touch:** 60 touch sensor readings per hand. Processed by a two-layer ReLU network.
 - **Vision:** RGB and depth images from three cameras. Encoded using separate ResNet-18 architectures (with GroupNorm instead of BatchNorm) for each camera, outputting 32-dimensional features.
- **Diffusion Architecture:** All encoded features are concatenated and fed into a CNN-based diffusion model.
- **Output Action:** The model predicts a 24-dimensional vector representing the normalized 6 DoF absolute desired joint positions for each UR5e arm and the 6 DoF normalized desired joint positions for each Ability Hand.
- **Optimization:** The AdamW optimizer is used with a learning rate of 0.0001, batch size of 128, and exponential weighted averaging of model weights for evaluation.

**E. Deployment:**
- **Asynchronous Inference:** A novel asynchronous setup is used where the diffusion model's prediction and robot execution run in parallel. A remote inference server continuously processes the latest observations and generates action sequences.
- **Temporal Ensemble:** The local process receives these action sequences and computes an average over multiple timesteps, significantly improving motion smoothness during real-world execution. Inference uses 15 diffusion steps.

**F. Tasks:**
Four challenging real-world bimanual tasks were designed to evaluate the system's capabilities:
1. **Slippery Handover:** Basic two-hand coordination involving a slippery object.
2. **Tower Block Stacking:** Manipulating bulky objects, demonstrating large contact area benefits.
3. **Wine Pouring:** Handling objects with changing center of mass, requiring stable power grasps.
4. **Steak Serving:** A long-horizon task requiring precise tool use and object balancing, demanding intricate force feedback.
Demonstrations ranging from 100 to 300 trajectories (30 minutes to 2 hours of total data) were collected for these tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness of the HATO system, the learned policies, and the importance of multimodal sensing:

**A. Capabilities from Teleoperation:**
- HATO proved highly effective for data collection. Inexperienced teleoperators were able to quickly and efficiently collect hundreds of high-quality demonstrations for complex bimanual tasks.
- A qualitative comparison with parallel-jaw grippers revealed significant advantages for multifingered hands. While parallel grippers frequently suffered from failures like object slipping, shaky holds, and unstable grasps (especially for slippery or large objects), multifingered hands provided superior redundancy, larger contact areas, and additional support, greatly mitigating these issues and leading to smoother teleoperation.

**B. Capabilities from Learning:**
- Policies learned from HATO-collected visuotactile data demonstrated remarkable dexterity. The policies achieved near-perfect success rates for three out of four challenging tasks:
 - **Slippery Handover:** 10/10 successful task completions.
 - **Tower Block Stacking:** 10/10 successful task completions.
 - **Wine Pouring:** 9/10 successful task completions.
- For the more complex and high-precision **Steak Serving** task, the policy achieved a 5/10 success rate, which is significant given the long horizon and delicate nature of the task.
- Across all tasks, the learned policies demonstrated 100% success in the critical intermediate step of picking up the target objects, highlighting their foundational grasping competency.

**C. Learning Efficiency:**
- An empirical investigation into dataset size confirmed that prediction error (ActionMSE) generally decreases with more training demonstrations, as expected.
- Crucially, the policies demonstrated high data efficiency, with performance saturating relatively quickly:
 - **Block Stacking:** Performance saturated around 75 demonstrations.
 - **Steak Serving:** Performance saturated around 100 demonstrations.
 - **Wine Pouring:** Performance saturated around 200 demonstrations.
 This suggests that effective bimanual dexterous policies can be learned with a manageable amount of human demonstration data.

**D. Importance of Vision and Touch:**
A thorough ablation study unequivocally highlighted the critical role of both vision and touch sensing for robust policy learning and execution:
- **Impact on ActionMSE:**
 - Policies trained **without vision** consistently showed significantly higher prediction errors across all tasks.
 - Policies trained **without touch** also generally exhibited higher prediction errors than those with all modalities, particularly for Slippery Handover, Wine Pouring, and Block Stacking.
 - Policies trained with **neither vision nor touch** consistently performed the worst, having the highest ActionMSE.
- **Impact on Success Rate (Steak Serving):**
 - Without vision, the policy completely failed to even pick up the objects (0/10 success), demonstrating vision's necessity for initial perception and localization.
 - Without touch, the policy could pick up the objects (10/10 pickup success) but consistently failed to transfer the steak onto the plate (0/10 task success). This stark difference, despite a similar ActionMSE to the full policy, underscores that touch is indispensable for the subtle force control and stability required for fine manipulation.
- **Impact on Robustness (Block Stacking with Rare Initialization):**
 - When faced with a less common, perturbed block configuration, policies relying solely on proprioception or lacking one of the key sensory modalities performed poorly.
 - The full visuotactile policy maintained 10/10 success, while the policy without touch succeeded only 4/10 times, and the policy without vision failed entirely (0/10 success). This clearly indicates that both vision and touch contribute significantly to the policy's robustness against scene variations and unseen scenarios.

**E. Camera Configuration and Depth Information:**
- **Wrist-mounted cameras** were found to be more beneficial than the third-view camera alone, consistently yielding lower prediction errors for most tasks. This is attributed to wrist cameras providing richer, less occluded, and more task-relevant visual information, particularly with induced perspective changes during arm movement.
- **Depth information** from the RGB-D cameras did not consistently improve policy performance and, in some cases (e.g., Wine Pouring), slightly degraded it. The authors hypothesize this might be due to the noise present in real-world depth sensor readings, which could introduce more harm than good to the learning process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant stride forward in the pursuit of human-level robotic dexterity, with profound implications for the field:

**A. Advancing Dexterous Bimanual Manipulation:** The paper successfully integrates and validates a bimanual system with multifingered hands and rich visuotactile sensing, demonstrating unprecedented levels of dexterity for complex, long-horizon tasks. This moves beyond the limitations of parallel-jaw grippers and pushes the boundary of what robots can accomplish, especially in tasks requiring intricate coordination and sensitive object interaction.

**B. Enabling Technology for Data Collection (HATO):** The development of HATO is a crucial contribution. By leveraging low-cost, off-the-shelf VR hardware and introducing intuitive control mappings, HATO dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for collecting high-quality, multimodal demonstration data for complex bimanual tasks. This accessibility is vital for the scalability of imitation learning approaches, which are heavily reliant on diverse and realistic human demonstrations. HATO effectively democratizes data collection for advanced robotics research.

**C. Empirical Validation of Multimodal Sensing:** The comprehensive ablation studies empirically confirm the indispensable role of both high-quality vision and touch sensing for robust and successful execution of dexterous manipulation policies. The findings highlight that touch is not merely supplementary but *critical* for fine manipulation, object stability, and robustness to variations, especially when subtle force feedback is required. This provides strong evidence for the necessity of integrating multiple sensory modalities for advanced robotic capabilities, moving beyond vision-only or proprioception-only paradigms.

**D. Practical Applicability and Real-World Impact:** The demonstrated skills, such as handing over slippery objects, stacking heavy blocks, pouring liquids, and serving steak, are directly relevant to real-world applications in household robotics, logistics, manufacturing, and assistive technologies. The ability to perform such tasks autonomously and robustly could lead to more capable and versatile robotic assistants.

**E. Catalyst for Future Research and Open Science:** The commitment to open-sourcing the hardware designs, software suite, and collected datasets is a major contribution to the research community. This will foster reproducibility, enable benchmarking of new algorithms, and accelerate further research into dexterous manipulation, multimodal perception, and imitation learning. It provides a valuable platform for other researchers to build upon and extend this work.

**F. Direction for Future Work:** The paper itself points to exciting future directions, including the integration of haptic feedback into the teleoperation system for an even richer user experience and higher-quality demonstrations. Furthermore, the need for more robust and generalizable policies that are less susceptible to appearance changes and can learn from scratch more efficiently remains an important area of exploration, potentially through pre-training or more advanced generalization techniques.

In conclusion, this paper presents a compelling solution to fundamental challenges in robotic dexterity, offering both innovative engineering (repurposed hands, low-cost teleoperation) and rigorous empirical validation of the critical components (multimodal sensing). Its contributions pave a clearer path towards intelligent robots that can interact with the physical world with human-like skill and adaptability.
