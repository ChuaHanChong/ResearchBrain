# PhysConvex: Physics-Informed 3D Dynamic Convex Radiance Fields for Reconstruction and Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysConvex is a physics-informed dynamic convex radiance field that unifies rendering and simulation. It represents deformable scenes as time-varying convex polytopes deformed via boundary-driven vertex and surface dynamics, advected by a reduced-order simulation using neural skinning eigenmodes under continuum mechanics. Jointly optimizing appearance, geometry, and elastic parameters from multi-view video, it beats prior methods on system identification, reconstruction, and prediction.

## Problem

- Center-driven Gaussian or voxel dynamics update primitives from central particles or pooled neighbors, lacking spatial sensitivity to non-uniform deformation within a primitive.
- Grid-based MPM coupling with NeRF or Gaussians cannot represent sharp or evolving boundaries, and predefined particle-primitive bindings ignore material and geometric variation.
- Fixed ellipsoidal kernels chosen for splatting efficiency limit anisotropic and nonlinear material modeling and cover planar or angular regions poorly.

## Method

- **PhysConvex** ==boundary-driven dynamic convex fields==: explicit vertex dynamics advect convex hull points under Newtonian motion, implicit half-space support-function surface dynamics give differentiable occupancy for splatting.
- ==Reduced-order convex simulation== advects convexes with spatially varying ==neural skinning eigenmodes== as shape- and material-aware bases, time-varying reduced DOFs solved by ==implicit Newton integration==.
- Two-stage differentiable pipeline: reconstruct undeformed convex field from first multi-view frame, then refine Young's modulus, Poisson ratio, and skinning field from single-view video, initialized by a ==video-ViT== regression head.

## Results

- System identification on **12** Google Scanned Objects cuts mean log(E) error to **0.37** versus **0.51** for Vid2Sim, **2.01** GIC, **2.50** PAC-NeRF; Poisson error **0.04** versus **0.06**.
- Dynamic reconstruction reaches **30.00** PSNR, **0.962** SSIM, **8.247** FoVVDP against **28.57**/**0.952**/**7.794** for Vid2Sim; future-state prediction **25.71** PSNR versus **25.07**.
- Trains in **6** minutes on one RTX A6000 with **23,681** primitives, versus **8** min Vid2Sim, **32** min Spring-Gaus, **51** min PAC-NeRF; gains saturate beyond **K=6** points per convex.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rendering primitives and simulation primitives demand different properties; convex polytopes reconcile compact appearance encoding with geometric and physical expressiveness better than fixed ellipsoids.
> - Deforming a primitive's boundary rather than its center adds spatial sensitivity, letting a single primitive express non-uniform strain and evolving sharp edges.
> - Mesh-free reduced-order subspaces make differentiable physics cheap enough to optimize jointly with rendering, but expressivity caps at the skinning network's capacity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses the challenge of reconstructing and simulating dynamic 3D scenes with both visual realism and physical consistency. This field of research broadly encompasses computer vision, computer graphics, and physics-based simulation.

Traditional physics-based simulation methods typically rely on predefined geometric models, which limits their applicability to real-world scenarios where precise geometry may not be available. Conversely, purely neural approaches, such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), have demonstrated proficiency in reconstructing object appearance and geometry for rendering. However, these methods often struggle to capture complex material deformations and dynamics because they tightly couple extrinsic geometric attributes with intrinsic physical motion, frequently disregarding underlying physical laws. This limitation restricts their generalization capabilities and interpretability in physically dynamic environments.

Recent advancements have attempted to integrate radiance field representations with differentiable physical simulators. These physics-informed frameworks aim to jointly optimize geometry, appearance, and physical parameters, offering a more unified approach to reconstruction and simulation. However, existing dynamic primitives used in these frameworks (e.g., voxels in NeRF or ellipsoids in 3DGS) are primarily designed for rendering efficiency rather than for modeling physically consistent deformation. This design choice leads to several limitations:
1. **Limited Spatial Sensitivity:** Center-driven dynamics, common in hybrid Eulerian-Lagrangian frameworks, update primitives based on central particles or pooled neighbors, often lacking the spatial sensitivity required for non-uniform deformation.
2. **Inaccurate Coupling:** Predefined particle-primitive bindings may neglect variations in material and geometry, resulting in imprecise physical coupling.
3. **Boundary Representation Challenges:** Mesh-free approaches using grid-based Material Point Method (MPM) simulation with NeRFs and Gaussians encounter difficulties in representing sharp or evolving boundaries, which hinders physically meaningful surface deformation.
4. **Limited Shape Expressiveness:** Fixed ellipsoidal kernels, constrained by splatting efficiency, restrict the modeling of anisotropic or nonlinear materials and provide suboptimal spatial coverage in regions with planar or angular features.

In essence, existing methods often fail to reconcile the differing requirements of rendering (which benefits from compact appearance encoding) and physical simulation (which demands geometric and physical expressiveness in dynamic 3D primitives). PhysConvex aims to address these identified limitations by proposing a physics-informed approach that integrates dynamic convex primitives, designed for both efficient rendering and robust physical simulation, into a unified framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of PhysConvex is to develop a unified framework for the reconstruction and simulation of dynamic 3D scenes that achieves both visual realism and physical consistency. This overarching goal is motivated by the persistent challenges observed in current approaches, particularly the disconnect between visually appealing neural representations and physically accurate simulation models.

The specific motivations and objectives can be detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Neural Representations:** Current neural representations like NeRFs and 3DGS excel at appearance reconstruction but struggle to model complex material deformation and dynamics with physical accuracy. They often treat dynamics as a learned pattern without explicit adherence to physical laws, leading to limitations in generalization and interpretability. PhysConvex aims to embed physical principles directly into the representation to overcome this.

- **Reconciling Rendering and Simulation Requirements:** A key challenge is that primitives optimized for rendering efficiency (e.g., ellipsoids in 3DGS, voxels in NeRF) are often unsuitable for physically consistent deformation modeling. These primitives exhibit limitations such as:
 - **Lack of Spatial Sensitivity:** Their "center-driven" dynamics fail to capture spatially adaptive, non-uniform deformations.
 - **Inflexible Bindings:** Predefined particle-primitive bindings do not adequately account for material and geometric variations.
 - **Boundary Handling Issues:** They struggle to represent sharp or evolving boundaries, which is crucial for realistic surface deformation.
 - **Geometric Inexpressiveness:** Fixed kernel shapes limit the modeling of anisotropic materials and result in poor spatial coverage in angular or planar regions.
 PhysConvex is motivated to introduce a primitive type—dynamic convexes—that can satisfy both demands simultaneously.

- **Developing a Boundary-Driven Dynamic Representation:** To enable flexible, spatially adaptive, and physically consistent deformation, a core objective is to introduce a "boundary-driven dynamic convex representation." This aims to model deformation explicitly through vertex dynamics of convex hulls and implicitly through surface dynamics of half-space support functions. This approach is intended to provide:
 - Spatial sensitivity to non-uniform deformation via vertex advection.
 - Capture of evolving edges and boundaries for physically meaningful surface deformation.
 - Capability for polyhedral structural modification, refinement, and simplification to enhance geometric expressiveness and adaptability for anisotropic materials.

- **Enabling Efficient and Accurate Physical Simulation:** To integrate convex primitives into physics-based dynamics, the work aims to develop a "reduced-order convex simulation." This component is designed to:
 - Advect dynamic convex fields under continuum mechanics principles.
 - Utilize spatially varying neural skinning eigenmodes as physics-informed, shape- and material-aware deformation bases.
 - Employ time-varying reduced degrees of freedom (DOFs) under Newtonian dynamics for efficiency.
 - Offer compact, gap-free volumetric coverage to enhance geometric efficiency and simulation fidelity, especially for complex geometries and heterogeneous materials without relying on explicit meshes or grids.

- **Joint Optimization for Comprehensive Scene Understanding:** A further objective is to establish a framework where appearance, geometry, and underlying physical parameters (e.g., Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio) can be jointly optimized from multi-view video observations. This holistic optimization aims to lead to a more coherent and robust reconstruction and simulation model.

In summary, PhysConvex seeks to bridge the gap between visual fidelity and physical accuracy by proposing a novel representation and simulation paradigm that grounds dynamic neural radiance fields in the principles of continuum mechanics, thereby enabling more interpretable, generalizable, and realistic simulations of deformable objects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PhysConvex is a unified framework that integrates visual rendering with physical simulation through a novel representation of deformable radiance fields. The methodology can be broadly divided into three core components: boundary-driven dynamic convex fields, physical reduced-order convex simulation, and differentiable rendering and simulation.

**Method Overview:**
The system operates in a two-stage training process:
1. An undeformed convex field is initially reconstructed from the first multi-view frame of an input video.
2. The boundary-driven dynamic convex radiance field is then advected using a reduced-order convex simulation under continuum mechanics. During this stage, physical properties are optimized through joint differentiable simulation and rendering, typically supervised by a single-view video.

#### 3.1. Boundary-driven Dynamic Convex Fields

PhysConvex represents a dynamic object as a collection of time-varying convex polytopes. A dynamic convex radiance field at time *t* is parameterized as a set of *N* time-dependent convex polytopes, C(t) = {K_n(t), c_n, σ_n, α_n, β_n}, where K_n(t) defines the *n*-th dynamic convex, and {c_n, σ_n, α_n, β_n} are its time-invariant color, opacity, smoothness, and sharpness parameters, respectively.

The deformation of these convexes is modeled in a boundary-driven manner, utilizing both explicit vertex dynamics and implicit surface dynamics:
- **Explicit Vertex Dynamics:** A dynamic convex is defined by a 3D time-varying point set K(t) = {x_k(t)} which forms its convex hull. During simulation, these points evolve under physical dynamics, allowing for spatial flexibility and non-uniform shape deformation. The undeformed state is K(t=0) = {X_k}.
- **Implicit Surface Dynamics:** The convex hull can also be defined by *H* time-varying planes, H(t) = {(n_h(t), d_h(t))}. The signed distance of a point *x* to the *h*-th plane is f_h(x, t) = n_h(t)·x + d_h(t).
 An occupancy function O(x, t) for each dynamic convex is defined using a Sigmoid function over φ(x, t), where φ(x, t) is a log-sum-exp approximation of the signed distance function to the convex hull's surfaces. Parameters α and β control the smoothness and sharpness of the convex.

For rendering, PhysConvex extends 3D Convex Splatting (3DCS) to dynamic scenes. It projects 3D time-varying points to 2D, constructs their dynamic convex hulls, and rasterizes them using α-blending. The time-varying pixel color C(q, t) at a location *q* and time *t* is computed by accumulating contributions from all dynamic convexes along the ray.

#### 3.2. Physical Reduced-order Convex Simulation

To integrate convex primitives into physics, a reduced-order convex simulation is developed.
- **Convex Vertex Dynamics Advection:** The vertex dynamics x_k(t) are modeled using spatially varying neural skinning eigenmodes W_θ as reduced deformation bases. The deformation map is expressed as ϕ(X_k, Z(t), W_θ) = X_k + Σ_m W_θ_m(X_k)Z_m(t)[X_k, 1]^T, where *M* is the number of reduced degrees of freedom (DOFs). The neural skinning field W_θ(X) is an MLP parameterized by θ, designed to provide smooth, mesh-free discretization for complex geometries and heterogeneous materials.
 The neural skinning field W_θ(X) is trained to minimize a deformation energy loss, which includes an elastic energy term (Ψ(·) is the strain energy density function) and an orthogonality regularization term. This training encourages the eigenmodes to encode low-dimensional, physically consistent, material- and shape-aware deformation behaviors.
- **Reduced DOFs (Z(t)):** Z(t) represents time-varying reduced DOFs, initialized to zero at t=0. During simulation, z(t) (the flattened version of Z(t)) is updated at each discrete time step using implicit time integration based on the incremental potential formulation (Eq. 2) and solved via a Newton-based solver. This process drives the dynamic convex field under physically consistent reduced-order dynamics.

#### 3.3. Differentiable Rendering and Simulation

The system jointly reconstructs appearance, geometry, and physical properties through an end-to-end differentiable process.
- **Appearance and Geometry Optimization:** The undeformed convex radiance field C(0) is optimized using the first multi-view frames. The loss function L combines L1 and D-SSIM image reconstruction losses with a regularization term L_m to manage the number of convexes, similar to 3DCS.
- **Physical Model Optimization:** To recover physical properties such as Young’s modulus (E) and Poisson’s ratio (ν), along with the neural skinning field W_θ, these parameters are jointly optimized. This optimization utilizes single front-view video supervision, minimizing an L2 loss between rendered and ground-truth pixel colors across time steps (L_sim). The differentiability of both the simulation and rendering components allows for end-to-end optimization of physical and material parameters from video observations.
- **Feed-forward Physical System Identification:** To enhance generalizability, the model incorporates a feed-forward physical system identification framework. A pretrained video Vision Transformer extracts motion cues and prior physical knowledge from large-scale video data. Small MLP regression heads then provide an initial coarse estimation of {E, ν, θ}, which is subsequently refined through the differentiable simulation process to align with reference videos.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PhysConvex involved four primary tasks: physical system identification, dynamic reconstruction, future state prediction, and physical generalization capability, comparing against baselines such as PAC-NeRF, Spring-GS, GIC, and Vid2Sim.

#### 4.1. Physical System Identification

PhysConvex demonstrated proficiency in recovering physical properties. Across 12 Google Scanned Objects (GSO) test cases, the method achieved the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) for both log(E) (0.37) and ν (0.04). This performance surpassed PAC-NeRF (log(E): 2.50, ν: 0.21), GIC (log(E): 2.01, ν: 0.16), and Vid2Sim (log(E): 0.51, ν: 0.06), indicating improved accuracy in estimating material elastic parameters.

#### 4.2. Dynamic Reconstruction

In dynamic reconstruction, PhysConvex achieved superior visual quality and physical realism. Quantitative metrics showed that PhysConvex outperformed baselines:
- **PSNR:** 30.00 (Ours) compared to 28.57 (Vid2Sim w/o LGM), 22.06 (PAC-NeRF), 20.91 (Spring-Gaus), and 21.01 (GIC).
- **SSIM:** 0.962 (Ours) compared to 0.952 (Vid2Sim w/o LGM), 0.924 (PAC-NeRF), 0.920 (Spring-Gaus), and 0.934 (GIC).
- **FoVVDP:** 8.247 (Ours) compared to 7.794 (Vid2Sim w/o LGM), 6.674 (PAC-NeRF), 6.410 (Spring-Gaus), and 6.544 (GIC).
Qualitatively, the method preserved sharp details and dynamic fidelity more effectively than prior methods, which often produced blurred textures or inaccurate dynamics, especially for complex elastodynamics.

#### 4.3. Future State Prediction

For temporal generalization, PhysConvex demonstrated robust performance in predicting future states. After observing 16 frames, the method predicted the subsequent 8 frames with high accuracy:
- **PSNR:** 25.71 (Ours) compared to 25.07 (Vid2Sim).
- **SSIM:** 0.948 (Ours) compared to 0.945 (Vid2Sim).
- **FoVVDP:** 7.683 (Ours) compared to 7.770 (Vid2Sim).
These results indicate that PhysConvex maintained competitive accuracy, suggesting improved physical consistency in temporal evolution compared to other methods.

#### 4.4. Generalization Capability

The research demonstrated that PhysConvex generalizes to varied physical scenarios:
- **Different Materials:** The method accurately captured the distinct behaviors of Elastic (stiff and soft), Plasticine, and Sand materials by utilizing corresponding energy densities for each constitutive model.
- **Complex Boundary and Force Conditions:** PhysConvex was shown to be robust to varying environmental dynamics, producing stable, high-quality reconstructions and physically consistent animations under novel external forces (e.g., continuous pushing force) and complex boundary interactions (e.g., sliding on a continuously moving floor).

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

Ablation studies were conducted on the number of 3D points *K* per convex hull and the number of reduced degrees of freedom *M* (skinning handles).
- **Number of Convex Points (K):** Increasing *K* from 3 to 6 generally improved reconstruction quality, with optimal performance observed around *K*=6 for dynamic reconstruction and future prediction. Further increases beyond *K*=6 showed diminishing returns or slight decreases in some metrics, alongside increased computational cost.
- **Number of Reduced DOFs (M):** Increasing *M* from 8 to 10 or 12 generally showed improvements in deformation expressivity and reconstruction, with a balance around M=10 for K=6 achieving good results.
These studies indicate that the chosen parameters provide a balance between model complexity and performance.

#### 4.6. Efficiency Comparison

PhysConvex exhibited efficiency advantages in terms of training time and primitive count:
- **Average Training Time:** 6 minutes (Ours) compared to 8 minutes (Vid2Sim), 32 minutes (Spring-Gaus), 51 minutes (PAC-NeRF), and 69 minutes (GIC).
- **Primitives Number:** 23,681 (Ours) compared to 31,363 (Vid2Sim) and 22,882 (Spring-Gaus).
This efficiency is attributed to the compact representation offered by convex primitives and the performance gains from reduced-order simulation with an implicit Euler solver. All tests were conducted on a single NVIDIA-RTX A6000 GPU.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PhysConvex framework contributes to the fields of computer vision, computer graphics, and physics-based simulation by addressing the challenge of unifying visual realism and physical consistency in the reconstruction and simulation of dynamic 3D scenes.

The introduction of **boundary-driven dynamic convex radiance fields** represents a significant advancement over existing neural primitives (e.g., voxels, ellipsoids). By enabling explicit vertex dynamics and implicit surface dynamics, this representation offers greater flexibility for spatially adaptive, non-uniform deformation and the modeling of evolving boundaries. This capability can lead to more geometrically expressive and physically meaningful deformations, particularly for objects with sharp features or anisotropic material properties, which previous methods struggled to capture accurately.

The development of a **reduced-order convex simulation** using neural skinning eigenmodes as physics-informed deformation bases allows for efficient and accurate simulation of complex geometries and heterogeneous materials. This mesh-free and grid-free approach mitigates common issues in traditional and hybrid simulation methods, such as numerical artifacts, oscillations in large-scale elastodynamics, and reliance on predefined meshes or background grids. The integration of continuum mechanics principles ensures that the simulated dynamics are physically consistent.

The empirical results demonstrate that PhysConvex achieves improved performance across critical metrics, including physical system identification, dynamic reconstruction, and future state prediction. Its ability to accurately estimate material properties, reconstruct dynamic appearances with higher fidelity, and predict future states with greater physical plausibility indicates a more robust and comprehensive understanding of dynamic scenes.

Furthermore, the framework's **generalization capabilities** across novel materials, forces, and boundary conditions highlight its potential for broad applicability. This adaptability suggests that PhysConvex could be used in diverse real-world scenarios where objects interact with their environment in complex ways. The demonstrated efficiency in terms of reduced training time and fewer primitives compared to contemporary baselines also makes the approach practical for deployment.

The potential impact of PhysConvex extends to several application domains:
- **Robotics:** For tasks requiring robots to interact with deformable objects, accurate physical simulation is crucial for planning and control.
- **Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality:** Creating immersive and interactive virtual environments with physically realistic deformable objects can enhance user experience.
- **Content Creation and Animation:** Animators and content creators could leverage this technology to generate complex, physically plausible deformations more efficiently.
- **Engineering and Design:** Understanding material properties and deformation under various conditions could benefit product design and material science research.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as integrating geometry-aware 3D reconstruction and developing intuitive user interaction mechanisms, could further enhance the utility and accessibility of physically grounded 3D dynamics, potentially leveraging advancements in large text-to-3D generation models to streamline interactive scene authoring.
