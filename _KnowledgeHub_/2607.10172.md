# On the Efficiency of LoRA Fine-Tuning for Vision-Language-Action Models in Industrial Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers systematically investigated Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for fine-tuning the π0 Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model for industrial robotic manipulation, demonstrating that a LoRA rank of 32 combined with a fully fine-tuned vision encoder achieves performance comparable to Full Fine-Tuning while reducing VRAM usage by 70% (from 36.2 GiB to 10.8 GiB) and trainable parameters by 85%. This efficiency makes VLA model training feasible on consumer-grade GPUs for industrial deployment.

## Problem

- Traditional industrial robotic manipulation systems are inflexible and expensive to reprogram for changing tasks, relying on precise, static programming.
- Deploying large, pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on specific industrial hardware faces an 'embodiment gap' and substantial computational demands from Full Fine-Tuning (FFT), often conflicting with data privacy requirements.
- Previous LoRA studies in VLA contexts lacked systematic characterization of performance scaling with rank and optimal adapter capacity allocation within dual-component VLA architectures like π0.

## Method

- Conducted a systematic experimental study on the efficiency of ==LoRA== for fine-tuning the **π0** VLA model on a Universal Robots UR5e for a suite of industrial assembly tasks.
- Investigated how robotic manipulation performance scales across a wide range of LoRA ranks (r=8 to 256) and evaluated different strategies for distributing ==LoRA adapter== capacity between the VLM backbone and the ==Action Expert==.
- Performed ablation studies by selectively freezing or applying LoRA to specific model components, including the ==SigLIP== vision encoder, and analyzed the intrinsic dimensionality of ==Full Fine-Tuning (FFT)== weight updates using ==Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)==.

## Results

- LoRA configurations with ranks **r ≥ 32** achieved **Average Task Progress (ATP)** statistically indistinguishable from Full Fine-Tuning (FFT), with the optimal **r=32** variant performing within **two percentage points** of FFT.
- The optimal LoRA configuration (r=32 for VLM and AE, full fine-tuning for SigLIP) reduced static peak VRAM requirements by approximately **70%** (from **36.2 GiB to 10.8 GiB**) and trainable parameters to **15%** of the FFT baseline.
- Ablation studies confirmed that freezing either the VLM backbone or the SigLIP vision encoder drastically impaired task performance (ATP dropping to **~0.15**), underscoring their critical roles in adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective embodiment adaptation for VLA models requires plasticity in both the Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone for semantic understanding and the dedicated Action Expert for motor control.
> - The vision encoder (SigLIP) is a critical component that requires full fine-tuning (or high-rank adaptation) to effectively bridge the visual domain shift from pre-training to the robotic workspace.
> - While Full Fine-Tuning (FFT) involves high-dimensional weight updates across model components, task-relevant adaptation for robotic manipulation can be effectively captured within a lower-dimensional subspace by LoRA at modest ranks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional industrial assembly systems rely on precisely programmed robotic manipulators, which are effective for repetitive, high-volume tasks but require extensive reprogramming when tasks or parts change. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm shift, offering the potential for robots to learn and execute complex manipulation tasks from natural language instructions and demonstrations, thus enhancing flexibility and reducing reprogramming costs.

However, deploying these large, often billion-parameter, foundation models on specific industrial hardware presents challenges. A significant "embodiment gap" exists between the diverse pre-training data used for these models and the specific kinematics and sensing modalities of a target robotic manipulator. This gap necessitates a fine-tuning phase to adapt the pre-trained model to the real-world robotic system.

Full Fine-Tuning (FFT), which updates all model parameters, offers maximal plasticity but demands substantial computational resources, typically requiring data center-grade GPUs. This can conflict with industrial data privacy requirements, as proprietary training data might need to be transferred to external cloud providers. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) introduced by Hu et al. (2021), offer an alternative by injecting small, trainable low-rank matrices while keeping most pre-trained weights frozen. LoRA has demonstrated effectiveness in natural language processing and computer vision domains, reducing memory consumption and the number of trainable parameters.

Previous work on VLA models, such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, and Octo, has explored various architectures and training methodologies. Notably, flow-matching VLAs like π0 separate a Vision-Language-Model (VLM) backbone, responsible for semantic understanding, from a dedicated Action Expert (AE) that generates continuous motor commands. While OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) compared adaptation strategies and reported that LoRA could match FFT performance for certain configurations, it did not systematically characterize the relationship between LoRA rank and robotic manipulation performance or investigate optimal adapter capacity allocation within dual-component VLA architectures like π0. Other VLA models like NeuroVLA, GR00T, and SmolVLA continue to explore efficient and specialized designs.

This research addresses these specific gaps by conducting a systematic study of LoRA's application to π0 for industrial robotic manipulation. It investigates how performance scales with LoRA rank, how adapter capacity should be distributed between the VLM backbone and the action expert, and the necessity of adaptation in different model components, including the vision encoder. By analyzing the intrinsic dimensionality of FFT weight updates, the study aims to provide insights into why low-rank adaptation can be effective for complex robotic tasks, thereby contributing to the practical deployment of VLA models in resource-constrained industrial settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the efficiency and effectiveness of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, specifically π0, for industrial robotic manipulation tasks. The overarching motivation stems from the need to bridge the "embodiment gap" that arises when deploying large, pre-trained VLA foundation models on specific industrial hardware, while simultaneously addressing practical constraints such as computational resources and data privacy.

The detailed motivations and specific objectives are:

- **Addressing Computational and Privacy Constraints:** Full Fine-Tuning (FFT) of billion-parameter VLA models requires high-end, data center-grade GPUs, which are often costly and may necessitate transferring proprietary industrial data to external cloud providers, raising privacy concerns. LoRA is explored as a parameter-efficient alternative that can reduce GPU memory requirements and enable training on more accessible, consumer-grade hardware, mitigating these issues.

- **Characterizing LoRA Performance Scaling with Rank:** Prior studies on LoRA in VLA contexts have typically evaluated only a limited number of LoRA configurations. A key objective is to systematically study how robotic manipulation performance scales across a wide range of LoRA ranks (r=8 to 256). This aims to characterize the full capacity-performance curve and identify the optimal rank for task-relevant adaptation.

- **Optimizing Adapter Capacity Allocation in Dual-Component VLAs:** Flow-matching VLA models like π0 employ a split architecture, consisting of a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone and a dedicated Action Expert (AE). A specific objective is to determine how a fixed LoRA adapter budget should be distributed between these two components to maximize performance. This involves comparing uniform allocation with asymmetric strategies (VLM-heavy vs. AE-heavy).

- **Assessing the Necessity of VLM Adaptation:** The research aims to ascertain whether embodiment adaptation is solely a motor control problem or if it also requires adaptation of the VLM's semantic understanding. This is investigated by freezing the VLM entirely and fine-tuning only the action expert.

- **Determining the Role of Vision Encoder Adaptation:** The Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-training (SigLIP) vision encoder is a critical component for visual perception. A specific objective is to evaluate whether LoRA's performance depends on unconstrained visual adaptation. This involves comparing full fine-tuning of SigLIP with applying LoRA to it or freezing it altogether.

- **Understanding the Intrinsic Dimensionality of Adaptation:** To provide a theoretical underpinning for LoRA's effectiveness, an objective is to perform a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) analysis of the weight deltas from Full Fine-Tuning (FFT). This aims to characterize the intrinsic dimensionality of the embodiment adaptation process, revealing if task-critical changes reside in a low-rank subspace despite high overall parameter counts.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to provide practical guidelines for efficiently fine-tuning VLA models for industrial robotic manipulation, making advanced AI-driven robotics more accessible and deployable in real-world industrial settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic experimental study involving a physical robotic manipulator, a defined task suite, controlled data collection, various fine-tuning configurations, and an intrinsic rank analysis.

#### 3.1. Hardware Platform

All experiments were conducted using a **Universal Robots UR5e**, a 6-Degree-of-Freedom (DoF) manipulator equipped with a **Robotiq 2F-85 gripper**. Visual observations were captured by two **Stereolabs cameras**: a ZED 2i mounted on the robot's upper arm link providing a global workspace view, and a wrist-mounted ZED Mini supplying a stereo egocentric perspective. These three RGB streams constituted the observation space for the π0 policy. Expert demonstrations were collected using a **GELLO teleoperation device**, a 3D-printed leader arm kinematically isomorphic to the UR5e, mapping joint-space commands to the robot at 60 Hz. The setup integrated custom hardware plugins for the UR5e, GELLO, and a ZeroMQ-based camera streaming pipeline within the open-source **LeRobot** and **OpenPi** frameworks.

#### 3.2. Task Suite

The evaluation involved four precision assembly tasks designed to represent various industrial manipulation challenges:
1. **Bolt Insertion (Easy):** Grasping a bolt and inserting it into a cylindrical hole, primarily requiring position accuracy.
2. **Bolt Insertion (Hard):** Inserting a bolt into a hexagonal recess, demanding precise joint position and orientation alignment.
3. **Pick & Place:** Identifying a specific bolt among distractors on a tray and placing it into a bin.
4. **Bearing Press-Fit:** Grasping a bearing and pressing it flush into a housing, a contact-rich task requiring sustained downward force.
Each task was decomposed into sequential sub-goals for evaluation using the Average Task Progress (ATP) metric.

#### 3.3. Data Collection

A total of 800 expert demonstrations were collected, 200 for each of the four tasks, amounting to approximately 5.5 hours of synchronized data. Each episode recorded 6-DoF joint positions, 1-DoF gripper state, 7-DoF teleoperator-demanded manipulator and gripper positions (actions), and three RGB camera streams at 672×376 resolution, all at 60 Hz. The data was stored in the LeRobot Dataset v3.0 format. Before training, per-dimension mean and standard deviation were computed across the entire corpus for state and action vectors, which were used for normalization.

#### 3.4. Training Configurations

A range of 12 distinct training configurations were evaluated (summarized in Table 1 of the paper), including:
- **Zero-shot:** No fine-tuning.
- **Full Fine-Tuning (FFT):** All model parameters updated.
- **LoRA with varying ranks:** Uniform LoRA ranks (r=8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256) applied to both the VLM backbone and action expert.
- **Asymmetric LoRA allocations:** "AE-heavy" (r_VLM=16, r_AE=128) and "VLM-heavy" (r_VLM=128, r_AE=16).
- **Component-freezing ablations:** "AE-only FFT" (VLM frozen, FFT on action expert), "LoRA r=32 + SigLIP LoRA", and "LoRA r=32 + SigLIP frozen".
All LoRA configurations used an α=r scaling factor. LoRA adapters targeted query, key, value, and output projections, as well as feedforward layers, in both the VLM and action expert. The SigLIP vision encoder was fully fine-tuned across all LoRA configurations unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Training parameters included:
- **Optimizer:** AdamW.
- **Learning Rate Schedule:** Cosine decay (1,000 warmup steps, peak 2.5×10⁻⁵, decay to 2.5×10⁻⁶).
- **Action Horizon:** H=30 steps.
- **Hardware:** FFT and high-rank LoRA (r=128, 256) experiments utilized a single NVIDIA H100 (80 GB) with batch sizes of 32 and 24, respectively. Other experiments ran on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB) with a batch size of 24.
- **Precision:** Mixed-precision training (bfloat16 activations, float32 optimizer states for FFT, bfloat16 backbone weights for LoRA).

#### 3.5. Intrinsic Rank Analysis

To understand the underlying dimensionality of adaptation, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) was performed on the weight deltas (∆W = W_fine-tuned - W_pre-trained) of FFT-adapted layers. The analysis focused on Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) layers (gating and down-projection weights), which constitute a significant portion of parameters in the VLM, action expert, and SigLIP vision encoder (18, 18, and 27 transformer layers, respectively). The metric for intrinsic rank was defined as the rank k at which the cumulative squared singular values reached 95% (τ=0.95) of the total Frobenius norm (P_k=1 σ²_i ≥ τ · ||∆W||²_F).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding LoRA fine-tuning for VLA models in industrial robotic manipulation:

#### 4.1. LoRA Performance and Rank Scaling

- **Performance Saturation:** Uniform LoRA configurations with ranks r ≥ 32 demonstrated performance statistically indistinguishable from Full Fine-Tuning (FFT). The best LoRA variant (r=32) achieved an Average Task Progress (ATP) within two percentage points of FFT.
- **No Significant Drop for Optimal LoRA:** Mann-Whitney U tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction indicated no significant differences between LoRA configurations with r ≥ 32 and FFT (all p > 0.05). Performance increased from r=8 to r=32 and then plateaued (Figure 3 in the paper), suggesting that a rank of 32 sufficiently captures the task-relevant adaptation.
- **Substantial Performance Degradation for Ablations:** Configurations with a frozen VLM or restricted SigLIP vision encoder (LoRA or frozen) performed significantly worse than FFT (p < 0.001), indicating their critical role.

#### 4.2. Uniform Allocation Sufficiency

- **Asymmetric vs. Uniform LoRA:** Both asymmetric LoRA allocations (VLM-heavy and AE-heavy) performed at or below the uniform r=32 configuration, despite utilizing a greater number of trainable parameters. This outcome suggests that adaptation capacity is beneficial for both the VLM and the action expert, and that concentrating resources on one component is not optimal in this context.

#### 4.3. Essential Role of VLM and Vision Encoder Adaptation

- **VLM Adaptation Necessity:** Freezing the VLM entirely and fine-tuning only the action expert resulted in an ATP of 0.15, which is significantly lower than FFT performance (p < 0.001, large effect size). This finding indicates that embodiment adaptation requires more than just motor control learning; the VLM's semantic and general understanding also needs to adapt.
- **Vision Encoder Adaptation Criticality:** Similarly, freezing the SigLIP vision encoder yielded an ATP of 0.14, comparable to the VLM-frozen result. Applying LoRA to SigLIP partially improved performance (ATP=0.43) but remained significantly worse than the baseline LoRA r=32 with a fully fine-tuned SigLIP (p < 0.001, medium effect size). These results collectively suggest that both semantic and visual plasticity within the VLA model are necessary for effective adaptation to the target environment.

#### 4.4. Intrinsic Rank Analysis of FFT Weight Updates

- **High Intrinsic Rank for FFT:** SVD analysis of FFT weight deltas revealed that adaptation in MLP layers is high-dimensional across all components. To capture 95% of the Frobenius norm energy, the required ranks were substantial: approximately 441 for action expert MLPs, 597 for SigLIP vision encoder MLPs, and 1,402 for VLM MLPs (Figure 4 in the paper).
- **Modest Energy Capture by LoRA:** At common LoRA ranks, only a fraction of the spectral energy was captured; for example, at r=32, action expert MLPs captured around 57% and VLM MLPs only about 37%.
- **Structural Differences in Adaptation:** After normalizing rank by the maximum possible rank, action expert MLPs consistently exhibited a smaller fraction of their available spectrum (around 31% at 95% energy) compared to VLM MLPs (around 47%) and SigLIP vision encoder MLPs (around 52%). This indicates that the lower intrinsic dimensionality of action expert updates is a structural property, and that the SigLIP vision encoder undergoes the most distributed adaptation among the MLP components.
- **Per-Layer Heterogeneity:** A per-layer breakdown revealed significant heterogeneity in intrinsic rank. The VLM's last MLP layer showed a sharp increase in intrinsic rank (reaching ~3,500), while the action expert's last layers showed a decrease (Figure 5 in the paper).

#### 4.5. Memory and Efficiency

- **Parameter and VRAM Reduction:** LoRA at r=32 reduced trainable parameters to 15.0% of the FFT baseline. Critically, it reduced the static peak VRAM requirement (for parameters and optimizer states, excluding activation memory) from 36.2 GiB for FFT to an estimated 10.8 GiB (Table 3 in the paper). This reduction enables training on consumer-grade GPUs, such as an NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB).
- **Further Reduction with SigLIP LoRA/Frozen:** While applying LoRA to SigLIP or freezing it further reduced trainable parameters and VRAM, it came with a significant performance penalty.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides insights into the effective and efficient fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for industrial robotic manipulation, with several practical and theoretical implications.

#### 5.1. Practical Guidelines for Deployment

The study establishes a concrete and resource-efficient fine-tuning strategy for flow-matching VLAs like π0. The results suggest that applying LoRA with uniform allocation at a rank of r=16 to 32 to the VLM and action expert, while **fully fine-tuning the SigLIP vision encoder**, offers comparable task performance to Full Fine-Tuning (FFT). This configuration significantly reduces static peak VRAM requirements (by up to 70%, from 36.2 GiB to 10.8 GiB), making it feasible to train these large models on single consumer-grade GPUs rather than requiring expensive data center-grade hardware. This addresses key barriers to industrial adoption, including hardware cost and potential data privacy concerns associated with cloud-based training for large models.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Understanding of VLA Adaptation Dynamics

The intrinsic rank analysis of FFT weight deltas offers a mechanistic explanation for why LoRA can match FFT performance despite its lower parameter count. The finding that FFT weight deltas are high-dimensional, yet LoRA at lower ranks achieves comparable task performance, suggests that the critical, task-relevant adaptation occurs within a lower-dimensional subspace. The remaining high-dimensional changes in FFT might represent noise or less pertinent adjustments that do not significantly contribute to downstream manipulation performance for the tasks evaluated. This contributes to the broader understanding of parameter-efficient fine-tuning effectiveness beyond empirical observations.

#### 5.3. Insights into Architectural Adaptation Requirements

The research provides insights into the distinct roles and adaptation requirements of different components within a dual-component VLA architecture like π0.
- **VLM's Crucial Role:** The necessity of VLM adaptation (semantic plasticity) for effective embodiment adaptation is confirmed. Freezing the VLM drastically impairs performance, indicating that the VLM is not merely a feature extractor but undergoes critical adjustments to align its understanding with the robotic embodiment.
- **Vision Encoder's Plasticity:** The study underscores the critical importance of full plasticity for the vision encoder (SigLIP). LoRA application or freezing of SigLIP resulted in significant performance degradation, suggesting that the visual domain shift from pre-training to the robot's workspace demands high-rank adaptation that low-rank updates cannot adequately provide. This has direct implications for PEFT strategies, indicating that not all model components can be similarly constrained.
- **Heterogeneity of Layer-wise Adaptation:** The per-layer rank analysis reveals that adaptation requirements vary significantly across transformer layers, with the VLM's final MLP layer showing particularly high intrinsic rank. This heterogeneity highlights the complex nature of model fine-tuning and the potential for more nuanced, layer-specific PEFT strategies in future work.

#### 5.4. Contribution to Reproducibility and Open Science

The authors' commitment to releasing open-source hardware integrations and training code (LeRobot and OpenPi extensions) contributes to reproducibility and facilitates further research in this domain. This accelerates the development and evaluation of VLA models for robotic manipulation by providing accessible tools and baselines.

#### 5.5. Limitations and Future Directions

The study acknowledges limitations, including its focus on a single robotic platform (UR5e) and a single VLA architecture (π0). Generalization to other embodiments or VLA architectures would require further investigation. The evaluation used 20 rollouts per task, which, while providing reasonable statistical power for ATP, may limit the detection of very small performance effects. A notable limitation concerns the LoRA scaling convention (α=r), which might induce gradient collapse at larger ranks. Future work could explore alternative rank-stabilized scalings like rsLoRA (α/√r) to fully characterize the capacity ceiling.
