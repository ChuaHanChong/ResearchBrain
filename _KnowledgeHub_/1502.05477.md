# Trust Region Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) introduces a method for optimizing deep neural network policies in reinforcement learning by constraining policy updates within a trust region defined by a KL divergence, ensuring more stable and monotonic improvements. The algorithm successfully trains deep policies for challenging continuous control tasks like robotic locomotion and achieves competitive results on Atari games using raw pixel inputs.

## Problem

- Policy gradient methods suffered from instability and non-monotonic performance when optimizing large, nonlinear policies (neural networks), often leading to performance collapse.
- Selecting appropriate learning rates (step sizes) for policy gradient updates was challenging; overly large steps could destabilize, while overly small steps slowed learning.
- Existing methods struggled to scale effectively to high-dimensional, deep policies for continuous control tasks while maintaining stability.

## Method

- Transforms the policy optimization problem into a constrained maximization, ensuring policy updates remain within a ==trust region== defined by a maximum allowable ==KL divergence== from the old policy.
- Approximately solves the constrained optimization by linearizing the objective function and quadratically approximating the KL divergence constraint (using the ==Fisher Information Matrix==).
- Uses the ==Conjugate Gradient== method to efficiently compute the search direction and a ==line search== to ensure actual improvement and constraint satisfaction after approximations.

## Results

- Demonstrated robust and stable learning of complex gaits for simulated 2D robots (swimmer, hopper, walker) using generic neural networks, outperforming traditional natural gradient and derivative-free methods.
- Achieved competitive and consistent performance on various Atari 2600 games using raw image inputs and convolutional neural network policies, showcasing its generality across diverse high-dimensional tasks.
- Empirically showed a consistent tendency towards monotonic policy improvement, requiring minimal hyperparameter tuning for the critical step size.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Replacing a penalty term on KL divergence with a hard constraint (trust region) is crucial for robustly controlling policy changes and guaranteeing more stable, monotonic improvements.
> - The use of the Fisher Information Matrix in the optimization naturally scales the policy gradient, effectively implementing a natural gradient update within the trust region framework.
> - Approximations to the theoretical monotonic improvement guarantee, coupled with careful optimization (conjugate gradient, line search), can lead to practical and effective deep reinforcement learning algorithms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Before TRPO, the field of reinforcement learning (RL) was broadly categorized into value-based methods (like Q-learning), policy iteration methods, policy gradient methods, and derivative-free optimization methods.

- **Policy gradient methods** (e.g., REINFORCE, Actor-Critic) aim to directly optimize a parameterized policy by estimating the gradient of the expected return. While theoretically appealing, they often suffered from instability, especially when applied to large, nonlinear policies like neural networks. Small changes in policy parameters could lead to drastic and unpredictable changes in policy behavior, causing performance to collapse or oscillate. Choosing an appropriate learning rate (step size) was a major hyperparameter tuning challenge.
- **Derivative-free methods** (e.g., CEM, CMA-ES) were robust and simpler to implement, often achieving good results on problems with low-dimensional policy parameterizations. However, they scaled poorly with high-dimensional policies, exhibiting high sample complexity.
- **Deep Q-Networks (DQN)**, popularized by DeepMind around the same time (Mnih et al., 2013), showed the power of deep learning for value-based methods on tasks like Atari games. However, DQN is primarily applicable to discrete action spaces and relies on off-policy learning with experience replay, which can complicate its theoretical guarantees and direct application to continuous control.

TRPO emerged as a response to the instability and scalability issues faced by policy gradient methods when attempting to train **deep, high-dimensional policies** for **continuous control tasks**. Previous attempts to achieve stable updates often involved using natural policy gradients (Kakade, 2002), which use the Fisher Information Matrix to correctly scale gradients in parameter space, preventing overly large steps. However, these methods typically relied on a fixed step size or penalty coefficient, which was still prone to instability or overly conservative updates.

TRPO builds directly on the theoretical foundations of natural policy gradients and conservative policy iteration (Kakade & Langford, 2002), which provided a lower bound on policy improvement. The key innovation is to transform this theoretical lower bound into a practical optimization procedure by replacing the *penalty* on the KL divergence with a *hard constraint* (a "trust region"). This trust region constraint ensures that each policy update remains within a region where the local approximation of the objective function is valid, thus guaranteeing more stable and monotonic improvements.

This shift from a penalty to a constraint proved critical. While penalty coefficients (like learning rates) are notoriously hard to tune and often lead to either overly small or catastrophically large steps, a fixed KL divergence constraint (δ) provides a more robust and intuitive way to control policy change. TRPO therefore bridges the gap between theoretical guarantees for policy improvement and practical, scalable deep reinforcement learning. It became one of the first widely successful algorithms for applying deep neural networks to continuous control problems, paving the way for subsequent off-shoots and improvements, notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), also developed by Schulman, which further simplified TRPO's optimization for broader applicability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind TRPO are:

- **Guaranteed Monotonic Policy Improvement:** The authors aim to develop an iterative policy optimization procedure that offers theoretical guarantees of non-decreasing expected returns. This directly addresses the instability and non-monotonic performance often observed in traditional policy gradient methods, especially when using complex function approximators.
- **Robust Step Size Selection:** A major challenge in policy gradient methods is determining how large a step to take in the parameter space. Too large a step can lead to policy collapse, while too small a step can make learning prohibitively slow. TRPO seeks a robust mechanism for controlling the magnitude of policy updates without extensive hyperparameter tuning. The idea of a "trust region" – where the update is constrained to a region where the local approximations are reliable – is central to this.
- **Scalability to Large, Nonlinear Policies (Neural Networks):** The paper explicitly targets the challenge of optimizing policies parameterized by high-dimensional, nonlinear function approximators like deep neural networks. Prior methods often struggled with this due to the aforementioned instability or unfavorable sample complexity.
- **Generality Across Diverse Tasks:** The authors aim for an algorithm that is general enough to be applied to a wide variety of reinforcement learning problems, including both continuous control tasks (like robotics) and tasks with complex, high-dimensional observations (like Atari games using raw image input).
- **Model-Free Learning:** The algorithm is designed to operate in a model-free setting, meaning it does not require an explicit model of the environment's dynamics, relying solely on interactions with the environment to collect data.

In essence, TRPO was motivated by the need for a theoretically sound, practically robust, and scalable algorithm that could effectively train deep neural network policies across a broad spectrum of reinforcement learning problems, overcoming the limitations of existing policy optimization techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TRPO's methodology combines theoretical insights from conservative policy iteration with practical approximations and efficient numerical optimization techniques.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Foundation: Monotonic Improvement Guarantee

The core theoretical contribution is extending the monotonic improvement guarantee for policy updates beyond mixture policies (as in Kakade & Langford, 2002) to general stochastic policies.
The key identity states that the expected return of a new policy ($\tilde{\pi}$) relative to an old policy ($\pi$) can be expressed as:
$\eta(\tilde{\pi}) = \eta(\pi) + E_{s_0,a_0,\dots \sim \tilde{\pi}} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t A_\pi(s_t, a_t) \right]$
where $A_\pi(s, a)$ is the advantage function.

The authors introduce a local approximation of $\eta(\tilde{\pi})$, denoted $L_\pi(\tilde{\pi})$, which uses the state visitation frequency of the *old* policy ($\rho_\pi$) instead of the new one ($\rho_{\tilde{\pi}}$):
$L_\pi(\tilde{\pi}) = \eta(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_\pi(s) \sum_a \tilde{\pi}(a|s) A_\pi(s, a)$
While $L_\pi(\tilde{\pi})$ matches $\eta(\tilde{\pi})$ to first order, it doesn't guarantee improvement for large steps. To address this, the authors prove a lower bound on the true policy improvement:
$\eta(\tilde{\pi}) \geq L_\pi(\tilde{\pi}) - C D_{\text{max KL}}(\pi, \tilde{\pi})$
where $D_{\text{max KL}}(\pi, \tilde{\pi}) = \max_s D_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot|s) \parallel \tilde{\pi}(\cdot|s))$, and $C = \frac{4\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}$ is a constant based on the discount factor and maximum advantage. This implies that maximizing $L_\pi(\tilde{\pi}) - C D_{\text{max KL}}(\pi, \tilde{\pi})$ guarantees monotonic improvement.

#### 3.2. Practical Algorithm: Trust Region Constraint

In practice, the theoretical constant $C$ often leads to impractically small steps. To enable larger, yet robust, steps, TRPO replaces the penalty term with a hard constraint, akin to a trust region method in numerical optimization:
$\underset{\theta}{\text{maximize}} \quad L_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\theta)$
$\text{subject to} \quad D_{\text{max KL}}(\theta_{\text{old}}, \theta) \leq \delta$

Solving this with $D_{\text{max KL}}$ (a maximum over states) is intractable due to the large number of constraints. TRPO uses a heuristic approximation by constraining the *average* KL divergence over states:
$\underset{\theta}{\text{maximize}} \quad E_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s) A_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(s, a) \right]$
$\text{subject to} \quad E_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} [D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|s) \parallel \pi_\theta(\cdot|s))] \leq \delta$

#### 3.3. Sample-Based Estimation

To implement this model-free, the expectations are replaced by sample averages using Monte Carlo simulations. Two primary sampling schemes are proposed:

- **Single Path:** Standard on-policy sampling. Trajectories are collected by rolling out the current policy ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$). Advantage estimates ($A_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) are derived from discounted sums of future rewards along these trajectories. This is common in policy gradient methods and is suitable for physical systems as it requires no state resets.
- **Vine:** This method involves collecting "trunk" trajectories to identify a set of states. From these states, multiple "branch" rollouts are performed for different actions, using common random numbers (CRN) to reduce variance in Q-value estimation. This method offers lower variance estimates of the advantage function but requires the ability to reset the environment to specific states (typically only feasible in simulation).

The objective function uses importance sampling to re-weight state-action pairs sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ to estimate the objective for $\pi_\theta$.

#### 3.4. Optimization Procedure

The constrained optimization problem is solved approximately using a sequence of steps:
1. **Linearize Objective, Quadraticize Constraint:** The objective $L_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\theta)$ is approximated linearly by its gradient at $\theta_{\text{old}}$. The KL divergence constraint is approximated quadratically by its Hessian (which is the Fisher Information Matrix, FIM). This transforms the problem into a form solvable by methods like conjugate gradient.
2. **Conjugate Gradient (CG) for Search Direction:** The FIM is typically too large to form explicitly. CG is used to find the search direction $s$ that solves $A s = g$, where $g$ is the gradient of the objective and $A$ is the FIM. A crucial detail is that FIM-vector products ($Ay$) can be computed efficiently via automatic differentiation, avoiding explicit matrix formation. The authors note they compute the *analytic* FIM rather than the empirical FIM (covariance of gradients) for computational benefits.
3. **Line Search:** After finding a direction $s$, a line search is performed along this direction (i.e., finding a step size $\beta$ such that $\theta_{\text{new}} = \theta_{\text{old}} + \beta s$). This line search ensures that the actual (nonlinear) objective improves and the actual (nonlinear) KL divergence constraint is satisfied, starting with a $\beta$ derived from the quadratic approximation of the constraint. This safeguards against performance degradation from the approximations made.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

TRPO's experimental results demonstrate its robustness, scalability, and ability to learn complex behaviors in challenging domains.

- **Robust Performance on Robotic Locomotion:**
 - TRPO successfully learned complex gaits for simulated 2D robots (swimmer, hopper, walker) in the MuJoCo simulator. These tasks are challenging due to underactuation, high dimensionality, and contact discontinuities.
 - Notably, TRPO learned these gaits using generic neural networks and simple reward functions, without task-specific hand-engineering of policy classes, which was common in prior locomotion studies.
 - **Comparison with Baselines:** TRPO (both single path and vine variants) significantly outperformed traditional Natural Gradient methods (which use a fixed penalty coefficient) and derivative-free methods (CEM, CMA) on these harder problems. This provides strong empirical evidence for the superiority of a fixed KL divergence constraint over a fixed penalty coefficient for stable and consistent progress.
 - The "Max KL" variant (which imposes a constraint on the maximum KL divergence across states, as justified by the theory) performed similarly to the average KL constraint, suggesting that the practical approximation of averaging KL divergence does not severely degrade performance.

- **Competitive Performance on Atari Games from Raw Images:**
 - TRPO was applied to seven Atari 2600 games, using raw pixel images as input and convolutional neural network policies with tens of thousands of parameters. This demonstrated its capability for high-dimensional, partially observed tasks.
 - While not always outperforming highly specialized methods like Deep Q-Learning or Monte-Carlo Tree Search variants on every single game, TRPO consistently achieved reasonable and often competitive scores. This highlights its generality: the same algorithm and largely the same hyper-parameters could be applied successfully to vastly different domains (continuous control robotics and discrete action game playing).
 - The "single path" and "vine" sampling methods generally showed similar qualitative performance, with "vine" sometimes being more effective on discrete tasks (e.g., Q*bert), suggesting its lower variance estimation of Q-values can be beneficial when state resets are possible.

- **Monotonic Improvement:** Despite the various approximations made to the theoretically justified procedure, the empirical results consistently showed TRPO tending to give monotonic improvement, requiring little hyperparameter tuning (especially for the critical step size).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Trust Region Policy Optimization" paper is a landmark contribution to reinforcement learning for several reasons:

- **Bridging Theory and Practice for Deep RL:** TRPO provided a robust, theoretically grounded framework for training large, nonlinear deep neural network policies. It showed how to combine the stability guarantees of trust region methods with the representational power of deep learning, a crucial step for the success of Deep Reinforcement Learning.
- **Addressing Instability of Policy Gradients:** By introducing the fixed KL divergence constraint, TRPO effectively solved the chronic instability problem of traditional policy gradient methods when used with deep function approximators. This made deep RL training far more reliable and less sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, enabling broader application.
- **Generality and Scalability:** The successful application of TRPO to diverse, challenging domains—from continuous control in robotics to complex pixel-based Atari games—demonstrated its broad applicability and scalability to high-dimensional problems. This generality was a significant leap forward, moving away from task-specific policy architectures or learning algorithms.
- **Foundation for Future Algorithms:** TRPO's core ideas, particularly the use of a trust region (or "proximal region") to constrain policy updates, have been highly influential. It directly inspired subsequent algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), also by Schulman, which further simplified the optimization problem, making it even more computationally efficient and widely adopted as a baseline for many RL tasks. PPO is essentially a simplified, more accessible version of TRPO, a testament to TRPO's foundational insights.
- **Enabling Complex Behaviors:** The ability to learn complex robotic gaits from scratch with minimal prior knowledge and general-purpose neural networks significantly advanced the state-of-the-art in robotic control learning. This opened up new possibilities for autonomous agents capable of learning intricate motor skills.
- **Catalyst for Deep Reinforcement Learning:** Along with DeepMind's DQN, TRPO solidified the potential of Deep Reinforcement Learning. While DQN excelled in discrete action spaces, TRPO offered a powerful solution for continuous control, together demonstrating the immense potential of combining deep learning with reinforcement learning.

In summary, TRPO provided a critical piece of the puzzle for deep reinforcement learning to move beyond academic toy problems and tackle complex, real-world challenges. Its principled approach to stable policy optimization laid the groundwork for much of the subsequent progress in the field, making it one of the most important algorithms in the modern RL landscape.
