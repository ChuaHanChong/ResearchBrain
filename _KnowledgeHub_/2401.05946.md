# Learning Cognitive Maps from Transformer Representations for Efficient Planning in Partially Observed Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google DeepMind developed the Transformer with Discrete Bottleneck (TDB) architecture, which enables transformers to learn explicit, interpretable cognitive maps from sequence data. This allows for near-perfect, exponentially faster planning and navigation in perceptually aliased and novel partially observed environments compared to vanilla transformer models.

## Problem

- Vanilla transformers, despite their predictive power, do not learn explicit, interpretable world models amenable to efficient, goal-directed planning.
- Planning and navigation in partially observed environments (POEs) is challenging due to perceptual aliasing, where identical observations correspond to different true states.
- Traditional transformer-based planning relies on computationally expensive forward rollouts, which are inefficient and often fail in aliased settings.

## Method

- The ==Transformer with Discrete Bottleneck (TDB)== integrates a ==Vector Quantization (VQ) layer== into a causal transformer to compress intermediate representations into discrete latent codes.
- TDB employs augmented training objectives, such as ==multi-step observation prediction== or next encoding prediction (with a teacher network), to force the discrete bottleneck to learn robust, disambiguated latent representations.
- A ==cognitive map (latent graph)== is explicitly constructed from the sequence of learned bottleneck indices, which an external solver then uses for efficient path planning.

## Results

- TDB achieved near-perfect planning accuracy (**99%** success rate with optimal path lengths) in aliased 2D and 3D environments, demonstrating an exponential speed-up over vanilla sequence models.
- The model successfully learned accurate cognitive maps, nearly isomorphic to ground truth environment dynamics (NormGED ~**0.005-0.12**), enabling robust planning even under perceptual aliasing.
- TDB generalized to novel, unseen partially observed environments at inference time, performing near-perfect in-context prediction and planning, and also successfully extracted interpretable structures from abstract text data (GINC dataset).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Augmented training objectives (multi-step prediction, next encoding prediction) are crucial for compelling the discrete bottleneck to learn disambiguated latent states, effectively resolving perceptual aliasing.
> - The TDB architecture can achieve emergent in-context learning, inferring and utilizing accurate cognitive maps for efficient planning in entirely novel, unseen partially observed environments.
> - Extracting an explicit, queryable cognitive map from latent representations allows for planning that is exponentially more efficient than forward rollouts and provides interpretability into the agent's world understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical limitation of modern large language models (LLMs) and transformers: despite their impressive capabilities in sequence prediction, in-context learning, and content generation, they generally *do not learn explicit, interpretable, and queryable world models* that can be leveraged for robust planning and navigation in complex environments. This gap is widely acknowledged in the AI community, as LLMs often struggle with tasks requiring strong planning skills (Valmeekam et al., 2022; Momennejad et al., 2023).

The broader research landscape can be categorized as follows:
- **Limitations of Transformers for Planning:** While transformers excel at sequence modeling (e.g., next-token prediction), their internal representations are often implicit and not easily amenable to querying for planning purposes. This paper directly confronts this by proposing a method to *extract* an explicit world model.
- **Reinforcement Learning and World Models:** In reinforcement learning (RL), learning a "world model" is a long-standing paradigm to enable agents to plan, explore, and generalize. While decision transformers (Chen et al., 2021a) abstract RL as sequence modeling, they often rely on rewards and struggle with high aliasing or large, complex planning problems. This work extends the idea of learning world models within a transformer framework, specifically for partially observed environments (POEs) without explicit reward signals.
- **Interpretable AI and Mechanistic Interpretability:** There's a growing interest in making complex neural networks more interpretable, with efforts to reverse-engineer LLMs to understand their "circuits" (Elhage et al., 2021). This paper contributes to interpretable AI by *designing* a transformer variant that learns an *explicitly interpretable* cognitive map, rather than post-hoc analysis.
- **Cognitive Maps and Spatial Navigation:** The concept of "cognitive maps" has deep roots in neuroscience and cognitive science, referring to mental representations of an environment's spatial layout. Prior work, such as CSCGs (George et al., 2021), has demonstrated the ability to learn such maps in aliased POEs. This paper aims to achieve similar, or superior, capabilities within the high-performance transformer architecture, addressing scalability and in-context learning challenges that traditional probabilistic models like CSCGs might face.
- **Partially Observed Environments (POEs):** Planning in POEs is inherently difficult due to perceptual aliasing, where different true states of the environment can produce identical observations. Agents must rely on historical context to disambiguate their location. This paper specifically targets these challenging environments, where vanilla transformers struggle with exponential costs for path planning.

By introducing the Transformer with Discrete Bottleneck (TDB), the authors aim to bridge the gap between powerful sequence models and the need for explicit, queryable, and interpretable world models for efficient planning, particularly in realistic, perceptually aliased scenarios. This work pushes towards more general and robust AI agents capable of intelligent navigation and decision-making in complex, unknown environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:

1. **Enable Transformers to Learn Explicit, Queryable World Models:** Address the limitation of vanilla transformers, which, despite their predictive power, do not naturally learn interpretable world models that can be flexibly queried for planning.
2. **Facilitate Efficient Planning and Navigation in Partially Observed Environments (POEs):** Develop a transformer-based system that can solve path planning problems in environments where an agent receives perceptually aliased observations, making direct localization and planning difficult.
3. **Extract Interpretable Cognitive Maps:** Design a mechanism within the transformer to automatically construct interpretable cognitive maps (latent graphs) of the environment, representing its states and transitions.
4. **Achieve Superior Planning Efficiency:** Enable planning that is significantly faster than the forward rollout strategies employed by vanilla sequence models, which can scale exponentially in aliased settings.
5. **Demonstrate In-Context Learning for Planning:** Show that the proposed model can not only learn world models from a fixed training environment but also rapidly infer and utilize cognitive maps in *novel, unseen* partially observed environments at inference time.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several critical shortcomings of current state-of-the-art models:

- **Inherent Limitations of Vanilla Transformers:** While excellent at next-token prediction and in-context learning, transformers lack an explicit mechanism to represent the underlying dynamics or "world model" of an environment. This prevents them from performing flexible, goal-directed planning or answering queries about the environment's structure, which is crucial for intelligent agents.
- **Challenge of Perceptual Aliasing:** Real-world environments are often partially observed, meaning identical sensory inputs can correspond to different true states. This "perceptual aliasing" makes path planning extremely difficult, as an agent cannot simply infer its location from its current observation alone but must rely on a history of observations and actions. Vanilla transformers, when forced to plan, resort to computationally expensive forward rollouts, which are inefficient and often fail in aliased settings.
- **Need for Interpretable Representations:** The "black-box" nature of many deep learning models makes it hard to understand *how* they arrive at decisions. Learning an interpretable cognitive map allows for better understanding of the agent's internal world representation and facilitates debugging or human oversight.
- **Desire for Generalist Agents:** For AI agents to operate effectively in diverse and unknown environments, they need to quickly adapt and build internal models of novel surroundings. The ability to learn *in-context* cognitive maps for planning in new POEs is a key step towards more general and adaptable AI.
- **Scalability and Efficiency:** Planning with explicit world models, if learned correctly, can be orders of magnitude more efficient than brute-force simulation or exhaustive search, particularly in complex or aliased scenarios.

In essence, the authors aim to augment the predictive power of transformers with the structured knowledge representation and planning capabilities typically associated with explicit world models, thereby making them more capable and versatile agents in dynamic and partially observed settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology lies in the **Transformer with Discrete Bottleneck(s) (TDB)**, an architectural modification designed to enable explicit world model learning and planning.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The agent operates in a Partially Observed Environment (POE), receiving perceptually aliased observations ($x_n$) and executing discrete actions ($a_n$). No rewards are provided. The objective is twofold:
1. Predict the next observation ($x_{n+1}$) given the history of observations and actions.
2. Build a queryable world model that disambiguates aliasing and can solve path planning tasks.
The model is trained with an autoregressive objective, minimizing the negative log-likelihood of predicting the next observation: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{obs}} = -\sum \log p(x_{n+1} | x_1, a_1, \ldots, x_n, a_n)$.

#### 3.2. Transformer with Discrete Bottleneck (TDB) Architecture

- **Vanilla Transformer Baseline:** The foundation is a standard causal transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) trained for next-token prediction. Observations and actions are mapped to linear embeddings and fed sequentially to the transformer. The output corresponding to each action embedding is then used to predict the next observation. While this achieves high predictive accuracy, it fails at efficient planning due to the lack of an explicit world model.
- **Discrete Bottleneck Integration:** To address this, TDB introduces one or more discrete bottlenecks on top of the transformer. Specifically, the transformer's outputs *before* observing the next action (i.e., representations corresponding to observations $x_n$) are passed through a **Vector Quantization (VQ) layer** (Van Den Oord et al., 2017). This layer compresses the continuous transformer output into a finite number of discrete latent codes from a learnable dictionary $\mathcal{D}$.
 - The quantized latent code, $d_{\phi(T_{2n-1})}$, then concatenates with the *next* action embedding $E_{\text{act}}(a_n)$ to predict the next observation $x_{n+1}$ via a simple MLP.
 - The VQ process introduces a "bottleneck loss" ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{bot}}$), which encourages the transformer to learn compressed representations that are faithful to the dictionary entries, typically involving a straight-through estimator and stop-gradient operations.

#### 3.3. Augmenting the Objective for Robust Representation Learning

Simply using $\mathcal{L}_{\text{obs}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{bot}}$ with a single-step prediction (S=1) can lead to merging of distinct spatial positions that happen to have identical immediate neighbors (perceptual aliasing). To force the discrete bottleneck to learn more robust, disambiguated representations, two solutions are proposed:
1. **Multi-step Observation Prediction (Solution 1):** Instead of only predicting the immediate next observation ($x_{n+1}$), the model is trained to predict the next $S$ observations ($x_{n+1}, \ldots, x_{n+S}$). This forces the latent code $d_{\phi(T_{2n-1})}$ to encode enough information to disambiguate its current state over a longer horizon, making it less likely to merge distinct states. The loss $\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\text{obs}}(n)$ averages predictions over these $S$ steps.
2. **Next Encoding Prediction (Solution 2):** Inspired by self-supervised learning, a teacher-student network architecture is employed. The student network (TDB) is trained to predict both the next observation and the *next quantized encoding from a teacher network*. The teacher network's weights are an exponential moving average of the student's. This "next encoding loss" ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{enc}}$) encourages the latent code to capture predictive information about future internal states, thereby improving the quality of the learned representations.

#### 3.4. Extension to Multiple Discrete Bottlenecks

The TDB architecture can utilize $M > 1$ discrete bottlenecks in parallel. Each bottleneck has its own dictionary of latent codes and contributes its own $\mathcal{L}_{\text{bot}}$. The concatenated outputs from all active bottlenecks form the final latent representation. This design is found to accelerate training, though it's noted to produce redundant rather than disentangled representations.

#### 3.5. Learning a Cognitive Map from Bottleneck Indices

Once the TDB is trained, a cognitive map (a latent graph $\mathcal{G}$) is explicitly constructed from the sequence of active bottleneck indices.
- **Transition Counts:** A count tensor $C$ is built, recording empirical transitions between latent states (tuples of bottleneck indices for $M>1$) conditioned on actions ($C_{ijk} = \mathbb{1}(s_n=i, a_n=j, s_{n+1}=k)$).
- **Graph Construction and Clustering:** This count tensor is then thresholded ($t_{\text{ratio}}$) to form an action-augmented empirical transition graph $\mathcal{G}$.
 - For multiple bottlenecks, initial clustering of bottleneck index tuples is performed using Hamming distance to merge "almost identical" latent states (Appendix C).
 - Further clustering merges nodes in $\mathcal{G}$ that connect to the same set of neighbors.
 - Crucially, any "discarded" latent indices (those not forming robust nodes in $\mathcal{G}$ due to low counts) are mapped to their closest "retained" node using an $L_1$ distance over transition probabilities, ensuring all states can be mapped to the graph.

#### 3.6. Planning with the Cognitive Map

For path planning tasks (e.g., shortest path from A to B), the TDB maps the start and end observations to corresponding nodes in the learned cognitive map $\mathcal{G}$. An external solver (e.g., `networkx.shortest_path`) is then queried to find the shortest path within this latent graph. This external solver can also incorporate constraints on demand.

The key insight for why TDB works is that the augmented objectives (multi-step prediction and next-encoding prediction) compel the discrete bottleneck to learn latent codes that are unique to specific spatial locations, even when those locations initially produce perceptually aliased observations or have identical immediate neighbors. This disambiguation allows the formation of an accurate and reliable cognitive map.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments across various environments to evaluate the TDB model against vanilla transformers and LSTMs.

#### 4.1. Performance in 2D Aliased Rooms and Aliased Cubes

- **Predictive Performance:** TDB, particularly with augmented objectives (S=3 or 'enc'), maintained near-perfect next-observation prediction accuracy (TestAccu > 99%), on par with or exceeding vanilla transformers and LSTMs.
- **Planning Efficiency and Accuracy:** This was the most striking result. Vanilla sequence models (transformer and LSTM) catastrophically failed at path planning in aliased rooms, only improving over a fallback path in ~30% of cases, and when they did, the paths were ~16 times longer than optimal. In contrast, TDB with augmented objectives achieved near-perfect planning, solving shortest path problems ~99% of the time with optimal path lengths (RatioSP=1.00). This demonstrates an **exponential speed-up** over forward rollouts.
- **Cognitive Map Accuracy:** TDB with augmented objectives learned cognitive maps nearly isomorphic to the ground truth environment dynamics (NormGED around 0.12), effectively disambiguating aliasing.
- **Impact of Augmented Objectives:** TDB with only single-step prediction (S=1) performed poorly, confirming the hypothesis that augmented objectives are crucial for learning disambiguated representations. These S=1 TDBs merged observations with identical neighbors, creating unrealistic shortcuts in the latent graph.
- **Multiple Bottlenecks:** Using multiple bottlenecks ($M=4$) significantly accelerated training convergence (e.g., from 8300 to 4000 steps for TDB(S=3)) but did not lead to disentangled representations, showing high redundancy (disentanglement accuracy ~85%).
- **Agent Localization & Teleportation:** The TDB could locate itself after a short context (TestAccu > 99.5% after 30 observations). Early in a sequence, "teleportation" (large jumps in the latent graph) occurred but dropped below 1% after 100 observations, attributed to initial uncertainty and clustering artifacts.
- **Robustness & Constrained Planning:** TDB handled noise during training and could solve constrained path planning problems (e.g., avoiding certain colors) by injecting constraints into the external solver. It also successfully learned maps for non-Euclidean dynamics (aliased cubes).

#### 4.2. Performance in 3D Simulated Environments (Egocentric Views)

- In visually rich 3D environments, TDBs with a single bottleneck struggled to converge, highlighting the increased complexity.
- However, TDBs with multiple bottlenecks ($M=4$) achieved near-perfect prediction accuracy and path planning performance, learning accurate cognitive maps (very low NormGED ~0.005-0.017).
- Vanilla sequence models again failed at planning (ImpFallback ~26%, RatioSP ~16x optimal).
- Redundancy in multiple bottlenecks was even higher (~99% disentanglement accuracy).

#### 4.3. Extension to Text via the GINC Dataset

- TDB was successfully applied to a text dataset (GINC) designed to study in-context learning, where observations (words) are aliased across different underlying "concepts" (factorial HMMs).
- TDB(S=1, M=4) outperformed vanilla transformer and LSTM in in-context accuracy for all context lengths. TDB(S=5, M=1) was best for large contexts.
- TDB achieved higher in-context accuracies (~95%) than previously reported for transformers on GINC.
- The learned latent graph exhibited interpretable structure, with distinct subgraphs corresponding to the five underlying concepts, demonstrating its ability to extract abstract cognitive maps from non-spatial data.

#### 4.4. In-Context Learning for Novel Partially Observed Environments

- This crucial experiment demonstrated TDB's ability to generalize to *unseen* POEs at test time.
- As the number of training rooms increased, a phase transition occurred where TDB (S=3, M=4) exhibited emergent in-context learning.
- It achieved near-perfect in-context prediction accuracy (matching transformers) and **almost perfectly solved in-context path planning problems** in these novel environments.
- The TDB learned accurate in-context cognitive maps (very low NormGED ~0.017), correctly modeling the dynamics of new rooms.
- Vanilla models, while transformers could predict well, still struggled significantly with in-context planning.
- In-context learning performance was found to be driven by spatial exposure to base targets in the training data, demonstrating the importance of diverse training experiences.

In summary, the TDB model successfully addresses major limitations of vanilla transformers, providing a robust, efficient, and interpretable solution for planning in perceptually aliased and novel environments. The augmented objectives are key to its success, and while multiple bottlenecks aid training, they do so through redundancy rather than disentanglement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the development of more capable and general-purpose AI agents, particularly in bridging the gap between powerful sequence modeling and explicit world understanding for planning. The potential impacts are far-reaching:

- **Towards Generalist AI Agents:** The ability of TDB to learn accurate cognitive maps and perform efficient planning *in novel, unseen environments* is a crucial step towards developing truly generalist AI agents. These agents could adapt quickly to new tasks and environments without extensive re-training.
- **Enhanced Navigation and Robotics:** The core application of learning cognitive maps for efficient path planning in partially observed environments has direct and profound implications for robotics and autonomous navigation. Robots equipped with TDB could navigate complex, aliased real-world settings more effectively, efficiently, and with greater understanding of their surroundings.
- **Interpretable AI and Trustworthiness:** By explicitly extracting interpretable cognitive maps, TDB provides transparency into an agent's understanding of its environment. This move away from "black box" planning mechanisms can increase trust in AI systems and facilitate debugging and human oversight in safety-critical applications.
- **Improved Efficiency in Complex Tasks:** The "exponentially faster" planning compared to vanilla transformer rollouts is a major practical benefit. This efficiency can unlock the application of transformer-based agents to larger, more complex planning problems that were previously computationally intractable.
- **Expanding the Capabilities of Transformers:** The work demonstrates a principled way to imbue transformers with structured knowledge representations, addressing a fundamental limitation. This could inspire further research into integrating symbolic or explicit reasoning capabilities into end-to-end deep learning architectures.
- **Applications Beyond Navigation:** The successful extension to the GINC text dataset highlights the generality of the TDB approach. Learning interpretable latent graphs from sequential data, regardless of modality (spatial or textual), could be beneficial for tasks like document understanding, code analysis, or scientific discovery, where underlying conceptual structures are critical.
- **Foundation for Future World Models:** The paper lays a strong foundation for building more sophisticated, planning-compatible world models. The identified limitations (categorical inputs, lack of disentanglement) also point towards important avenues for future research, such as handling high-dimensional continuous observations (images) and learning truly disentangled, factored latent dynamics.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by authors):**
The authors acknowledge two main limitations:
1. **Categorical Inputs Only:** The current TDB only accepts categorical inputs, which limits its direct applicability to high-dimensional continuous observations like raw images.
2. **Lack of Disentanglement:** While multiple discrete bottlenecks accelerate training, they learn highly redundant representations rather than truly disentangled latent factors.

Future work will focus on addressing these limitations: modifying TDB to accept high-dimensional continuous observations and designing architectures that promote non-redundant, disentangled latent representations, potentially in factored Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). This will further advance the goal of building planning-compatible world models in rich, complex environments.

Overall, this research represents a significant step forward in making deep learning models, particularly transformers, more robust, efficient, and interpretable for tasks requiring real-world understanding and planning.
