# Learning Force-Regulated Manipulation with a Low-Cost Tactile-Force-Controlled Gripper

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Virginia and Columbia University developed a low-cost, force-controlled gripper (TF-Gripper) with integrated tactile sensing and a novel policy framework, RETAF. This framework enables robots to learn reactive force-regulated manipulation, achieving an average stable grasp rate of 68% and a task success rate of 60% across five force-sensitive tasks, outperforming existing position-controlled and non-reactive methods.

## Problem

- Manipulating fragile, soft, or deformable objects is challenging for robots, as it requires precise force regulation that traditional position-controlled grippers cannot robustly provide.
- Commercial force-controlled grippers are often expensive, have high minimum force thresholds, and lack broad compatibility, limiting research into delicate object interaction.
- Learning effective force-regulated policies is difficult due to the frequency mismatch between slow policy updates and fast contact dynamics, and the challenge of stably integrating sparse tactile signals with dense visual observations.

## Method

- Developed a low-cost ($150) TF-Gripper with integrated piezo-resistive tactile sensors and a precise force control range (0.45-45 N) suitable for delicate objects.
- Designed a low-cost teleoperation device that directly records human-applied grasping forces, enabling the collection of high-fidelity ground-truth force data for training.
- Introduced RETAF, a policy framework that decouples manipulation into a low-frequency Base Policy (arm pose, gripper open/close) and a high-frequency (80 Hz+) Force Adaptation Policy, which uses wrist-view vision and tactile input for reactive force regulation during contact.

## Results

- Direct force control consistently led to higher stable grasp rates across all evaluated policies, with RETAF achieving an average stable grasp rate of 68% compared to 38% for the Diffusion Policy baseline.
- RETAF outperformed non-reactive baselines, achieving an average task success rate of 60% on five force-sensitive tasks, whereas Diffusion Policy with tactile achieved 20%, and ViTac-MAE achieved 30%.
- RETAF demonstrated versatility by consistently improving reach, stable grasp, and task success rates when integrated with different base policies (e.g., increasing stable grasp rate from 30% to 90% for cherry tomato picking with a VLA model base).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct force control significantly improves grasp stability and overall task performance for force-sensitive objects compared to traditional position control.
> - Decoupling high-frequency reactive force adaptation from lower-frequency arm pose prediction effectively addresses the frequency mismatch problem and enables stable learning of force-regulated policies.
> - Effective integration of tactile sensing for reactive adjustments is crucial for precise force-regulated manipulation, enhancing a policy's ability to adapt to contact dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation of everyday objects, particularly those that are fragile, soft, or deformable, presents a challenge in achieving human-like dexterity and adaptability. Humans routinely apply precise force regulation, guided by tactile feedback, to grasp and manipulate delicate items like potato chips or soft fruits, adjusting their grip quickly upon contact.

Within the existing research landscape, most robotic systems simplify gripper control to binary open/close actions or direct gripper position/width prediction. While these methods are effective for many tasks, they treat gripper position as an indirect proxy for contact force. This approach often proves insufficient for force-sensitive objects, as the relationship between gripper position and applied force is highly object- and task-dependent. For instance, small variations in object size can significantly alter the stable grasping width range, whereas the stable grasping force range might remain more consistent. Furthermore, existing commercial force-controlled grippers often exhibit high costs, limited cross-robot compatibility, or high minimum force thresholds, rendering them less suitable for research into delicate object manipulation.

Tactile sensing has been increasingly integrated into robotic systems to enhance contact awareness and robustness. However, many approaches treat tactile feedback as an auxiliary observation rather than a primary driver for explicit force control. Challenges in integrating tactile information with global visual observations—such as the disparity between sparse, contact-dependent tactile signals and dense, globally structured visual data—can lead to unstable learning or difficulties in weighting these disparate inputs. Traditional low-frequency policies, typically used for predicting robot arm pose and gripper commands, are also often too slow to capture the fine-grained contact dynamics necessary for reactive force regulation during short, contact-rich interactions.

This work addresses these limitations by focusing on direct, precise force control for delicate objects, proposing both a hardware solution and a policy framework. It aims to bridge the gap between human capabilities in force-regulated manipulation and current robotic systems by providing a low-cost, force-controlled gripper with integrated tactile sensing and a policy architecture designed for reactive force adaptation. This positions the work at the intersection of robotic hardware design, tactile sensing, and learning-based manipulation, specifically targeting the problem of force-sensitive object interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to learn and perform force-regulated manipulation of everyday force-sensitive objects, mimicking human capabilities in adapting force based on tactile feedback.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key challenges and observations:

1. **Limitations of Position-Controlled Grippers:** Many everyday manipulation tasks require precise force regulation to prevent damage to fragile objects (e.g., potato chips) or ensure stability with soft or deformable items (e.g., cherry tomatoes). Existing robotic systems often rely on position control, which serves as an indirect method for force control. This indirect approach is highly sensitive to minor variations in object properties (size, stiffness) and task conditions, making it less robust for delicate objects. A small change in gripper width can lead to significant force differences, potentially causing breakage or slippage. Direct force control, by contrast, can maintain a stable grasp across objects with varied physical properties by regulating the applied contact force directly.

2. **Cost and Capabilities of Commercial Force-Controlled Grippers:** Current commercial force-controlled grippers are typically expensive, have high minimum force thresholds, and may lack cross-robot compatibility. These characteristics limit their accessibility for research into learning force-controlled policies, especially for manipulating delicate objects that require very low and finely regulated forces. There is a need for an affordable, adaptable gripper that provides precise force regulation in the low-force range.

3. **Challenges in Learning Force-Regulated Policies:** Learning policies for force regulation presents distinct difficulties:
 - **Frequency Mismatch:** Stable force regulation necessitates rapid, reactive adjustments based on transient tactile events (e.g., slip), often requiring response times of tens of milliseconds. Conventional visuomotor policies, however, operate at lower frequencies (1-10 Hz) due to the inference latency of large neural networks, making them too slow to react effectively to contact dynamics.
 - **Integrating Tactile Feedback:** Tactile signals are sparse and highly dependent on contact, contrasting with dense, globally structured visual observations. Directly fusing these modalities in a single policy can lead to unstable learning, as the policy may struggle to appropriately weigh local tactile information against global visual context.

To address these motivations, the authors set out to:
- Develop a low-cost, force-controlled parallel-jaw gripper (TF-Gripper) with integrated tactile sensing, capable of fine-grained force regulation suitable for delicate objects.
- Design a teleoperation device specifically for collecting human demonstrations that capture explicit, finely modulated grasping forces.
- Propose a novel policy framework, RETAF (REactive Tactile Adaptation of Force), that decouples high-frequency, reactive force control from low-frequency arm pose prediction, facilitating stable learning and effective utilization of tactile feedback.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a novel hardware system, TF-Gripper, for force-controlled manipulation and a policy framework, RETAF, for learning reactive force regulation.

#### 3.1. TF-Gripper Hardware System Design

The TF-Gripper is a low-cost (approximately $150) parallel-jaw gripper designed for precise force control and integrated tactile sensing.
- **Mechanical Design:** It is driven by two Dynamixel XL430-W250-T actuators. A pulley-and-timing-belt transmission mechanism translates motor torque to fingertip force, minimizing backlash and ensuring a consistent moment arm. The main gripper frame is 3D printed, and each fingertip moves along a low-friction linear rail, providing stable and smooth parallel motion. Idler pulleys with flanged bearings further reduce friction.
- **Force Control Capabilities:** The Dynamixel actuators exhibit a near-linear current-to-force relationship, enabling accurate and stable force control. The gripper operates in an effective force range of 0.45–45 N with a resolution of approximately 0.2 N. Temperature compensation, using the motor's built-in sensor, reduces force drift to below 4% over extended operation.
- **Tactile Sensing:** The gripper incorporates soft fingertip pads from UMI, integrated with thin and soft piezo-resistive tactile sensors (FlexiTac). These sensors capture contact area geometry and force-related information while maintaining mechanical compliance.
- **Cross-Robot Compatibility:** The gripper uses an interchangeable adapter (e.g., ISO 9409-1-A50 compatible for Franka Research 3 and UR5), allowing it to be mounted on various robot arms.
- **Multimodal Sensing:** A RealSense 435 camera is mounted on the wrist to provide object-centric visual observations.

#### 3.2. Teleoperation Device

To collect human demonstrations with explicit force control, a low-cost (under $100) teleoperation device was developed.
- **Design:** The device integrates a VR controller for robot end-effector pose control. Two finger rings are connected to a Dynamixel actuator, mirroring the gripper's actuation.
- **Functionality:** The actuator provides spring-like kinesthetic feedback to the human operator while measuring the applied force via motor current. This calibrated force signal is mapped in real-time to the robot gripper, allowing direct recording of human-applied grasping force during demonstrations. This eliminates the need for indirect inference from proxy signals or offline estimation.

#### 3.3. RETAF: REactive Tactile Adaptation of Force Framework

RETAF addresses the challenges of frequency mismatch and tactile integration by decoupling the manipulation policy into two components: a Base Policy ($\pi_{base}$) and a Force Adaptation Policy ($\pi_{force}$).
- **Problem Formulation:** The work highlights two issues with traditional coupled policies:
 1. **Frequency Mismatch:** Low-frequency pose prediction (1-10 Hz) cannot accommodate the high-frequency reactivity (e.g., >30 Hz) required for precise force regulation in contact-rich interactions.
 2. **Learning with Tactile:** Direct fusion of sparse, local tactile signals ($T_t$) with dense, global visual observations ($V_t$) often leads to unstable learning due to their disparate nature.
- **Base Policy ($\pi_{base}$):**
 - **Function:** Operates at a low frequency (e.g., 1-15 Hz). It predicts the robot arm pose ($a^{pose}_t$) and a discrete gripper open/close action ($k_t$) using non-tactile observations ($V_t$, e.g., global RGB images, proprioception).
 - **Training:** Trained via behavior cloning to match expert actions ($a^{*}_t = (a^{pose*}_t, k^{*}_t)$), minimizing a standard architecture-specific loss. Diffusion Policy (DP) or VLA models can serve as the backbone for $\pi_{base}$.
- **Force Adaptation Policy ($\pi_{force}$):**
 - **Function:** Activated only when the base policy signals a grasp intent ($k_t = \text{close}$). It predicts the continuous target grasping force ($f_t$) at a high frequency (30 Hz+). When $k_t = \text{open}$, the gripper is fully opened.
 - **Input:** Attends exclusively to wrist-view visual observations ($I^{wrist}_t$) and tactile sensing ($T_t$). The wrist view provides compact, object-centric visual information, while tactile sensing captures local feedback like deformation and incipient slip.
 - **Architecture:** Employs a joint-attention layer for fusing wrist-view images and tactile inputs, processed by respective encoders (CLIP for vision, two-layer CNN for tactile) and a force decoder.
 - **Training:** Trained via supervised regression to match the demonstrator’s applied force ($f^{*}_t$), minimizing mean squared error (MSE).
 - **Efficiency:** This lightweight design allows for high-frequency inference (80 Hz+) on a single GPU.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Hardware:** Franka Research 3 robot, TF-Gripper, two Intel RealSense D435i cameras (third-person and wrist-view).
- **Data Collection:** 50 human demonstrations per task (4-10 seconds each) recorded at 15 Hz using the teleoperation device.
- **Tasks:** Five real-world manipulation tasks requiring precise force regulation: Tofu Grasping, Chip Picking, Cherry Tomato Picking, Liquid Transfer, and Cherry Tomato Harvest.
- **Baselines:** Diffusion Policy (DP) and ViTac-MAE (DP with pre-trained tactile encoder via masked autoencoding).
- **Metrics:** Average success rate across three stages for 10 rollouts per task:
 - **Reach:** Successfully moving to and making contact with the target object.
 - **Stable Grasp:** Holding the object stably throughout execution without slipping, dropping, excessive deformation, or damage.
 - **Task Success:** Completing the full task (e.g., placing, transferring).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the TF-Gripper and RETAF framework across five real-world force-sensitive manipulation tasks.

**RQ1. Does direct force control improve force-sensitive tasks compared to position control?**
- **Result:** Force control consistently led to higher stable grasp rates compared to position control across all evaluated policies (Table II). For the DP baseline, switching from position to force control increased the stable grasp rate from 28% to 38%. For ViTac-MAE, it improved from 32% to 40%. When combined with RETAF, force control yielded a stable grasp rate of 68%, significantly higher than the 44% achieved with position control. Reach stage performance remained similar or slightly lower for force control.
- **Conclusion:** Direct force control improves grasp stability and overall task performance in force-regulated manipulation, particularly during the physical contact phase.

**RQ2. Is tactile sensing necessary for precise and effective force control?**
- **Result:** Adding tactile input directly to DP showed only minor improvements in stable grasp rates (28% with tactile vs. 24% without tactile for DP/position, and 38% with tactile vs. 32% without tactile for DP/force). ViTac-MAE, which uses a stronger tactile representation, achieved better performance (32% for position, 40% for force), but its overall improvement remained limited. RETAF, which explicitly leverages tactile feedback for reactive force adjustment, achieved significantly higher stable grasp rates (68% for force control with tactile). Qualitative analysis (Fig. 8) showed that RETAF's force predictions tracked human demonstration more closely and captured correct open/close timing, adapting to contact changes effectively, whereas DP often failed to predict accurate force or open/close timings.
- **Conclusion:** Tactile sensing is essential for precise force-regulated manipulation, and its effective integration, particularly through a reactive mechanism like RETAF, is critical for performance gains.

**RQ3. Does RETAF improve force-sensitive tasks compared to non-reactive baselines?**
- **Result:** RETAF consistently outperformed both DP and ViTac-MAE baselines across all stages (Reach, Stable Grasp, Task Success) and all five tasks (Table II). For force-controlled gripper actions, RETAF achieved an average stable grasp rate of 68% and a task success rate of 60%, compared to 38% and 20% for DP w/ Tac, and 40% and 30% for ViTac-MAE. RETAF also showed improved success in the reach stage, suggesting that decoupling gripper actions from pose prediction simplifies policy learning and leads to more reliable pose execution.
- **Conclusion:** RETAF significantly improves overall performance in force-regulated manipulation by enhancing both reach accuracy and grasp stability through its decoupled, reactive force adaptation mechanism.

**RQ4. How well can RETAF be integrated with different base policies?**
- **Result:** RETAF was evaluated with two different base policies: $\pi_{0.5}$ (a VLA model) and DP. As shown in Table III, RETAF consistently improved reach, stable grasp, and task success rates for both base policies on Cherry Tomato Picking and Liquid Transfer tasks. For instance, with $\pi_{0.5}$ as the base, RETAF increased stable grasp rate from 30% to 90% for Cherry Tomato Picking.
- **Conclusion:** RETAF is a versatile policy framework that can be integrated with various existing pose-prediction base policies, consistently enhancing their performance in force-regulated manipulation tasks.

**Qualitative Observations (Fig. 9):**
- DP variants predicting positions or forces exhibited higher failure rates in the reach-to-target stage compared to DP predicting binary open/close actions.
- DP often produced inaccurate end-effector poses regardless of gripper action type.
- DP with position control tended to predict overly large gripper widths, leading to objects slipping.
- DP with direct force prediction often applied excessive forces, resulting in object breakage due to a lack of reactive tactile feedback.
- RETAF demonstrated more appropriate and well-regulated grasping forces, avoiding both slippage and object damage.

**Ablation Study (Appendix A):**
- An ablation showed that if the base policy *only* predicted arm pose and RETAF was tasked with predicting both open/close and force, performance dropped significantly in grasp stability and task success. This indicated that RETAF, relying solely on wrist images and tactile feedback, struggles to infer global task progress and decide *when* to grasp.
- **Conclusion:** Deciding when to close the gripper requires global task context, which is best handled by the base policy, while RETAF excels at reactive force regulation *during* contact.

**Force and Tactile Dynamics Around Contact (Appendix B):**
- Analysis of force commands and tactile signals revealed that human demonstrations show dynamic force adjustments immediately after contact. RETAF's force adaptation policy accurately reproduced these reactive force modulation patterns observed in human demonstrations with higher fidelity than the DP baseline.
- **Conclusion:** RETAF effectively learns the fine-grained dependency between tactile feedback and force regulation, demonstrating a reactive control capability around contact.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving force-sensitive objects.

1. **Enabling Accessible Research in Force-Controlled Manipulation:** The introduction of TF-Gripper provides a low-cost, force-controlled parallel-jaw gripper ($150) with integrated tactile sensing and a wide, precise force range (0.45–45 N). Its cross-robot compatibility reduces barriers to entry for researchers to explore force-regulated manipulation, which was previously hindered by the high cost and limited applicability of commercial grippers. This could foster broader research and development in dexterous manipulation with delicate objects.

2. **Advancing Data Collection Methodologies:** The developed teleoperation device for the TF-Gripper enables the direct recording of human-applied grasping forces during demonstrations. This method offers a more accurate and flexible way to collect ground-truth force data compared to inferring force indirectly or post-hoc estimation. High-quality, force-aware demonstration data is crucial for training robust learning-based policies for complex manipulation tasks.

3. **Introducing a Novel Policy Framework for Reactive Force Regulation:** The proposed RETAF framework addresses fundamental challenges in learning force-regulated manipulation by decoupling high-frequency, reactive force adaptation from low-frequency arm pose prediction. This design allows for rapid force adjustments based on wrist-view images and tactile feedback during contact, while the base policy handles broader trajectory generation. The framework's ability to integrate with various existing base policies enhances its versatility and potential for widespread adoption.

4. **Demonstrating Improved Performance in Delicate Object Manipulation:** Through extensive real-world experiments across five diverse force-sensitive tasks, the research demonstrates that direct force control consistently outperforms position control in grasp stability and overall task success. RETAF further enhances performance, proving the effectiveness of reactive tactile adaptation. This provides a clear path for robots to handle fragile and deformable objects more reliably, expanding their capabilities in domestic, industrial, and medical applications.

The work lays a foundation for scaling the learning of force-controlled policies by providing both affordable hardware and an effective policy architecture. Future research directions suggested include theoretical analysis of force- versus position-based control, extending evaluations to a wider range of objects, and large-scale data collection for force and tactile sensing to further generalize force-regulated manipulation policies.
