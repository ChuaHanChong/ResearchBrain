# Privileged Reinforcement and Communication Learning for Distributed, Bandwidth-limited Multi-robot Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> A deep reinforcement learning framework enables multi-robot teams to efficiently explore unknown environments with a 99.2% reduction in communication bandwidth by learning to encode critical information into compact, fixed-sized messages. The system, trained with a privileged critic network that accesses ground truth data, maintains competitive exploration efficiency and demonstrates scalability to larger team sizes without retraining.

## Problem

- Multi-robot exploration requires extensive information exchange (e.g., full maps or trajectories), creating a bottleneck in bandwidth-limited environments like underwater or underground settings.
- Existing communication reduction methods, such as decreasing frequency, lead to suboptimal cooperation due to outdated information.
- Compressing message size can incur high computational costs or compromise exploration efficiency by losing critical map details.

## Method

- A deep ==reinforcement learning== (DRL) framework with a homogeneous policy, using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), is developed to allow robots to learn to encode salient information into compact, fixed-sized messages.
- The policy network processes an 'informative graph' of its local observations, encodes its current state into a low-dimensional message, and aggregates messages from other robots via attention mechanisms for cooperative reasoning.
- Privileged reinforcement learning is integrated during training, where a critic network accesses global ground truth map information to provide more accurate long-term return estimations and guide policy learning.

## Results

- The proposed framework achieved a **99.2%** reduction in communication volume compared to a conventional multi-robot TSP-based baseline, with upload and download data volumes reduced by approximately two orders of magnitude.
- For N=4 robots, exploration efficiency (total travel distance) was comparable to the baseline, showing only a **2.4%** increase, while for N=8 robots, the framework outperformed the baseline by **7.2%** in travel distance without retraining.
- An ablation study confirmed that privileged learning improved performance by **23.8%** (reduced travel distance) and enhanced training stability, demonstrating its effectiveness in guiding the policy network.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robots can autonomously learn to identify and encode only the most salient information from their partial maps into compact messages, drastically reducing communication overhead without explicit map sharing.
> - Cooperative reasoning can be effectively achieved by aggregating these compact learned messages from other robots, allowing for an implicit understanding of the global exploration state.
> - Leveraging privileged access to ground truth knowledge during training significantly enhances the DRL policy's performance and stability by providing superior guidance for long-term planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-robot exploration is a field that emphasizes the cooperative mapping and coverage of unknown environments by a team of autonomous robots. A critical aspect of this cooperative task is the exchange of information among robots to facilitate collective decision-making, accelerate task completion, and prevent redundant efforts. Conventionally, many exploration planners exchange significant amounts of data, such as complete partial maps or robot trajectories, on a continuous basis.

However, this reliance on extensive and frequent communication presents a substantial challenge in bandwidth-constrained environments, such as underwater and underground settings, where communication range often inversely correlates with bandwidth availability. For example, while transmitting an occupancy grid map may require approximately 2 Mbps, underwater communication bandwidth rarely exceeds 100 kbps. This communication bottleneck is further exacerbated in larger multi-robot teams, where existing channels may struggle to sustain high data throughput.

Current strategies for mitigating communication throughput typically fall into two categories. The first involves reducing the frequency of communications. While this approach decreases bandwidth consumption, it often leads to suboptimal cooperation due to the lack of up-to-date information among robots. The second strategy focuses on reducing message size, for instance, by sharing low-dimensional representations of partial maps that recipients then process to reconstruct or estimate the full map. These methods, however, can incur substantial computational costs or compromise exploration efficiency if critical details from the original map are lost during the compression and exchange process.

This research addresses the gap between the need for comprehensive information sharing in multi-robot exploration and the practical limitations imposed by bandwidth-constrained environments. It builds upon advancements in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for autonomous exploration, including frontier-based and sampling-based methods, and specifically extends recent work in attention-based neural networks for single-robot long-term planning (ARiADNE) to a multi-robot context under bandwidth limitations. The work also incorporates principles from communication learning, an area within multi-agent reinforcement learning where agents learn to identify and share salient information to overcome partial observability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework for distributed multi-robot exploration that achieves a substantial reduction in communication bandwidth consumption while maintaining high exploration efficiency. This framework is specifically designed for scenarios where communication bandwidth is a critical limiting factor.

The motivation for this work stems from the recognized challenges in multi-robot exploration within bandwidth-constrained environments. Existing methods for reducing communication, such as decreasing communication frequency or compressing message content, present significant trade-offs. Reducing frequency often leads to poor cooperation due to outdated information, while message compression can either be computationally intensive or result in a loss of critical map details, thereby reducing exploration efficiency. These trade-offs make current approaches impractical for real-world applications in environments like underwater or underground settings, where robust and efficient exploration is paramount.

To address these limitations, the specific objectives of the proposed framework are:
1. **Communication Learning:** To enable robots to autonomously learn to encode the most salient information from their individual belief maps (partial understanding of the environment) into fixed-sized, compact messages. This process aims to ensure that crucial information is conveyed efficiently within predefined message length constraints (e.g., maximum bandwidth).
2. **Cooperative Reasoning:** To allow robots to effectively process their own local belief maps in conjunction with received compact messages from other robots. This enables them to form an implicit understanding of the overall explored environment, facilitating cooperative decision-making and preventing redundant exploration efforts.
3. **Enhanced Training with Privileged Learning:** To leverage privileged learning during the training phase. Specifically, the critic (teacher) network is granted access to ground truth map knowledge, which provides more accurate action evaluations. This privileged information is intended to guide the policy (student) network's training effectively, thereby enhancing the robots' long-term planning capabilities and their ability to reason across different spatial and temporal scales.

By pursuing these objectives, the researchers aim to overcome the fundamental dilemma of balancing communication constraints with exploration performance, paving the way for more practical and robust multi-robot exploration systems in challenging environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates the multi-robot exploration task as a partially observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The objective is to minimize the maximum trajectory length (makespan) among all robots required to achieve a specified exploration rate (θ, typically between 0.9 and 1.0) of the free area within an environment. A key constraint is the bandwidth limitation on communication, which restricts robots from sharing their full local belief maps, enforcing a maximum message size (e.g., ~256 bytes per message in practice).

The proposed framework employs a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approach, integrating communication learning and privileged reinforcement learning. A homogeneous policy is trained for all agents using parameter sharing. The core DRL algorithm is Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), augmented with a privileged critic network.

**Policy Network:**
Each robot's policy network operates based on its partial observation of the environment.

- **Observation:** The observation for each robot is represented as an "informative graph" G_t = (V_t, E_t), constructed from the free explored space within its individual partial map. Each vertex v_i in V_t contains components such as 2D coordinates, a utility metric (number of observable frontiers from that vertex), a guidepost indicator (whether the vertex has been visited), and an occupancy status (-1 for the current robot, +1 for another robot, 0 for unoccupied). To account for other robots outside a robot's explored graph, a temporary vertex is introduced. Edges E_t connect each vertex to its k-nearest collision-free neighbors.

- **Graph Encoder:** A graph encoder, composed of multiple attention layers, processes the informative graph. It projects each node into a high-dimensional (d=64) feature vector. Through a self-attention mechanism, the encoder intelligently fuses neighboring node features, generating "enhanced node features" (F̂). An edge mask ensures attention is only applied between connected neighbors.

- **Current State Decoder:** This module distills the relationships between the current robot's position and all other graph vertices. It uses an attention layer where the enhanced feature of the current node acts as the query, and all enhanced node features serve as keys and values. The output is a d-dimensional feature vector, μ, which represents the robot's learned message about its current accessible state. This message is then broadcast to other robots.

- **Cooperation Decoder:** Upon receiving messages from other robots, the cooperation decoder aggregates these messages with the robot's own learned message (μ_t_i). A cross-attention layer uses the robot's own message as the query and the concatenated messages from other robots (μ_t_-i) as key-and-value sources. This process generates a "cooperative feature" (F_co), which serves as an implicit representation of the overall explored environment based on collective knowledge. The design allows for scalability to varying team sizes.

- **Action Space:** The action space consists of neighboring vertices to the robot's current position. A pointer layer takes the cooperative feature (F_co) and the enhanced features of neighboring nodes (F̂_nbr) to output a logarithmic probability distribution. During training, an action (next waypoint) is sampled from this distribution; during testing, the action is selected greedily.

**Training:**
The training process incorporates several key elements:

- **Privileged Critic Network:** To enhance the accuracy of predicted long-term returns and improve policy guidance, a privileged critic network is utilized. This critic network differs from the policy network in two ways:
 1. **Observation:** It accesses a "ground truth graph" (Ĝ_t) derived from the global ground truth state of the environment, rather than the partial informative graph. Node utilities in Ĝ_t are based on the actual number of unexplored free areas.
 2. **Communication Layer:** The communication layer is removed from the critic network because direct access to global ground truth renders inter-agent messages redundant for return estimation. An ablation study indicated that this privileged learning significantly improved training performance and stability.

- **Reward Structure:** The reward function is modified from prior work to promote cooperative exploration:
 1. **r_o (Observation Reward):** Changed from counting observed frontiers to measuring the unexplored area around a new viewpoint, utilizing the ground truth map for accuracy.
 2. **r_c (Cost Reward):** A penalty for the distance traveled between viewpoints.
 3. **r_f (Finishing Reward):** Awarded upon sufficient environment exploration.
 4. **ξ_t (Team Reward):** A newly introduced reward proportional to the newly explored area discovered by the *entire team* between decision steps. This explicitly encourages collective exploration.
 5. **λ_t_i (Momentum Reward):** A new reward designed to encourage consistent movement and minimize backtracking, aiding in trajectory optimization during early training. It is calculated based on the dot product of the previous and current movement directions.
 The overall reward is: r_t = r_o + λ_t_i + ξ_t + r_f - r_c.

- **Training Details:** The model was trained on 4000 procedurally generated 100m x 100m dungeon maps. Robots had a sensor range of 20 meters, with graph nodes distributed 4 meters apart and connected up to 12 meters (max 24 neighbors). Training involved 4 robots (N=4), a maximum episode length of 128 steps, a batch size of 256, a learning rate of 1e-5, and a discount factor (γ) of 1. Data collection was parallelized using Ray. Convergence typically occurred after approximately 20,000 episodes, requiring about 108 hours on specified hardware.

**Baselines and Communication Comparison:**
The proposed model (`ours`) was compared against:
1. `mtsp based`: An extended version of TARE's TSP-based local planner for multi-robot settings, which uses ORtools to solve a multiple Traveling Salesman Problem. In this baseline, robots share their complete belief maps to form a global belief map.
2. `global map`: A variant of the authors' DRL model where robots are allowed to share both learned messages and explicit partial maps, similar to the `mtsp based` baseline. This variant was used to assess the framework's performance in high-bandwidth scenarios.

Communication volume was compared: `ours` transmits fixed-sized 1x64-dimensional float32 tensors (256 bytes per message), while baselines exchange HxW int8 belief map messages (H_t x W_t x 1 bytes), which are substantially larger.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated the capabilities of the proposed DRL framework in bandwidth-limited multi-robot exploration.

**Ablation Study on Privileged Learning:**
An ablation study confirmed the impact of the privileged critic network. The introduction of privileged ground truth map information during training led to a 23.8% improvement in performance, measured by reduced travel distance. Furthermore, privileged learning contributed to improved training stability, characterized by lower variance in performance metrics. These results indicate that the privileged critic network provided more accurate long-term return estimations, effectively guiding the policy network's training.

**Comparisons with Baselines (N=4 Robots, 100m x 100m Maps, Exploration Threshold θ=0.95):**
The primary evaluation focused on communication bandwidth reduction and exploration efficiency.

- **Communication Volume:** The proposed model (`ours`) achieved a 99.2% reduction in communication volume compared to the `mtsp based` baseline. Specifically, `ours` recorded an average upload data volume of 5.7 MB and a download data volume of 17.2 MB for N=4, whereas `mtsp based` utilized 656.2 MB for upload and 1791.1 MB for download. This represents a significant decrease in bandwidth consumption, approximately two orders of magnitude.
- **Exploration Efficiency (Travel Distance):** For N=4 robots, the `ours` model exhibited a marginal increase in total travel distance, performing 2.4% less efficiently than `mtsp based` (205.7 m for `ours` vs. 200.8 m for `mtsp based`). This indicated that the substantial communication savings came with a minor compromise in exploration path length under this specific team size.
- **Performance with Full Map Sharing (`global map` variant):** When the DRL-based model was allowed to share explicit partial maps in addition to learned messages (`global map`), it surpassed the `mtsp based` baseline by 9.2% in terms of travel distance (182.2 m for `global map` vs. 200.8 m for `mtsp based`). Furthermore, `global map` outperformed the map-sharing-free `ours` model by 11.4% (182.2 m vs. 205.7 m). This demonstrated the general effectiveness of the DRL framework, even in high-bandwidth scenarios where detailed maps can be exchanged.

**Scalability to Larger Team Sizes (N=8 Robots, without retraining):**
The framework's scalability was assessed by doubling the team size from 4 to 8 robots without retraining the model.

- **Exploration Efficiency (Travel Distance):** For N=8, the `ours` model demonstrated improved performance, outperforming `mtsp based` by 7.2% in travel distance (168.9 m for `ours` vs. 182.2 m for `mtsp based`). This result suggests that the communication learning approach can be more beneficial for larger teams in terms of exploration efficiency.
- **Comparison with `global map` (N=8):** The travel distance margin between `global map` (150.1 m) and `ours` (168.9 m) remained comparable, with `ours` being 11.1% higher. This consistency, despite the larger team size, indicates robust scalability of the approach.
- **Communication Volume (N=8):** The communication savings trend continued for N=8, with `ours` using significantly less data (4.5 MB upload, 32.0 MB download) compared to `mtsp based` (554.7 MB upload, 3303.1 MB download).

Overall, the findings indicate that the proposed framework can enable robots to understand and reason about the global state of an exploration task using compact learned messages, without explicit reliance on detailed map sharing, while maintaining competitive, and in some cases superior, exploration efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-robot exploration, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of communication bandwidth limitations. The proposed DRL framework, which integrates communication learning and privileged reinforcement learning, offers a viable solution for scenarios where conventional high-bandwidth communication is impractical or impossible.

The primary significance lies in the demonstration of a **two-orders-of-magnitude reduction in communication bandwidth consumption** (up to 99.2% savings) with a minimal, and in some larger team configurations, a positive impact on exploration efficiency. This quantitative achievement directly tackles a major bottleneck for multi-robot systems in environments such as underwater, underground, or extraterrestrial settings, where robust and efficient information exchange under severe constraints is crucial.

The core innovation is enabling robots to **learn to embed the most salient information from their partial maps into compact, fixed-sized messages**. This moves beyond simplistic data compression by allowing the DRL agent to autonomously determine what information is critical for cooperative decision-making. The ability of robots to reason about a global context and generate high-quality, distributed exploration paths *without explicitly sharing detailed belief maps* represents a conceptual advancement in multi-agent coordination under partial observability.

Furthermore, the incorporation of **privileged learning** during the training phase is significant. By providing the critic network with ground truth knowledge, the training process is made more efficient and effective, leading to a policy network with enhanced long-term planning capabilities. This methodology suggests a valuable paradigm for training robust robotic policies in complex, partially observable environments.

The framework's demonstrated **scalability to larger team sizes without retraining** highlights its potential for real-world deployment in diverse multi-robot applications. The `global map` variant also indicates the generalizability of the DRL architecture, showing strong performance even in high-bandwidth scenarios where full map sharing is possible.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. It paves the way for the deployment of intelligent, cooperative multi-robot teams in previously inaccessible or highly challenging environments. Applications could include:
- **Underwater exploration:** For mapping vast ocean floors or inspecting subsea infrastructure, where acoustic communication is severely limited.
- **Underground mining or cave exploration:** For creating 3D maps in GPS-denied and communication-sparse environments.
- **Search and rescue operations:** In disaster zones where communication infrastructure is compromised.
- **Space exploration:** For autonomous planetary surface mapping by rover teams.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as further reducing communication frequency and exploring intermittent communication, suggests continued development towards even more robust and adaptable multi-robot systems. The eventual validation of this method through real-world experiments would further solidify its practical utility and impact.
