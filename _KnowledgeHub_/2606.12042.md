# KinematicRL: A Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning Framework For Social Navigation With Kinodynamic Feasibility

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tongji University developed KinematicRL, a deep reinforcement learning framework that enables mobile robots to navigate socially in real-world, pedestrian-rich environments by addressing the sim-to-real gap. The framework integrates higher-order control, a robust LiDAR-only human tracking pipeline, and a gated spatio-temporal transformer, achieving smoother, kinodynamically feasible trajectories and successful real-world deployment.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policies for social navigation often assume simplified first-order robot dynamics, leading to dynamically infeasible actions in real robots.
- Existing DRL methods typically assume perfect knowledge of human states, whereas real-world data from noisy sensors requires robust estimation.
- DRL architectures struggle to adapt to time-varying crowd sizes and maintain stability under sensor noise, especially with common transformer limitations.

## Method

- Proposed using ==higher-order control== inputs (accelerations) as the DRL action space, warm-starting the policy with a stochastic iLQR using a divergence minimization objective to ensure dynamic feasibility.
- Developed a robust, LiDAR-only human tracking pipeline featuring cluster-based detection, historical averaging for velocity estimation, and spatial-velocity data association to handle noisy sensor data.
- Introduced a gated spatio-temporal transformer with unbiased residual gating and scenario-based domain randomization to adapt to variable crowd densities and enhance learning stability.

## Results

- KinematicRL achieved a simulated success rate of **0.84** with zero velocity oscillation frequency and low jerk and curvature, demonstrating smoother and dynamically feasible trajectories compared to baselines.
- The LiDAR-only tracking pipeline attained a Mean Squared Error of **0.11m**/s for velocity estimation, outperforming other methods and improving robustness by incorporating velocity similarity in data association.
- The learned policy successfully transferred to a real differential drive robot, exhibiting proactive, socially compliant, and smooth collision avoidance in a human-populated environment, robust to sensor limitations and occlusions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Higher-order control, specifically using accelerations, significantly reduces the tracking error between simulated and actual robot positions, effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap for actuator dynamics.
> - Initializing DRL policies with expert demonstrations from stochastic iLQR using a mode-covering M-projection objective improves training convergence and transfers action uncertainty for better policy quality.
> - A custom gated spatio-temporal transformer architecture, combined with scenario-based domain randomization, is crucial for developing proactive navigation behaviors and robustly handling fluctuating crowd sizes and sensing inaccuracies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of social navigation for mobile robots, particularly in pedestrian-rich environments, presents challenges related to dynamic obstacles, human behavior uncertainty, and maintaining social norms. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising approach for modeling Human-Robot Interactions (HRI) and enabling robots to navigate safely and cooperatively. However, a significant "sim-to-real gap" impedes the real-world deployment of DRL-trained policies.

Existing DRL-based crowd navigation methods are often trained in simulators that rely on simplified first-order robot dynamics, assuming instantaneous velocity changes, which do not reflect real-world actuator limitations. Additionally, these methods often assume perfect knowledge of human states (positions and velocities), whereas in reality, these must be estimated from noisy sensor data.

Previous attempts to address the sim-to-real gap in social navigation have focused on:
1. **Enforcing Dynamic Feasibility**: Approaches include generating subgoals for separate model-based controllers (e.g., MPC) or constraining the DRL action space to platform-specific feasible commands. These methods often introduce additional system complexity, computational overhead, or limit the action space's expressivity.
2. **Human State Estimation**: Many pipelines use LiDAR-camera fusion, which adds system complexity and can suffer from calibration drift. LiDAR-only approaches often face stability issues when humans are in close proximity.
3. **Adapting to Variable Crowd Sizes**: The number of detected humans can vary due to sensor limitations and occlusions. While attention mechanisms like transformers have been used, they can exhibit optimization instability, especially with padded inputs during training.

This work contributes to bridging these gaps by proposing a unified framework that addresses all three limitations simultaneously, aiming to produce policies that are dynamically feasible, robust to sensor noise, and adaptable to varying crowd densities for real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a deep reinforcement learning framework, termed KinematicRL, that enables mobile robots to perform social navigation in real-world, pedestrian-rich environments with minimal modifications from simulation training. This objective is driven by the persistent sim-to-real gap identified in existing DRL approaches for social navigation.

The motivation for this work stems from three specific limitations prevalent in the current research landscape:

1. **Dynamic Feasibility of Robot Actions**: Current DRL policies often assume first-order dynamics, allowing instantaneous changes in velocity. Real robots, however, are subject to kinematic and dynamic constraints (e.g., maximum acceleration, motor force limits). Policies trained without these considerations result in dynamically infeasible actions that cannot be accurately executed by physical robots, leading to degraded performance or unsafe behavior in real-world deployment. The objective here is to ensure that the learned policy generates physically executable commands.
2. **Robust Human State Estimation from Limited Sensors**: Most social navigation DRL methods operate under the assumption of perfect human state information. In reality, human positions and velocities must be inferred from sensor measurements, which are often noisy and incomplete. LiDAR-camera fusion, while effective, introduces complexity. The motivation is to develop a reliable and practical human tracking pipeline using only 2D LiDAR to provide accurate and stable state estimates suitable for policy input.
3. **Adaptation to Time-Varying Crowd Sizes and Sensor Noise**: The number of humans detected by sensors can fluctuate due to occlusions, sensor range limits, and environmental dynamics. Existing attention-based DRL architectures, particularly standard transformers, can exhibit instability when trained with padded variable-length inputs and may struggle with the balance between reactive and memory-based behaviors crucial for social navigation. The objective is to design a robust DRL architecture that effectively handles variable crowd sizes and maintains stability under noisy observations.

By addressing these three limitations, the authors aim to create a comprehensive DRL framework that facilitates the seamless transfer of learned social navigation policies from simulation to real-world robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The KinematicRL framework integrates several components to address the identified challenges, focusing on higher-order control, a robust tracking pipeline, and an adapted transformer architecture.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation

The social navigation task is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The robot's objective is to learn a policy that maximizes discounted cumulative reward while avoiding collisions and completing the task. The robot receives observations ($o_t$) rather than full states ($s_t$) of other agents. The framework then targets three sim-to-real challenges: actuator latency (dynamic feasibility), time-varying number of humans, and human state estimation from raw sensor data.

#### 3.2. B. Higher-Order Control for Dynamic Feasibility

To ensure dynamic feasibility, the authors propose using higher-order control inputs as the DRL action space.
- **Theoretical Justification**: A theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) demonstrates that the tracking error between simulated and actual robot position decays exponentially as the control order ($n$) increases. This implies that higher-order control can significantly reduce the sim-to-real gap related to actuator dynamics.
- **Second-Order Control for Differential Drive Robots**: For differential drive robots, linear and angular accelerations ($u_a, u_\alpha$) are chosen as the second-order control inputs. This formulation naturally enforces acceleration constraints that map directly from wheel force limits. This choice represents a balance between reducing tracking error and maintaining computational tractability for real-time learning and planning. The second-order unicycle model is utilized, where velocities are integrals of accelerations, leading to smoother trajectories. The action space is defined by bounded accelerations.

#### 3.3. C. Imitation Learning with Stochastic iLQR

To facilitate training of higher-order policies, which are inherently more complex for DRL, the policy is initialized through imitation learning.
- **Maximum Entropy Control**: Trajectory optimization with an entropy term (Maximum Entropy Control) is used to generate expert demonstrations. This encourages exploration and smooth policies.
- **Stochastic iLQR**: A stochastic iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR) is employed as the solver. It approximates the system dynamics linearly and the cost quadratically, iteratively converging to an optimal trajectory. Theorem 2 states that the solution to this maximum entropy objective under linear dynamics and quadratic cost is a stochastic Gaussian policy.
- **Divergence Minimization for Imitation**: The DRL policy is initialized by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the iLQR output distribution (Gaussian, encoding action uncertainty) and the policy's distribution. This is framed as an M-projection, which is mode-covering, aligning with the iLQR's covariance structure.
- **Social Navigation Cost Function**: The iLQR's cost function includes terms for goal-reaching, trajectory smoothness, and collision avoidance (modeled as a quadratic penalty for violating a safety threshold). Human motions are predicted using a constant velocity assumption within the control horizon. Hessian regularization and a line search method are incorporated to handle the non-convexity of the collision avoidance term and improve robustness of the optimization process.
- **DAgger**: The DAgger algorithm is used to mitigate distribution shift during imitation learning by iteratively collecting expert labels in states visited by the learned policy.

#### 3.4. D. Tracking Pipeline

A cluster-based human state estimation pipeline is introduced, relying solely on 2D LiDAR data.
- **Human Detection**: Li2Former is used to detect human positions from raw 2D LiDAR point clouds.
- **Cluster Maintenance**: Each tracked human is represented as the centroid of a point cluster. A buffer stores past positions and velocities for each cluster. Centroid position is the newest buffer entry, while centroid velocity is computed via weighted historical averaging, providing stable estimates.
- **Spatial-Velocity Data Association**: To differentiate spatially close humans, a data association procedure is implemented. The distance metric for assigning new detections to existing clusters combines both spatial proximity and velocity similarity. The Hungarian Algorithm is used for optimal assignment.
- **Cluster Update**: Matched detections are processed by a Kalman filter to obtain denoised positions and preliminary velocity estimates, which are then added to the cluster's historical buffer.
- **State Space**: The robot's observation includes its own state (position, orientation, goal, velocities) and the estimated states of all detected humans (radius, position, velocity).

#### 3.5. E. Gated Spatio-Temporal Transformer

To enable adaptation to time-varying crowd sizes and balance reactive and memory-based behaviors, a modified transformer architecture is proposed.
- **Reaction and Memory Pathways**: The transformer's residual stream is interpreted as a "reaction pathway" (for immediate responses), and the attention stream as a "memory pathway" (for historical context).
- **Unbiased Residual Gating**: To address issues with standard transformer initialization (Layer Normalization mixing pathways, preventing Markovian initialization) and to balance the two pathways, the authors adapt Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) within the residual block. Key modifications include:
 - Removing the bias term ($b_u$) from the GRU update gate to prevent issues with padded variable-length inputs during batch processing.
 - Modifying the residual connection so that the second GRU block receives its skip connection directly from the input embedding, preserving information flow more effectively upon initialization compared to a standard residual structure.
- **Spatio-Temporal Structure**: The architecture employs separate spatial and temporal encoders. The spatial encoder models human-robot and human-human interactions. The extracted spatial features are then passed to a temporal encoder to capture relationships across time.
- **Scenario-Based Domain Randomization**: To prevent the policy from converging to passive behaviors in low-entropy environments (e.g., waiting for crowds to clear), the training distribution is enriched. This involves randomizing human start-goal configurations and velocities, increasing state-space entropy and encouraging proactive navigation.

#### 3.6. F. Deep Reinforcement Learning

- **Reward Function**: The reward function encourages goal-reaching, penalizes collisions, and incorporates a soft penalty for violating a defined social comfort distance ($r_s$) to promote socially compliant behavior. A small time cost encourages efficiency.
- **PPO Algorithm**: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a clipped surrogate objective is used for reinforcement learning. The advantage function is estimated via truncated Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). The policy outputs a normal distribution over accelerations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings from extensive evaluations in both simulation and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Implementation Details

Experiments utilized PyTorch and stable-baselines3 for PPO. The training scenario, "Highly Dynamic," featured a mix of pedestrians on a circle and within a square, with randomized velocities, creating high state-space entropy. Baselines included geometric methods (ORCA, NH-ORCA), a model-based control method (MPC), and DRL methods (SARL, SARL (second-order), Feasible SARL). Performance was evaluated using metrics like success rate (SR), collision rate (CR), average navigation time ($\mu$T), danger frequency (DF), minimum separating distance (Dist.), velocity/angular velocity oscillation frequency ($v_{of}, \omega_{of}$), jerk ($\Delta a/\Delta t$), and trajectory curvature ($\kappa$).

#### 4.2. B. Imitation-Reinforcement Learning Evaluation

- **Expert Policies**: The proposed stochastic iLQR (second-order) demonstrated superior kinematic performance compared to ORCA, NH-ORCA, and MPC. It achieved lower oscillation frequencies and jerk, indicating smoother, dynamically feasible trajectories. While ORCA had a slightly faster average navigation time, iLQR exhibited a significantly lower danger frequency (0.03 vs. 0.25) and higher minimum separating distance (0.088m vs. 0.003m), suggesting safer behavior. The comparison between iLQR (first-order) and iLQR (second-order) indicated that kinematic performance improvements stemmed from the model-based trajectory optimization approach rather than solely the higher-order control. MPC showed limitations in dynamic environments due to its constant-velocity prediction assumption and computational scaling issues.
- **Imitation Learning**: Divergence minimization objectives for imitation learning (M-projection and I-projection) led to faster convergence during subsequent reinforcement learning compared to a Mean Squared Error (MSE) objective. The M-projection method, which is mode-covering, outperformed I-projection in this context, likely due to the unimodal nature of iLQR demonstrations. This indicated the benefit of transferring action uncertainty information from the expert to the policy.
- **Reinforcement Learning**:
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap**: SARL (second-order) showed a substantial drop in success rate (from 0.99 to 0.52) compared to SARL (first-order) when subjected to realistic dynamic constraints. This performance degradation highlighted the sim-to-real gap, where policies trained on idealized first-order dynamics generate dynamically infeasible actions that are problematic in real-world scenarios.
 - **Navigation Performance**: KinematicRL (ours) achieved a success rate of 0.84 and a collision rate of 0.15, outperforming all baselines except SARL (first-order), which achieved a higher success rate but by executing dynamically infeasible actions. Crucially, KinematicRL maintained zero oscillation frequency for both linear and angular velocities ($v_{of}, \omega_{of}=0.00$) and had low jerk ($\Delta a/\Delta t=0.16$) and curvature ($\kappa=0.43$), indicating smooth and comfortable trajectories. Its danger frequency (0.03) was lower than SARL's (0.10), suggesting safer collision avoidance maneuvers.
 - **Qualitative Comparison**: Visualizations showed KinematicRL executing proactive collision avoidance by anticipating human motions and choosing safer rotation paths, in contrast to SARL's more aggressive, often dynamically infeasible, last-moment evasive maneuvers. KinematicRL's action distributions consistently fell within feasible regions, resulting in smoother velocity profiles.

#### 4.3. C. Network Evaluation

- **Generalization to Variable Crowd Densities**: KinematicRL trained with a varying number of humans (4-6) generalized more effectively to crowd densities exceeding nine agents compared to SARL (global attention) and KinematicRL trained with a fixed number of humans (5). This indicated that self-attention scales better in dense crowds and that training with variable-cardinality inputs is crucial for generalization.
- **Robustness to Sensing Inaccuracies**: The KinematicRL network demonstrated robustness to position noise up to 0.5m and across the full range of tested velocity noise magnitudes. Performance dropped significantly beyond 0.5m position error, which is beyond typical sensor inaccuracies.
- **Ablation Study**:
 - **Scenario-Based Domain Randomization**: Ablations confirmed that increasing state-space entropy through randomization of human positions and velocities was critical for training the gated transformer. Without sufficient randomization, the network failed to learn proactive behaviors.
 - **Network Modules**: Removing any of the key design components—forward Layer Normalization (pre-LN), GRU-based gating, or the modified residual connection—significantly hindered the learning process, demonstrating their individual contributions to stability and performance.
 - **Temporal Encoder**: Placing the spatial encoder before the temporal encoder yielded better performance, suggesting that stable spatial features improve the extraction of temporal relations. While GRU provided some short-horizon memory, the full spatio-temporal architecture achieved a higher success rate.

#### 4.4. D. Tracking Pipeline Evaluation

- **Matching Robustness**: Simulations showed that incorporating velocity similarity (weight $\alpha = 0.4$) into the data association metric significantly improved matching robustness compared to using only spatial proximity ($\alpha = 0.0$). The velocity-based approach prevented identity switches between spatially close humans and reduced the creation of spurious clusters.
- **Velocity Estimation Accuracy**: The proposed historical averaging method for velocity estimation achieved the lowest Mean Squared Error (0.11m/s) compared to Kalman Filter, Savitzky-Golay Filter, and Bézier Regression. Velocity-based matching further improved accuracy across all estimation methods by enhancing data association.

#### 4.5. E. Real-World Experiments

The learned KinematicRL policy was successfully transferred to a real differential drive robot.
- **Deployment**: The robot, equipped with a 2D LiDAR and an ARM processor, executed the policy. Li2Former inference ran on a laptop (17Hz) or Jetson Orin Nano (26Hz), with tracking and planning modules operating at 10Hz on the robot. This control frequency was deemed adequate for mitigating jerk-related errors.
- **Qualitative Observations**: In a test scene with four cooperative and non-cooperative humans, the robot demonstrated proactive and socially compliant collision avoidance maneuvers. It maintained smooth trajectories and exhibited robustness to time-varying numbers of detected humans due to sensor limitations and occlusions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to bridging the sim-to-real gap in social navigation, with implications for the deployment of DRL-based mobile robots in human environments.

The **theoretical analysis and practical implementation of higher-order control** for DRL policies represent a significant step towards ensuring dynamic feasibility. By using accelerations as actions, the framework inherently accounts for robot kinematic and dynamic constraints, a departure from many DRL methods that rely on simplified dynamics. This directly addresses a core challenge in transferring policies from simulation to real hardware, leading to smoother, safer, and more executable robot motions. The warm-starting of the DRL policy with a stochastic iLQR using a divergence minimization objective also improves training efficiency and policy quality by transferring expert knowledge and uncertainty awareness.

The **development of a robust, LiDAR-only human tracking pipeline** enhances the practicality and accessibility of social navigation systems. By avoiding camera-LiDAR fusion, it reduces system complexity, mitigates calibration drift, and allows for deployment on simpler hardware setups. The cluster-based approach, incorporating both spatial and velocity similarity for data association, addresses the common issue of distinguishing spatially proximate humans, providing more stable and accurate state estimates crucial for real-time decision-making.

The **introduction of a gated spatio-temporal transformer architecture with unbiased residual gating and scenario-based domain randomization** improves the DRL agent's ability to adapt to dynamic crowd environments. The architectural modifications enhance training stability, balance reactive and memory-based behaviors, and effectively handle variable-length inputs arising from fluctuating crowd densities. The emphasis on diverse training scenarios through domain randomization is shown to be crucial for developing proactive, non-passive navigation behaviors, leading to policies that can interact cooperatively rather than merely avoiding obstacles.

The **extensive simulation and real-world validation** of KinematicRL on a physical differential drive robot provides empirical evidence of the framework's effectiveness. The demonstrations of superior kinematic performance and successful deployment with minimal modifications suggest that this approach offers a viable pathway for deploying DRL-trained social navigation systems in practical applications such as service robotics and industrial settings.

Potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Robot Safety and Acceptability**: By ensuring kinodynamic feasibility and promoting socially compliant behavior (e.g., maintaining comfort distances), robots can operate more safely and predictably around humans, increasing public acceptance.
- **Reduced Development Time**: A more effective sim-to-real transfer reduces the need for extensive real-world fine-tuning, accelerating the development and deployment cycle of DRL-based robotic solutions.
- **Broader Applicability**: The reliance on standard 2D LiDAR sensors and computationally efficient methods makes the framework accessible for a wider range of robot platforms and resource-constrained environments.

Future work directions include integrating explicit safety guarantees (e.g., Control Barrier Functions) to further reduce collision rates in highly constrained scenarios and investigating model compression techniques for the perception module to improve inference efficiency on edge platforms.
