# Sequential Planning via Anchored Robotic Keypoints

> [!summary] Summary
> SPARK, a neurosymbolic robotic manipulation system from Case Western Reserve University, enhances robustness to environmental changes and reduces computational overhead by leveraging adaptive neural perception for object grounding with symbolic behavior tree planning. It achieves an average 43.7% success rate on LIBERO-PRO position-and-task cells, outperforming several VLA and code-generation baselines, and demonstrates training-free cross-embodiment transfer across various robot platforms.

## Problem

- Frontier Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models exhibit significant performance degradation on perturbed environments (e.g., LIBERO-PRO) due to memorizing absolute end-effector positions rather than adapting to object displacement.
- "Code-as-Policy" agents, while somewhat more robust, incur high computational costs from numerous Large Language Model (LLM) queries for plan generation and re-querying upon minor policy failures.
- Existing systems misallocate test-time compute to plan reformulation, even though environmental changes primarily impact visual grounding of objects rather than the fundamental task plan structure.

## Method

- **SPARK** is a training-free ==neurosymbolic== system combining multi-camera ==SAM3== perception for dynamic 3D object localization with Gemini-generated ==Behavior Tree (BT)== planning, which uses a typed grammar of low-level robot primitives.
- It employs ==Adaptive Perception Self-Consistency==, where the LLM proposes alternative text prompts to improve object grounding, and a ==tiered recovery layer== to handle primitive failures without requiring LLM re-planning.
- The system uses an ==inverse-kinematics controller== for execution and is designed for ==cross-embodiment transfer==, applying the same primitive grammar and planning framework across different robot platforms.

## Results

- SPARK achieved an average **43.7%** success rate on LIBERO-PRO position-and-task cells, more than double that of CAP-AGENT0 (**18.2%**) and MolmoAct2 (**18.6%**), and demonstrated a **27.7 percentage point** increase in spatial robustness with adaptive perception.
- On CaP-Bench, SPARK matched or exceeded CAP-AGENT0 on pick-and-place tasks (e.g., **100%** on Lift and CubeRestack, **97%** on Stack) using only a single LLM planning call, showcasing superior computational efficiency.
- The system demonstrated training-free cross-embodiment transfer, averaging **68%** success across three distinct robot families (UR10e, Franka FR3, bimanual Franka) and nine unique tasks in physical experiments, with perception errors identified as the dominant failure mode.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Shifting test-time computational focus from plan regeneration to robust, adaptive perception and object grounding significantly enhances robustness to environmental perturbations, as scene shifts primarily impact object location, not task logic.
> - A modular neurosymbolic architecture, which separates symbolic task planning from dynamic neural perception, allows for a fixed, adaptable plan structure that resolves object references in real-time, reducing reliance on costly re-planning.
> - Integrating a structured behavior tree output with per-primitive post-condition checks provides an interpretable failure diagnosis, enabling clear localization of issues to specific modules (perception, planner, controller) and facilitating automated generation of labeled training data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and code-generation agents. End-to-end VLA models, such as OpenVLA, π0, π0.5, and MolmoAct2, have demonstrated high performance on simulated benchmarks like LIBERO. However, their reliance on scene-specific training layouts leads to fragility when faced with perturbed environments, as evidenced by their performance decline on the LIBERO-PRO benchmark. Mechanistic studies suggest this brittleness stems from VLAs encoding latent features as absolute end-effector positions, essentially memorizing trajectories that fail when objects are moved.

An alternative approach, "Code-as-Policy," involves Large Language Models (LLMs) generating control programs at test time. CAP-AGENT0, a representative of this method, partially recovers robustness on LIBERO-PRO but incurs high computational costs, requiring multiple LLM queries for plan generation and re-querying upon policy failure. Other LLM-to-skill planning systems like SayCan and Inner Monologue sequence pretrained skills, and Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 scales a planner-executor split. Grounded symbolic planning approaches, such as ReKep and MOKA, also leverage keypoints and VLMs but often involve re-optimization or per-trial keypoint selection. LLM-driven Behavior Tree (BT) synthesis methods, including LLM-OBTEA, BETR-XP-LLM, and LLM-as-BT-Planner, focus on generating structured plans from typed grammars.

This work, Sequential Planning via Anchored Robotic Keypoints (SPARK), positions itself as a neurosymbolic manipulation system designed to enhance robustness to environmental perturbations while maintaining computational efficiency. It addresses the limitations of VLAs by avoiding trajectory memorization and the high computational overhead of code-generation agents by re-allocating test-time compute. SPARK integrates neural perception with symbolic planning, departing from methods that re-plan frequently or rely on extensive training data. It builds upon the concept of using behavior trees for structured control but grounds these plans in adaptive, real-time perception.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research arises from the identified limitations of existing robotic manipulation systems when exposed to environmental variability.
1. **Brittleness of VLAs:** Frontier VLA models, despite achieving high scores on standard simulated benchmarks, exhibit significant performance degradation (approaching zero success rates) when object positions or task descriptions are perturbed, as quantified by the LIBERO-PRO benchmark. This is attributed to their tendency to encode latent features as absolute end-effector positions, leading to memorized trajectories that are not robust to changes in the scene layout.
2. **Computational Cost of Code-as-Policy Methods:** While "Code-as-Policy" agents like CAP-AGENT0 offer some recovery of robustness by generating control programs at test time, they are computationally intensive. These agents often require multiple queries to frontier LLMs (e.g., 9 calls per turn for CAP-AGENT0) and must re-query the model to recover from minor policy failures, making them inefficient.
3. **Misallocation of Test-Time Compute:** The authors observe that both VLAs and code-generation agents primarily dedicate their test-time computational budget to reformulating or encoding the manipulation plan. However, environmental shifts (like object displacement or altered language descriptions) primarily impact the visual grounding of objects, rather than the fundamental structure of the task plan itself. The critical failure point under such perturbations is often the perception layer's inability to accurately locate target objects.

Given these observations, the key objectives of the SPARK research are:
1. **Develop a Training-Free Neurosymbolic System:** To create a manipulation system that combines neural perception with symbolic planning without requiring task-specific training.
2. **Reallocate Test-Time Compute to Perception:** To shift the computational focus from plan reformulation to robust object detection and grounding, recognizing that accurate perception is crucial for handling scene perturbations.
3. **Enhance Robustness to Perturbations:** To achieve high success rates on challenging benchmarks like LIBERO-PRO, specifically against position and task-instruction perturbations, outperforming existing VLA and code-generation baselines.
4. **Enable Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** To demonstrate that the same primitive grammar and planning framework can operate effectively across different robot platforms without retraining.
5. **Generate Labeled Training Data:** To provide a mechanism for generating semantically labeled trajectories and execution traces, which can serve as valuable data for training future robotic foundation models, thereby reducing the reliance on costly human teleoperation.
6. **Provide Interpretable Failure Diagnosis:** To offer a system where failures can be traced to specific modules (e.g., perception, planner, controller) or kinematic limits, facilitating debugging and system improvement, a capability often lacking in end-to-end VLA models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPARK (Sequential Planning via Anchored Robotic Keypoints) is a training-free neurosymbolic manipulation system. It comprises four main components: multi-camera perception (SAM3), behavior-tree planning (Gemini), an inverse-kinematics (IK) controller, and a tiered recovery layer.

#### 3.1. Multi-Camera Perception

The system utilizes multiple cameras to capture RGB and depth data at 640 × 480 resolution. For single-arm robots (UR10e, FR3), a fixed bird's-eye camera, a fixed side camera, and a wrist camera are used. The bimanual setup employs a rear camera and one wrist camera per arm. In simulation, the standard LIBERO-PRO agentview third-person camera and robot wrist camera are used.
SAM3, an open-vocabulary detection and segmentation model, is employed to generate masks from text prompts. For each mask, the centroid pixel and median masked depth are computed, then backprojected through known intrinsics to derive a 3D pose in the world frame.
A key aspect of perception in simulation is **Adaptive Perception Self-Consistency**. This involves an additional Gemini call that proposes three alternative text prompts per object, varying descriptors like color, shape, and material. SAM3 evaluates each prompt, and the prompt-label pair yielding the most confident and singular detection is selected. This mechanism aims to improve grounding accuracy by mitigating issues where a single prompt might miss or fragment an object.

#### 3.2. Behavior-Tree Planning

The planning module utilizes Gemini to generate a YAML Behavior Tree (BT) in a single call (Gemini 3.1 Pro in simulation, Gemini 3.5 Flash on hardware, both at temperature 0.3). The BT is built from a typed grammar comprising five base primitives (move to keypoint, move relative, grasp, release, wait) which can compose into over thirty multi-step skills. This grammar is designed to encapsulate low-level control aspects such as quaternion math, depth projection, force calibration, and retry logic, which code-generation agents might otherwise regenerate per trial.
The plan is symbolic; it refers to objects by their labels (e.g., "bowl"), and these labels are resolved to live 3D positions by the perception system at the moment the robot acts. This design allows the plan structure to remain fixed while adapting to dynamic object locations. The grammar is parsed and type-checked before execution; malformed output triggers the recovery layer.

#### 3.3. Execution and Control

An inverse-kinematics (IK) controller executes each move. Pyroki, a JAX-based constrained 6-DOF solver, handles orientation-constrained moves (e.g., drawer pulls). Unconstrained moves fall back to MuJoCo’s Jacobian-pseudoinverse. Cartesian movements are performed using Robosuite's operational-space controller (OSC) with predefined stiffness and damping gains. Consecutive Cartesian waypoints within a plan are executed as a single time-optimal trajectory.

#### 3.4. Tiered Recovery

SPARK incorporates a tiered recovery mechanism that activates when a primitive's post-condition check fails (e.g., a grasp failing to achieve contact). This recovery process does not involve a new LLM call for plan regeneration, thus saving computational resources. The tiers are:
1. **In-place perturbation:** Minor adjustments like a wiggle or grasp perturb to re-establish contact.
2. **Perception re-grounding:** The robot retracts 10 cm along the Z-axis, re-runs SAM3 to obtain fresh object detections, and retries the same plan against these updated positions.
3. **Plan regeneration:** This tier, involving a fresh Gemini call to regenerate the plan, was not utilized in the reported experiments to demonstrate the efficiency of the lower tiers.

#### 3.5. Training-Free Cross-Embodiment Transfer

The same primitive grammar, Gemini planning call, and SAM3 pipeline are applied to three distinct robot families: UR10e, Franka FR3, and a bimanual Franka setup. This demonstrates the system's ability to transfer across different physical embodiments without requiring additional training or task-specific code.

#### 3.6. Data Generation

During execution, every trial logs a semantically labeled record. This includes the behavior tree, full trajectories, timestamped per-primitive traces, object groundings, and the SAM3 detection map. This data is collected automatically without human teleoperation, providing a source of labeled demonstrations for potential future training of foundation models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across simulated and physical environments:

#### 4.1. LIBERO-PRO Results (Simulation)

- **Overall Performance:** SPARK achieved an average success rate of 43.7% across six LIBERO-PRO position-and-task cells. This performance is comparable to RATS (43.8%) and more than double that of CAP-AGENT0 (original 18.2%), MolmoAct2 (18.6%), and π0.5 (12.8%).
- **Spatial Suite Robustness:** SPARK demonstrated strong performance on the spatial suite, averaging 64.2% across position and task perturbations. This significantly outperformed RATS (30.0%), MolmoAct2 (below 23%), and CAP-AGENT0 (below 14%).
- **Impact of Adaptive Perception Self-Consistency:**
 - This mechanism, which proposes three alternative text prompts per object, increased the spatial mean by 27.7 percentage points (from 36.5% to 64.2%) and the object mean by 10.0 points (from 29.9% to 39.9%). This gain was most pronounced where object identity remained consistent but its position changed.
 - However, on goal-task perturbations, adaptive prompts led to a decline in performance (14.0% compared to SPARK's fair baseline of 22.4%). This was attributed to goal rewrites that redefined task objectives or target regions rather than simply displacing objects, causing appearance-varying prompts to introduce noise.
- **Impact of Recovery Loop:** Disabling the tiered recovery loop resulted in an approximate 5-point drop in overall LIBERO-PRO success, indicating its effectiveness in resolving first-frame SAM3 misses.

#### 4.2. CaP-Bench Results (Simulation)

- **Pick-and-Place Tasks:** SPARK matched or exceeded CAP-AGENT0 (M4, which uses multi-turn refinement and a visual differencing module) on all pick-and-place tasks: Lift (100% for both), Stack (97% for SPARK vs. ~95%), and CubeRestack (100% vs. ~95%). This was achieved with a single planning call, in contrast to CAP-AGENT0's multi-turn approach.
- **Tasks Requiring Mid-Execution Feedback:** SPARK's performance trailed on tasks that benefit from observational feedback during execution: Wipe (60% vs. ~85% for CAP-AGENT0), TwoArmLift (63% vs. ~70%), and TwoArmHandover (24% vs. ~30%). This limitation is attributed to SPARK's single-shot plan commitment without a turn-level observation gate.
- **Kinematic Limits:** Both SPARK and CAP-AGENT0 scored 0% on NutAssemblySquare due to shared kinematic constraints of the operational-space controller.

#### 4.3. Physical Experiments

- **Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The same primitive grammar and SAM3 pipeline were deployed on three robot families (UR10e, Franka FR3, bimanual Franka) across nine unique tasks (11 task-embodiment cells), averaging 68% success over 20 trials per task.
- **High-Success Tasks:** Plushie in bowl and Sponge-wash plate tasks saturated at 100% success. Utensils in tray achieved 90% (UR10e) and 80% (FR3).
- **Dominant Failure Modes:** The main failures in physical experiments were attributed to:
 - SAM3 object confusion (e.g., misclassifying utensil handles).
 - Handle-localization offsets (e.g., depth noise on narrow profiles for mug pouring).
 - Dark-fabric detection issues (e.g., inconsistent hem labels on black shirts for garment folding).
 - The traceback scores indicated that the planner and controller were rarely the primary source of failure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPARK system presents several contributions to robotic manipulation, offering potential impact across research and development:

1. **Enhanced Robustness to Scene Perturbations:** SPARK demonstrates a significant improvement in robustness to object position and task-instruction perturbations on the challenging LIBERO-PRO benchmark. By shifting computational focus from plan regeneration to adaptive perception, it addresses a core limitation of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, whose performance collapses under such variability. This implies a step towards more generalizable robotic systems in dynamic environments.

2. **Computational Efficiency:** SPARK achieves its performance with a single Large Language Model (LLM) call for planning, dramatically reducing the LLM compute cost compared to multi-turn code-generation agents like CAP-AGENT0, which require numerous LLM queries per execution turn. This efficiency is critical for real-world deployment where computational resources and latency are constraints.

3. **Interpretable Failure Diagnosis:** The structured Behavior Tree (BT) output and per-primitive post-condition checks provide an interpretable trace of execution. This allows for clear localization of failures to specific modules (e.g., incorrect SAM3 grounding, kinematic limits, planner error), which is often not possible with opaque, end-to-end VLA models. This capability streamlines debugging, analysis, and targeted system improvements.

4. **Training-Free Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The system's ability to operate the same primitive grammar across three distinct robot families (UR10e, Franka FR3, bimanual Franka) without retraining highlights its inherent generalizability. This capacity for hardware agnosticism can accelerate the deployment of robotic solutions across diverse platforms.

5. **Automated Data Generation for Foundation Models:** SPARK's training-free planner not only performs manipulation tasks but also automatically logs interpretable, labeled episode records (BTs, trajectories, object groundings). This provides a valuable, large-scale dataset for training future robotic foundation models, reducing the dependency on labor-intensive and expensive human teleoperation for data collection. This could significantly impact the development pipeline for next-generation robotic learning.

6. **Modular and Extensible Architecture:** The design of SPARK, with independent detector, planner, and controller modules sitting behind a typed plan, allows for individual components to be swapped or upgraded without necessitating retraining of the entire system. This modularity facilitates incremental improvements and integration of new technologies.

In summary, SPARK advances neurosymbolic robotics by offering a robust, efficient, and interpretable framework for manipulation, particularly in environments with visual and linguistic variability. Its ability to generate valuable training data without human intervention also positions it as a facilitator for the development of future generalist robot learning systems.
