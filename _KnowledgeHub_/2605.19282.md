# Rethinking Muon Beyond Pretraining: Spectral Failures and High-Pass Remedies for VLA and RLVR

> [!summary] Summary
> Pion, a new matrix-aware optimizer, rethinks the spectral conditioning of momentum beyond large language model pretraining, introducing a high-pass Newton–Schulz mechanism and a per-head mode to mitigate Muon's limitations in low-rank vision-language-action (VLA) and low-SNR reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) settings. It achieved up to an 85.6% success rate on real robot tasks and prevented model collapse in RLVR, outperforming Muon and AdamW while maintaining computational efficiency.

## Problem

- Muon, a prominent matrix-aware optimizer, showed limitations beyond LLM pretraining, specifically in cross-modality VLA training and RLVR.
- In VLA, Muon's uniform spectral whitening amplifies noise in inherently low-rank action-module gradients, leading to suboptimal performance.
- In RLVR post-training, Muon's uniform whitening exacerbates the impact of low-SNR policy gradients from sparse rewards and high variance, often causing model collapse.

## Method

- Introduces **Pion** (sPectral hIgh-pass Optimization on momeNtum), a drop-in replacement for Muon that replaces uniform spectral whitening with a two-stage Promotion+Suppression Newton–Schulz (NS) mechanism.
- The NS iterations are redesigned to induce a sharp spectral high-pass effect, anchoring dominant singular values at 1 while strategically suppressing noisy tail components towards 0.
- Proposes a 'per-head' mode for attention projection matrices in RLVR, applying high-pass NS independently to each sub-matrix to enable heterogeneous, layer-dependent updates across attention heads.

## Results

- Pion consistently outperformed Muon and AdamW in VLA training, achieving a **100%** success rate on LIBERO Object and an **85.6%** success rate on real robotic tasks, compared to **38.9%** for Muon.
- In RLVR post-training, Pion prevented model collapse that consistently occurred with Muon, yielding significantly higher validation accuracy and faster convergence than AdamW.
- Pion maintained computational efficiency identical to Muon, being approximately 15 times faster than existing low-rank Muon variants, while also exhibiting superior performance across tested ranks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Muon's uniform spectral whitening, while effective for LLM pretraining, is detrimental in regimes with inherently low-rank or low-SNR gradients, such as VLA action modules and RLVR post-training.
> - The effective rank and Signal-to-Noise Ratio of gradients vary significantly across different architectural modules and training phases, necessitating spectrally adaptive optimization strategies.
> - Redesigning Newton–Schulz iterations via distinct scalar polynomials for 'Promotion' and 'Suppression' stages can effectively induce a controlled spectral high-pass filter, stabilizing training without increasing computational cost.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep learning optimization has largely relied on element-wise optimizers such as AdamW. A recent direction in research involves matrix-aware optimizers, which leverage the spectral geometry of weight matrices to guide updates. Muon (MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton–Schulz) is a prominent optimizer in this category. It approximates steepest descent under the spectral norm by using Newton–Schulz (NS) iterations to orthogonalize the momentum matrix, effectively driving its singular values towards 1. Muon has demonstrated performance gains in large language model (LLM) pretraining and has inspired various derivative works focusing on aspects such as per-parameter learning rates, noise robustness, NS coefficient refinement, distributed orthonormalization, and low-rank momentum.

Despite its success in LLM pretraining, Muon's applicability and effectiveness in other deep learning paradigms, particularly beyond initial model pretraining, remain underexplored. Existing low-rank Muon variants, which project momentum onto a top-k subspace, primarily focus on LLM pretraining and often incur substantial computational overhead due to reliance on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) or sketching methods, or use a fixed rank that does not adapt dynamically.

This work addresses this research gap by investigating Muon's performance in two distinct regimes beyond LLM pretraining: cross-modality vision-language-action (VLA) training and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). It identifies specific limitations of Muon in these contexts and proposes a computationally efficient alternative that maintains Muon's core structure while introducing a redesigned spectral shaping mechanism.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the performance and limitations of the Muon optimizer in training paradigms beyond large language model (LLM) pretraining, specifically in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). The central research question posed by the authors is: "(Q) Does Muon exhibit promise or limitations in underexplored training paradigms such as VLA and RLVR? If limitations arise, what are the causes and remedies?"

The motivation for this investigation stems from observed or hypothesized spectral mismatches inherent in Muon's design when applied to these specific training regimes:

- **Motivation for VLA Training:** VLA models integrate vision, language, and action modalities, often adapting a pre-trained vision-language model (VLM) backbone with an additional action head. The authors hypothesize that the gradients associated with different modules (vision, language, action) can exhibit heterogeneous intrinsic dimensionalities. Specifically, the action-module gradients, which typically represent low-dimensional robot control commands, are often inherently low-rank. Muon's core mechanism, which uniformly whitens the spectrum by driving all singular values of the momentum matrix towards 1, could amplify noisy tail directions in these low-rank gradients. This amplification might lead to unstable or suboptimal updates for the action module, despite potentially benefiting higher-rank vision and language modules. Existing low-rank Muon variants are considered unsuitable due to their computational cost or lack of adaptiveness.

- **Motivation for RLVR Post-training:** RLVR is a post-training paradigm for LLMs that refines an initially supervised fine-tuned (SFT) policy using policy gradients guided by rule-based, verifiable rewards. Unlike SFT, which utilizes dense, token-level teacher signals, RLVR often operates with trajectory-level rewards, resulting in sparser learning signals and consequently lower Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) in gradients. The stabilization mechanisms employed in RLVR (e.g., importance sampling, clipping, group-relative normalization) can further increase gradient variance. Muon's uniform spectral whitening, which promotes exploration by assigning equal magnitude to all singular directions, is hypothesized to be detrimental in such low-SNR gradient environments. It risks amplifying noisy directions to the same magnitude as informative ones, potentially corrupting the policy update and leading to model collapse. Furthermore, Muon applies updates to each weight matrix as a single block, disregarding the per-head specialization (heterogeneous update scales) often established in attention projections during pretraining, which may be important for maintaining performance during post-training.

The overarching motivation is to develop an optimizer that can adapt to these spectral characteristics (low-rank, low-SNR, and per-head heterogeneity) while preserving the computational efficiency characteristic of Muon's Newton–Schulz iterations, without resorting to computationally intensive operations like SVD.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose Pion (sPectral hIgh-pass Optimization on momeNtum), an optimizer designed as a drop-in replacement for Muon. Pion's core methodology is to replace Muon's uniform spectral whitening with a two-stage "Promotion+Suppression" mechanism that induces a sharp spectral high-pass effect. This design aims to anchor dominant singular values at 1 while suppressing noisy tail components towards 0, with controllable filter strength.

The methodology is based on the insight that each Newton–Schulz (NS) iteration, as used in Muon, reshapes the normalized singular values of the momentum matrix via a scalar polynomial, while preserving the singular vectors. Therefore, improving NS iterations involves redesigning this polynomial map.

#### 3.1. Two-Stage High-Pass NS Mechanism

Pion splits the default five NS iterations into two stages, each with a distinct scalar polynomial and set of coefficients:

- **Promotion Stage (f_p):** This polynomial is applied for an initial number of steps, denoted as $k_p$. Its purpose is to monotonically amplify all singular values, aiming to lift as many as possible above the subsequent suppression threshold while preserving their relative ordering. The coefficients for $f_p(\sigma) = a_p\sigma + b_p\sigma^3 + c_p\sigma^5$ are derived from three constraints:
 - $f_p(1) = 1$: Ensures that singular values already at 1 remain unchanged.
 - $f_p'(1) = 0$: Guarantees first-order stationarity at $\sigma=1$.
 - $f_p''(1) \le 0$: Ensures boundary concavity, preventing values near 1 from being mapped above 1.
 - Additionally, the coefficient $a_p = f_p'(0)$ is maximized to aggressively promote small singular values. These conditions lead to the fixed coefficients $(a_p, b_p, c_p) = (1.875, -1.25, 0.375)$. This derivative $f_p'(\sigma) = 1.875(1-\sigma^2)^2 \ge 0$ ensures monotonicity on $$.

- **Suppression Stage (f_s):** This polynomial is applied for the remaining $k_s$ steps, where $k_s = 5 - k_p$. Its role is to pin large singular values at 1 while contracting smaller ones towards 0. The coefficients for $f_s(\sigma) = a_s\sigma + b_s\sigma^3 + c_s\sigma^5$ are determined by:
 - $f_s(1) = 1$: Maintains the fixed point at $\sigma=1$.
 - $f_s'(1) = 0$: Maintains first-order stationarity at $\sigma=1$.
 - $f_s'(0) = 0$: This is the spectral filtering condition, removing the linear term near the origin so that small singular values are driven towards 0 by higher-order terms. These constraints uniquely determine the coefficients as $(a_s, b_s, c_s) = (0, 2.5, -1.5)$. The derivative $f_s'(\sigma) = 7.5\sigma^2(1-\sigma^2) \ge 0$ ensures monotonicity.

The parameter $k_p$ (number of promotion steps) acts as a single hyperparameter controlling the high-pass cutoff strength. Empirically, suppression-dominant schedules ($k_s \ge 3$) are found to perform optimally.

#### 3.2. Computational Efficiency

Pion is designed to be a "drop-in" replacement for Muon. It inherits Muon's computational flow and, by only changing the polynomial coefficients of the NS iterations (without introducing SVD or sketching), maintains an identical per-step computational cost.

#### 3.3. Per-Head Mode for Attention Projections

For scenarios like RLVR post-training where pre-trained attention layers exhibit functionally meaningful per-head weight norm heterogeneity, Pion introduces a "per-head" mode.
- **Default Mode:** Applies the high-pass NS iteration to each weight matrix as a single block, similar to standard Muon.
- **Per-Head Mode:** For attention projection matrices, this mode first reshapes the matrix along its head dimension into multiple per-head sub-matrices. The high-pass NS iteration is then applied independently to each sub-matrix. This enables heterogeneous, layer-dependent updates across attention heads, which is hypothesized to preserve crucial pre-trained head structures. This reshape operation incurs no additional computational cost for the NS iterations themselves.

#### 3.4. Optimizer Assignment Strategy

The authors implement specific optimizer assignments based on the spectral characteristics of different model components:
- **VLA Training:** Pion is applied to the action module's 2D matrices, Muon is applied to the vision and language modules' 2D matrices, and AdamW is used for all other parameters (e.g., embeddings, output layer). This is motivated by the action module's low-rank gradients benefiting from Pion's high-pass filtering, while the higher-rank vision and language modules benefit from Muon's uniform whitening.
- **RLVR Post-training:** Pion is applied in its per-head mode to all 2D weight matrices (excluding embeddings and the output layer), with AdamW used for other parameters. This addresses both the low-SNR gradients and the need to preserve per-head heterogeneity.

This methodology aims to provide a spectrally adaptive optimizer that is both effective and efficient across diverse deep learning applications beyond the initial pretraining phase.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive experimental validation for Pion across various VLA and RLVR settings.

#### 4.1. Limitations of Muon in VLA and RLVR

- **VLA Training (Rank Adaptiveness):** Analysis of gradient effective rank (erank) during VLA-Adapter training on LIBERO Object revealed that the action module consistently exhibited a significantly lower erank compared to the vision and language modules. This indicated a low-rank gradient structure for action. Muon, which uniformly whitens this spectrum, underperformed AdamW in this setting. Low-Rank Muon (LRMuon) achieved higher success rates than Muon, confirming the benefit of rank-aware optimization for the action module, but at a computational cost approximately 15 times higher than AdamW and Muon. This empirically supports "Limitation 1: Lack of rank adaptiveness" for conventional Muon.
- **RLVR Post-training (Noise Adaptiveness):** Gradient Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) analysis showed that RLVR (using GRPO) consistently exhibited a much lower SNR than SFT during training. This was attributed to sparser, trajectory-level rewards and the additional variance introduced by stabilization mechanisms. In this low-SNR regime, Muon caused model collapse, with accuracy dropping to near zero. This empirically supports "Limitation 2: Lack of noise adaptiveness" for conventional Muon.

#### 4.2. Pion Performance in VLA Training

- **VLA-Adapter (ℓ1-regression):** Pion consistently outperformed both Muon and AdamW across all four LIBERO task suites (Object, Spatial, Goal, Long). For instance, on LIBERO Object, Pion achieved a 100% success rate after 1,500 training steps, compared to 97.0% for Muon and 32.2% for AdamW. Pion also demonstrated faster convergence, reaching high success rates in fewer training steps.
- **VLANeXt (Flow-matching):** Pion's advantage extended to the flow-matching VLA model, VLANeXt. It consistently surpassed Muon and AdamW on both LIBERO and the more challenging, perturbed LIBERO-Plus benchmark. Notably, Pion showed improved robustness under specific perturbations, such as Language (+9%), Noise (+6%), and Robot (+6%), suggesting more generalizable policies. Qualitative rollouts illustrated Pion generating smoother, collision-free trajectories, in contrast to jittery movements observed with Muon.
- **Real Robot Evaluation:** On a Franka Research 3 robot using a $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone for three grasp-and-place tasks, Pion significantly outperformed both AdamW and Muon. The average success rate increased from 31.1% (AdamW) and 38.9% (Muon) to 85.6% with Pion, under a low-budget training regime (20,000 steps). This indicated that Pion's training efficiency gains extended from simulation to real hardware.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - Pion outperformed LRMuon across all tested top-k ranks while matching Muon’s total training time, highlighting its efficiency and adaptiveness.
 - For VLA training, the default mode of Pion slightly outperformed the per-head mode, suggesting that per-head heterogeneity is less critical when the action head is trained from scratch.
 - A modality-wise optimizer sweep confirmed that the chosen assignment (Pion for action module, Muon for vision/language modules, AdamW elsewhere) was optimal, aligning with the spectral characteristics identified.

#### 4.3. Pion Performance in RLVR Post-training

- **Muon Collapse vs. Pion Success:** Across eight settings (GRPO/GMPO with Qwen3-1.7B/4B on MATH/GSM8K), Muon consistently failed, exhibiting model collapse with accuracy near zero. In contrast, Pion (using its per-head mode) recovered a meaningful training signal and consistently outperformed AdamW, demonstrating faster convergence and higher validation accuracy.
- **Gradient SNR:** Pion consistently achieved a higher gradient SNR than AdamW throughout RLVR training, indicating its effectiveness in handling noisy gradients.
- **Reverse Ablation with Low-Pass Muon (LPMuon):** A low-pass counterpart (LPMuon), designed to contract large singular values and amplify small ones, also failed to train (accuracy remained at the initial checkpoint). This result provides reverse-ablation evidence, indicating that the specific high-pass filtering direction, rather than generic spectral transformation, is crucial for Pion's success in RLVR.

In summary, the main findings establish that Muon's uniform spectral whitening is detrimental in low-rank and low-SNR gradient regimes. Pion, with its high-pass spectral filtering and per-head mode, effectively mitigates these limitations, yielding consistent performance improvements and enhanced training stability and efficiency across VLA and RLVR tasks, often surpassing both AdamW and Muon while maintaining computational efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of deep learning optimization by systematically analyzing the behavior of matrix-aware optimizers beyond their established efficacy in large language model (LLM) pretraining.

The work identifies two distinct yet spectrally related limitations of Muon:
1. **Lack of rank adaptiveness:** In cross-modality VLA training, Muon's uniform spectral whitening amplifies noise in inherently low-rank action-module gradients, leading to suboptimal performance.
2. **Lack of noise adaptiveness:** In RLVR post-training, Muon's uniform whitening exacerbates the impact of low-SNR policy gradients, often resulting in model collapse.

The proposed solution, Pion, addresses these limitations effectively. Its primary significance lies in the introduction of a novel high-pass Newton–Schulz (NS) iteration, which strategically promotes dominant singular values while suppressing noisy tail components. This mechanism is demonstrated to stabilize training and improve performance across diverse applications.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Enhanced Performance in VLA:** Pion's ability to achieve higher success rates and faster convergence in VLA training, both in simulation and on real robotic hardware, could accelerate the development and deployment of more capable and robust embodied AI agents. This can broaden access to advanced robotic capabilities by reducing the computational and data requirements for training.
- **Improved Stability in RLVR:** By preventing model collapse and yielding consistent gains over AdamW in RLVR post-training, Pion offers a more stable and effective approach for fine-tuning reasoning LLMs. This could lead to more reliable and accurate AI systems for complex tasks like mathematical problem-solving.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Pion's design as a drop-in replacement for Muon, maintaining identical per-step computational costs, means that these performance benefits do not come with increased resource demands, making it practical for large-scale model development.
- **Theoretical Contributions:** The spectral analysis of gradient characteristics in different training regimes, combined with the principled design of the Promotion and Suppression polynomials, contributes to a deeper understanding of optimizer behavior and spectral conditioning in deep learning. The reverse ablation with Low-pass Muon provides empirical evidence for the specific benefits of high-pass filtering.

**Broader Implications:**

The findings suggest that the optimal spectral conditioning for a model's gradients can vary significantly depending on the training regime and specific architectural modules. This challenges the notion of a single "universal" optimizer configuration and advocates for more context-aware or adaptively designed optimization strategies.

While the positive impacts include advancing AI capabilities and potentially lowering development barriers, the authors also acknowledge the standard dual-use risks associated with more capable robotic and language-based agents. These risks encompass unsafe deployment scenarios and the potential for misuse in generating harmful content. The research encourages continued integration of safety practices alongside advancements in matrix-aware optimization techniques.
