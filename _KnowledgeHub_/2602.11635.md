# Do MLLMs Really Understand Space? A Mathematical Reasoning Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> Lu et al. introduce MathSpatial, a unified framework for evaluating and improving mathematical spatial reasoning in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). This framework, comprising a benchmark, training corpus, and structured reasoning traces, reveals a significant gap between human (96.3% accuracy) and top MLLM performance (58.5% for GPT-5) in spatial tasks, while improving fine-tuned open-source models by up to 4.3% accuracy and reducing token usage by 25%.

## Problem

- MLLMs struggle with "mathematical spatial reasoning," showing a significant performance gap compared to human accuracy (MLLMs typically < 60% vs. human > 95%).
- Existing spatial reasoning benchmarks are confounded by visual perception complexities that obscure true reasoning failures, and the field lacks large-scale, high-quality training data for systematic MLLM improvement.
- Current MLLM approaches for spatial problem-solving are often black-box, lacking interpretability and transparent intermediate reasoning steps.

## Method

- Developed ==MathSpatial==, a unified framework comprising ==MathSpatial-Bench== (2K problems) for evaluation, MathSpatial-Corpus (8K problems) for training, and MathSpatial-SRT for structured reasoning.
- Constructed a large-scale dataset using a semi-automated pipeline that curates and verifies mathematical spatial problems from educational sources, ensuring geometric consistency and solution reliability.
- Introduced ==MathSpatial-SRT== (Structured Reasoning Traces) to decompose spatial problem-solving into three atomic, interpretable operations (Correlate, Constrain, Infer) for supervised fine-tuning of MLLMs.

## Results

- Humans achieved **96.3%** average accuracy on MathSpatial-Bench, while the best MLLM (GPT-5) reached only **58.5%**, confirming a significant performance deficit.
- Fine-tuning open-source MLLMs with MathSpatial-Corpus and SRTs improved accuracy by up to **4.3%** (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B to **22.1%**) with ~**25%** less token usage, and generalized to external OOD spatial benchmarks (**+1.3%** to **+3.4%** accuracy, **7.1%**-**21.3%** fewer tokens).
- Error analysis revealed that reasoning gaps (**34.4%**) and geometry violations (**33.0%**) were the primary causes of MLLM failures on mathematical spatial tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A profound gap exists between human and MLLM capabilities in mathematical spatial reasoning, underscoring a fundamental limitation of current MLLMs; these failures predominantly stem from core reasoning gaps and violations of geometric rules, highlighting challenges in multi-step logical deduction and consistency enforcement.
> - Disentangling spatial reasoning from perceptual noise is crucial for accurate diagnosis and targeted improvement of MLLMs' cognitive abilities.
> - Structured reasoning traces provide an effective method for enhancing both the accuracy and interpretability of MLLM spatial problem-solving, leading to more concise and efficient reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated substantial progress in perception-oriented tasks, integrating textual and visual information to achieve high performance in areas such as visual question answering and image captioning. However, a significant gap in their capabilities pertains to mathematical spatial reasoning—the ability to interpret and manipulate two- and three-dimensional relationships found in geometric problems. This area is crucial for higher-level cognitive functions and underpins various real-world applications, including robotic manipulation, autonomous driving, and embodied artificial intelligence.

Existing research and benchmarks on spatial reasoning in MLLMs have made contributions but present several limitations. Many current benchmarks embed spatial tasks within visually complex natural scenes or 3D environments. This approach can introduce perceptual confounds, where errors may arise from issues with visual processing rather than reasoning deficiencies, thereby obscuring a clear assessment of true spatial reasoning abilities. Furthermore, the field has largely lacked large-scale, high-quality training datasets specifically designed for mathematical spatial reasoning, resulting in a data bottleneck that hinders systematic model improvement. Finally, a common approach in prior work treats spatial reasoning as an end-to-end black-box process, often relying on free-form chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision without providing interpretable intermediate steps. This makes it challenging to diagnose specific failure modes or to implement targeted improvements in reasoning pathways.

This research addresses these identified limitations by introducing MathSpatial, a dataset and benchmark ecosystem. MathSpatial aims to isolate mathematical spatial reasoning from perceptual noise by utilizing clean geometric diagrams, provides a large-scale training corpus, and incorporates structured reasoning annotations to enable interpretable and verifiable reasoning. By doing so, this work seeks to move beyond merely exposing MLLM deficits in spatial reasoning to providing a systematic foundation for both evaluating and advancing these capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically investigate and evaluate the capacity of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to perform mathematical spatial reasoning, defined as the ability to parse and manipulate two- and three-dimensional relations. The authors observe a substantial disparity: humans readily solve textbook-style spatial reasoning problems with high accuracy (over 95%), whereas leading MLLMs frequently achieve less than 60% accuracy on identical tasks. This performance gap serves as the primary motivation for the study, highlighting spatial reasoning as a fundamental weakness in current MLLMs.

The specific motivations and objectives that guided the creation of the MathSpatial dataset and the associated evaluation framework are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Perceptual Confounds in Evaluation**: Existing spatial reasoning benchmarks often involve visually complex scenes with rendered details. This complexity can introduce "perceptual noise," making it difficult to ascertain whether model failures stem from visual interpretation challenges or genuine reasoning deficiencies. A key objective was to create an evaluation environment, MathSpatial-Bench, that uses clean geometric diagrams with minimal backgrounds and textures. This design aims to isolate and rigorously test pure spatial reasoning ability by mitigating perceptual distractions.
- **Overcoming Data Scarcity for Training**: The research identifies a lack of large-scale, high-quality training corpora specifically tailored for mathematical spatial reasoning. This data bottleneck limits the ability to systematically improve MLLMs' spatial understanding. Therefore, a major objective was to develop MathSpatial-Corpus, a comprehensive training set of problems sourced from authentic educational materials, equipped with verified solutions, to facilitate systematic model development.
- **Enabling Interpretable Reasoning**: Most prior approaches treat MLLM spatial reasoning as an end-to-end black-box process, lacking explicit intermediate supervision. This makes it difficult to understand *why* models fail and to guide targeted improvements. A critical objective was to provide structured reasoning traces for each problem in MathSpatial-Corpus. These traces, based on atomic operations (Correlate, Constrain, Infer), are intended to offer interpretable, step-by-step guidance, moving beyond simple answer-only or free-form chain-of-thought supervision.
- **Comprehensive Diagnostic Evaluation**: The authors aimed to create a benchmark that not only assesses overall performance but also enables fine-grained diagnostic analysis. MathSpatial-Bench was designed to cover a diverse range of spatial reasoning tasks, categorized into Holistic Recognition, Generative Inference, and Abstract Deduction, with 11 distinct subtypes. This allows for a detailed understanding of specific strengths and weaknesses across different facets of spatial reasoning.
- **Establishing Robust Human Baselines**: To provide a clear reference point for MLLM performance, an objective was to establish a rigorous human upper bound through evaluation by human experts under controlled conditions. This helps contextualize MLLM accuracy relative to human capabilities.
- **Demonstrating Practical Value**: Beyond identifying weaknesses, the research aims to demonstrate the practical utility of the MathSpatial dataset for improving MLLMs. This involves showing that training models on MathSpatial-Corpus, especially with its structured reasoning annotations, leads to measurable improvements in both accuracy and reasoning efficiency.

In summary, the motivation stems from a recognized fundamental limitation in MLLMs' spatial reasoning abilities, crucial for advanced AI applications, compounded by the inadequacies of existing evaluation and training resources. The objectives collectively aim to provide a systematic, high-quality, and interpretable framework for advancing this critical area of AI research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the creation and utilization of **MathSpatial**, a large-scale dataset comprising 10,000 mathematical spatial reasoning problems. This dataset is divided into two complementary subsets: MathSpatial-Bench for diagnostic evaluation and MathSpatial-Corpus for systematic training.

#### 3.1. MathSpatial Data Collection and Curation Pipeline

The dataset construction involved a semi-automated, multi-stage pipeline designed for both scale and quality:
1. **Data Collection and Curation**: Problems were systematically gathered from diverse public educational repositories and textbooks (e.g., Baidu Wenku, Zujuan), spanning primary to high school levels. The focus was on formalized spatial reasoning tasks with objective numeric or multiple-choice answers, such as multi-view matching and unfolding/folding. An initial pool of 35,428 raw candidates was collected, followed by a preliminary curation to filter out structurally incomplete items and non-spatial queries, retaining 21,673 problems.
2. **Preprocessing and Standardization**: All retained problems were standardized into a unified schema including images, questions, choices, answers, and solutions. Rigorous cross-split deduplication was performed using MD5 hashing, GPT-4.1 vision-aided visual similarity analysis, and semantic text filtering, followed by manual verification, to prevent data leakage. Low-quality items (e.g., blurry figures, ambiguous statements) were discarded. This process yielded approximately 11,000 unique, high-quality samples. Chinese source problems were then translated into English via API, with quality assured through random spot-checks.
3. **Geometric Consistency Checking**: To ensure the geometric validity of the problems, a rule-based verification process with human-in-the-loop review was applied. This involved checking correspondences of length–width–height across views, enforcing dashed/solid line conventions, and validating orthographic projection rules. Approximately 0.4K geometrically inconsistent items were identified and removed. All remaining items were evaluated against a geometric QA rubric, and cases without annotator consensus were discarded.
4. **Solution Verification**: For problems with official solutions, these were directly retained. For the approximately 0.8K items lacking official solutions, graduate students with backgrounds in geometry and engineering drawing were recruited to derive comprehensive solutions. Each generated solution underwent dual-reviewer cross-validation to ensure logical completeness and reproducibility.
5. **Final Output**: After all filtering stages, 10,000 fully verified problems remained. These were partitioned into MathSpatial-Bench (2,000 problems) for evaluation and MathSpatial-Corpus (8,000 problems) for training. This pipeline ensured data integrity, label reliability, and strict separation between the evaluation and training sets.

#### 3.2. MathSpatial-Bench

This 2,000-problem subset is designed for diagnostic evaluation, emphasizing clarity and isolating reasoning skills by minimizing perceptual distractions. It covers three main categories, further subdivided into eleven subcategories:
- **Holistic Recognition**: Tasks requiring overall understanding of spatial arrangements.
- **Generative Inference**: Tasks involving inferring or generating views/properties from given information.
- **Abstract Deduction**: Tasks demanding multi-step reasoning and deduction of geometric properties.
The distribution across subcategories (e.g., Geometric Property Calculation (GPC) 24.1%, Missing-View Completion (MVC) 17.9%) allows for fine-grained error analysis.

#### 3.3. MathSpatial-Corpus

This 8,000-problem subset is for training, providing problem images, textual descriptions, final answers, and detailed solutions. A significant feature is the inclusion of **Structured Reasoning Annotations (MathSpatial-SRT)** for every problem. These traces are organized around three atomic operations:
- **Correlate**: Establishing cross-view correspondences between geometric entities.
- **Constrain**: Applying geometric and projection rules to enforce consistency.
- **Infer**: Deducting latent attributes or final answers.
GPT-4o was used to generate these operation-level traces under a constrained schema (operation type, arguments, assertion). A dual-role review scheme (Reviewer agent and Checker agent) was implemented to validate and correct approximately 10% of the generated traces, ensuring annotation quality and reducing structural or logical noise. The linearized (trace, answer) sequence is then used for supervised fine-tuning.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Models Evaluated**: 16 leading MLLMs were benchmarked:
 - **Closed-source**: GPT-5, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5/3.7/4, Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash.
 - **Open-source**: Qwen2.5-VL-7B/72B, InternVL3-8B, Llama3-8B, GLM-4.5V.
- **Fine-tuning**: To demonstrate the training value of MathSpatial-Corpus, three open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, InternVL3-8B, Llama3-8B) were fine-tuned on the corpus using a batch size of 128, a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁵, and 5 epochs.
- **Human Performance Baseline**: 80 students were recruited to solve problems under closed-book conditions. Each problem was answered by 20 independent annotators, and results were reported as micro-averaged accuracy.
- **Robustness**: All model results were averaged over two independent runs.
- **Error Analysis**: A fine-grained error analysis categorized errors into six types: Projection Error, Feature Error, Scale Error, Geometry Violation, Reasoning Gaps, and Deduction Failure, to identify dominant failure modes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 16 leading MLLMs on MathSpatial-Bench revealed several key findings regarding their mathematical spatial reasoning capabilities:

- **Significant Human-Model Performance Gap**: A substantial disparity exists between human and MLLM performance. Humans achieved over 95% accuracy across all categories on MathSpatial-Bench, while the majority of MLLMs scored below 60%. This finding consistently highlights mathematical spatial reasoning as a notable weakness for current models.
- **Performance of Closed-Source Models**:
 - **GPT-5** demonstrated the highest overall accuracy among all MLLMs, achieving 58.5%. While leading across most subtypes, this still represents only about 60% of human performance (96.3%).
 - **Gemini-2.5-flash** (48.5%) and **Gemini-2.5-pro** (44.9%) formed the second tier, showing relative strength in visual transformation tasks (VT) and missing-view completion (MVC).
 - The **GPT-4 series** (19.6%–22.6%) and **Claude family** (21.5%–26.7%) generally lagged significantly, with particularly low scores (near 0.0%–0.2%) on numerically demanding tasks like Geometric Property Calculation (GPC) and Visual Transformation (VT).
- **Performance of Open-Source Models**:
 - Most open-source models clustered in a lower accuracy range of 15%–21%.
 - Even increasing model scale provided limited benefits, as **Qwen2.5-VL-72B** (19.7%) showed only a marginal improvement over its **7B variant** (17.8%).
 - Many open-source models scored 0.0% on GPC tasks, indicating a complete inability to perform geometric property calculations.
 - **GLM-4.5V** (21.0%) was identified as the strongest base open-source model but still trailed most closed-source systems.
- **Varying Difficulty Across Reasoning Categories**:
 - **Holistic Recognition** was found to be the most accessible category for models, with top MLLMs achieving 40%–66% on subtypes such as Inverse View Identification (IVI) and Three-View Matching (3VM).
 - **Generative Inference** presented mixed results, with Missing-View Completion (MVC) being tractable for stronger models, while Visual Transformation (VT) remained challenging for most.
 - **Abstract Deduction** was the hardest category overall. Property Relationship Assertion (PRA) was relatively more manageable (GPT-5: 71.7%), but Geometric Property Calculation (GPC) proved to be a near-universal failure point (≤4% for open-source models, ≤52% for GPT-5), highlighting multi-step geometric computation as a fundamental bottleneck.
- **Value of Training on MathSpatial-Corpus**:
 - Fine-tuning on MathSpatial-Corpus consistently improved performance across all three backbone open-source models. For instance, **MathSpatial-InternVL3-8B** reached 22.6% (from a 17.4% base), and **MathSpatial-Qwen2.5-VL-7B** improved to 22.1%.
 - These fine-tuned models also demonstrated improved efficiency, reducing token consumption by 20%–30%, suggesting that the structured annotations in the corpus promote both accuracy and more efficient reasoning.
- **Fine-grained Error Analysis**:
 - The majority of errors stemmed from **Reasoning Gaps** (34.4%) and **Geometry Violations** (33.0%), indicating difficulties in maintaining coherent multi-step reasoning chains and enforcing low-level geometric consistency.
 - Other significant error types included Projection Errors (12.6%) and Feature Errors (10.5%). Scale Errors (7.2%) and Deduction Failures (2.3%) were less frequent but still present.
 - Error profiles varied among model families: GPT-5/4 series were dominated by reasoning gaps, Claude models by geometric rule violations, and Gemini-2.5-flash by projection and scale errors. Open-source models like Qwen2.5-VL-7B primarily exhibited reasoning gaps.
 - Notably, fine-tuning on MathSpatial-Corpus was observed to reduce reasoning gaps, suggesting the structured annotations aid in mitigating this specific failure mode.

In summary, the results underscore that despite advancements in other domains, current MLLMs possess significant limitations in mathematical spatial reasoning, particularly in complex geometric computations and maintaining consistent reasoning chains. The MathSpatial dataset, especially its training corpus with structured annotations, offers a promising avenue for addressing these deficiencies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research, through the introduction of the MathSpatial dataset and its comprehensive evaluation framework, provides several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of multimodal large language models and artificial intelligence.

First, **MathSpatial represents the first large-scale, systematically curated dataset specifically dedicated to mathematical spatial reasoning in MLLMs**, offering a critical resource for both diagnostic evaluation and model training. By providing 2,000 carefully curated evaluation problems in MathSpatial-Bench and 8,000 training problems with verified solutions and structured reasoning traces in MathSpatial-Corpus, the work directly addresses the existing data bottleneck and lack of rigorous evaluation methodologies in this domain.

Second, the design of **MathSpatial-Bench significantly advances diagnostic capabilities**. By employing clean geometric diagrams, the benchmark successfully isolates pure spatial reasoning abilities from perceptual noise that often confounds evaluations in visually complex scenes. This allows for a clearer identification of specific weaknesses, categorized across 3 major types and 11 subtypes of spatial reasoning tasks. The established human performance baseline (over 95% accuracy) serves as a robust upper bound, highlighting the considerable gap between human and current MLLM capabilities (most MLLMs below 60%, even GPT-5 at 58.5%).

Third, the **fine-grained error analysis provides actionable insights for future model development**. The identification of reasoning gaps (34.4%) and geometry violations (33.0%) as the dominant failure modes is crucial. This diagnosis indicates that MLLMs struggle not only with enforcing low-level geometric consistency but also with sustaining coherent, multi-step reasoning chains. These findings pinpoint fundamental architectural or training deficiencies that researchers can now target with specific interventions.

Fourth, the **MathSpatial-Corpus, with its unique structured reasoning annotations (Correlate, Constrain, Infer), demonstrates practical value for advancing spatial reasoning capabilities**. The consistent improvements observed across various open-source models after fine-tuning on MathSpatial-Corpus—manifesting as increased accuracy and reduced token consumption—suggest that these explicit, interpretable intermediate supervisions are effective in promoting more accurate and efficient reasoning. This structured approach offers a clear alternative or complement to free-form chain-of-thought methods, potentially leading to more robust and verifiable spatial understanding.

The potential impact of this work extends to various downstream applications. Improved mathematical spatial reasoning in MLLMs could significantly accelerate progress in:
- **Robotic Manipulation**: Enabling robots to better understand and interact with 3D environments, plan complex movements, and manipulate objects with precision.
- **Autonomous Driving**: Enhancing the spatial perception and predictive capabilities of self-driving vehicles, particularly in interpreting complex road geometries, object relationships, and dynamic spatial changes.
- **Embodied Intelligence**: Advancing the development of AI agents that can navigate, reason, and act within physical or simulated environments with a deeper understanding of spatial layouts and object properties.

Furthermore, MathSpatial sets a new standard for evaluating and training MLLMs on a critical cognitive ability, inspiring future research into more advanced automated self-verification frameworks and the expansion of spatial reasoning tasks to dynamic transformations and complex 3D environments. By providing a publicly available resource, MathSpatial is positioned to foster collaborative research and drive innovation in bridging the identified human-model gap in spatial intelligence.
