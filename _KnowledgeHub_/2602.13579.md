# TactAlign: Human-to-Robot Policy Transfer via Tactile Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> A cross-sensor tactile alignment method, TactAlign, facilitates human-to-robot policy transfer for contact-rich tasks by creating a shared latent representation for heterogeneous human and robot tactile sensors using unpaired demonstrations and rectified flow. This approach enables robust policy generalization and zero-shot dexterous manipulation across various tasks and objects.

## Problem

- Transferring dexterous manipulation skills from humans to robots, especially those reliant on tactile feedback, is challenging due to the 'embodiment gap' between human and robot hands.
- Heterogeneous tactile sensors used on human hands (e.g., tactile gloves) and robot manipulators (e.g., fingertip sensors) present a significant barrier to direct tactile signal transfer.
- Existing human-to-robot policy transfer methods often require identical sensors, strictly paired datasets, or extensive manual labeling, limiting their scalability and generalizability for contact-rich tasks.

## Method

- The method employs self-supervised learning to independently encode human and robot tactile signals into modality-specific latent features.
- Noisy 'pseudo-pairs' are extracted from unpaired human and robot demonstrations using a robust pipeline for hand and object pose estimation, followed by binary contact filtering to refine matches.
- Tactile alignment is performed via rectified flow, guided by these pseudo-pairs, to learn a continuous mapping that transforms human tactile features into an aligned robot-like latent space.

## Results

- TactAlign achieved high average success rates across various contact-rich tasks: 76% for pivoting, 72% for insertion, and 74% for lid closing, demonstrating strong generalization to unseen objects and task categories.
- The method enabled 100% success for the complex, dexterous light-bulb screwing task through zero-shot human-to-robot policy transfer, trained purely on human demonstrations.
- Cross-sensor force prediction error was substantially reduced by 93-99% compared to unaligned tactile features, demonstrating that TactAlign's alignment preserves crucial physical properties of contact.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rectified flow can effectively align heterogeneous tactile sensor data from different embodiments and without paired demonstrations, by leveraging noisy pseudo-pairs derived from hand-object interactions and filtered by contact states.
> - The aligned latent space significantly reduces the cross-domain tactile distribution gap, decreasing Earth Mover's Distance by 78%, and intrinsically preserves physically meaningful contact information such as normalized force magnitudes, without explicit force supervision.
> - Aligned tactile feedback is crucial for robust policy generalization in contact-rich manipulation tasks, enabling high success rates on unseen objects and tasks where policies relying solely on proprioception or unaligned tactile input frequently fail.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

The paper "TactAlign: Human-to-Robot Policy Transfer via Tactile Alignment" presents a novel approach to overcome a significant challenge in robot learning: transferring dexterous manipulation skills from humans to robots, particularly when relying on tactile feedback. Humans offer a rich source of demonstration data that is both fast to collect and inherently multi-modal, including critical tactile sensations. However, bridging the "embodiment gap" between human hands and robot manipulators, especially concerning heterogeneous tactile sensors, has remained a formidable hurdle. TactAlign proposes a cross-sensor tactile alignment method that enables effective policy transfer without requiring paired datasets, identical tactile sensors, or extensive manual labeling, thereby significantly advancing the scalability and generality of human-to-robot (H2R) policy learning for contact-rich tasks.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly focused on acquiring complex skills efficiently. Human demonstrations have emerged as a compelling data source due to their inherent dexterity, multi-modality, and faster collection rates compared to robot teleoperation. However, a critical bottleneck in Human-to-Robot (H2R) policy transfer has been the embodiment gap—the fundamental differences in physical form, sensing capabilities, and kinematic structures between humans and robots.

Most existing H2R approaches have traditionally focused on transferring readily available observations like egocentric vision or state-action pairs in kinematic space. Tactile feedback, despite being indispensable for dexterous and contact-rich manipulation in humans, has remained largely underexplored. Initial efforts to incorporate tactile sensing into H2R learning often assume identical tactile sensors between human and robot or a minimal embodiment gap, simplifying the transfer but severely limiting scalability and applicability across diverse robot hands. For instance, concurrent work like UniTacHand addresses cross-sensor tactile transfer but demands strict spatiotemporal correspondence between human and robot demonstrations, which is prohibitively difficult to maintain during dynamic, contact-rich interactions.

Beyond H2R, the broader challenge of cross-sensor tactile transfer has been studied, but existing methods primarily focus on static contact scenarios or coarse categorical alignments. These approaches frequently rely on paired supervision or manual labels, which restrict their scalability and effectiveness for continuous tactile reasoning during dynamic interactions involving sliding contact or object motion.

Wearable devices, such as tactile gloves, represent a promising avenue for collecting rich human demonstration data, preserving natural human dexterity and haptic feedback. However, these devices often involve trade-offs between degrees of freedom (DOF) and tactile signal richness, with many being limited to kinematics-only data or sensing only normal forces. The OSMO tactile glove used in this work stands out by combining high dexterity with rich shear and normal force sensing, offering a superior platform for human data collection.

TactAlign directly addresses these critical limitations. By proposing a method that can align tactile signals from *heterogeneous* sensors across *different embodiments* using *unpaired* demonstrations, it significantly broadens the applicability of human-collected tactile data for robot learning. It moves beyond the restrictive assumptions of identical sensors, paired data, and static contacts, thereby enabling a more generalizable and scalable paradigm for H2R policy transfer. This positions TactAlign as a crucial advancement for making human expertise readily accessible to a wide range of robotic platforms performing complex, contact-rich tasks.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of TactAlign is to enable effective human-to-robot (H2R) policy transfer by addressing the fundamental challenge of transferring human-collected tactile signals to robots with heterogeneous tactile sensors and different embodiments. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives and is driven by clear motivations:

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Develop a Cross-Embodiment Tactile Alignment Method:** The core objective is to create TactAlign, a novel method capable of transforming human and robot tactile observations into a shared latent representation. This alignment must occur despite significant differences in sensing modalities and embodiment (e.g., human tactile glove vs. robot fingertip sensors).
2. **Enable Alignment from Unpaired Datasets:** A crucial objective is to achieve this cross-sensor alignment without requiring strictly paired datasets, manual labels, or privileged information. This significantly reduces data collection burden and increases scalability.
3. **Utilize Rectified Flow with Pseudo-Pairs:** Specifically, the method aims to leverage rectified flow, guided by noisy pseudo-pairs derived from hand-object interactions, to learn a robust latent mapping between human and robot tactile spaces.
4. **Improve H2R Policy Co-Training Success:** A practical objective is to demonstrate that the learned tactile alignment significantly improves the success rates of H2R co-trained policies across various contact-rich manipulation tasks.
5. **Achieve Generalization to Unseen Objects and Tasks:** The method aims to show that policies trained with TactAlign can generalize effectively to novel object instances and even entirely new task classes, using only a small amount of human demonstration data.
6. **Facilitate Zero-Shot Dexterous Manipulation:** A high-level objective is to enable zero-shot human-to-robot policy transfer for highly dexterous and challenging tasks, relying solely on human demonstrations without any robot-specific training data for that task.

**Motivation:**
- **Leveraging Rich Human Data:** Human demonstrations are a valuable, fast, and dexterous source of supervision for policy learning, inherently guided by rich tactile feedback. Capitalizing on this data source can significantly accelerate robot skill acquisition.
- **Overcoming Embodiment and Sensor Gaps:** The inherent differences in sensing modalities (e.g., how a human tactile glove measures touch versus a robot's fingertip sensor) and physical embodiment (human hand vs. robot hand) create a substantial barrier to direct tactile transfer. Current H2R methods largely ignore touch or make restrictive assumptions (identical sensors, paired data) that limit their real-world applicability.
- **Enabling Contact-Rich Manipulation:** Many real-world manipulation tasks, such as insertion, pivoting, or screwing, are highly contact-rich and demand sophisticated tactile reasoning. Without effective tactile transfer, robots struggle to perform these tasks robustly.
- **Reducing Data Collection Costs:** Collecting robot demonstration data is notoriously expensive, time-consuming, and often requires specialized expertise. By enabling robots to learn from easily collected human demonstrations, TactAlign offers a low-cost, scalable alternative.
- **Fostering Generalization:** Robust tactile alignment can foster more generalizable policies that are less susceptible to variations in objects, environments, or even entirely new tasks, making robots more adaptable.

In essence, TactAlign is motivated by the desire to unlock the full potential of human expertise for robot learning by making the critical sense of touch transferable across diverse robotic platforms, ultimately leading to more capable and adaptable autonomous systems.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

TactAlign is designed to facilitate human-to-robot (H2R) policy transfer by creating a shared latent representation for tactile observations from heterogeneous sensors and embodiments. The method proceeds in two main stages: self-supervised representation learning and cross-sensor alignment via rectified flow, followed by H2R policy co-training.

#### 4.1. Problem Statement

The goal is to learn a latent-space mapping `g: T_h → T_r`, where `T_h` and `T_r` are latent spaces derived from human and robot tactile observations, respectively. The system assumes access to two offline, unpaired datasets of task demonstration trajectories: `H` (human, collected with a tactile glove) and `R` (robot, with heterogeneous fingertip tactile sensors). A crucial assumption is that a subset of these trajectories (`A_h ⊂ H` and `A_r ⊂ R`) corresponds to the same task and shares similar start/end object states, allowing for initial correspondences through object pose estimates.

#### 4.2. Tactile Self-supervised Learning

To handle the heterogeneous sensing modalities, TactAlign first learns unique encoders and decoders for human and robot tactile signals independently.
- **Input:** Per-fingertip tactile observations (e.g., `f_g_i` for human glove, `f_r_j` for robot Xela sensor) are treated as spatio-temporal windows (0.1 seconds, with different spatial resolutions and dimensions for human and robot).
- **Architecture:** The architecture (Fig. 2 left) is based on the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), with a decoder adapted from an online probe module.
- **Training:** Encoders are trained using a mean squared error (MSE) reconstruction loss to preserve modality-specific structure in a self-supervised manner.
- **Output:** This stage yields pre-trained encoders `Enc_h` and `Enc_r` that map raw tactile observations into a shared latent feature dimension `R^d` (e.g., `h_i ∈ R^d` and `r_i ∈ R^d`). A cross-attention based pooling module is used at the end of each encoder to ensure consistent latent feature length despite varying input dimensions.

#### 4.3. Pseudo-Pair Extraction from Demonstrations

To bridge the gap between human and robot latent spaces without explicit pairing, TactAlign constructs "pseudo-pairs" from the offline demonstrations.
- **Hand and Object Pose Extraction:** An advanced pipeline is used to extract human hand and object poses from each trajectory. This involves WiLoR (for fingertip and wrist poses), SAM3 (for hand masks), ICP and FoundationStereo (for pose refinement), and a Savitzky–Golay filter (for temporal consistency). For object pose, a mesh-less pipeline adapted from Any6D is used, involving FoundationPose, FoundationStereo, and SAM3D (for object mesh reconstruction from prompts). This pipeline runs in a single pass without privileged information like CAD models.
- **Transition Definition:** Transitions `O_h_i` and `O_r_j` are defined by the current and next fingertip and object poses (`p_t, o_t`).
- **Similarity Metric:** A similarity metric `S(O_h_i, O_r_j)` is computed in a normalized pose space, accounting for both absolute poses and changes in poses (velocities) of fingertips and objects. A balancing scalar `λ = 1` weights position and velocity terms.
- **Pseudo-Pair Creation:** Pseudo-pairs `P = {(h*_i, r*_j)}` are formed by matching human and robot transitions (`O_h_i`, `O_r_j`) where `S(O_h_i, O_r_j)` falls below a similarity threshold `δ`. The method identifies nearest-neighbor matches within this threshold, making the pairs inherently noisy.
- **Binary Contact Filtering:** To refine the noisy pseudo-pairs, a crucial step involves filtering based on contact information. Only pseudo-pairs that map contact-to-contact states or non-contact-to-non-contact states are retained, determined by a simple threshold on the raw tactile signal norm (`||f_t||`). This helps exclude semantically inconsistent matches.

#### 4.4. Tactile Alignment via Rectified Flow

The core alignment mechanism is formulated as a rectified flow problem.
- **Concept:** Unlike standard rectified flow which uses random pairings, TactAlign guides the flow with the extracted noisy pseudo-pairs. This means the model learns a velocity field `v_θ` that transports human tactile features to robot tactile features.
- **Training:** For each pseudo-pair `(h*_i, r*_j) ∈ P`, an interpolated latent state `x_t = t ⋅ h*_i + (1-t) ⋅ r*_j` is defined, with a constant velocity `h*_i - r*_j`. The velocity field `v_θ` is trained by minimizing a least-squares regression loss over `t ∈`, effectively learning to map `h*_i` to `r*_j` while minimizing transport cost (Fig. 3).
- **Inference:** To transform a human tactile feature `h_i` to an aligned robot-like feature `^h_i`, the learned ordinary differential equation (ODE) `dx_t = v_θ(x_t, t)` is simulated from `x_0 = h_i` to `x_1 = ^h_i`.
- **Architecture:** `v_θ` is parameterized by a compact multilayer perceptron (MLP).

#### 4.5. Human to Robot Policy Learning

Finally, for policy learning, an ACT-adapted policy `π_φ` is used, which is shared across both human and robot embodiments.
- **Input:** The policy takes per-fingertip tactile latent features (aligned `^h_t,k` for human, raw `r_t,k` for robot) and proprioceptive information (fingertip poses `P_t`, wrist pose `w_t`).
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs action chunks specifying desired fingertip locations and wrist orientation relative to the robot base frame. For human demonstrations, the wrist orientation is adjusted by a constant offset.
- **Training:** Only the modules of the ACT policy (proprioceptive encoder, transformer decoder) are trained; the tactile encoders and the rectified flow alignment module are kept frozen. The policy operates at 10-30 Hz during execution, converting predicted action representations into joint commands via inverse kinematics (PyRoki).

This integrated methodology allows TactAlign to effectively bridge the tactile embodiment gap, enabling robots to learn complex, contact-rich manipulation skills from human demonstrations using readily available, unpaired data.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

TactAlign's effectiveness is evaluated through a series of experiments focusing on tactile alignment quality, H2R policy co-training, zero-shot dexterous manipulation, and cross-sensor force estimation.

#### 5.1. Hardware and Dataset Overview

- **Human:** Demonstrations are collected using the OSMO tactile glove, which provides 3-axis magnetic tactile signals, and for the light-bulb screwing task, a Manus glove for robust hand pose estimation.
- **Robot:** A Franka Emika Panda arm equipped with an Allegro Hand, featuring Xela magnetic tactile sensors on its fingertips. A RealSense D455 camera is used for visual input, and an ATI Gamma force-torque (F/T) sensor is used exclusively for force prediction evaluation.
- **Datasets:**
 - **Self-supervised Learning:** Approximately 10 minutes of "play data" combined with in-domain tactile alignment data.
 - **Tactile Alignment:** 100 robot (kinesthetic teaching) and 200 human demonstrations across pivoting and insertion tasks, focusing on object-hand interactions.
 - **Policy Co-training:** For pivoting, insertion, and lid-closing (new task for alignment), 140-160 human demonstrations (including "seen-by-both," "human-only," and additional "unseen-by-both" objects) and 50 robot demonstrations (kinesthetic teaching on a single training object).
 - **Dexterous Policy Learning (Human-only):** 20 human demonstrations for light-bulb screwing, collecting only human data.
 - **Force Prediction:** 1,472 robot and 1,527 human force samples, labeled with an F/T sensor, used solely for evaluation (robot data for training the force decoder, human for testing cross-sensor prediction).

#### 5.2. Learned Rectified Flow Analysis

- **UMAP Projections (Fig. 5):** Visualizations show that after alignment, human tactile features (from the OSMO glove) shift significantly towards and largely overlap with robot tactile features (from Xela sensors). This demonstrates a substantial reduction in the cross-domain tactile distribution gap.
- **Earth Mover's Distance (EMD):** Quantitatively, the EMD between the human and robot tactile distributions decreases by 78% after alignment (from 0.091 to 0.020), confirming the effectiveness of the rectified flow in bringing the distributions closer.
- **Physical Meaningfulness:** Intriguingly, the UMAP projections also reveal a consistent trend in normalized force magnitudes across domains. Glove features with higher contact forces map to robot features with similarly high normalized forces, and vice-versa, despite force information *not* being explicitly used during alignment training. This suggests that the aligned latent space preserves physically meaningful contact information.

#### 5.3. Human-Robot Policy Co-Training

Table I and II summarize the performance on three contact-rich tasks: pivoting, insertion, and lid closing (the latter serving as an unseen task for alignment training). Policies are evaluated on "seen-by-both," "human-only," and "unseen-by-both" object categories.
- **Robot-only Baseline:** Shows limited generalization, particularly to human-only or unseen objects (e.g., 0% success in many cases). This highlights the need for human data diversity.
- **TactAlign w/o Tactile Baseline:** Policies relying only on proprioception exhibit significantly lower success rates (average +59% lower than full TactAlign), especially for pivoting (0% success). This underscores the critical role of tactile feedback in these tasks.
- **TactAlign w/o Alignment Baseline:** Removing tactile alignment results in a drastic performance drop (overall -51% average success compared to full TactAlign). Non-aligned tactile features are often detrimental, leading to near-complete failure for pivoting and insertion on "seen-by-both" objects. This conclusively proves that *alignment is crucial* for effective tactile transfer.
- **TactAlign (Full Method):** Achieves strong and consistent performance across all tasks and object categories (76% pivoting, 72% insertion, 74% lid closing). It boasts 100% success on "seen-by-both" objects and strong generalization to "human-only" (71%) and "held-out" (65.5%) objects. These results affirm TactAlign's ability to leverage diverse human data and tactile alignment for robust cross-task and cross-object generalization.

#### 5.4. Dexterous Robot Policy Learning with Human Data Only

The light-bulb screwing task (Fig. 9, Table III) is a challenging, dexterous, and occlusion-heavy manipulation scenario.
- **TactAlign:** Achieves 100% success rate, completing the task in approximately 61 seconds on average. This demonstrates *zero-shot* H2R transfer for highly dexterous tasks, trained purely on human demonstrations.
- **Without Tactile:** 0% success, with failure modes including inability to establish stable contact.
- **Without Alignment:** 0% success, with failures due to jamming and unrecoverable states.
The stark performance difference highlights the indispensable role of both tactile input and alignment for complex dexterous tasks where robot data is scarce or impossible to collect for every variation.

#### 5.5. H2R Force Estimation

A cross-sensor force prediction task evaluates if the aligned latent space preserves physically meaningful information. A robot force decoder `D_r` is trained on robot tactile features.
- **H → R w/o alignment:** High L1 force prediction errors across all axes (e.g., ~15N for Fx, ~19N for Fz).
- **H → R with TactAlign:** Reduces L1 force prediction error by approximately 98% (Fx), 99% (Fy), and 93% (Fz) compared to without alignment.
- **R → R Baseline:** Represents the ideal scenario (training and testing on robot data). With alignment, H → R performance reaches within 2% (Fx) and 13% (Fy) of this best-case baseline. A larger gap remains for Fz (~0.7N difference).
This quantitative analysis confirms that TactAlign's alignment preserves crucial physical properties, enabling robust force estimation across heterogeneous tactile sensors without explicit force supervision during alignment training.

### 6. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

TactAlign represents a significant step forward in human-to-robot policy transfer, particularly for contact-rich manipulation tasks. Its core contribution is enabling the transfer of valuable human tactile information to robots despite fundamental differences in sensing modalities and physical embodiments. This is achieved without the restrictive requirements of paired datasets, identical sensors, or manual labeling, making the approach highly scalable and practical.

The ability to leverage low-cost, dexterous human demonstrations to train robust robot policies has profound implications:
- **Reduced Data Collection Costs:** By making human data directly usable, TactAlign significantly lowers the financial and time investment typically required for robot learning, democratizing access to advanced robotic capabilities.
- **Enhanced Robot Dexterity and Generalization:** The research demonstrates substantial improvements in robot policy success rates and robust generalization to unseen objects and even new tasks. This capability moves robots closer to performing complex, real-world tasks that currently demand significant human intervention or are beyond current robotic capabilities.
- **Zero-Shot Skill Transfer:** The success in the light-bulb screwing task highlights the potential for zero-shot transfer of highly dexterous manipulation skills solely from human demonstrations. This can unlock new domains for robot autonomy, especially in scenarios where collecting robot data is difficult, dangerous, or impractical.
- **Physically Meaningful Latent Spaces:** The success in cross-sensor force prediction, without explicit force supervision during alignment, indicates that TactAlign creates latent representations that capture underlying physical interactions. This could lead to more interpretable and robust tactile perception systems.
- **Foundation for Multi-Modal Learning:** By effectively addressing the tactile transfer challenge, TactAlign lays groundwork for future multi-modal learning systems that can integrate vision, proprioception, and touch seamlessly from human demonstrations.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that offer clear avenues for future research:
- **Limited Hardware Evaluation:** The current evaluation is restricted to a single human glove (OSMO) and robot (Xela/Allegro Hand) pairing due to hardware availability. Expanding the approach to a broader array of tactile modalities (e.g., vision-based tactile sensors) and diverse robot hands would be crucial to fully validate its sensor-agnostic claims.
- **Tactile-Only Focus:** TactAlign primarily addresses tactile discrepancies and does not incorporate other modalities like vision or proprioception into the alignment process itself. While the policy learning stage uses proprioception, a holistic multi-modal alignment strategy could offer further improvements.
- **Multi-Hand and Full-Palm Sensing:** The current work focuses on fingertip tactile sensing. Extending the method to multi-hand coordination or full-palm tactile coverage could unlock even more complex manipulation capabilities.

Despite these limitations, TactAlign provides a compelling framework for scalable H2R policy transfer, effectively bridging the tactile embodiment gap and paving the way for more dexterous, adaptable, and human-informed robots.
