# Interaction Replica: Tracking Human-Object Interaction and Scene Changes From Human Motion

> [!summary] Summary
> iReplica captures human-object interaction using only wearable sensors: body-mounted IMUs plus a head-mounted camera. It localizes the person in a prescanned interactive environment, predicts hand-contact timing from pose sequences alone with a transformer, corrects the trajectory to snap the human onto the contacted object, then drives the object along its degrees of freedom, cutting object localization error to 6.88 cm.

## Problem

- Prior human-object interaction capture relies on external cameras or object trackers, infeasible for non-expert users who cannot mount and calibrate multi-camera rigs in large multi-room scenes.
- Egocentric trackers like HPS localize the person but treat scenes as static, so doors or furniture moved by the subject are never updated.
- Head-camera contact prediction fails because interactions are often out of view, and existing contact datasets cover few object types or only hand-held items.

## Method

- ==Egocentric human visual localization==: an angle-based trajectory optimization energy refines the IMU trajectory toward head-camera control points, preserving IMU-measured distance while correcting drift-prone curvature.
- A 21.9k-parameter ==sequence-to-sequence Transformer== maps 61 frames of SMPL pose vectors to per-hand contact probabilities, trained with binary cross-entropy on the new H-contact dataset of 680k annotated frames.
- ==Contact-based trajectory correction== re-runs the optimization with the nearest object contact point as sole constraint; interaction modeling then attaches the object and drives it by its degrees of freedom until predicted release.

## Results

- Object localization error averages **6.88 cm** and **7.13 degrees**, versus **60.81 cm** for HPS, **38.58 cm** with RGB contact detector HOD, and **11.68 cm** without contact correction.
- Pose-only contact prediction reaches **0.807** average precision and **0.905** accuracy on unseen subjects, far above RGB baselines HOD at **0.044** AP and VISOR at **0.217** AP.
- Mean hand-to-object distance during interaction stays at **2.93 cm** versus **33.61 cm** for HPS and **10.44 cm** for the ground-truth-interpolation baseline; users preferred **iReplica** in **84.2%** of comparisons.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Full-body pose sequences alone carry strong contact signal: body kinematics beat state-of-the-art egocentric vision for detecting when hands grab objects the camera never sees.
> - Jointly solving coupled sub-problems pays off: contact timestamps regularize localization, and better localization sharpens contact placement, an interdependence each sub-field misses when solving them in isolation.
> - Cheap low-dimensional inertial signals can substitute for vision in power-constrained wearables, but the approach still presumes a prescanned scene with known object degrees of freedom.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of creating dynamic digital representations of human interactions within real-world environments, a problem central to developing immersive augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and robotics applications. Existing approaches to 3D human motion estimation primarily rely on external camera systems. While effective, such setups are often complex, expensive, and not suitable for widespread adoption by non-expert users or for capturing interactions in large, unconstrained spaces.

A more accessible approach involves wearable, egocentric sensors such as body-mounted Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) and head-mounted cameras. Prior works, such as HPS, EgoLocate, and HSC4D, have successfully used such setups to estimate human movement and localize individuals within pre-scanned 3D environments. However, these methods typically assume static environments and do not track changes caused by human-object interactions (e.g., opening a door or moving furniture). This limitation results in discrepancies between the digital twin and the physical reality, leading to visually implausible reconstructions when interactions occur.

Research in human-object interaction often uses external cameras or focuses on static scenes or single dynamic objects without broader scene context. Some methods utilize egocentric video to predict contact, but these generally do not extend to inferring the full 3D position and motion of the interacted object. Embodied research using body-mounted sensors often concentrates on upper body capture or suffers from accumulating position drift without visual cues. Visual localization techniques aim to estimate camera pose in known environments but typically assume a sufficiently large static portion of the scene, an assumption violated when humans actively modify the environment.

This paper positions itself by bridging these gaps. It proposes a novel approach, iReplica, that combines egocentric visual localization of humans with contact-based reasoning derived from IMU data to dynamically track both human motion and consequent scene changes. The work extends previous egocentric human tracking methods by specifically addressing the modeling of dynamic objects and human-scene interactions, doing so exclusively with wearable sensors. This eliminates the need for external cameras or object trackers, thus representing a step towards more practical and scalable solutions for dynamic scene reconstruction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that can accurately estimate human-object interaction and track dynamic changes within a 3D scene, using only wearable sensors. Specifically, the system should operate without any external cameras or dedicated object trackers, relying solely on body-mounted IMUs and a head-mounted camera.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several key challenges and emerging application areas:

1. **Demand for Dynamic Digital Twins:** Modern applications, particularly in AR/VR (e.g., metaverses, collaborative development, virtual meeting rooms) and robotics (e.g., training machines), require digital twins that can accurately reflect changes made to the environment by human interaction. Current AR/VR experiences are often limited to static, small spaces, making common actions like opening doors or moving furniture difficult to integrate seamlessly.
2. **Accessibility and Ease of Use:** For widespread adoption of AR/VR and similar technologies, the sensor setup for capturing interactions must be inexpensive and user-friendly for non-expert users. Complex multi-camera systems are impractical for mass consumer use. Wearable sensors, such as head-mounted cameras and IMU suits, offer a more viable solution due to their portability and simpler setup.
3. **Limitations of Existing Egocentric Systems:** While previous egocentric tracking systems (like HPS) can localize a person, they cannot infer object movements caused by interaction. If a person opens a door, these systems will only track the person, leading to an inconsistent and visually implausible digital replica where the door remains closed. The small localization errors (e.g., 4-16 cm for HPS) that might be acceptable for human tracking become significant inconsistencies for human-object interaction.
4. **Challenges of Egocentric Interaction Capture:** Directly observing human-object interactions with a head-mounted camera is challenging because the object is often out of view, only partially visible, or occluded by the human's body (as illustrated in Figure 3 of the paper). Furthermore, the camera's motion makes it difficult to infer an object's motion relative to a static world.
5. **Hypothesis about Contact-Based Reasoning:** The authors hypothesize that even without direct visual observation of interactions, human-scene contacts and interactions can be realistically predicted from human pose sequences alone. Their key observations are:
 - Contact poses possess distinctive characteristics detectable without visual cues.
 - Precise contact timestamps can be used to regularize and improve human localization.
 - Objects (specifically static objects moved by the captured human) move in coherence with human contact.

The research aims to integrate these observations into a unified framework to overcome the limitations of prior work, creating a digital replica of human interaction and dynamic scene changes from a consumer-friendly sensor setup. The system assumes a pre-scanned 3D scene (Interactive Environment - IE) where interactive objects are marked with their initial positions and degrees of freedom (e.g., a door rotates around a hinge, a sofa slides on the floor).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The iReplica method is a human-centric approach that processes egocentric sensor data through a four-step pipeline to estimate human-object interaction and track scene changes.

**Input and Output:** The system takes as input data from a set of body-mounted IMUs (specifically, 17 XSens sensors) to capture human body motion and a video stream from a head-mounted RGB camera for visual localization. The output is a virtual replica of the interaction, encompassing coherent human and object motion within the 3D scene.

**Overview of the iReplica Pipeline:**

1. **Egocentric Human Visual Localization in the Scene (Section 4.1):**
 - **Initial Localization:** iReplica builds upon an improved version of the Human POSEitioning System (HPS). HPS combines IMU-based human pose estimation with head-mounted camera visual localization to provide initial estimates of human pose and global position within a pre-scanned 3D scene. IMU data offers accurate local kinematics but accumulates drift, while camera localization provides global anchors but can be noisy. HPS jointly optimizes these to produce smooth and accurate human trajectories.
 - **Problem with HPS for Interaction:** Even HPS's average error of 10 cm, while acceptable for general human localization, is insufficient for precise human-object interaction modeling where tight contact is crucial.
 - **Trajectory Optimization and Correction:** iReplica introduces a flexible trajectory optimization scheme. It models the human trajectory as a 2D curve on the ground plane (gravity axis constrained). An energy function ($E_{tr}$) measures angular deviation from the initial IMU-based trajectory. This trajectory is optimized ($F_{tr}$) to fit control points (derived from camera localization) while maintaining local angular consistency.
 - **Contact-Based Correction:** A key innovation is a second optimization pass. Once contact with an object is detected, a contact point on the object is identified (closest point to the interacting hand). This contact point serves as an additional, crucial control point, forcing the human trajectory to smoothly "snap" to the object, ensuring visual plausibility during interaction.

2. **Contact Detection (Section 4.2):**
 - **Motivation for Pose-Based Detection:** Contacts are frequently out of the head camera's view, and IMUs offer a more power-efficient and computationally lighter alternative to camera-based detection.
 - **Training Data (H-contact):** A new dataset, H-contact, was collected and annotated specifically for this task. It comprises over 3 hours of recordings (approximately 680k pose frames) from 8 subjects interacting with 12 different objects, with ground-truth contact timestamps.
 - **Transformer Network:** A sequence-to-sequence Transformer is trained to predict per-hand contact probabilities. It takes a sequence of 61 SMPL pose vectors (representing a temporal window) and outputs contact probabilities for each hand at the central frame.
 - **Post-processing:** To mitigate false negatives, any temporal gaps of 0.5 seconds or less between active contact predictions are filled.
 - **Flexibility:** While currently focused on hand contacts, the method can be extended to other body parts. It also supports multi-object interaction by considering the closest object within a predefined radius at the start of contact and can distinguish between interaction classes (e.g., sliding vs. hinged objects) using separate transformers.

3. **Interaction Modeling (Section 4.3):**
 - When the contact prediction network indicates an active contact, the system models the object's motion. The object is dynamically "attached" to the human's interacting body part.
 - **Degrees of Freedom (DOFs):** The interaction modeling incorporates the object's predefined DOFs from the Interactive Environment (IE). For example, a door rotates around a hinge, while a sofa slides on the floor.
 - **Object Motion Recovery:**
 - **Two-hand interaction with sliding objects:** The translation and rotation of the object are inferred by comparing the relative positions and orientations of the two hands before and during movement.
 - **One-hand interaction with sliding objects:** Only translation can be inferred from a single hand's movement, assuming no object rotation without further physical information.
 - **Interaction with hinged objects:** The object's rotation angle is calculated based on the change in the vector connecting the hinge point to the contacting hand.
 - These inferred transformations (translation and rotation) are then applied to the 3D points of the object, updating its pose in the scene.

4. **Contact End Detection:** The transformer network also predicts the release of contact. Once the contact is predicted to end, the object is "released" from the human, ceasing to be driven by their motion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents iReplica as the first method to capture human-scene interactions and dynamic scene changes using only wearable sensors.

**Dataset Contributions:**
The authors introduced two new datasets:
- **H-contact:** A dataset of over 2300 human-object interactions (3+ hours, 680k frames) with ground-truth contact timestamps, collected from 8 subjects and 12 objects, designed for training contact predictors.
- **EgoHOI:** A dataset for evaluating human-scene interactions in real scanned environments. It includes wearable sensor data (Xsens IMUs, head-mounted RGB camera), 3D scene scans (IE) with segmented objects and their DOFs, and ground-truth external RGBD data for validation. It covers interactions with 14 objects across multiple environments, totaling over 100k motion frames.

**Qualitative Results:**
- **Visual Plausibility (Figure 6, 7):** iReplica demonstrates the ability to generate visually plausible reconstructions of human-object interactions. It effectively models interactions where the object is not consistently visible in the head camera's view, or where grasping is complex.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **HPS:** Fails to track scene changes, resulting in unrealistic scenarios (e.g., doors remaining closed despite human interaction, sofas remaining stationary when dragged).
 - **HPS w/ GT:** Despite having ground-truth final object pose and contact times, simple linear interpolation of object motion proves visually implausible due to the non-linear nature of human-scene interaction. A user study indicated iReplica results were preferred in 84.2% of cases over HPS and HPS w/ GT.
 - **HPS w/ RGB Obj. Loc.:** Attempts object localization using head camera RGB frames but suffers from unreliability due to occlusions, head shaking, and objects frequently being out of view, leading to misaligned or failed object localization.
 - **iReplica w/ HOD and iReplica w/ VISOR:** These baselines, which substitute iReplica's pose-based contact prediction with state-of-the-art RGB-based methods, suffer from false negatives, resulting in sudden contact loss during interaction and missed contacts, leading to inconsistent object tracking.
- iReplica consistently ensures that the human's hands remain in plausible contact with the object throughout the interaction, contributing to higher visual plausibility.

**Quantitative Results:**

- **Object Localization Accuracy (Table 1):** iReplica significantly improves object localization accuracy. Across various object types (door, sofa, table, box), it achieves an average distance error of 6.88 cm and an average angular error of 7.13 degrees at the end of interactions, outperforming all baselines. For example, it reduces door distance error from 79.27 cm (HPS) to 9.97 cm.
- **Visual Plausibility (Table 2):** Measured by the mean distance between the object and the contacting hand during interaction, iReplica achieves the lowest values (2.93 cm average), confirming its ability to maintain consistent and plausible contact.
- **Ablation Study (iReplica w/o Contact corr.):** Removing the contact-based human trajectory correction from iReplica significantly degrades performance, increasing the average object distance error to 11.68 cm and angle error to 10.28 degrees. This confirms the critical role of trajectory correction.
- **Human Localization Improvement (Table 5 in Supplementary):** An evaluation using an external multi-view RGBD system for ground truth shows that iReplica also improves human body localization ($E_{body}$) from 9.771 cm (HPS) to 8.981 cm, demonstrating that incorporating interaction constraints benefits overall human tracking accuracy.
- **Contact Prediction Accuracy (Table 3):** The pose-based contact predictor developed for iReplica achieves high performance in isolation: Average Precision (AP) of 0.807, Precision@0.5 of 0.786, Recall@0.5 of 0.880, and Accuracy@0.5 of 0.905. These metrics significantly outperform RGB-based contact prediction baselines (HOD and VISOR), highlighting that 3D human pose is a highly informative indicator for interaction contacts, especially when interactions are not visually observable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of human-computer interaction and scene understanding.

**Key Significance:**
- **Novel Problem Formulation and Solution:** iReplica is the first method to successfully address the problem of capturing comprehensive human-scene interactions and dynamic scene changes exclusively from wearable sensors. This capability is crucial for creating realistic and interactive digital twins without the need for cumbersome external infrastructure.
- **Enhanced Realism for AR/VR:** By enabling egocentric capture of dynamic scenes, iReplica serves as a foundational step for future AR/VR applications. It can facilitate more immersive and seamless blending of real and digital worlds, allowing users to interact naturally with virtual environments that accurately reflect their real-world actions. This extends AR/VR experiences beyond static, confined spaces.
- **Advancement in Robotics and AI Training:** The ability to model human interactions with dynamic environments from an egocentric perspective can provide valuable data for training machines to understand and replicate human-like behaviors, particularly in tasks involving manipulation and scene modification.
- **Increased Accessibility:** The reliance solely on inexpensive and easy-to-use wearable sensors makes the technology more accessible to non-expert users and applicable in a wider range of large-scale environments, moving beyond specialized lab setups.
- **New Datasets and Metrics:** The release of the H-contact and EgoHOI datasets, along with evaluation protocols and code, fosters future research and provides benchmarks for this emerging area. These resources are valuable contributions to the research community.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that point to interesting avenues for future research:
- **Lack of Physics and Collision Modeling:** The current method does not incorporate physical simulations or collision detection, which could enhance realism and robustness, potentially through integration with game engines.
- **Reliance on Pre-scanned Environments (IE):** iReplica assumes a pre-scanned 3D scene with predefined object degrees of freedom. Future work could integrate online environment reconstruction (e.g., SLAM-based models) and on-the-fly object segmentation to remove this assumption, allowing for interaction in arbitrary, unknown environments.
- **Simple Interaction Model:** The current approach focuses on relatively simple interactions (sliding, hinged objects). Expanding to more complex object manipulations, such as articulated or non-rigid deformations, represents a significant challenge and an exciting research direction, potentially leveraging new human-object interaction datasets.

In conclusion, iReplica represents a substantial contribution by demonstrating the feasibility of robust human-scene interaction and dynamic scene modeling from consumer-grade wearable sensors. Its advancements address a critical need for realism in immersive digital experiences and provide a strong foundation for future developments in AR/VR and robotics.
