# Coding Agents with an Obstacle-Aware Harness for Safe Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> SafeHarness is a training-free harness that makes coding-agent robot controllers respect obstacle constraints. It splits each manipulation phase into a free-space route, planned as waypoints over segmented bounding boxes, verified outside the language model and replanned on failure, and a contact step whose approach side and wrist rotation are chosen to clear adjacent obstacles. On SafeLIBERO with GPT-6 it reaches 71.9% task success and 87.5% collision avoidance.

## Problem

- Coding-agent manipulation work, from Code as Policies to Harness VLA, scores only goal completion, so whether the robot collides with objects along the way was never measured.
- Stating the safety constraint in the prompt fails: agents recognize and restate the obstacle, yet take the shortest route and never replan, because the constraint fades over long contexts.
- Existing safe-manipulation methods act after the decision: barrier-function filters like AEGIS veto unsafe actions but cannot pick another route or grasp side, costing task success.

## Method

- **Obstacle-aware route planning**: ==SAM-3== grounds target and obstacle as bounding boxes, the agent proposes a ==waypoint polyline==, and an external verifier rejects routes where gripper or held object intrudes.
- **Plan-verify-execute cycle** per contact-delimited phase: replanning triggers at verification failure or mid-execution halts (too close, stuck), restarting from current pose, with a bound on rereading the route-plan image.
- **Obstacle-aware contact execution**: an ==adjacency test== runs at each grasp and release; an adjacent obstacle forces approach from the farthest clearing side, or, if none clears, a ==tangent-rotated gripper== lowered vertically.

## Results

- On SafeLIBERO (32 tasks, 10 seeds each), SafeHarness with GPT-6 reaches **71.9%** task success and **87.5%** collision avoidance, versus AEGIS at **67.5**/**68.9** and frozen π0.5 at **57.8**/**17.1**.
- Ablation versus the same GPT-6 agent with skills and frozen VLA but no harness: task success rises **31.0** to **71.9** and collision avoidance **59.0** to **87.5**, a **2.3x** and **1.5x** gain.
- Harnessed GPT-5.5 and GPT-6 land within two points (**86.0** vs **87.5** collision avoidance); without harness, the stronger planner lifts Level I avoidance **18.8** to **69.0** but Level II only **43.8** to **50.0**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prompted constraints secure agreement, not behavior: in long agentic episodes, invariants belong in stateless tool calls re-applied at each step, not sentences at the context's start.
> - Put safety into the decision that proposes motion, not a downstream filter; vetoing removes needed motion, while choosing among admissible options lets success and safety rise together.
> - Model scale fixes local, single-view decisions like grasp side but not non-local route safety; once a harness verifies routes, safety becomes a harness property, largely planner-independent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper examines the safety of coding agents for robotic manipulation. In the coding-agent paradigm, a language model does not directly output low-level robot actions. Instead, it writes and executes a program that combines perception tools, motion primitives, learned policies, and task-specific skills. This approach has developed from work such as *Code as Policies*, in which language models compose robot-control APIs into executable programs. Subsequent systems have added structured prompting, self-repair, skill libraries, test-time exploration, and frozen vision-language-action (VLA) models as callable primitives.

The paradigm differs from conventional VLA and world-action models. VLA systems such as RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, and the π-family generally map visual observations and language instructions to actions through a trained policy. Their behavior is determined primarily by the data and objectives used during training. Coding agents instead express the policy as inspectable and potentially editable code. This provides greater flexibility across tasks and robot embodiments, but it also creates a dependency on the tools, prompts, and control primitives surrounding the language model.

Prior manipulation studies have largely evaluated whether the robot reaches a task goal. Safety constraints, such as avoiding a nearby object, have often been omitted from the primary objective. In parallel, robotics has developed explicit motion planners, including RRT, CHOMP, and TrajOpt, as well as runtime safety mechanisms such as control barrier functions. These methods either plan collision-free trajectories or modify unsafe actions after a controller has proposed them. The paper argues that coding agents require a different treatment because safety should influence the program’s decisions before motion is issued, rather than being applied only as a downstream correction.

The work is evaluated on SafeLIBERO, a safety-oriented extension of the LIBERO manipulation benchmark. SafeLIBERO introduces an obstacle into otherwise standard pick-and-place tasks and separately measures task completion and collision avoidance. The paper’s central contribution is to connect coding-agent manipulation with explicit collision-scored evaluation and to propose a training-free safety harness around an existing coding-agent system.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether coding agents that perform well on manipulation tasks also behave safely when given an explicit obstacle-avoidance requirement. The authors focus on tasks such as placing one object on another without touching a mug, bottle, box, or other obstacle.

The motivation is that task success and safety are distinct properties. A robot may successfully grasp and place an object while colliding with another object on the way. In physical settings, such collisions can damage equipment, spill contents, damage property, or create risks to people. The paper therefore treats safe manipulation as requiring both a successful task predicate and continuous avoidance of the obstacle.

The authors’ preliminary evaluation identifies a gap between recognizing a safety constraint and acting on it. The coding agent often identifies the obstacle in its visual reasoning and may repeat the instruction not to touch it. Nevertheless, it frequently chooses a direct or shortest route that intersects the obstacle. The paper attributes this behavior neither primarily to perception nor to the wording of the prompt. Instead, it identifies a planning problem: the safety condition is not maintained as an operational priority throughout a long sequence of tool calls and robot actions.

The work further decomposes manipulation into two distinct phases. The first is a **route phase**, in which the robot moves through free space toward a grasp or placement location. The second is a **contact-rich phase**, in which the gripper opens, approaches the object, grasps or releases it, and potentially changes orientation. An obstacle may be avoided during the route but contacted during grasping or placement, or it may obstruct the route while being distant from the contact target. The authors argue that both phases need separate safety mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. System Configuration

SAFEHARNESS is built on the Harness VLA coding-agent framework. The agent uses a language model to write and execute a controller, a skill memory, and a frozen π^0.5 VLA model for contact-rich manipulation. The π^0.5 policy and skill memory are kept unchanged relative to the underlying system. The main modification is the addition of two obstacle-aware harnesses. The system is evaluated with GPT-5.5 and GPT-6 language-model backbones.

The approach is training-free with respect to the safety mechanism. No gradients are used to modify the VLA or language model. Instead, safety is introduced through additional perception, route-planning, verification, and contact-selection tools.

#### 3.2. Obstacle-Aware Route Planning

Route planning begins separately for each manipulation phase. This is important because the robot’s geometry changes after it grasps an object: a path that clears an obstacle with an empty gripper may not clear it when carrying a bowl or bottle.

The scene is first grounded using a segmentation model, identified as SAM-3 in the paper. The target object and obstacle are represented as bounding boxes. The language model is then asked to propose a route as a polyline consisting of waypoints. The route must terminate at the target and must not cross the obstacle’s bounding box.

The proposed route is checked by an external verifier before any robot motion is issued. If the route intersects the obstacle representation, it is rejected and the agent must propose another route. This creates a plan–verify–execute cycle. Verification is intended to prevent the language model from issuing an unsafe direct path based only on its reasoning trace.

Replanning can also occur during execution. If the robot approaches the obstacle more closely than expected, becomes stuck, or encounters a physical condition not represented by the bounding boxes, execution halts and a new route is planned from the robot’s current pose. The system also limits how often the same route-planning image is inserted into the language-model context. The authors argue that repeatedly appending identical images unnecessarily increases context length and can reduce the quality of later predictions.

#### 3.3. Obstacle-Aware Contact Execution

The second harness handles movements that occur below the pre-grasp or release pose and are not covered by route verification. It tests whether the obstacle is adjacent to the contact target.

If no obstacle is adjacent, the system permits the normal contact strategy. If an obstacle is nearby, it selects an approach direction that maximizes clearance, generally approaching from the side farthest from the obstacle. The same logic is applied to both grasping and placing.

If no direction offers adequate clearance, the system changes the gripper orientation so that its opening axis is tangent to the obstacle and lowers the payload vertically. This reduces lateral motion toward the obstacle. The contact strategy is recomputed at every grasp and placement event rather than fixed for the full task.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design

The evaluation uses 32 SafeLIBERO tasks: four tasks from each of the Spatial, Goal, Object, and Long suites. Each task contains one obstacle selected from categories including a moka pot, storage box, milk carton, wine bottle, mug, and book. The task language does not mention the obstacle, although the safety evaluation records whether it is displaced.

SafeLIBERO includes two obstacle configurations. In Level I, the obstacle is near the target and interferes with grasping or releasing. In Level II, the obstacle is away from the target but lies on the natural transport route. Ten seeds are used per task. The reported metrics are:

- **Task Success Rate (TSR):** the percentage of episodes in which the manipulation goal is achieved.
- **Collision Avoidance Rate (CAR):** the percentage of episodes in which the obstacle is never displaced by more than 1 mm.

The comparisons include OpenVLA-OFT, the frozen π^0.5 policy, AEGIS—a barrier-function safety layer applied to π^0.5—and SAFEHARNESS with GPT-5.5 or GPT-6. Ablation experiments compare language-model-only control, the coding agent with skills and the frozen VLA, and the full SAFEHARNESS system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SAFEHARNESS with GPT-6 achieves an average **71.9% TSR** and **87.5% CAR**. These are the highest average values reported in the paper’s comparison table. AEGIS achieves 67.5% TSR and 68.9% CAR, while π^0.5 achieves 57.8% TSR and 17.1% CAR. OpenVLA-OFT achieves 26.2% TSR and 5.7% CAR.

The full system therefore improves both task completion and safety relative to the principal prior method. The paper reports increases of 6.5 percentage points in task success and 27.0 percentage points in collision avoidance compared with AEGIS. Relative to the same coding agent with skills but without SAFEHARNESS, GPT-6 improves from 31.0% to 71.9% TSR and from 59.0% to 87.5% CAR. These correspond to approximately 2.3-fold and 1.5-fold improvements, respectively.

The GPT-5.5 version produces 70.0% TSR and 86.0% CAR, close to the GPT-6 results. This supports the authors’ claim that the safety gains arise mainly from the harness rather than from language-model scaling. In the ablation, GPT-6 with skills but without the harness reaches 31.0% TSR and 59.0% CAR, whereas the harnessed version reaches 71.9% TSR and 87.5% CAR.

The level-wise results reveal different sources of failure. Without the harness, the stronger language model improves substantially on Level I, where the key decision is which side of a target to approach. GPT-6 raises collision avoidance from 18.8% with GPT-5.5 to 69.0% in the skills-only comparison. However, it provides only limited improvement on Level II, where avoiding the obstacle requires maintaining a safe route over a longer motion sequence. This supports the paper’s distinction between local contact decisions and persistent route constraints.

SAFEHARNESS raises collision avoidance to approximately 85% or higher at both levels under both language models. However, task success remains lower on Level II than Level I. With GPT-6, TSR is 81.2% on Level I and 62.5% on Level II. The authors suggest that verified detours make the task longer and may cause episodes to exceed the fixed action-step budget. Thus, the remaining cost of safety appears mainly as task incompletion rather than collision.

Qualitative traces show that the unmodified agent acknowledges the obstacle early, then gradually stops referring to it as the conversation accumulates tool calls and images. The resulting controller often follows the shortest path through the obstacle. SAFEHARNESS instead reintroduces the constraint through stateless verification tools at the point where the route or contact action is selected.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper’s main significance is architectural. It demonstrates that safety can be incorporated into a coding agent through the harness that mediates perception and action, without retraining the language model or the underlying robot policy. The approach shifts safety from a sentence in the prompt to an executable condition checked during decision-making.

This design may be useful for other persistent constraints, including workspace boundaries, force limits, forbidden semantic regions, and object-specific handling requirements. The modular structure also allows the route planner and contact strategy to be expanded independently. Verified routes or contact strategies could potentially be cached as reusable skills.

However, the evidence is limited to a simulation-first benchmark with one obstacle per scene and an axis-aligned bounding-box representation. Multiple obstacles, irregular geometries, occlusions, and moving people would require more advanced scene models and route planners. The contact strategy is tailored to a parallel-jaw gripper and may not transfer directly to dexterous or multi-fingered hands. Safety also depends on segmentation accuracy and approximate scene geometry. Although verification reduces risk, the paper reports that roughly one in eight GPT-6 SAFEHARNESS episodes still displaces the obstacle. Finally, the benchmark measures obstacle displacement rather than all forms of physical risk, and the computational cost of repeated language-model planning may be substantial. These limitations leave real-robot validation and broader safety guarantees as important directions for subsequent research.
