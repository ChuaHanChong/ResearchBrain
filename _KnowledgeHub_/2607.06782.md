# G-PROBE: Cross-FOV Place Recognition and Certainty-Coupled Localization for 3D Point Clouds

> [!summary] Summary
> A learning-free global localization framework, G-PROBE, enables robust place recognition and metric pose estimation for 3D point clouds under diverse and asymmetric sensor fields of view and heterogeneous sensor types. It retains substantial reliability in cross-sensor and narrow-FOV matching where other methods struggle, enhancing registration accuracy by integrating observational certainty from its front-end.

## Problem

- Existing global LiDAR localization methods struggle with limited, asymmetric, or heading-dependent fields of view (FOVs) and implicitly assume panoramic sensor coverage.
- Achieving robust place recognition and localization across heterogeneous LiDAR sensor types (e.g., mechanical, solid-state, FMCW) without requiring supervised training remains a significant challenge.
- Prior approaches often fail to integrate per-region reliability information from place recognition descriptors into the geometric registration process, potentially leading to biased pose estimates.

## Method

- Introduces a front-end for ==cross-FOV ensemble place recognition== that partitions any sensor configuration into "==virtual sensors==" to handle varying fields of view and heading changes.
- Employs ==FOV-aware geometric scoring== with a two-factor verification mechanism (occupancy-weighted height cross-correlation and ==Bernoulli-KL divergence==) and a ==Softmax Gap Ratio Test== to refine heading and suppress ambiguity.
- Integrates a back-end, **Certainty-Guided GICP (CG-GICP)**, which extracts a per-bin certainty map from the front-end to filter unreliable points in the query scan, enabling robust pose refinement.

## Results

- Demonstrated substantial robustness to FOV asymmetry, retaining approximately **54%** Recall@1 for **60°** query FOV against a panoramic database, where other learning-free methods experienced a **>96%** performance collapse.
- Was the only method to consistently achieve non-trivial AUC scores across challenging cross-sensor pairings (e.g., **360°** Ouster vs. **70°** Livox) on the HeLiPR dataset, where other baselines yielded near-zero AUCs.
- Achieved up to **55.0%** end-to-end global localization success in wide↔narrow cross-sensor scenarios, providing a **10-13x** improvement over baselines and recovering accurate metric poses, while its Certainty-Guided GICP yielded **96.1%** registration success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learning-free approach can effectively handle complex sensor heterogeneity and partial or asymmetric FOVs by abstracting sensor configurations into virtual sectors and employing FOV-aware matching.
> - Coupling front-end descriptive certainty directly with back-end metric registration significantly improves robustness, especially by mitigating bias from unreliable correspondences at FOV boundaries or sparse regions.
> - Probabilistic evaluation of heading hypotheses combined with a Softmax Gap Ratio Test can effectively suppress ambiguity, particularly critical for narrow-FOV observations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Global localization, the process of estimating a robot's metric pose from a prior map without an initial pose estimate, is a foundational component for robust autonomous navigation and loop closure within Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) systems. Place recognition, a key enabler for global localization, is well-established for camera-based systems and 360-degree panoramic LiDAR sensors. These mature LiDAR solutions, however, implicitly assume dense, symmetric, and panoramic field-of-view (FOV) coverage.

A significant gap in current research pertains to localization using diverse and FOV-constrained point clouds, which are increasingly generated by modern sensors known for their long sensing range, compact size, and robust solid-state designs. Recent surveys highlight limited FOV, cross-sensor configuration, and unbalanced matching as open challenges in global LiDAR localization. Existing methods often address these problems in isolation. For instance, panoramic descriptors generally assume full 360-degree coverage. FOV-aware methods frequently rely on fixed heuristics or supervised fine-tuning, which may not generalize across sensor types or environmental conditions. Furthermore, degeneracy-aware registration and odometry approaches primarily operate in geometric space, often decoupling descriptor evaluation from metric pose estimation. A common limitation across many hand-crafted descriptors is the discarding of internal state after retrieval, and registration modules typically do not explicitly incorporate observational confidence derived from the descriptor stage. This lack of integration means that the per-region reliability computed by a descriptor is not directly linked to the alignment performed by registration, leaving a potential for bias from unreliable correspondences.

This research aims to address the limitations inherent in existing methods by developing a global localization framework that operates effectively under restricted or asymmetric FOVs and across heterogeneous sensor types, without requiring supervised training or external verification modules.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a global localization framework that can function effectively despite the mismatch between the panoramic assumptions of many existing place recognition methods and the constrained, heterogeneous FOVs characteristic of modern sensor configurations. The authors identify two primary challenges motivating this work:

**Problem 1: Sensor Heterogeneity and Modality Invariance.**
Modern autonomous systems increasingly deploy a diverse array of LiDAR sensors, including spinning mechanical, directional solid-state, and frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) LiDARs. Each of these sensor types exhibits distinct point densities, noise distributions, and FOV characteristics. Achieving modality-invariant matching across such heterogeneous sensor types remains an open challenge. While recent methods employing deep learning (e.g., HeLiOS, UniLGL) attempt to address this through overlap-based learning or vision foundation models, they typically require supervised training to adapt to new sensors. Without such adaptation, their performance can degrade on unseen scanning patterns or sensor modalities. The motivation here is to develop a learning-free approach that inherently accommodates diverse sensor properties without requiring re-training.

**Problem 2: Heading-Dependent Coverage.**
Many robotic platforms are equipped with sensors that provide only a partial azimuthal view, such as a single narrow-FOV solid-state LiDAR (e.g., 70-degree Livox sensor) or multiple sensors with combined blind spots. When a robot revisits a scene from a different heading, it observes the environment through an altered angular sector. This scenario poses a significant problem for traditional polar descriptors that achieve rotation invariance through full-ring column shifting, as these methods become unreliable under partial and heading-dependent coverage. Furthermore, a narrow FOV inherently limits the distinctive structural information available for reliable matching, exacerbating the geometric degeneracy often encountered in long, featureless paths, which can corrupt both recognition and registration processes. The motivation is to create a system that is robust to heading changes and partial FOVs, making it suitable for platforms with non-panoramic sensing capabilities.

The authors note that prior works often address these problems in isolation, or with methods that assume specific conditions (e.g., dense 360-degree coverage for panoramic descriptors, or reliance on fixed heuristics/fine-tuning for FOV-aware methods). A key limitation identified is the failure of existing approaches to link the per-region reliability computed by a descriptor to the alignment performed by registration. Hand-crafted descriptors typically discard their internal state after retrieval, and registration often proceeds without explicit knowledge of observational confidence.

G-PROBE's motivation is to integrate these aspects. Rather than learning modality invariance, the system aims to "probe per-bin structural reliability directly from sensor geometry." During the cross-FOV ensemble evaluation, the descriptor computes per-bin mutual occupancy agreement. This internal state, extracted as a bird's-eye-view (BEV) certainty map, is then fed to the registration module, establishing a "certainty-coupled architecture." This coupling allows the front-end (recognition) to inform the back-end (metric pose estimation) about observational reliability, thereby integrating descriptor evaluation with 6-DoF metric pose estimation without requiring an external verification module. This approach aims to incur no additional computational cost for the coupling, as the certainty map is a by-product of the front-end scoring process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

G-PROBE (Generalized PROBE) is a learning-free global localization framework designed to produce a metric SE(3) pose from a single query scan. It consists of a front-end for cross-FOV ensemble place recognition and a back-end for certainty-guided registration (CG-GICP).

#### 3.1. Front-End: Cross-FOV Ensemble Place Recognition

The front-end is designed to handle arbitrary sensor configurations and heading variations.

1. **Virtual Sensor Decomposition:**
 - Any sensor configuration with contiguous azimuthal coverage (single panoramic or gap-free multi-sensor array) is mathematically partitioned into $N$ virtual sensors. This abstraction decouples the pipeline from specific hardware.
 - $N$ is determined by $max(1, round(\Phi_{total}/90^\circ))$, where $\Phi_{total}$ is the total azimuthal coverage. For a 360° LiDAR, $N=4$ sectors. For a narrow-FOV LiDAR (e.g., 70°), $N=1$.
 - The virtual sensor array is decomposed into all $\binom{N}{2}$ pairwise subsets (or a single self-pair for $N=1$). Each virtual sensor pair $P_{ij}$ has a union FOV mask $F_{ij}$ and encodes an explicit relative-heading hypothesis.

2. **Density-Robust Retrieval (Stage 1):**
 - For each sensor pair $P_{ij}$, a D-dimensional L2-normalized retrieval key (derived from range-stratified structural statistics, including mean occupancy and mean structure height, leveraging PROBE's probabilistic ring-key formulation) is computed. These keys are robust to query-database translational offset.
 - **Cross-Pair Retrieval and Branch Generation:** All combinations of query-database pairs ($P_q_{ij}$, $P_{db}_{kl}$) form a branch. Each branch is associated with a heading hint ($\Delta\psi_{hint}$) and an expected FOV overlap ratio ($w_{FOV}$), normalized by the smaller FOV. Branches with $w_{FOV} < w_{min}$ are discarded.
 - **Symmetric FOV-overlap retrieval:** To enable meaningful comparison under FOV asymmetry, query and database ring keys are restricted to their shared physical observation region using FOV masks ($O_q(b)$, $O_{db}(b)$). This approach depends only on FOV masks, allowing generalization across physically distinct sensors.
 - **Vote Pooling and Ranking:** Top-$k_b$ results per branch are pooled. Database candidates are ranked lexicographically by FOV-weighted branch support ($v_w(m)$) and cumulative similarity ($\bar{s}(m)$). The top-K candidates proceed to geometric verification.
 - **Heading-Hypothesis Consistency Filtering:** Branches are grouped into heading hypotheses {H_ψ} (quantized at 90° intervals). Candidates are scored only over branches that nominated them, preventing incompatible heading evidence from mixing.

3. **FOV-Aware Geometric Scoring (Stage 2):**
 - For each sensor pair, a polar BEV grid ($N_r \times N_s$) is constructed with three channels:
 - **Occupancy $\mu(r,s)$:** Binary occupancy smoothed by an adaptive polar Gaussian blur (angular bandwidth $\sigma_\theta \propto 1/r$ and density-adaptive $\sqrt{\rho}$ scaling, plus radial blur), inherited from PROBE for translational robustness.
 - **Bernoulli uncertainty $\sigma(r,s)$:** Derived as $\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}$.
 - **Max-Height $z_{max}(r,s)$:** Represents structural signatures.
 - **FOV-Aware Cross-Correlation Alignment:** The heading is refined using FFT-based cross-correlation on the max-height channel, restricted to the branch's overlapping wedge $O$ and a hint-centered window $W$.
 - **Two-Factor BKL Scoring:** The verification score $S_b$ combines two factors:
 - **Occupancy-Weighted Height Cross-Correlation ($CC_z$):** A Pearson correlation weighted by joint occupancy probability and mean-centered heights, clamped to $$. It anchors correlation on mutually confidently observed structures and cancels shared ground levels.
 - **Bernoulli-KL Divergence (BKL):** Symmetric divergence $D_{sym}$ between shrinkage occupancies (which pull unreliable cells towards uninformative 0.5), aggregated over co-observed cells.
 - **Column-Wise Trimmed Aggregation:** To reject localized dynamic obstacles or occlusions, the $\eta$ highest-divergence azimuthal columns are discarded from the aggregation of $D_{sym}$.
 - The per-branch score is $S_b = w_{FOV} \times CC_z \times BKL$. The multiplicative form enforces a veto, requiring agreement in all channels.

4. **Probabilistic Softmax Gap Ratio Test (γ-SGRT):**
 - This step suppresses heading aliasing by evaluating the dominance of the best heading axis.
 - **Stage 1: Hypothesis Scoring:** Branch scores are averaged for each hypothesis $H_\psi$.
 - **Stage 2: Antipodal Axis Grouping:** Heading hypotheses are merged into structural axes A (ψ mod 180°) by taking the maximum score.
 - **Adaptive Softmax:** A score-scale-invariant Softmax calculates $P_{dom}$, representing the probability of the best axis being unambiguous. It uses an adaptive temperature.
 - **Final Score:** $S_{final} = (\max_b S_b) \times P_{dom}^{(1-\gamma)}$, where $\gamma = \min(\Phi_q, \Phi_{db})/360^\circ$ is an FOV coverage factor. This exponent allows SGRT to be inert at panoramic FOVs ($\gamma=1$) and increasingly suppress ambiguous matches for narrower FOVs ($\gamma < 1$). This mechanism penalizes heading ambiguity while preserving the quality of the best single-branch match.

#### 3.2. Back-End: Certainty-Guided Registration (CG-GICP)

CG-GICP is a two-pass coarse-to-fine Generalized-ICP, designed to mitigate bias from unreliable correspondences, especially at FOV boundaries or in sparse regions.

1. **BEV Certainty Map Extraction:**
 - A per-bin certainty weight $c(r,s)$ is extracted as a by-product of the BKL scoring from the winning front-end branch. This weight is a conservative conjunction of mutual certainty and mutual occupancy, zeroed outside the FOV overlap, or where either scan has high uncertainty or low occupancy. It isolates reliably co-observed bins.

2. **Certainty-Based Point Filtering:**
 - Only the query (source) point cloud is filtered. Points with $c(r_i,s_i) < \tau_c$ are discarded, typically removing 20-60% of the input. This asymmetric filtering retains target (database) points outside the query's filtered FOV as valid correspondence targets.

3. **Coarse-to-Fine Two-Pass Registration:**
 - **Stage 1: Coarse GICP on all points:** Generalized-ICP is applied to the unfiltered source and target clouds, initialized with G-PROBE's refined heading and median height offset. This step uses broad geometric constraints (e.g., ground plane, vertical structure) to achieve a stable coarse pose $T_{GICP}$.
 - **Stage 2: Certainty-filtered refinement:** A second Generalized-ICP is initialized at $T_{GICP}$. It operates with the source cloud restricted to its certainty-filtered points $S_c$, matched against the full target. This pass focuses refinement on mutually co-observed structures, primarily correcting residual translation, as the rotation is already well-constrained by the coarse pass.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation was conducted across five LiDAR datasets (KITTI, NCLT, HeLiPR, SNAIL, GrandTour) encompassing three LiDAR modalities (mechanical spinning, solid-state, FMCW) and addressing various challenges including seasonal drift, extreme weather, cross-sensor operation, and large-scale environments. Ten baseline methods, including learning-free (M2DP, LiDAR Iris, SC, SC++, SOLiD, RING++, PROBE) and deep learning (HeLiOS, BEVPlace++, UniLGL) approaches, were used for comparison.

1. **Standard LiDAR Place Recognition (360° Panoramic):**
 - In symmetric, same-sensor single-session settings, G-PROBE was competitive among learning-free methods (average AUC 0.743), slightly behind the learning-free leader SC++ (0.754) and PROBE (0.807). The supervised BEVPlace++ generally led this category.
 - In multi-session settings, G-PROBE demonstrated stronger performance, achieving the highest average AUC among learning-free methods (0.835 vs. PROBE's 0.823), driven by its performance on HeLiPR.

2. **Limited-FOV Robustness:**
 - **Symmetric Limited FOV (DB = Query = F°):** G-PROBE remained functional, leading in AUC for 120° and 90° FOVs, and being a close second at 60° among all methods (including supervised ones).
 - **Asymmetric FOV (DB 360° → Query F°):** This was a critical test. All other learning-free baselines experienced a severe performance collapse, with Recall@1 dropping by 96% or more when the query FOV was reduced from 360° to 60° against a panoramic database. Supervised baselines also degraded significantly. G-PROBE, in contrast, retained substantial reliability, maintaining approximately 54% Recall@1 even at 60° FOV, which was approximately 18 times higher than the strongest learning-free baseline. This indicates its robustness to FOV asymmetry.

3. **Cross-Sensor Generalization (HeLiPR Dataset):**
 - G-PROBE was the only method that consistently retained non-trivial AUC scores across all off-diagonal cross-sensor pairings (e.g., 360° Ouster vs. 120° Aeva or 70° Livox). Other learning-free and supervised baselines generally yielded near-zero AUCs (≤ 0.08) in these challenging cross-sensor scenarios, due to their inability to handle the unobserved field or the training-distribution shift.
 - G-PROBE maintained usable similarity (e.g., Ouster→Aeva AUC 0.488/0.508 for single/multi-session). On same-sensor diagonals, it was competitive.

4. **FOV-Controlled Cross-Sensor Study on a Legged Platform (GrandTour Dataset):**
 - This experiment isolated sensor heterogeneity from FOV asymmetry, as all three LiDARs shared a 360° FOV.
 - Here, zero-shot supervised baselines that struggled with HeLiPR's cross-sensor cells recovered performance (up to 49-52% R@1 on the strongest site). This suggests that FOV asymmetry, rather than heterogeneity alone, was a primary cause of their collapse.
 - G-PROBE led the learning-free methods in cross-sensor mean R@1 (40.3%) and was competitive overall (second by 0.9 percentage points to BEVPlace++).

5. **Cross-Sensor Geometric Registration:**
 - On multi-session cross-sensor loop-closure pairs from HeLiPR, CG-GICP (2-Stage) demonstrated the best average registration success (96.1%) and the tightest worst-case translation error (TE$_{95}$=1.4 m). This was an improvement over standard GICP (TE$_{95}$=1.5 m) and P2P-ICP (TE$_{95}$=2.5 m).
 - The certainty filter was identified as critical for tightening the worst-case error tail. The two-pass coarse-to-fine structure of CG-GICP effectively addressed the bias from one-sided FOV-edge correspondences.

6. **End-to-End Metric Global Localization:**
 - On same-sensor diagonals, G-PROBE achieved high success rates (e.g., 91.5% and 82.1% on KAIST and Riverside Ouster→Ouster pairings, respectively) and was competitive with SC++.
 - On off-diagonal (wide↔narrow cross-sensor) pairings, G-PROBE showed a substantial advantage. Baselines often collapsed with 0-7% success, while G-PROBE achieved up to 55.0% success (Ouster→Avia, KAIST) and 50.3% (Ouster→Aeva), representing a 10-13x improvement over the best baseline, while also recovering accurate metric poses. This indicates that G-PROBE effectively addressed the front-end retrieval challenge, which subsequently enabled robust back-end localization.

7. **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Column-Trimmed Aggregation:** The robustness step of discarding the highest-divergence azimuth columns provided a small, tuning-free gain, particularly on cross-sensor cells.
 - **SGRT:** The Softmax Gap Ratio Test effectively flagged ambiguous matches, with SGRT-flagged matches being significantly less likely to be correct (6.2% correct vs. 30.3% for confident matches), confirming its calibration for aliasing suppression.
 - **Virtual-Sector Count N:** An ablation showed $N=4$ to be the optimal choice for 360° sensors, balancing accuracy and computational cost across FOV scenarios.
 - **Retrieval Depth K:** A depth of K=20 was found to be a good balance, significantly improving recall for cropped queries while keeping computational cost manageable.

8. **Computational Analysis:**
 - The G-PROBE front-end operated in real-time on a single CPU thread (median ~26 ms per keyframe, ~38 Hz) on the tested hardware, comparable to SC++ (22 ms). The CG-GICP back-end, triggered only on successful front-end retrievals, amortized to a low per-keyframe cost.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents G-PROBE as a learning-free global localization framework that addresses key challenges in 3D point cloud processing, particularly related to diverse sensor configurations and limited or asymmetric fields of view.

1. **Robustness to FOV Limitations and Sensor Heterogeneity:** The framework demonstrates reliable performance in scenarios where conventional panoramic descriptors and even zero-shot supervised methods largely fail, such as when matching narrow-FOV queries against panoramic databases or across different sensor modalities. This is a notable step towards enabling robots equipped with diverse and compact LiDAR sensors to perform global localization in environments mapped by different or wider-FOV sensors.
2. **Learning-Free Paradigm:** By explicitly modeling geometric symmetries and probabilistic BEV occupancy, G-PROBE avoids the need for supervised training. This characteristic enhances its adaptability, allowing for deployment on new sensor types or in new environments without requiring extensive data collection and re-training, which is a common limitation of deep learning-based approaches. This reduces development overhead and increases deployment flexibility.
3. **Certainty-Coupled Architecture:** The integration of the front-end's descriptive power with the back-end's metric refinement via a by-product certainty map represents an architectural innovation. This "certainty-coupling" improves registration robustness by focusing on reliably co-observed structures, particularly benefiting the worst-case error tails in challenging cross-sensor and asymmetric FOV scenarios, without adding separate verification modules.
4. **Unified Framework through Virtual Sensor Decomposition:** The concept of virtual sensor decomposition provides a generalized framework that can seamlessly handle various sensor configurations, from single narrow-FOV LiDARs to multi-sensor rigs. This abstraction simplifies the system design and promotes broader applicability.
5. **Enhanced Reliability for Real-World Scenarios:** The demonstrated performance across diverse datasets, including multi-session operation, extreme weather conditions, and long-term dynamic changes, suggests G-PROBE's utility for robust global localization. This has implications for applications like long-term autonomous navigation, loop closure in SLAM systems, and kidnapped robot problem solving, particularly for platforms utilizing varied LiDAR setups.
6. **Establishing a Usable Operating Point:** For challenging wide↔narrow cross-sensor pairings, G-PROBE establishes an operating point with usable recall and accurate metric pose recovery where previous methods largely collapsed. While absolute performance in the hardest regimes remains modest, it represents a substantial improvement and opens avenues for further research in these difficult cross-modal matching tasks.

In summary, G-PROBE contributes to the field by providing a robust, learning-free, and architecturally integrated solution for global localization in complex and heterogeneous LiDAR sensing environments, which are becoming increasingly prevalent in autonomous systems.
