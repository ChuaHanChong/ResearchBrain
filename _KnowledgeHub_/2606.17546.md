# SEAGym: An Evaluation Environment for Self-Evolving LLM Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> SEAGym is an evaluation environment for self-evolving LLM agents that treats the agent harness (prompts, memory, tools, middleware) as the object of study. It converts Harbor-compatible benchmarks into train batches, frozen update-validation, held-out in-distribution and out-of-distribution transfer views, replay diagnostics, and cost records, while letting each method keep its native update rule. On Terminal-Bench 2.0 and HLE it shows harness-editing gains that validation curves alone hide.

## Problem

- Most agent benchmarks score a fixed agent on isolated episodes with state reset, removing the persistent harness state that self-evolving agents exist to exploit.
- Sequential and lifelong evaluations study task streams but leave single-task and epoch-level evolution, plus forgetting and regression diagnostics, largely uncovered.
- Reported self-evolution gains collapse into one final score or curve, hiding whether updates transfer off the update source, break old behavior, or inflate cost.

## Method

- ==MDP-style formulation== over agent snapshots: environment fixes task sampling, feedback visibility and schedules, while each method supplies its own policy and ==harness-update rule==.
- ==Schedule parameters== (state persistence, task reuse, batch size, updates per batch, assessment timing) express online, single-task, batch and epoch-based evolution in one protocol.
- Split ==rollout/update adapter== over a Harbor execution backend, plus ==frozen update-validation==, ==ID/OOD transfer==, replay views and metrics (update-validation gain, ID gain, OOD gain, forgetting rate) computed from saved snapshot records.

## Results

- Under DeepSeek-V4-Flash, harness-editing AHE alone improved all three views (validation **+17.1**, ID **+9.1**, OOD **+6.3** points); context-evolving ACE gained only **+2.9**/**+3.6**/**+2.5**, and group-rollout TF-GRPO matched **+17.1** validation but dropped OOD by **2.5** at the highest rollout cost.
- Train replay of **80** tasks is non-monotonic: the final agent solves **43** versus **34** initially (**13** fixed, **4** forgotten), yet an intermediate snapshot collapsed to **6/80** with **66** rollout errors from a broken middleware message contract before recovery.
- Batch size is non-monotonic under a roughly fixed **3-4M**-token update budget: batch **20** gained **+17.1** validation and **+9.1** ID, while batch **10** and **80** regressed by about **14-17** points; single-source HLE training peaked mid-run then collapsed to **0.0%** everywhere.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evaluate self-evolution as a process, not a final score: intermediate snapshots can be the best ones, and collapses can be execution-contract bugs rather than genuine forgetting.
> - Editable scope trades leverage against reliability: rewriting tools and middleware transfers furthest but lets one bad edit break many otherwise unrelated tasks at once.
> - LLM-driven updates have a fixed analysis budget, so more evidence per update or more frequent updates both hurt; validation gains and cross-backend transfer hold only where failure surfaces match.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents has progressed beyond static systems, with agents now capable of self-evolution by updating their "agent harness." This harness encompasses the structured execution layer around a base model, including prompts, memory, tools, middleware, runtime state, and the model–tool interaction loop. This work addresses a recognized gap in the evaluation of such evolving agents.

Existing evaluation methodologies are frequently designed for static agents, where each task is treated as an isolated episode and the agent's state is reset. This approach inherently overlooks the state persistence that self-evolving agents are designed to leverage. While some research has explored sequential or lifelong learning evaluations, these often lack the granularity to assess specific self-evolution settings, such as single-task or epoch-level adaptation, and provide limited insights into phenomena like forgetting or regression.

The concept of an "agent harness" is becoming increasingly systematized in the field, with research focusing on its role as a configurable runtime that can be adapted. This makes the harness a natural target for adaptation through environmental feedback, revising prompts, memories, tool interfaces, or workflows without necessarily retraining the base model. This distinction between model-parameter learning and harness-level learning is relevant for evaluation.

Within the broader landscape of agent benchmarks, a shift from text-only scoring to interactive environments with executable actions and task-specific verifiers is evident. However, the standard protocol for these benchmarks typically still evaluates a fixed agent on independent episodes. Recent efforts, such as SEA-Eval and LifelongAgentBench, have begun to address the need for self-evolution or lifelong learning benchmarks. SEAGym contributes to this evolving landscape by proposing a unified environment that converts existing static agent benchmarks into dynamic sources suitable for evaluating the *process* of self-evolution across various configurable settings, thereby offering a more comprehensive assessment of harness updates and their effects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce SEAGym, a comprehensive evaluation environment designed to measure the evolution of LLM-based agent harnesses. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current evaluation practices for self-evolving agents:

Firstly, traditional evaluations often simplify the agent evolution process by reducing it to isolated task scores or a single sequential performance curve. This approach obscures critical aspects of how an agent improves, such as whether an update yields genuinely reusable improvements, if it overfits to recently encountered tasks, if it incurs prohibitive costs, or if it negatively impacts previously acquired behaviors. A more granular and multi-faceted assessment is needed to understand the true impact of harness updates.

Secondly, many existing agent benchmarks are structured for evaluating static agents. They typically reset the agent's internal state before each task episode, which fundamentally conflicts with the operational paradigm of self-evolving agents that are designed to accumulate and utilize persistent experience and harness modifications across tasks. This mismatch prevents a realistic assessment of agents that learn and adapt over time.

Thirdly, while some sequential and lifelong learning evaluation frameworks exist, they do not consistently provide fine-grained analyses for various self-evolution scenarios, such as single-task adaptation or epoch-based learning. They also often lack explicit mechanisms to diagnose specific aspects of evolution like catastrophic forgetting, skill regression, or the transferability of learned improvements to unseen tasks or different operational domains.

To address these motivations, SEAGym aims to:
- Provide a standardized, extensible environment for evaluating agent harness updates across a range of operational contexts, including training, validation, testing, replay, and cost analysis.
- Enable the conversion of existing Harbor-compatible static benchmarks into dynamic sources that can generate training batches, frozen validation views, held-out in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) transfer views, replay diagnostics, and records of agent snapshots and metrics.
- Formulate self-evolution within an RL-style environment, allowing for configurable schedules (e.g., single-task adaptation, online transfer, epoch-based batch learning) that separate the assessment of update rules from arbitrary exposure patterns.
- Offer complementary signals that clarify the evolution process, such as the effectiveness of updates on held-out performance, the stability of intermediate snapshots, the impact of source diversity and model backend on harness reliability, and the associated resource costs.

By achieving these objectives, SEAGym seeks to provide a more detailed and interpretable framework for comparing and understanding the mechanisms of self-evolving LLM agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SEAGym frames a self-evolution run as an MDP-style evaluation process, where the state `s_t` at any given time `t` includes the current agent snapshot, the schedule position, and the available task context. An agent snapshot `A_t` comprises a fixed base model `M` and a mutable harness state `H_t`. The environment samples a task batch `B_t`, the agent performs tasks, observes trajectories `T_t` and feedback `F_t`, and then applies its update rule `H_{t+1} = U(H_t, B_t, T_t, F_t)` to transition to the next state. SEAGym defines the environment's aspects (task distribution, feedback, schedules, evaluation views) while the agent provides its policy and update rule.

**Evolution Schedule:** SEAGym employs configurable schedule parameters to represent various self-evolution settings rather than distinct benchmark formats. These parameters include:
- **State Persistence**: Whether the harness state resets or persists across tasks.
- **Task Reuse**: Whether task streams are one-pass or repeated across epochs.
- **Batch Size**: Number of task episodes before a train-batch update.
- **Number of Epochs**: Traversals over the selected train set.
- **Update Attempts per Batch**: Number of rollout-update attempts.
- **Assessment Timing**: Boundaries for frozen snapshot evaluations.
The default experimental setting uses persistent state, repeated train batches, train-batch updates, and epoch-end frozen update-validation.

**Data Splits and Evaluation Views:** The framework separates dataset splits (D_train, D_val, D_test) which control task visibility for updates, from evaluation views that assess different aspects of the self-evolution process:
- **Update Evidence (B_t ⊂ D_train)**: Trajectories and visible feedback for harness updates.
- **Process Assessment (V_update-val ⊂ D_val)**: Measures intermediate snapshots without using validation tasks as update evidence.
- **ID Transfer (V_ID ⊂ D_test)**: Assesses transfer to unseen tasks from the source distribution.
- **OOD Transfer (V_OOD ⊂ D_test)**: Assesses transfer to shifted or target-domain tasks.
- **Replay/Retention (V_replay)**: Measures preservation or recovery of earlier behavior.
This separation ensures that evaluation views are used for frozen assessment only, preventing direct adaptation to the assessment itself.

**Benchmark and Baseline Integration:**
- **Benchmark Integration**: SEAGym reuses the Harbor framework for task execution, which provides task definitions, environments, verifiers, and parallel execution. SEAGym then adds the self-evolution schedule, converting static benchmark tasks into a dynamic environment.
- **Baseline Integration**: SEAGym adopts a "rollout from update" separation. A rollout component executes tasks with the current harness and yields a trajectory batch. An update component then processes this batch to apply the method's native self-evolution rule. This modularity allows various prompt, memory, skill, or harness-editing methods to be integrated through thin wrappers.

**Metrics:** SEAGym computes a suite of metrics from saved records:
- `Perf(A, D)` and `SR(A, D)`: Overall verified task performance and success rate.
- `UVG_prev`, `UVG_base`: Local and cumulative update-validation gains.
- `IDG`, `OODG`: In-domain and out-of-domain transfer gains for the final agent.
- `FR`: Forgetting rate on replay tasks.
- Additional metrics include token usage, tool calls, wall-clock time, and cost reduction. Metrics are macro-averaged by domain by default.

**Saved Records and Process Diagnostics:** The environment saves trajectory references, public feedback, update summaries, harness diffs, snapshot records, and metric records at each evaluation point. These artifacts support offline process diagnostics, enabling researchers to investigate the root causes of improvements or regressions.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Benchmarks**: Terminal-Bench 2.0 (command-line/software engineering) and HLE (text-only Math/Physics for source, CS/AI/Engineering for OOD).
- **Schedule**: Epoch-based (5 epochs, batch size 20), persistent state, train-batch updates, epoch-end update-validation.
- **Task Splits**: 80 train, 35 validation, 55 in-domain test, 80 OOD test tasks.
- **Baselines**: ACE (Agentic Context Engineering), TF-GRPO (Training-Free Group Relative Policy Optimization), and AHE (Agentic Harness Engineering).
- **Model Backends**: DeepSeek-V4-Flash for the main comparison; GLM-5.1 and GPT-5.4 for ablations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using SEAGym on Terminal-Bench 2.0 and HLE revealed several findings regarding the dynamics of self-evolving LLM agents.

**Baseline Comparisons:**
- **Agentic Harness Engineering (AHE)** demonstrated improvements across validation, in-distribution (ID) test, and out-of-distribution (OOD) test sets. Its effectiveness was attributed to its ability to modify the agent harness itself, including prompts, tool-use constraints, and middleware, which allowed for broader changes in agent behavior. This broader scope, however, also introduced a larger reliability burden.
- **Agentic Context Engineering (ACE)** showed modest gains in ID and OOD performance. Its mechanism, which focuses on accumulating reusable task-handling knowledge in a skillbook, provided benefits for certain types of tasks but had less influence over failures stemming from interaction policies or environment handling, as it does not directly alter the execution path.
- **Training-Free Group Relative Policy Optimization (TF-GRPO)** achieved a substantial validation gain and a small ID gain. However, it exhibited an OOD performance drop and the highest rollout cost, suggesting that its adaptation, based on grouped rollout evidence, did not reliably transfer to shifted target tasks. The results collectively indicate that the nature of an agent's update mechanism strongly influences its generalization capabilities and stability.

**Training Fix and Forgetting Diagnostics:**
- Analysis of AHE's performance on replay of training tasks showed a non-monotonic trajectory. While the final agent improved over the initial agent (43/80 tasks vs. 34/80), there was a significant performance drop with numerous rollout errors after Epoch 4 before eventual recovery.
- The final agent fixed 13 tasks that were initially failed while forgetting 4 tasks that were initially solved, resulting in a net gain of 9 tasks.
- The intermediate performance collapse was identified as a widespread runtime message-contract violation within the middleware, rather than a loss of task-specific knowledge. This specific failure was later repaired, leading to recovery. This finding underscores the importance of snapshot-level diagnostics to differentiate between genuine task forgetting and execution-path instability.

**Effect of Batch Size:**
- An ablation study with AHE demonstrated that batch size had a non-monotonic effect on performance. A batch size of 20 yielded significant positive validation and ID gains, whereas batch sizes of 10 and 80 led to regressions.
- The update token cost per update was relatively consistent across batch sizes (3-4M tokens), implying a bounded analysis capacity for the agent's harness-editing process.
- This suggests that too small a batch size (10) led to high-variance updates and accumulation of runtime regressions, while too large a batch size (80) diluted per-task attention and increased the risk of broad, brittle middleware changes. Batch 20 provided a balance of evidence diversity, analysis depth, and opportunities for correcting earlier harness edits.

**Source Diversity:**
- Comparing AHE trained on a mixed source (Terminal-Bench + HLE) versus HLE-only tasks revealed that the HLE-only run achieved a useful intermediate snapshot but its final snapshot collapsed to 0% success rate across all views.
- The mixed-source run, despite also experiencing an intermediate performance dip, recovered and sustained positive gains.
- This indicates that a narrow source of training evidence can lead the harness to optimize for benchmark-specific local optima, potentially resulting in fragility, particularly in core verification subsystems. Diverse sources provide varied failure evidence (e.g., tool errors, environment issues from Terminal-Bench; reasoning errors from HLE), which can aid in recovery from harmful updates and foster broader generalization.

**Cross-Model Transfer:**
- Evaluation of AHE harnesses evolved with different LLM backends (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GLM-5.1, GPT-5.4) showed that same-backend ID gains were consistently positive. This suggests that AHE successfully adapts the harness to the specific interaction patterns and failure surfaces of the LLM it was trained with.
- Cross-backend transfer was less stable and often asymmetric. For example, a DeepSeek-evolved harness could improve GLM ID but hurt GPT-5.4 ID.
- Furthermore, ID gains did not reliably translate to OOD gains, and most cross-backend OOD results were neutral or negative. Analysis of update artifacts indicated that each backend's trajectories exposed different failure patterns (e.g., DeepSeek: verification, tool recovery; GLM: workflow entry, output production; GPT-5.4: artifact constraints, validation sufficiency), leading AHE to modify different harness subsystems.
- This implies that harness gains transfer only when the evaluation trajectories expose similar failure patterns to those encountered during training. When failure modes shift across backends or task distributions, the learned harness changes may not remain effective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SEAGym introduces an evaluation framework that significantly advances the assessment of self-evolving LLM agents. Its primary significance lies in shifting the focus from merely reporting final task scores to analyzing the intricate *process* of agent evolution. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of how agents adapt, learn, and generalize, addressing key questions about the reusability of improvements, the occurrence of overfitting or regression, and the costs associated with updates.

The framework's capability to convert existing static benchmarks into dynamic self-evolution environments has the potential to democratize research in this area. It allows researchers to leverage established datasets and tasks while simultaneously studying the temporal and adaptive aspects of agent behavior without requiring extensive re-engineering of evaluation suites. This can accelerate the development and rigorous testing of new self-evolution mechanisms.

The detailed evaluation views—including update-validation, in-distribution and out-of-distribution transfer, and replay diagnostics—offer a powerful toolkit for researchers. These views enable a fine-grained analysis of agent performance trajectory, helping to differentiate between true generalization and mere adaptation, identify instances of catastrophic forgetting or execution-path instability, and understand the impact of various training parameters. This diagnostic capability is crucial for debugging and iteratively improving agent designs.

The empirical findings from the study have several implications for the field:
- **Agent Design Insights**: The distinct performance patterns of AHE, ACE, and TF-GRPO suggest that the *mechanism* of harness update (e.g., broad structural changes vs. skill accumulation vs. experience memory) fundamentally dictates an agent's learning dynamics, generalization, and stability. This can guide future agent designs towards more robust and generalizable self-evolution strategies.
- **Training Protocol Optimization**: The non-monotonic effect of batch size and the impact of source diversity on harness reliability provide practical guidance for optimizing training protocols. It highlights that simply increasing data or update frequency is not always beneficial, and a balanced approach tailored to the agent's update mechanism is often required.
- **Transferability Challenges**: The cross-model transfer results underscore the challenges in achieving stable generalization across different LLM backends and shifted task distributions. This emphasizes the need for evaluating agents not just on their native training setup but also across diverse deployment contexts to ensure reliability and broad applicability. It also suggests that harness changes are often specific to the failure surfaces exposed by the training environment and model.

In sum, SEAGym's contribution lies in providing a robust, standardized, and diagnostically rich environment for studying self-evolving LLM agents. It has the potential to foster a deeper, more mechanistic understanding of agent intelligence and adaptation, ultimately guiding the development of more capable, reliable, and generalizable autonomous systems. Future work, as noted by the authors, can extend this protocol to other agentic domains and explore more efficient evaluation strategies, further broadening its impact.
