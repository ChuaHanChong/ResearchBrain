# Learning When to Think: Shaping Adaptive Reasoning in R1-Style Models via Multi-Stage RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Institute of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Pengcheng Laboratory, and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences developed AutoThink, a multi-stage reinforcement learning framework that teaches R1-style Large Language Models to adaptively determine when to employ explicit reasoning. This approach reduced token usage by up to 52% while maintaining or improving accuracy on mathematical and general benchmarks.

## Problem

- Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) generate verbose, step-by-step reasoning for all problems, leading to significant computational overhead, increased latency, and excessive token usage, even for simple tasks.
- Prior methods for efficient reasoning often rely on manual mode selection, uniform pruning, or hard-coded prompt designs, lacking the ability for LLMs to intrinsically adapt to problem complexity.
- Current RL-based pruning techniques primarily aim to shorten outputs by penalizing length, rather than enabling adaptive, difficulty-aware reasoning.

## Method

- **AutoThink**, a ==multi-stage Reinforcement Learning== (RL) framework, was developed using the GRPO algorithm.
- A foundational "ellipsis prompt" (\n...\n) was identified to stochastically trigger latent "thinking" or "no-thinking" modes in R1-style models.
- Reward shaping across three stages: ensuring mode stability, reinforcing accuracy in both modes, and pruning unnecessary reasoning with length-aware rewards.

## Results

- AutoThink-trained models on Distill-R1-**1.5B** achieved a **52%** reduction in token usage with a **6.4%** relative accuracy improvement over standard prompt baselines.
- The framework successfully fostered adaptive and difficulty-aware reasoning, with models engaging in explicit thinking more frequently for harder problems.
- Adaptive reasoning capabilities generalized to diverse non-mathematical benchmarks (e.g., GPQA, MMLU), often surpassing baseline accuracy with reduced response length.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A minimal ellipsis prompt (\n...\n) can unlock latent "thinking" or "no-thinking" behaviors in R1-style LLMs, offering a new control signal.
> - A multi-stage reinforcement learning approach with carefully designed reward functions is essential to stabilize dual reasoning modes and progressively optimize for both accuracy and efficiency.
> - LLMs can be taught to intrinsically adapt their reasoning depth based on problem complexity, enabling a form of metacognitive ability beyond manual or uniform strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to the emergence of "Large Reasoning Models" (LRMs) or "R1-style models," exemplified by DeepSeek-R1, which excel at complex reasoning tasks by generating explicit, step-by-step thought processes. This Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting or inherent reasoning capability is widely recognized for enhancing solution quality through self-reflection and validation. However, a significant drawback, termed the "overthinking problem," has become apparent: these detailed reasoning sequences introduce substantial computational overhead, increased latency, and unnecessary token generation, particularly for problems that are relatively simple.

The paper "Learning When to Think" directly addresses this critical efficiency bottleneck, positioning itself at the intersection of advanced LLM reasoning and computational optimization. Prior efforts to mitigate overthinking fall into several categories, each with limitations that AutoThink aims to overcome:

- **Increasing Thinking Token Allocation:** A common paradigm (e.g.,) enhances accuracy by allocating more "thinking" tokens. While effective for quality, this exacerbates the overthinking problem by consuming more resources.
- **Manual Hybrid Reasoning (Industry Approaches):** Commercial models like Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Qwen3 offer controllable reasoning frameworks, allowing users to switch between standard and extended reasoning modes. However, these rely on *manual* intervention, shifting the burden of mode selection to the user rather than endowing the model with adaptive intelligence.
- **Prompt-Guided and Pruning-Based Methods (Academic Approaches):** Academic research has explored prompt-guided efficient reasoning (e.g.,) and training pruning-based models (e.g.,) to achieve concise reasoning. While promising, these often involve uniform pruning or hard-coded prompt designs, which may degrade performance on harder problem instances where explicit reasoning is genuinely needed. RL-based pruning methods like Concise-RL, ShorterBetter, and ThinkPrune also primarily focus on shortening outputs by penalizing length, without explicitly accounting for *adaptive*, difficulty-aware reasoning.
- **DeepSeek-R1 and its Distilled Models:** The paper builds upon "R1-style models" which inherently follow a `<think>` and `<answer>` format. DeepSeek-R1 is a notable example, incentivizing explicit reasoning via RL with verifiable rewards. AutoThink specifically targets the optimization of such models.

AutoThink distinguishes itself by seeking to enable LLMs to *learn* to adaptively determine when to think deeply and when to provide succinct responses, based on the *problem complexity itself*. This moves beyond manual control or uniform compression, offering a more autonomous and intelligent approach to resource allocation in LLMs. By identifying a latent controllability through a minimal "ellipsis prompt" and then shaping this behavior with multi-stage reinforcement learning, the work proposes a novel way to imbue LLMs with a crucial form of metacognitive ability – the ability to reason about its own reasoning process. This contribution is significant for advancing the practical deployment of LLMs, where efficiency and low latency are paramount, without sacrificing the complex reasoning capabilities essential for challenging tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to equip Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) with *adaptive thinking capabilities*, enabling them to dynamically decide whether to engage in explicit, step-by-step reasoning based on the inherent complexity of a given problem. This overarching goal is driven by a clear motivation to address the prevalent "overthinking problem" in current R1-style LLMs.

Specifically, the key objectives include:

- **Dynamic Reasoning Allocation:** To enable LLMs to adaptively determine when to perform explicit, detailed reasoning ("think slow") for complex problems and when to generate succinct, direct responses ("think fast") for simpler ones.
- **Mitigate Computational Overhead and Latency:** To reduce the substantial computational cost and increased latency associated with current LRMs that tend to generate verbose reasoning sequences for all problems, regardless of difficulty.
- **Achieve Optimal Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-offs:** To develop a framework that can significantly reduce token usage and computational resources without compromising, and ideally improving, the model's reasoning accuracy.
- **Seamless Integration:** To propose a method that can be easily integrated into existing R1-style models, including both distilled and further fine-tuned variants, ensuring broad applicability.
- **Learn Intrinsic Adaptive Behavior:** Unlike prior approaches that rely on manual prompts or uniform pruning, the work aims for the LLM to *learn* this adaptive behavior intrinsically through reinforcement learning, making it a more robust and intelligent solution.

The motivation for AutoThink stems directly from the inefficiencies observed in powerful reasoning models. While detailed reasoning is crucial for accuracy in hard problems, applying this verbose approach universally leads to wasted computational resources and slower inference times for easy problems. The lack of a robust, learned mechanism for "knowing when to think" has been a significant gap in the development of efficient LLMs. By providing such a mechanism, AutoThink aims to make LRMs more practical, scalable, and responsive for real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AutoThink introduces a novel multi-stage Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to teach R1-style LLMs adaptive reasoning behaviors. The methodology is built upon a surprising empirical observation and structured through progressive reward shaping across three distinct training stages.

#### 3.1. Core Insight: The Ellipsis Prompt

The foundational discovery of AutoThink is that inserting a simple ellipsis ("...") into the prompt, specifically in the format `<think>\n...\n`, can stochastically trigger either a "thinking" (detailed reasoning) or "no-thinking" (succinct response) mode in R1-style models, even without additional training. This minimal prompt modification acts as an open-ended signal, revealing a latent controllability in the model's reasoning behavior. While this ellipsis prompt shows a modest drop in accuracy for a substantial reduction in token usage, it doesn't intrinsically enable difficulty-aware reasoning; the "no-thinking rate" remains flat across various problem difficulties. This limitation highlights the need for a learning mechanism.

#### 3.2. Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework

AutoThink employs the GRPO (Grouped Proximal Policy Optimization) algorithm with a token-level policy gradient loss to progressively optimize the reasoning policies. The training is divided into three stages, each with a specific objective and tailored reward design:

- **Stage 1: Preventing Mode Collapse by Batch Reward Balance**
 - **Problem:** A naive reward function (e.g., higher reward for correct answers without thinking, penalties for incorrect no-thinking) can lead to instability and "mode collapse" where the model exclusively adopts either a thinking or no-thinking policy.
 - **Approach:** To ensure the coexistence and exploration of both modes, AutoThink introduces a batch-level reward balancing mechanism. It dynamically adjusts rewards based on the proportion of thinking trajectories (`z`) within a training batch, relative to a target balance ratio (`γ`) and a penalty slope (`λ`). Soft penalty factors (`δ_think`, `δ_nothink`) modulate the naive reward (`r_naive`), gently pushing the model to restore balance by favoring the less frequent behavior. This resembles a hinge-like transformation, preventing the policy from degenerating.

- **Stage 2: Reinforcing Reliable Behavior within Dual Modes**
 - **Objective:** Once dual modes are stabilized, this stage focuses on enhancing the quality and accuracy of reasoning within both the thinking and no-thinking modes.
 - **Approach:** The batch-level balancing from Stage 1 is removed, allowing the reasoning policy to evolve freely. The reward is set directly to the naive definition (`r_adj = r_naive`). A larger context budget is allocated during training to allow for longer, more elaborate responses when necessary. The stability fostered in Stage 1 ensures that the proportion of thinking remains balanced, fluctuating naturally rather than collapsing.

- **Stage 3: Pruning Unnecessary Reasoning Paths via Length-Aware Reward**
 - **Objective:** To improve reasoning efficiency by pruning redundant steps, thereby reducing token usage without significantly compromising accuracy.
 - **Approach:** Inspired by GRPO-LEAD, this stage introduces a length-aware reward modulation. For correct responses, the reward decays with the response length (controlled by hyperparameter `α`), encouraging brevity. Conversely, for incorrect responses, the reward grows with length (controlled by `β`), encouraging more thorough "failure analysis" or exploration. The length (`y_i`) is standardized within its query group. This mechanism enables the model to adaptively regulate its reasoning depth, promoting succinctness when successful and elaboration when facing difficulty.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** Training was conducted on 40K mathematical problems from the DeepScaleR dataset. Evaluation spanned five mainstream mathematical benchmarks: MATH, Minerva, Olympiad, AIME24, and AMC23. Additionally, generalizability was tested on non-mathematical benchmarks (GPQA, MMLU, Live-Code-Bench).
- **Model Backbones:** AutoThink was applied to various R1-style models, including DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B/7B (Distill-R1) and DeepScaleR-Preview-1.5B (DeepScaleR).
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against prompt-only strategies (standard, no-thinking, ellipsis) and RL-trained baselines aimed at concise reasoning (Concise-RL, ShorterBetter, ThinkPrune), as well as other open-source R1-style models.
- **Metrics:** Evaluation used average pass@1 accuracy, total token usage, and a novel Efficiency-F1 score (E-F1) to jointly assess accuracy gain and token reduction relative to standard and no-thinking baselines.

This methodical, stage-wise approach allows AutoThink to progressively learn and refine adaptive reasoning behaviors, building stability and accuracy before optimizing for efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that AutoThink effectively enables R1-style LLMs to learn adaptive reasoning behaviors, achieving superior accuracy-efficiency trade-offs compared to existing methods.

- **Ellipsis Prompt's Latent Control:** The initial insight proved critical: a minimal ellipsis prompt (<think>...</think>) alone can stochastically switch R1-style models between thinking and no-thinking modes. However, this base behavior is not difficulty-aware, indicating the necessity of further training.
- **Multi-Stage RL's Progressive Refinement:**
 - **Stage 1's Stability:** The batch reward balancing in Stage 1 was crucial for preventing mode collapse. Without it, models either always thought or always skipped, failing to develop dual-mode behavior. This stage successfully stabilized the coexistence of thinking and no-thinking patterns.
 - **Stage 2's Accuracy Boost:** Following stabilization, Stage 2, which focuses purely on reinforcing correct behavior, yielded significant accuracy improvements across all model backbones (e.g., +6.6% on Distill-R1-1.5B compared to its Stage 1 output). This confirmed the model's ability to effectively control when to reason for better task performance.
 - **Stage 3's Efficiency Gain:** The length-aware reward in Stage 3 successfully pruned unnecessary reasoning. On Distill-R1-1.5B, AutoThink-Stage3 achieved 51.7% accuracy while reducing token usage by 52% (from 10,633 to 5,108 tokens) compared to the standard prompt baseline, alongside a 6.4% relative accuracy improvement. For models like DeepScaleR (already highly optimized), Stage 2 improved accuracy by 0.6% with a 10% token reduction; Stage 3 further reduced tokens but sometimes with a slight accuracy drop, suggesting optimal pruning levels depend on the base model's pre-optimization.
- **Adaptive and Difficulty-Aware Reasoning:** AutoThink-trained models exhibit a clear positive correlation between thinking rate and task difficulty. As problems become harder, the model tends to invoke explicit reasoning more frequently and produce longer responses (Figure 2b, Figure 7). This confirms the acquisition of genuine adaptive reasoning, allocating effort based on perceived complexity.
- **Lexical and Behavioral Profiling:** Analysis of linguistic patterns showed that AutoThink, in "no-thinking mode," significantly reduces "soliloquize & thinking" keywords, indicating a shift towards direct computation. In "think mode," it allocates a larger proportion of steps to core reasoning phases like "computing or simplifying expressions" and "applying known theorems," suggesting a more targeted and efficient reasoning trajectory.
- **Readability and Accuracy across Modes:** "No-thinking" responses were found to be shorter and *more accurate*, indicating effective handling of simpler problems. "Thinking" responses, though longer, had slightly lower overall accuracy, reflecting their allocation to harder cases where the model might still struggle or explore extensively. Both modes maintained acceptable readability (perplexity scores).
- **Generality Beyond Math:** AutoThink's adaptive reasoning capabilities generalized to non-mathematical benchmarks (GPQA, MMLU, Live-Code-Bench). Stage 2, in particular, often surpassed baseline accuracy while significantly reducing response length, demonstrating transferability across diverse domains.
- **Training Cost:** AutoThink's training cost was comparable to other RL-based concise reasoning methods (e.g., ThinkPrune, ShorterBetter) but significantly lower than models primarily focused on maximizing performance through extensive RL (e.g., DeepScaleR), highlighting its efficiency in training adaptive behaviors.

In summary, AutoThink successfully addresses the overthinking problem by fostering a learned, adaptive reasoning policy that balances accuracy and efficiency, marking a significant step towards more intelligent and resource-aware LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AutoThink represents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Models, particularly for reasoning-focused architectures. Its potential impact spans several crucial dimensions:

- **Mitigation of the Overthinking Problem:** AutoThink provides a robust and learnable solution to the "overthinking problem," a critical bottleneck in the practical deployment of highly capable LLMs. By dynamically adjusting reasoning depth based on problem complexity, it dramatically reduces unnecessary computational costs, token generation, and inference latency. This is paramount for scaling LLM applications in real-world scenarios, especially where speed and resource efficiency are key performance indicators.
- **Enhanced Computational Efficiency and Sustainability:** The demonstrated ability to achieve comparable or even improved accuracy with significantly reduced token usage (e.g., 52% reduction on Distill-R1-1.5B) makes LLM inference more energy-efficient and cost-effective. This contributes to the sustainability of AI development and deployment, which often incurs substantial environmental and financial burdens.
- **Advancing Adaptive Intelligence in LLMs:** By enabling LLMs to *learn* when to engage in deep reasoning, AutoThink imbues them with a form of metacognitive ability—the capacity to reason about their own cognitive processes. This moves beyond rigid, predefined reasoning pipelines or manual control, bringing LLMs closer to exhibiting more human-like, adaptive intelligence. This intrinsic adaptability makes the models more autonomous and versatile.
- **Broad Applicability and Scalability:** The framework is designed for seamless integration into any R1-style model, including distilled and fine-tuned variants. The lightweight ellipsis prompt and the principled multi-stage RL framework offer a scalable paradigm for enhancing existing and future reasoning models. Its successful generalization beyond mathematical reasoning to diverse domains like scientific inquiry, language understanding, and code generation further underscores its broad applicability.
- **New Directions for LLM Optimization:** AutoThink opens several promising avenues for future research.
 - **Controlled Reasoning Budgets:** Integrating explicit token-budget control mechanisms, as seen in systems like Qwen3, could provide even finer-grained control over resource allocation.
 - **Improved Reward Shaping:** Addressing "reward hacking" (where models reason outside the designated `<think>` tags) and exploring a unified, holistic reward formulation could streamline the multi-stage training process into a single, more elegant solution.
 - **Data Efficiency:** Incorporating curriculum learning or difficulty-based filtering of training data could further enhance learning quality and robustness.
- **Impact on AI System Design:** The findings suggest a paradigm shift in how reasoning models are optimized. Instead of solely focusing on maximizing reasoning depth or uniformly pruning, future AI systems could incorporate adaptive reasoning modules as a standard component, leading to more intelligent, responsive, and resource-aware agents. This could foster the development of "smart" assistants that instinctively know when a quick answer suffices and when deep contemplation is required.

In conclusion, AutoThink represents a significant stride towards creating more intelligent, efficient, and adaptable Large Language Models. By teaching models to dynamically manage their cognitive effort, this work enhances their practical utility, scalability, and ability to navigate complex problem spaces with human-like discernment.
