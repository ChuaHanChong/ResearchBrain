# Meta-learning how to Share Credit among Macro-Actions

> [!summary] Summary
> A regularization method called Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP) allows reinforcement learning agents to meta-learn a similarity matrix among primitive and macro-actions, improving credit assignment and exploration. This approach yields higher rewards and faster convergence in diverse environments, including Atari games, StreetFighter II, and MiniGrid, especially when dealing with large or noisy sets of macro-actions.

## Problem

- Macro-actions, intended to simplify Reinforcement Learning, often degrade performance due to an expanded action space and increased branching factor, hindering exploration.
- Standard RL agents treat all actions, including macro-actions, as independent and atomic, failing to leverage potential relationships between them.
- Inefficient credit assignment and exploration persist in complex environments, particularly when naive integration of macro-actions further complicates the learning task.

## Method

- Introduces the ==Macro-Action Similarity Penalty== (**MASP**), a regularization term that encourages Q-values of similar actions to remain close, thereby sharing credit among related actions.
- Utilizes a meta-learning framework to dynamically learn the action similarity matrix (Σ) concurrently with the agent's policy parameters (θ), adapting action relationships as learning progresses.
- The value function and policy are conditioned on a low-dimensional embedding of the evolving similarity matrix Σ, integrating this structural information into the Q-network.

## Results

- MASP agents achieved consistently higher cumulative rewards and faster convergence across Atari games, often surpassing human-level performance (e.g., Breakout: **884.4** raw score with MASP vs. **379.5** for Rainbow-DQN).
- The method demonstrated robustness to increasing numbers of macro-actions and noisy macro-action sets, maintaining strong performance where baselines experienced significant degradation (e.g., Breakout with **1024** macro-actions: **379.5** raw score for MASP vs. **13.2** for Rainbow-DQN + Macro-Actions).
- The meta-learned similarity matrix (Σ) proved transferable, with a Σ learned from one Atari game performing comparably in related target environments, indicating generalizable action semantics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly leveraging the inherent geometric relationships between actions and macro-actions through a meta-learned similarity structure significantly improves credit assignment and effectively reduces the perceived dimensionality of the action space.
> - Meta-learning enables the agent to discover adaptive inductive biases regarding action similarities, leading to more flexible and robust learning than relying on predefined or handcrafted knowledge.
> - The learned action similarity structure exhibits transferability across different, related tasks, suggesting its potential to facilitate knowledge transfer and accelerate learning in new environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved notable successes across various domains, including game playing (e.g., Go, StarCraft II, Dota 2) and control tasks (e.g., autonomous navigation, tokamak plasma control). However, a persistent challenge in RL, particularly in environments characterized by large or complex action spaces or sparse reward signals, is efficient exploration and effective credit assignment over extended episodes.

One approach proposed to address these challenges is the incorporation of *macro-actions* (also known as temporally extended actions or options). Macro-actions are predefined sequences of primitive actions that, when executed, can reduce the number of decisions an agent needs to make within an episode. This reduction in decision points is theoretically intended to simplify exploration and credit assignment. Early work by Randlov and McGovern and Sutton demonstrated that constructing and utilizing macro-actions could accelerate learning in various tasks.

Despite these theoretical benefits, the naive integration of macro-actions often leads to a paradoxical outcome: a degradation in performance. This counter-intuitive result is frequently attributed to the implicit assumption that each new macro-action, when added to the agent's action space, is atomic and independent. Consequently, the expanded action space increases the *branching factor* at each decision step, potentially enlarging the overall search space more significantly than the reduction in episode length simplifies it. Prior research has attempted to mitigate this by focusing on methods for discovering environment-specific, useful macro-actions.

Recent advancements have extended the concept of macro-actions into deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and meta-RL contexts. Examples include meta-learning strategies for hierarchical agents to reuse skills (Frans et al.), tri-level meta-RL frameworks for learning task-agnostic macro-actions (Cho and Sun), and applications in multi-agent settings (Tan et al., Xiao). Macro-actions have also been combined with intrinsic motivation (Panda et al.), utilized for transfer learning across tasks (Chang et al.), and integrated into classical planning (Allen et al., Newton et al.) and Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) (Amato et al., Lee et al.). Further, object-oriented and topological macro-actions have been explored for spatial reasoning in visually complex environments (Hakenes and Glasmachers).

This existing body of work highlights the potential of macro-actions to improve RL efficiency but also underscores the persistent challenge of their effective integration, particularly regarding how their relationships are handled. The current work positions itself within this landscape by focusing not solely on macro-action discovery, but on addressing the inefficiencies arising from treating an augmented action space as unstructured. It proposes to leverage the inherent similarities between actions and macro-actions to refine the credit assignment mechanism, thereby aiming to improve exploration and learning effectiveness in complex action spaces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from an observed inefficiency in reinforcement learning (RL) agents that utilize macro-actions. While macro-actions are intended to simplify learning by reducing the number of sequential decisions required in an episode, their naive addition to an agent's action space often leads to performance degradation. This is attributed to a trade-off: though the average number of decisions per episode (N) might decrease, the increase in the size of the action space (branching factor, *f*) can make the overall search space (conceptually *f*^N) larger, thus hindering exploration rather than improving it. Standard exploration strategies typically treat each action, including macro-actions, as independent and atomic, failing to account for underlying relationships.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Exploit Relationships between Actions and Macro-Actions:** To challenge the assumption that actions and macro-actions are independent. The authors hypothesize that many macro-actions are inherently related to each other, and to primitive actions, often leading to similar environmental outcomes due to shared underlying primitive actions or functional equivalence.
2. **Develop a Novel Regularization Term for Credit Assignment:** To formalize this intuition, the paper aims to introduce a regularization term, termed the Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP), that exploits these relationships. This term is designed to distribute credit received by a chosen macro-action to other similar macro-actions, thereby reducing the *effective* dimensionality of the action space. This credit sharing is intended to promote a smoother Q-function landscape and facilitate learning.
3. **Meta-learn the Similarity Structure (Σ) Jointly with the Policy:** Rather than relying on handcrafted similarities or fixed inductive biases, a core objective is to enable the agent to dynamically learn which actions and macro-actions are functionally similar. This is achieved through a meta-learning framework that updates a similarity matrix (Σ) concurrently with the agent's policy parameters.
4. **Improve Exploration and Learning Efficiency:** The ultimate goal is to demonstrate that by leveraging the inherent geometry of the action space through a meta-learned similarity matrix, an agent can achieve more effective exploration and faster convergence in environments augmented with macro-actions.
5. **Validate Transferability of Learned Similarities:** To investigate whether the learned similarity matrix can generalize and be reused across different, potentially related, environments, suggesting its potential as a transferable knowledge component.

In summary, the research aims to address the exploration challenge posed by augmented action spaces by introducing a mechanism that leverages the structural relationships between actions and macro-actions, thereby improving credit assignment and overall learning performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on integrating a novel regularization term, the Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP), into a value-based reinforcement learning framework and dynamically learning its parameters using a meta-gradient approach.

#### 3.1. Background: Reinforcement Learning and Rainbow-DQN

The work operates within the standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework, where an agent learns a policy to maximize expected discounted rewards. The focus is on value-based methods, specifically Q-learning, where a Q-function (approximated by a neural network) estimates the expected return for taking an action in a given state. The baseline algorithm used is RAINBOW-DQN, an advanced DQN variant that combines several improvements, including prioritized experience replay, n-step returns, double Q-learning, dueling networks, and noisy nets. The proposed MASP regularization is applied on top of this RAINBOW-DQN baseline.

#### 3.2. Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP)

The core idea behind MASP is to exploit the notion that some actions and macro-actions are not entirely independent but rather lead to similar outcomes. Treating them as distinct increases the search space unnecessarily. MASP imposes a structure on the action space by encouraging the Q-values of similar actions to remain close.
Formally, MASP assumes the existence of a similarity matrix, Σ ∈ ℝ<sup>|A|×|A|</sup>, where |A| is the size of the augmented action space (primitive actions + macro-actions). Each element Σ<sub>ij</sub> represents the similarity between action a<sub>i</sub> and a<sub>j</sub>. This matrix is symmetric and non-negative.
The MASP regularization term is added to the standard Temporal Difference (TD) loss:
L<sub>MASP</sub> = η · ||Q(s<sub>i</sub>, ·; θ) − ΣQ(s<sub>i</sub>, ·; θ)||^2<sub>2</sub>
Here, Q(s<sub>i</sub>, ·; θ) is the vector of Q-values for all actions at state s<sub>i</sub>, and η is a hyperparameter controlling the regularization strength. This term penalizes large deviations between the current Q-values and a "smoothed" version obtained by multiplying the Q-value vector by the similarity matrix Σ, effectively encouraging credit sharing among related actions.

#### 3.3. Meta-learning Σ

A crucial aspect of MASP is that the similarity matrix Σ is not predefined but is meta-learned simultaneously with the agent's policy parameters (θ). This adaptive approach allows Σ to evolve and capture relevant action relationships as the agent learns. The meta-learning process employs a two-phase meta-gradient framework:
1. **Inner Update (Agent Phase):** The agent's parameters (θ) are updated using a standard RL loss augmented with the MASP regularization, given the *current* similarity matrix Σ. This results in updated parameters θ'.
2. **Outer Update (Meta Phase):** The quality of the updated θ' is evaluated on a separate held-out trajectory using a meta-objective (the standard TD error term, L<sub>TD</sub>). The meta-gradient of this meta-objective with respect to Σ (∇<sub>Σ</sub>L<sub>meta</sub>) is computed via the chain rule, backpropagating through the inner update step. This gradient is then used to update Σ with a separate learning rate (β).
To manage the non-stationarity introduced by an evolving Σ, the value function and policy are conditioned on Σ through a low-dimensional embedding (e<sub>Σ</sub>). This embedding is derived by flattening Σ and passing it through a trainable linear transformation: e<sub>Σ</sub> = W<sub>emb</sub> · vec(Σ), where W<sub>emb</sub> is a learned weight matrix. This embedding is concatenated with the state representation and fed into the Q-network.
Implementation details from the appendix include: Σ is enforced to be symmetric, entries are clipped to, and a small entropy penalty is added to the row-normalized Σ during meta-learning to prevent degeneration. The computational overhead of MASP, including meta-gradient computation, is reported to be approximately 20% over standard DQN.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

The method's efficacy was evaluated across several challenging environments:
- **Atari 2600 Games (Arcade Learning Environment):** Macro-actions were generated using a frequency-based heuristic applied to human expert trajectories (Atari Grand Challenge Dataset), extracting the top-*k* most frequent action subsequences of lengths 3 to 8. A default *k*=32 was used, with ablations for larger *k*. Agents were trained for 2 billion frames.
- **StreetFighter II (Gym Retro):** Macro-actions were explicitly defined as attack combinations (combos), leveraging domain knowledge. Two sets of macro-actions, "Macro-Actions I" (simpler) and "Macro-Actions II" (more complex), were used. Agents were trained for 500 million frames.
- **MiniGrid Environments (Gym-MiniGrid):** Tasks requiring planning and structured exploration (e.g., DoorKey, FourRooms). Macro-actions were fixed sequences based on typical interaction patterns. Agents were trained for 500,000 steps.
All agent variants (standard RAINBOW-DQN, RAINBOW-DQN + Macro-Actions, and RAINBOW-DQN + MASP) shared identical architectures and optimization hyperparameters, differing only in the application of macro-actions and MASP regularization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that the Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP) consistently improves learning efficiency and performance across a diverse set of environments when macro-actions are employed.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Improvements

- **Atari Games:** MASP significantly improved performance over both the RAINBOW-DQN baseline and the RAINBOW-DQN + Macro-Actions baseline (which often performed worse than the standard baseline due to the expanded action space). In many Atari games, MASP achieved higher cumulative rewards and faster convergence. For instance, in Breakout, MASP obtained a raw score of 884.4 ± 74.0, compared to 379.5 ± 25.1 for Rainbow-DQN and 252.9 ± 22.2 for Rainbow-DQN + Macro-Actions. Similar improvements were observed in games like Space Invaders (16,668.2 ± 2,050.5 for MASP vs. 12,629.0 ± 1,413.2 for Rainbow-DQN), and Frostbite (5,566.7 ± 317.3 for MASP vs. 4,141.1 ± 175.4 for Rainbow-DQN). In several instances, MASP-trained agents surpassed human-level performance (human-normalized score ≥ 100).
- **StreetFighter II:** MASP consistently outperformed both baselines in the StreetFighter II environment. The performance gains were more pronounced with the more complex "Macro-Actions II" set, where MASP achieved 593,784 ± 18,262.9, surpassing Rainbow-DQN's 537,388.8 ± 23,829.9 and Rainbow-DQN + Macro-Actions' 565,319.2 ± 9,441.2. This suggests MASP's ability to scale and provide benefits in environments with structured, combinatorially large action spaces.
- **MiniGrid Environments:** MASP improved success rates across all tested MiniGrid tasks. For example, in DoorKey, MASP achieved a success rate of 1.0, compared to 0.83 for Rainbow-DQN and 0.88 for Macro-Actions. In more complex tasks like ObstructedMaze-Full, MASP also showed a higher success rate (0.91) than the baselines (0.57 and 0.84, respectively).

#### 4.2. Robustness to Action Space Size

An ablation study varying the number of macro-actions (*k*) in Atari games (from 64 to 1024) revealed that while the RAINBOW-DQN + Macro-Actions baseline suffered significant performance degradation as *k* increased, MASP maintained strong and stable performance. For instance, in Breakout with *k*=1024 macro-actions, RAINBOW-DQN + Macro-Actions scored 13.2 ± 0.5, whereas MASP achieved 379.5 ± 30.1. This indicates MASP's robustness in managing larger action spaces by effectively reducing their intrinsic dimensionality through credit sharing.

#### 4.3. Robustness to Noisy Macro-Actions

Experiments introducing noise into the macro-action set (randomly replacing macro-actions with random sequences with probability P(replace)) demonstrated MASP's resilience. Even at high noise levels (P(replace) = 0.75), MASP continued to provide substantial performance benefits, while RAINBOW-DQN + Macro-Actions degraded sharply. This suggests that the learned similarity structure enables MASP to identify and effectively handle non-informative or corrupted macro-actions, preventing them from hindering learning.

#### 4.4. Transfer Learning and Generalization of Σ

A significant finding is the transferability of the meta-learned similarity matrix Σ. When Σ learned from one Atari game was applied to another, related target environment, MASP-trained policies performed comparably to or better than policies trained with a Σ learned directly in the target environment (in-domain baseline). For example, a Σ learned from Breakout achieved 815.3 ± 66.2 in Space Invaders, which is close to the in-domain MASP score of 16,668.2 ± 2,050.5 (though a direct numerical comparison here with the provided table is hard as the reference score of 815.3 is HN not raw) and notably better than the Rainbow-DQN baseline (12,629.0 ± 1,413.2 raw score). Strong transferability was observed between games with similar mechanics, such as Montezuma’s Revenge and Private Eye. This indicates that MASP can learn generalizable action semantics, potentially facilitating knowledge transfer across tasks and reducing retraining requirements. Visualizations of the learned Σ matrix in Street Fighter II further illustrate the formation of distinct clusters of functionally related macro-actions, while primitive actions remained largely independent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by offering a mechanism to address a recognized challenge: the effective integration of macro-actions into learning agents. By proposing the Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP) and a meta-learning framework for its underlying similarity matrix (Σ), the work moves beyond treating actions as atomic entities and explicitly leverages the inherent structure within augmented action spaces.

The significance of this work can be understood through several points:

1. **Improved Credit Assignment and Exploration:** The central impact is the demonstrated improvement in credit assignment and exploration efficiency when using macro-actions. By allowing similar actions to share learning signals, MASP effectively reduces the "intrinsic dimensionality" of large action spaces, making it easier for agents to discover effective policies. This addresses the paradox where adding macro-actions can increase, rather than decrease, exploration difficulty.
2. **Robustness to Action Space Complexity:** The findings indicate that MASP enables agents to handle a significantly larger number of macro-actions without catastrophic performance degradation, a limitation observed in prior macro-action approaches. This robustness is critical for scaling RL to more complex real-world problems where the optimal set of macro-actions might be extensive.
3. **Resilience to Imperfect Macro-Actions:** The ability of MASP to maintain performance even with noisy or partially corrupted macro-action sets suggests its practical utility. In many scenarios, automatically extracted macro-actions may not be perfectly optimal or entirely relevant, and MASP's ability to learn and adjust for these imperfections is a valuable attribute.
4. **Meta-Learning of Inductive Biases:** The meta-learning framework for Σ is a notable methodological contribution. It allows the agent to adapt its own inductive biases regarding action similarity, rather than relying on predefined or handcrafted knowledge. This self-adaptive capability makes the approach more flexible and potentially applicable across a wider range of domains without extensive manual feature engineering.
5. **Potential for Knowledge Transfer:** The demonstrated transferability of the learned similarity matrix Σ across related tasks suggests that MASP can capture generalizable action semantics. This opens avenues for more efficient transfer learning, where the structural knowledge of action relationships learned in one environment could accelerate learning in new, similar environments, reducing the computational cost and data requirements for training new agents.
6. **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated in discrete action spaces (Atari, StreetFighter II, MiniGrid), the underlying principle of leveraging action space geometry for improved credit assignment has potential implications for other RL settings, including those with continuous action spaces or hierarchical structures.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge limitations, such as the state-independence of the current Σ parametrization, the quadratic computational cost of Σ for extremely large action spaces, reliance on meaningful macro-action extraction, and the need for further understanding of Σ's interpretability. Addressing these limitations in future research could further amplify the impact of this approach, potentially leading to more scalable, robust, and generalizable reinforcement learning systems capable of effectively utilizing composite actions. This work represents a step towards understanding and exploiting the geometry of action spaces to enhance learning efficiency in complex environments.
