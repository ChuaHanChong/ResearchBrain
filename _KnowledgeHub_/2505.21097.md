# Thinker: Learning to Think Fast and Slow

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Cambridge, The University of Hong Kong, and Shanghai AI Lab introduce the "Thinker task," a structured approach to Large Language Model reasoning that decomposes problem-solving into distinct stages for intuition, verification, deliberation, and summarization. This method improved accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks by 6.7% to 11.1% (relative) for 1.5B and 7B parameter models, demonstrating enhanced efficiency and more direct reasoning.

## Problem

- Current Reinforcement Learning-trained LLMs exhibit inefficient and verbose Chain-of-Thought reasoning, often with redundant self-correction steps.
- Standard RL reward mechanisms struggle with precise temporal credit assignment, often rewarding both useful and futile search paths equally within long reasoning sequences.
- LLMs frequently lack explicit training signals to develop "intuition" for quickly identifying promising solution paths and "verification" for confidently evaluating correctness.

## Method

- Proposed the "**Thinker** task," a four-stage, multi-turn Reinforcement Learning environment for LLMs, inspired by human dual-process cognition.
- Each stage (Fast Thinking, Verification, Slow Thinking, Summarization) receives specific, isolated reward signals to train distinct cognitive functions like intuition and deliberation.
- The approach utilizes Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) fine-tuning on LLMs, with pre-defined token budgets for each reasoning stage.

## Results

- The Thinker agent demonstrated relative accuracy improvements of **6.7%** (Qwen2.5-**1.5B**) and **11.1%** (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-**1.5B**) on mathematical reasoning benchmarks compared to baseline models.
- "Thinker-Fast" mode achieved accuracy competitive with or exceeding baselines using significantly fewer tokens, offering improved inference efficiency.
- Training with the Thinker task led to a decrease in "reflection patterns" and a reduction in average token length for larger models, indicating more streamlined and confident reasoning processes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing complex reasoning into distinct, rewarded stages enables more precise temporal credit assignment, fostering the development of specific cognitive abilities in LLMs.
> - Training for separate "fast" (intuitive) and "slow" (deliberative) thinking processes enhances both reasoning accuracy and token efficiency.
> - The "Summarization" stage is essential for distilling correct, extensive reasoning into concise patterns, thereby improving the model's intuition for subsequent fast thinking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, specifically focusing on enhancing their performance and efficiency in question-answering (QA) tasks, particularly in domains like mathematics and coding. Recent studies have demonstrated that applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) can improve LLMs' reasoning capabilities, often manifesting as emergent self-correction behaviors within Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning. These behaviors, such as step verification and backtracking, are akin to search processes.

However, a recurring observation is that this emergent search is frequently inefficient, leading to verbose and redundant CoT outputs. For instance, DeepSeek R1 has been noted for excessive backtracking. The authors attribute this inefficiency to imprecise temporal credit assignment in standard RL algorithms like GRPO, where the entire generation sequence receives the same scalar reward if the final answer is correct, potentially rewarding futile search paths alongside useful ones. This implies that critical capabilities such as intuition (rapid identification of promising paths) and verification (confident evaluation of a path) may remain underdeveloped due to a lack of explicit training signals.

Existing RL solutions to address this issue include using more precise token-level credit assignment (e.g., with PPO critics), but studies suggest these may not be sufficiently accurate. Alternative approaches, such as lower discount rates or shorter context lengths, risk hindering the emergence of beneficial search behaviors, which often require long context lengths.

This work introduces an alternative approach inspired by the Dual Process Theory in psychology, which posits two cognitive systems: System 1 (fast, intuitive) and System 2 (slow, deliberate). Rather than solely modifying RL algorithms or token budgets, this research proposes a structured modification to the QA task itself, termed the "Thinker task." This task decomposes the reasoning process into distinct stages, each designed to train specific cognitive functions, thereby providing more targeted learning signals. This aligns with a broader area of research known as environment augmentation in RL, which explores designing environments to offer richer inputs, structured interactions, or nuanced reward signals to enhance agent capabilities.

The paper differentiates its approach from other related works. It contrasts with "Thinker MDPs," which equip agents with a simulated world model for interaction, by structuring QA into stages where self-generated intermediate outcomes guide subsequent reasoning. It also differs from multi-attempt tasks that require ground-truth access during inference, a constraint the Thinker task avoids. Furthermore, while many works focus on controlling generation length (token budgets) within a single response, the Thinker task integrates token budgets into a multi-step RL framework to train distinct abilities. It also distinguishes itself from prompt engineering-based self-correction methods like Self-Refine and Reflexion, as the Thinker task involves RL fine-tuning of the agent for these self-correction behaviors rather than eliciting them solely at inference time through prompting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from observed limitations in current RL-trained LLMs, particularly their inefficient and often unconfident reasoning processes. While LLMs exhibit self-correction, this behavior frequently manifests as lengthy and redundant Chain-of-Thought (CoT) sequences, indicating deficiencies in intuitive problem-solving and confident verification. The authors hypothesize that this inefficiency is partly due to the inability of conventional RL reward mechanisms to precisely assign credit to specific useful steps within a long reasoning trace, leading to both promising and futile paths being equally rewarded.

Inspired by the Dual Process Theory of human cognition, the paper's core objective is to introduce a structured approach to LLM reasoning that explicitly trains distinct cognitive capabilities. This theory describes System 1 as fast and intuitive, and System 2 as slow and deliberate. The researchers posit that by designing a task that mirrors this dual-process interaction, LLMs can develop more efficient and accurate reasoning.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

1. **To enhance "Intuition" (Fast Thinking):** Motivated by the observation that LLMs struggle to rapidly identify promising search paths under constraints. The goal is to train the model to generate a plausible initial answer quickly and concisely, simulating System 1's heuristic-based processing. This aims to improve inference efficiency.
2. **To improve "Evaluation Capability" (Verification):** LLMs often lack confidence in their generated solutions. The motivation here is to train the model to explicitly and confidently assess the correctness of an initial answer, akin to System 2's evaluative role. This allows for early termination if an answer is deemed correct, further boosting efficiency.
3. **To strengthen "Refinement and Deliberation" (Slow Thinking):** When initial intuition is flawed, LLMs need to meticulously revise their approach. The objective is to train the model to systematically refine or re-derive an answer with a generous token budget, addressing errors identified during verification and exploring alternative solution paths, mirroring System 2's deliberate reasoning.
4. **To develop "Integration and Concise Reasoning" (Summarization):** Long, successful reasoning traces can be inefficient for future fast thinking. This stage is motivated by the need to distill complex, correct reasoning into a concise, actionable summary. This summary, by re-presenting the original question, is intended to reinforce a strong association between the problem and an efficient solution path, thereby improving the agent's intuition for future "Fast Thinking" tasks and enhancing its ability to integrate information.
5. **To enable more precise temporal credit assignment:** By decomposing the QA task into distinct stages with specific, isolated reward signals, the research aims to overcome the limitations of scalar rewards in conventional RL, allowing the model to receive targeted feedback for each cognitive skill it is attempting to learn.
6. **To facilitate a "virtuous loop" between intuition and reasoning:** The design intends for Fast Thinking to provide promising initial paths for Slow Thinking, and for Slow Thinking (via Summarization) to refine flawed intuition for Fast Thinking, promoting a continuous improvement cycle.
7. **To demonstrate empirical performance gains and inference efficiency:** The ultimate goal is to show that this structured task design leads to consistently higher accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, along with improved token efficiency, especially in the Fast Thinking mode.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the "Thinker task," which modifies the standard single-turn QA task into a four-step, multi-turn dialogue process. Each step is designed to train specific cognitive abilities and is associated with a distinct reward signal. The entire interaction occurs within a single dialogue, meaning the agent has access to all previous prompts and responses.

**Task Description:**

1. **Fast Thinking:**
 - **User Prompt:** "Please answer the question concisely. This is the question: <Question>"
 - **Agent Response:** Generates an initial answer (`y_fast`) under a strict token budget (e.g., 1000 tokens), which is truncated if exceeded.
 - **Reward (`R_fast`):** Binary (1 if `y_fast` matches ground-truth `y*`, 0 otherwise).
 - **Motivation:** Explicitly train intuition by rewarding correct answers generated rapidly with limited search. The agent always proceeds to the next step during training.

2. **Verification:**
 - **User Prompt:** "Is your above answer correct?"
 - **Agent Response:** Evaluates `y_fast` and outputs "Yes" or "No" within a short token budget (e.g., 2000 tokens).
 - **Reward (`R_verify`):** Weighted binary. If `y_fast = y*`, reward is `(1 - p_fast-acc)` if agent says "Yes". If `y_fast ≠ y*`, reward is `p_fast-acc` if agent says "No". `p_fast-acc` is the batch-averaged fast thinking accuracy, used to balance class weighting.
 - **Transition:**
 - **Inference Mode:** If agent says "Yes", episode terminates with `y_fast` as final answer. If "No", proceeds to Slow Thinking.
 - **Training Mode:** If `y_fast` is correct, episode terminates. If `y_fast` is incorrect, proceeds to Slow Thinking (ensuring Slow Thinking focuses on correction).
 - **Motivation:** Train evaluation capability; save computational cost during inference by terminating if verified correct.

3. **Slow Thinking:**
 - **User Prompt:** "Your initial answer is verified to be incorrect. Please try again."
 - **Agent Response:** Generates an alternative answer (`y_slow`) using a larger token budget (e.g., 6000 tokens). Reasoning process is enclosed within `<think>...</think>`.
 - **Reward (`R_slow`):** Binary (1 if `y_slow` matches ground-truth `y*`, 0 otherwise).
 - **Transition:**
 - **Inference Mode:** `y_slow` is chosen as the final answer; episode terminates.
 - **Training Mode:** If `y_slow` is correct, proceeds to Summarization. If `y_slow` is incorrect, episode terminates.
 - **Motivation:** Encourage agents to refine incorrect answers, revise errors, or try alternative approaches using extensive deliberation.

4. **Summarization:**
 - **User Prompt:** "Your final answer is correct. Please summarize the steps concisely... This is the problem: <Question>"
 - **Agent Response:** Summarizes the reasoning from the Slow Thinking step into a concise output (`a_summary`) within a strict token budget (e.g., 1000 tokens). The original question is re-presented.
 - **Reward (`R_summary`):** `1{y_summary = y_slow} + c log P(a_summary | x_fast)`. A scalar hyperparameter `c` is used. Reward is gated to 0 if the generated response length is below a threshold (e.g., 300 tokens).
 - **Motivation:** Reinforce concise reasoning patterns. Re-presenting the question aims to distill successful lengthy reasoning from Slow Thinking into a compact form, thereby improving intuition for future Fast Thinking. Also trains integration ability.

**Training Considerations:**

- **Reward Propagation:** A key aspect is that rewards for each step are specific to that step alone; they are not propagated backward to earlier steps. This means the discount factor *between* steps is effectively 0, while the discount factor *within* each step is high (e.g., 1) for effective token-level credit assignment.
- **RL Algorithm:** The Thinker task defines the RL environment but is algorithm-agnostic. The experiments utilize PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization).
- **Model Architectures:** The task is compatible with various LLM architectures. Experiments were conducted using Qwen2.5-1.5B (Q1.5B) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (R1.5B), and additionally DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (R7B) for scalability.
- **Training Data:** The 129K math question-answering dataset from Open-Reasoner-Zero was used.
- **Hyperparameters:** Standard PPO hyperparameters were used (discount rate γ=1, GAE lambda λ=1, sampling temperature=1), with no KL penalty. Specific token budgets for each stage (1000 Fast, 2000 Verify, 6000 Slow, 1000 Summarize, 6000 for R7B Verify) and a `c` coefficient of 1e-3 for `R_summary` were chosen.
- **Computational Resources:** Training required approximately 7 days on two compute nodes, each with 8 A100 GPUs, using DeepSpeed, vLLM, and Ray libraries.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - A study on the importance of the Summarization step involved training a Q1.5B model without this stage.
 - An analysis of Fast Thinking's importance used earlier checkpoints of the R1.5B Thinker agent for generating Fast Thinking responses, while the fully trained agent handled subsequent stages.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the Thinker task against a standard QA task baseline, focusing on mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

**Performance Gains:**
- **Qwen2.5-1.5B (Q1.5B):** The Thinker agent achieved an average accuracy of 27.3% across benchmarks, a relative gain of 6.7% over the baseline (25.6%). The Thinker-Fast mode alone reached 25.2%, demonstrating performance comparable to the baseline using significantly fewer tokens.
- **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (R1.5B):** The Thinker agent attained an average accuracy of 51.0%, an 11.1% relative improvement over the baseline (45.9%). Thinker-Fast achieved 41.05%, outperforming the pretrained model significantly and showing strong efficiency despite being slightly lower than the full baseline.
- **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (R7B) (Scalability Experiment):** Thinker task also benefited larger models, achieving 59.09% average accuracy compared to 54.41% for the baseline, an 8.6% relative improvement. Thinker-Fast performance (47.19%) scaled well with model size.
- **Benchmark Comparisons:** The Thinker agent consistently outperformed the baseline and reported figures from other RL methods like Open-Reasoner-Zero and SimpleRL across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MATH500, AIME, GPQA Diamond, Olympiadbench, AMC23, Minerva Math, CollegeMath).
- **Token Efficiency Comparison:** When compared to concurrent works on token efficiency (ThinkPrune, Concise Reasoning, SR-FLOW, AdaptThink), the Thinker agent achieved superior accuracy with competitive token counts on benchmarks like MATH500, AIME24, and AMC23. Thinker-Fast mode showed substantial token reduction while maintaining competitive accuracy, particularly on easier datasets.

**Training Dynamics and Efficiency:**
- **Accuracy Trends:** Both Fast and Final accuracy for the Thinker agent improved steadily throughout training, in contrast to the baseline, which plateaued more quickly. This suggests a sustained learning of underlying intuition and reasoning strategies.
- **Response Length:**
 - For Q1.5B, the Thinker task’s cumulative response length was initially greater than the baseline’s due to its structured stages.
 - For R1.5B, which already possesses self-correction abilities, the Thinker agent's total response length eventually became shorter than the baseline's. This indicates that the Thinker task refined the model’s existing search process, pruning inefficiencies.
 - The Thinker agent’s overall average token length gradually decreased during training. This was attributed to rising Fast Thinking accuracy, enabling the model to solve more problems correctly in the early stage and bypass the token-intensive Slow Thinking stage.
- **Reflection Patterns:** The Thinker agent exhibited fewer "reflection patterns" (terms associated with self-doubt like "wait," "however") in its reasoning traces compared to the baseline, suggesting it learned to reason more directly and efficiently.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Role of Summarization:** Removing the Summarization step led to a more frequent increase in reflection patterns and highly fluctuating response lengths, suggesting that Summarization contributes to learning stability and discourages excessive reflection. Fast Thinking accuracy dropped (from 26.75% to 24.84%) without Summarization, supporting its role in enhancing intuition by distilling concise reasoning. Final accuracy, however, remained comparable, indicating Slow Thinking could often compensate.
- **Importance of Fast Thinking:** An analysis using less-trained agents for Fast Thinking showed a positive correlation between Fast Thinking quality and final accuracy. While Slow Thinking could recover from moderately flawed initial intuition, very poor Fast Thinking (e.g., from a pretrained model) led to significant drops in overall performance, highlighting the importance of a meaningful starting point.

**Case Studies:**
- Representative case studies demonstrated the agent's ability to progress from an uncertain, flawed initial attempt (Fast Thinking) to a corrected solution.
- In one example, Verification explicitly questioned the initial flawed logic, leading to a correct geometric insight. Slow Thinking then leveraged this insight for systematic re-derivation and numerical verification. Summarization distilled these corrected steps concisely.
- Another case illustrated error propagation: Fast Thinking made a guess. Verification correctly identified it as wrong but introduced its own plausible but incorrect heuristic. Slow Thinking then adopted and reinforced this flawed heuristic, leading to an incorrect final answer, indicating that deliberation can sometimes be superficial if core assumptions are not sufficiently challenged.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the "Thinker task" as a novel approach to improve the reasoning capabilities and efficiency of Large Language Models (LLMs) by structuring their problem-solving process. Its significance and potential impact span both theoretical contributions to AI and practical implications for LLM deployment.

**Theoretical Contributions:**

- **Structured Reasoning and Dual Process Theory:** The work provides empirical evidence that decomposing LLM reasoning into distinct, psychologically inspired "Fast Thinking" and "Slow Thinking" stages can lead to improved performance. This validates the applicability of Dual Process Theory concepts to artificial intelligence, suggesting that separate training for intuitive and deliberative processes can be beneficial.
- **Environment Augmentation in RL:** The paper underscores the potential of designing richer, more structured environments for Reinforcement Learning. By demonstrating that modifying the task environment and reward signals can yield significant improvements beyond just algorithmic advancements, it opens new avenues for research into dynamic tasks, adaptive environments, and targeted development of specific cognitive capabilities in RL agents.
- **Targeted Credit Assignment:** The proposed method addresses a known limitation in current RL for LLMs—the inefficiency of temporal credit assignment for long reasoning sequences. By isolating reward signals for intuition, evaluation, refinement, and integration, it enables more precise learning, fostering the development of distinct, complementary skills.

**Practical Implications:**

- **Enhanced LLM Performance:** The consistent and substantial accuracy gains observed across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks for different model sizes indicate that the Thinker task can effectively train more capable LLMs for complex problem-solving. This could lead to more reliable AI systems in domains requiring accurate, multi-step reasoning.
- **Inference Efficiency and Flexibility:** The Thinker-Fast mode offers a practical advantage by providing a highly efficient inference path for simpler problems or when computational resources are constrained. This flexible trade-off between speed/cost and accuracy allows for more optimized deployment strategies in real-world applications, where not all tasks require extensive deliberation.
- **Improved Interpretability (Potential):** By explicitly segmenting the reasoning process into stages like "Fast Thinking," "Verification," and "Slow Thinking," the Thinker task could inherently provide more structured and potentially interpretable reasoning traces. This could aid in understanding how LLMs arrive at solutions, diagnosing errors, and building more trustworthy AI systems.
- **General Applicability:** The design of the Thinker task is independent of the underlying RL algorithm and model architecture. This broad compatibility means it can be readily adopted and integrated with various existing and future LLM fine-tuning pipelines.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a framework for exploring how to further optimize the interplay between fast and slow thinking in LLMs. Future research could investigate dynamic adaptation of token budgets, more sophisticated verification mechanisms, or tailored summarization strategies, as well as applying this multi-stage paradigm to other complex domains beyond mathematical reasoning.

By open-sourcing the trained models and source code, the authors facilitate further research and development in this area, potentially accelerating the creation of more intelligent and efficient language agents.
