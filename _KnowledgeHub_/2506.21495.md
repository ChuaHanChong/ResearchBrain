# Bridging Offline and Online Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at FAIR at Meta and NYU demonstrated that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), when adapted to online or semi-online training, consistently outperforms offline DPO and performs comparably to online GRPO for LLM alignment, achieving up to a 56.6% increase in AlpacaEval LC winrate for non-verifiable tasks. They also showed the feasibility of training a single LLM on both verifiable and non-verifiable tasks simultaneously to improve generalization.

## Problem

- Traditional offline reinforcement learning methods for LLM alignment (like DPO) often underperform compared to online methods, but fully online methods (like PPO/GRPO) are computationally expensive due to frequent model synchronization.
- There is a lack of systematic comparison and understanding of the trade-offs between offline, semi-online, and fully online LLM alignment training paradigms across diverse tasks.
- Training LLMs on different types of rewards (e.g., human preferences for open-ended tasks vs. binary correctness for verifiable tasks) can lead to models specialized in one domain but lacking generalization to others, or even exhibiting 'reward hacking'.

## Method

- The researchers systematically explored three training regimes for LLM alignment — ==offline==, ==semi-online (periodic synchronization)==, and ==fully online (per-step synchronization)== — varying the synchronization frequency.
- They adapted ==Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)== and utilized ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== across these regimes, comparing their performance on both verifiable math problems and non-verifiable open-ended instruction following tasks.
- They investigated ==multi-task training== by sequentially fine-tuning and jointly fine-tuning a base Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model with mixed reward signals from both verifiable and non-verifiable sources.

## Results

- Semi-online and fully online DPO consistently and significantly outperform offline DPO across both verifiable math tasks and non-verifiable instruction following, with online DPO showing up to a **56.6%** increase in AlpacaEval LC winrate over offline DPO for non-verifiable tasks.
- Online DPO achieves performance comparable to or marginally better than online Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) in both verifiable and non-verifiable settings.
- Jointly training a single LLM with both verifiable and non-verifiable reward signals yields performance gains across both task types, demonstrating the feasibility of developing more general-purpose models without performance degradation in specific domains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Semi-online training (periodic generator synchronization) achieves performance comparable to fully online methods while offering substantial computational efficiency gains by enabling asynchronous response generation.
> - Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), traditionally an offline algorithm, can achieve performance comparable to or better than on-policy methods like GRPO when adapted to online or semi-online settings.
> - To develop general-purpose LLMs, exposure to both verifiable (e.g., math) and non-verifiable (e.g., instruction following) reward signals is crucial, and joint training with mixed rewards is feasible and beneficial.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "Bridging Offline and Online Reinforcement Learning for LLMs" is positioned directly at the forefront of the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment and post-training. LLMs, after their initial pre-training on vast datasets, require a crucial "alignment" phase to adapt them for specific tasks, improve their helpfulness, harmlessness, and adherence to instructions or verifiable correctness. This alignment is predominantly achieved through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or its derivatives.

Historically, the dominant methods for LLM alignment have included:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Training on high-quality human-written prompt-response pairs. While effective, it relies heavily on extensive and costly human annotation.
- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF):** Pioneered by works like InstructGPT, RLHF involves training a "reward model" (RM) on human preferences over multiple model responses, and then using this RM to fine-tune the LLM via reinforcement learning algorithms, most notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO is an *on-policy* algorithm, meaning it typically requires samples to be generated from the *current* version of the policy being optimized, which can be computationally intensive as it necessitates frequent model rollouts.

More recently, alternative and often simpler algorithms have emerged:
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** DPO simplifies the RLHF pipeline by removing the explicit need for a separate reward model. It directly optimizes the LLM's policy to satisfy pairwise preferences (chosen vs. rejected responses). A key characteristic of DPO, as initially proposed, is its *offline* nature, meaning it can be trained on a fixed dataset of pre-generated preference pairs without requiring live model interactions during training. This simplicity and efficiency have made DPO a popular choice in the community. However, some studies have indicated that offline DPO might lag behind online methods in performance.
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):** GRPO is an *online* RL algorithm that builds upon PPO. It leverages the ability to generate a *group* of responses for a given prompt and uses these to estimate relative advantages for optimization. GRPO has shown success in training sophisticated LLMs, including those with "thinking" capabilities.

The critical gap this paper addresses lies in the systematic comparison and understanding of these different training paradigms—offline, semi-online (iterative DPO), and fully online (PPO-based like GRPO, or truly online DPO)—across a diverse set of tasks. While prior work has explored iterative DPO and online DPO variants, a comprehensive investigation across both *verifiable* tasks (e.g., math, where correctness can be definitively checked) and *non-verifiable* tasks (e.g., open-ended instruction following, requiring a reward model) has been lacking. The authors aim to understand the trade-offs in performance, stability, and computational efficiency as one transitions from purely offline to fully online settings, especially for DPO which was initially conceived as an offline method.

Furthermore, the paper ventures into the less explored but highly important domain of **multi-task learning with mixed reward signals**. Previous research has noted potential conflicts or performance degradation when LLMs trained on human preferences (non-verifiable) are applied to tasks requiring strict correctness (verifiable), and vice-versa. This work explores the feasibility and benefits of jointly optimizing a single LLM with both types of rewards, seeking to create more general-purpose and robust models. By bridging these different training regimes and task types, this research offers crucial insights for the practical deployment and future development of advanced LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objectives and motivations driving this research can be summarized as follows:

1. **Systematic Exploration of Training Regimes:** The primary objective is to conduct a systematic and comprehensive investigation into the effectiveness of various LLM post-training methods (specifically DPO and GRPO) across three distinct training setups:
 - **Offline:** Where the model is trained on a fixed dataset of pre-generated responses.
 - **Semi-Online (Iterative):** Where the model periodically generates new responses using its updated parameters for subsequent training iterations.
 - **Fully Online:** Where the model's parameters for generating responses are synchronized with the training model parameters at every step.
 The motivation here is to understand how altering the frequency of model syncing impacts performance, stability, and efficiency.

2. **Comparative Analysis of DPO and GRPO:** To rigorously compare the performance and convergence characteristics of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) objectives. This comparison is critical because DPO is known for its simplicity and offline nature, while GRPO (and PPO) are on-policy methods often associated with stronger performance in online settings. The researchers aim to clarify their relative strengths and weaknesses when applied across different online/offline spectrums.

3. **Generalization Across Task Types:** To evaluate the performance of these RL methods on both:
 - **Verifiable Tasks:** Such as mathematical reasoning, where the correctness of an answer can be objectively confirmed.
 - **Non-Verifiable Tasks:** Such as open-ended instruction following, which typically relies on a learned reward model to assess response quality.
 The motivation is to understand if certain training regimes or algorithms are inherently better suited for one task type over another, or if consistent performance improvements can be achieved across diverse problem domains.

4. **Optimization for Performance and Efficiency:** To identify strategies and provide recommendations for optimizing these methods to achieve better performance and computational efficiency. This includes analyzing training dynamics, hyperparameter selection (e.g., `s` for semi-online syncing), and understanding issues like response length dynamics and entropy collapse. The efficiency gains of semi-online variants are particularly motivated by the high computational cost of LLM inference in fully online settings.

5. **Investigation of Multi-Task Training with Mixed Rewards:** To explore the feasibility and benefits of jointly optimizing an LLM using both verifiable rewards (e.g., from a math verifier) and non-verifiable rewards (e.g., from an LLM-based reward model). The motivation stems from the desire to develop single, more general-purpose LLMs that can excel across a broad range of human-like tasks, addressing the known challenges and potential "reward hacking" that can occur when optimizing solely on one type of reward signal.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide a systematic empirical foundation for understanding the intricate relationship between RL algorithms, training paradigms, and task characteristics in the context of LLM alignment, ultimately aiming to inform the development of more effective, efficient, and versatile future models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a rigorous experimental methodology to investigate the interplay between RL methods, training regimes, and task types. Their approach is characterized by a controlled comparison across well-defined settings.

#### 3.1. Training Framework and Infrastructure

- **Core Libraries:** All models are trained using the `fairseq2` library, an open-source sequence modeling toolkit developed by Meta, known for its flexibility and performance. Model inference, particularly critical for fast rollouts in online settings, is performed using `vllm`.
- **Distributed Setup:** The training pipeline is designed for distributed computing, utilizing 32 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for training workers and 8-16 H200 GPUs for inference workers (running as Ray actors), demonstrating the substantial computational resources required and available to the research team. This setup enables efficient parallelization of generation and training.
- **Model Synchronization:** A key aspect of their investigation is controlling the synchronization of generator and training model parameters. This is achieved via direct NCCL-based GPU-to-GPU weight synchronization, ensuring minimal overhead.

#### 3.2. Training Regimes

The core of their experimental design revolves around varying the `s` parameter, which denotes the number of parameter update steps performed between each synchronization of the generation model with the training model.
- **Offline:** `s = ∞` (infinity), meaning responses are generated once at the beginning and the model trains on this fixed dataset without updating the generator.
- **Semi-Online:** `s ∈ {5, 10, 100}`. The generator model is periodically synchronized with the trainer, but not at every step. This allows for batches of responses to be generated asynchronously and in parallel, offering potential efficiency gains.
- **Fully Online:** `s = 1`, meaning responses are generated using the very latest model parameters at every training step.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

The study focuses on two leading LLM alignment algorithms:
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** Traditionally an offline algorithm, the authors adapt DPO to semi-online and fully online settings by actively re-generating preference pairs (chosen vs. rejected responses) from the updated policy. They also experiment with keeping the `π_ref` (reference model) fixed or updating it along with the generator model. DPO typically uses a single chosen-rejected pair per prompt.
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):** Based on the on-policy PPO algorithm, GRPO optimizes using a group of responses for each prompt. The authors restrict GRPO to a purely on-policy setup without importance sampling for theoretical clarity, meaning responses are always from the current policy and used for a single update.

#### 3.4. Task Types and Data

The research uses two distinct task types to assess generalization and method applicability:

- **Non-Verifiable Instruction Following:**
 - **Dataset:** `WildChat-1M`, a large collection of user interactions with ChatGPT, from which 1,000 first-turn prompts are sampled for training.
 - **Reward Model:** `Athene-RM-8B`, an open-source LLM-based reward model that provides a scalar score for an input-response pair. For DPO, preference pairs are created by selecting the highest-scoring and lowest-scoring responses.
 - **Evaluation:** AlpacaEval 2.0 (length-controlled winrates) and Arena-Hard (scores), using GPT-4-1106 and GPT-4o as judges. Checkpoints are selected based on the highest length-normalized Athene-RM-8B rewards on a held-out validation set.

- **Verifiable Math Problems:**
 - **Dataset:** `NuminaMath`, a large dataset of math problems and reference answers, filtered to exclude proofs, multiple-choice questions, and synthetic data (261,440 problems, with 1,980 for validation and test).
 - **Verifier:** `Math-Verify`, an open-source toolkit for robust answer matching (not just exact string match) to handle various forms of correct answers.
 - **Reward:** Binary (correct/incorrect) based on the verifier. For DPO, chosen responses are randomly selected from correct predictions, and rejected from incorrect predictions.
 - **Evaluation:** Math500, AMC23, and the NuminaMath test set. N=50 solutions are generated per problem, and average accuracy is reported.

#### 3.5. Combined Tasks

To investigate multi-task training, three scenarios are explored using online DPO:
1. Finetuning a WildChat-trained model with NuminaMath data.
2. Finetuning a NuminaMath-trained model with WildChat data.
3. Finetuning the base Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model directly on a mixed dataset of NuminaMath and WildChat prompts, combining both binary and scalar rewards within a single batch. Checkpoint selection is based on the average of length-normalized non-verifiable and verifiable rewards on their respective validation sets.

#### 3.6. Hyperparameters and Base Model

- **Base Model:** Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is used as the initial seed model for all experiments.
- **Sampling:** Default parameters (temperature=1.0, top-p=1.0) for exploration rollouts.
- **Optimization:** Adam optimizer with specific learning rates, Adam epsilon, and gradient clipping values tuned per task (detailed in Appendix Table 4). The authors note that increasing Adam epsilon value helps reduce divergence and improve stability for DPO.

#### 3.7. Additional Experiments and Observations

The study also includes analyses of:
- **Response Length Dynamics:** How training regimes influence the length of generated responses, observing both length collapse in verifiable tasks and length inflation (reward hacking) in non-verifiable tasks.
- **Entropy Collapse:** Monitoring the entropy of the next token distribution during training across different algorithms and tasks.
- **Loss Function Modifications:** Experiments with adding an NLL loss term to DPO, using "GroupDPO" (pairing all correct with all incorrect responses), and combining GRPO and GroupDPO objectives.

This comprehensive methodological approach allows the authors to draw robust conclusions about the performance, stability, and efficiency of different LLM alignment strategies across a range of practical scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings across the different training regimes, algorithms, and task types:

#### 4.1. Superiority of Online/Semi-Online Methods over Offline

- **Consistent Outperformance:** A central finding is that both semi-online and fully online training methods consistently and significantly outperform traditional offline DPO across both verifiable math tasks and non-verifiable instruction following. This highlights the critical importance of training on responses generated by an *updated* model, as opposed to a fixed, pre-collected dataset.
- **Magnitude of Improvement:** For non-verifiable tasks, Online DPO resulted in a 56.6% increase in AlpacaEval LC winrate and 45.6% increase in ArenaHard score compared to offline DPO (averaged across judges). Similar substantial gains were observed in verifiable tasks.

#### 4.2. Performance of DPO vs. GRPO

- **Comparable Performance:** Surprisingly, online DPO (s=1) achieved performance comparable to online GRPO in both verifiable and non-verifiable settings. In fact, online DPO marginally outperformed GRPO in several evaluations. This suggests that DPO, when adapted to an online regime, can be as effective as algorithms like GRPO that are inherently designed for on-policy learning.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Semi-Online DPO

- **Efficiency and Performance Balance:** A key and perhaps most impactful finding is that semi-online DPO, where model weights are synchronized only periodically (e.g., s=5, 10, or 100 steps), performs very similarly to fully online DPO. This indicates that pure online training (synchronizing at every step) might not be strictly necessary to achieve state-of-the-art performance. This offers a substantial advantage in terms of computational efficiency, as responses for multiple training steps can be generated asynchronously and in parallel using a slightly older policy model, reducing the latency and overhead associated with frequent model synchronization.

#### 4.4. Task-Specific Observations

- **Verifiable Math Tasks:** Online and semi-online methods achieved substantial gains over the seed model and offline DPO. Online DPO marginally outperformed GRPO. Scaling the number of responses in GRPO beyond 8 did not yield further performance boosts.
- **Non-Verifiable Instruction Following:** All training regimes showed improvements over the baseline. Again, semi-online and online methods significantly outperformed offline DPO. Similar performance trends between semi-online and online DPO were observed, reinforcing the finding that frequent, but not necessarily per-step, syncing is sufficient. The authors acknowledge potential length bias from the Athene-RM-8B reward model but note that their evaluation benchmarks (AlpacaEval, ArenaHard) are robust and control for such biases.

#### 4.5. Combined Verifiable and Non-Verifiable Tasks

- **Limited Cross-Task Transfer:** Directly training on only one type of task (e.g., verifiable math) and evaluating on the other (non-verifiable instruction following) resulted in poor "cross-task" transfer or even performance degradation compared to the base model. This indicates that skills learned for one domain do not automatically generalize to the other with isolated training.
- **Benefits of Sequential Finetuning:** Significant improvements were observed on non-verifiable evaluations when a model finetuned on one task (e.g., WildChat) was subsequently finetuned on the *opposite* task (NuminaMath), and vice-versa. This suggests that exposure to both reward signals is crucial for general instruction following, especially because benchmarks like AlpacaEval contain "verifiable" prompts.
- **Effectiveness of Joint Training:** Fine-tuning the seed model directly with a mixed dataset of both verifiable and non-verifiable prompts (using both reward signals simultaneously) resulted in performance gains across both task types. Performance was comparable to models trained individually on each task, with slight improvements noted in non-verifiable evaluations. This demonstrates the feasibility and benefits of training a single model with diverse reward signals to achieve broad capabilities.

#### 4.6. Additional Observations

- **Response Length Dynamics:** In verifiable tasks, disabling reference model synchronization or increasing training speed could lead to "length collapse" (models producing excessively short, often correct, answers). In non-verifiable tasks, there was a tendency for response length to *increase* over time, potentially due to the reward model's length bias ("reward hacking"). The authors mitigated this by normalizing for length during checkpoint selection.
- **Entropy Collapse:** A substantial decrease in logit entropy (next token distribution uncertainty) was observed in verifiable tasks for all semi-online and online methods, except for offline DPO. This suggests models become more deterministic. Non-verifiable tasks exhibited less collapse. Attempts at entropy regularization did not fully mitigate this.
- **Loss Function Experimentation:** Adding an NLL loss term to DPO, using "GroupDPO" (which utilizes multiple preference pairs), or combining GRPO and GroupDPO objectives did not yield substantial benefits in performance or entropy compared to standard DPO or GRPO.

Overall, the findings strongly advocate for online or semi-online training paradigms for LLM alignment, demonstrating that DPO is highly effective in these settings. The successful integration of multi-task learning with mixed reward signals opens promising avenues for developing more generalized and robust LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research carry substantial significance for the future development and deployment of large language models, particularly in the realm of post-training and alignment.

#### 5.1. Practical Significance

- **Paradigm Shift for DPO:** The most impactful practical contribution is the demonstration that DPO, traditionally viewed as an offline algorithm, achieves comparable or even superior performance to online, PPO-based methods like GRPO when implemented in semi-online or fully online configurations. This extends DPO's utility far beyond its initial scope, making it a viable and perhaps even preferred choice for complex, high-performance LLM alignment.
- **Efficiency Gains through Semi-Online Training:** The finding that semi-online DPO (with periodic synchronization) performs nearly identically to fully online DPO is highly significant. Fully online RL requires constant, fast synchronization between generation and training, which is computationally expensive for large LLMs where inference latency is a bottleneck. Semi-online methods allow for asynchronous, embarrassingly parallel generation of responses, leading to substantial efficiency gains. This makes high-performance RL fine-tuning more accessible and scalable, especially for models where inference is resource-intensive. This concept builds on asynchronous reinforcement learning principles, offering a pathway to adapting offline methods to more dynamic settings for "agentic applications" where models must update while processing long rollouts.
- **Robust Multi-Task LLMs:** The successful demonstration of combining verifiable and non-verifiable rewards during training is a crucial step towards developing truly general-purpose LLMs. Current models often excel at specific tasks but struggle with others if optimized on a single reward signal. This work shows that a single model can be trained to be both factually accurate (on verifiable tasks) and helpful/coherent (on open-ended tasks), mitigating the "reward hacking" issues associated with single-objective optimization. This will lead to more versatile and reliable LLM assistants.

#### 5.2. Scientific and Theoretical Impact

- **Challenging Assumptions about Online RL:** The results challenge the implicit assumption that *fully* online RL (synchronizing at every step) is strictly necessary for optimal performance in LLM alignment. The strong performance of semi-online DPO suggests a broader sweet spot for synchronization frequency, opening new theoretical questions about the optimal balance between policy exploration/exploitation and computational overhead.
- **Understanding Training Dynamics:** The detailed analysis of phenomena like response length dynamics and entropy collapse provides valuable insights into the internal workings and potential failure modes of LLM alignment algorithms. This informs future research into regularization techniques and more stable training protocols for deep reinforcement learning applied to language models.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work provides a robust experimental baseline and framework for future investigations. It explicitly highlights areas for further exploration, such as optimizing multi-task reward mixing strategies, exploring different base LLM architectures, and developing more sophisticated verifiers and reward models.

#### 5.3. Broader Impact

The ability to efficiently fine-tune LLMs for improved performance across diverse tasks has broad implications for various applications, from more accurate mathematical assistants to more versatile and reliable chatbots. By improving both the effectiveness and efficiency of LLM alignment, this research contributes to the democratization of advanced AI capabilities, making high-quality LLMs more practical and accessible for a wider range of users and developers. The emphasis on bridging offline and online methods also paves the way for hybrid learning systems that can leverage both pre-collected data and real-time interaction for continuous improvement.
