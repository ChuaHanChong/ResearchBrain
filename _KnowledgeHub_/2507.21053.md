# Flow Matching Policy Gradients

> [!summary] Summary
> Flow Policy Optimization (FPO) integrates flow-based generative models into on-policy reinforcement learning by using conditional flow matching loss as a proxy for action likelihoods, enabling policies to learn expressive, multimodal action distributions. This approach demonstrated improved performance over traditional Gaussian policies and prior diffusion-RL methods on various continuous control tasks, including challenging high-dimensional humanoid control in under-conditioned settings.

## Problem

- Integrating expressive flow-based generative models as policies into reinforcement learning systems.
- The computational intractability of calculating exact action likelihoods for flow-based and diffusion models, which is a fundamental requirement for policy gradient methods like PPO.
- Limitations of prior diffusion-RL methods, which often reformulate denoising as an MDP, increasing credit assignment complexity and coupling training to specific stochastic sampling procedures.

## Method

- Introduces ==Flow Policy Optimization (FPO)==, an on-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that extends PPO to optimize flow-based policies.
- Proxies the log-likelihood ratio in the PPO objective using the difference in ==Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) loss== between the current and old policies.
- Treats the flow sampling process as a ==black box during rollouts==, making the method agnostic to the choice of diffusion or flow integration method (deterministic or stochastic, varying steps).

## Results

- In a GridWorld task, FPO learned multimodal action distributions at saddle points, enabling diverse and optimal trajectories towards multiple goal regions.
- Outperformed both Gaussian PPO and DPPO in **8 out of 10** continuous control tasks from the MuJoCo Playground benchmark.
- Achieved significantly higher success rates in high-dimensional, under-conditioned humanoid control scenarios, achieving **70.6%** (vs **46.5%** for Gaussian PPO) with root + hands conditioning and **54.3%** (vs **29.8%** for Gaussian PPO) with root-only conditioning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The weighted denoising loss (which includes CFM loss) is equivalent to the negative Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) plus a constant, allowing its use as a proxy for the likelihood ratio.
> - Constructing the PPO ratio based on the difference in CFM losses enables gradient-based optimization of flow-based policies without requiring exact likelihood computations.
> - Flow-based policies can effectively learn and represent complex, multimodal action distributions, which is advantageous in under-determined or ambiguous environments where single-modal policies struggle.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work resides at the exciting and rapidly evolving intersection of flow-based generative models, particularly diffusion models, and reinforcement learning (RL).

#### 1.1. Flow-based Generative Models and Diffusion Models

Flow-based generative models, including the prominent diffusion models, have emerged as state-of-the-art tools for modeling continuous distributions in high-dimensional spaces. Their success spans diverse domains such as image and video generation, speech and audio synthesis, robotics, and molecular dynamics. These models learn to transform a simple, tractable distribution (e.g., Gaussian noise) into a complex target data distribution by learning a sequence of denoising steps or a continuous vector field. Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) is a particularly relevant advancement, simplifying and generalizing the diffusion framework by directly learning a vector field that transports samples from a prior to a target distribution, often through a simple Mean Squared Error (MSE) objective. A key challenge with these models has traditionally been the computational expense of exact likelihood estimation, which is often intractable.

#### 1.2. Reinforcement Learning and Policy Gradients

Reinforcement Learning provides a powerful framework for optimizing neural networks in settings where objectives are non-differentiable, making it ideal for learning control policies in dynamic environments. It has proven highly effective in various domains, from continuous control tasks to robot locomotion and manipulation. More recently, RL has been extensively used as a post-training strategy to align large foundation models with specific task goals or human preferences, particularly in large language models and text-to-image generation. Policy gradient methods, like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), are central to on-policy RL due to their stability, simplicity, and performance, but they fundamentally rely on the ability to compute action likelihoods.

#### 1.3. Diffusion Policies in RL and the Research Gap

The strong generative capabilities of diffusion models have naturally led researchers to explore their use as policies in RL. Initial approaches often focused on behavior cloning, where the diffusion policy learns to imitate expert trajectories. However, integrating these models directly into RL frameworks to optimize for reward feedback presents significant challenges. The main hurdle is the need for exact likelihood computations, which are prohibitively expensive for flow-based and diffusion models.

Prior attempts to combine RL with diffusion models have adopted various strategies:
- **Off-policy training:** Methods like Q-score matching have explored off-policy approaches.
- **Denoising MDP reformulation:** On-policy methods like DDPO, DPPO, and Flow-GRPO reframe the denoising process itself as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). They treat each step in the sampling chain as a distinct action, parameterized often as a Gaussian policy, and then apply standard policy gradient techniques like PPO.

While these approaches show promise, the denoising MDP reformulation introduces several limitations:
1. **Increased Credit Assignment Horizon:** It effectively multiplies the environment's horizon length by the number of denoising steps (typically 10-50), making credit assignment significantly more difficult.
2. **Increased Observation Space:** Initial noise values are often treated as observations, inflating the dimensionality of the learning problem.
3. **Sampler-Specific Limitations:** These methods are inherently bound to stochastic sampling procedures, limiting the flexibility of choosing faster deterministic or higher-order samplers at inference time.

The research gap that "Flow Matching Policy Gradients" addresses is the lack of a simple, on-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that can effectively optimize the expressive power of flow-based generative models without requiring exact likelihood computations, while remaining flexible regarding sampling methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations behind the development of Flow Policy Optimization (FPO) are:

1. **Integrate Flow Matching into Policy Gradients:** The primary goal is to seamlessly bring the power of flow-based generative models, particularly conditional flow matching (CFM), into the well-established and stable policy gradient framework of reinforcement learning.
2. **Overcome Likelihood Computation Challenges:** A major motivation is to sidestep the computational intractability of calculating exact likelihoods for flow-based models. FPO aims to achieve this by leveraging the conditional flow matching loss itself as a proxy within the policy gradient objective.
3. **Enable Expressive Policies:** By using flow-based models, the authors aim to train policies capable of representing richer, multimodal action distributions, which are often overlooked by traditional Gaussian policies but are crucial in complex, under-determined environments.
4. **Maintain Sampling Flexibility:** Unlike prior diffusion-RL methods that couple training to specific sampling procedures, FPO is designed to be agnostic to the choice of diffusion or flow integration method, offering flexibility at both training and inference time.
5. **Develop a Simple and Compatible Algorithm:** The authors aim for a method that is simple to implement and can be a drop-in replacement for Gaussian policies within popular frameworks like PPO, ensuring broad applicability and ease of use.
6. **Demonstrate Robustness and Performance:** The motivation includes empirically validating FPO's ability to train flow-based policies from scratch across a diverse set of continuous control tasks, including challenging high-dimensional and under-conditioned settings, where standard Gaussian policies might struggle.

In essence, the paper seeks to unlock the full potential of advanced generative modeling techniques for decision-making in reinforcement learning, addressing a critical computational bottleneck and offering enhanced policy expressivity and robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Flow Policy Optimization (FPO) introduces a novel approach to integrate flow-based generative models into the on-policy reinforcement learning paradigm, specifically building upon the success of PPO-style algorithms.

#### 3.1. Policy Gradients and PPO Foundation

FPO begins by acknowledging the standard policy gradient objective, which aims to maximize expected return by weighting the log-probabilities of actions with their observed advantages ($\hat{A}_t$). PPO enhances this by introducing a clipped surrogate objective that regularizes policy updates using a likelihood ratio $r(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|o_t)}{\pi_{old}(a_t|o_t)}$, preventing drastic changes and ensuring stable learning. However, this framework fundamentally relies on the ability to compute exact likelihoods for sampled actions, a challenging task for flow-based generative models.

#### 3.2. Flow Policy Optimization (FPO) Mechanics

FPO addresses the likelihood problem by introducing a proxy, $\hat{r}_{FPO}(\theta)$, for the log-likelihood ratio. The overall FPO objective retains the PPO-style clipped form:

$\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t|o_t)} \left[ \min \left( \hat{r}_{FPO}(\theta) \hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(\hat{r}_{FPO}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\text{clip}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{clip}}) \hat{A}_t \right) \right]$

Intuitively, FPO aims to "steer" the policy's probability flow towards high-return behaviors. Instead of exact likelihoods, FPO constructs $\hat{r}_{FPO}(\theta)$ using the Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) loss:

$\hat{r}_{FPO}(\theta) = \exp \left( \hat{L}_{CFM,\theta_{old}}(a_t; o_t) - \hat{L}_{CFM,\theta}(a_t; o_t) \right)$

Here, $\hat{L}_{CFM,\theta}(a_t; o_t)$ is an estimate of the per-sample CFM loss, computed as an average over $N_{mc}$ Monte Carlo samples of flow timesteps $\tau_i$ and noise $\epsilon_i$:

$\hat{L}_{CFM,\theta}(a_t; o_t) = \frac{1}{N_{mc}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{mc}} ||\hat{v}_\theta(a_{\tau_i t}, \tau_i; o_t) - (a_t - \epsilon_i)||^2_2$

where $a_{\tau_i t} = \alpha_{\tau_i} a_t + \sigma_{\tau_i} \epsilon_i$ represents the partially noised action. The model $\hat{v}_\theta$ estimates the velocity field that transforms noise into the original data.

#### 3.3. FPO Surrogate Objective and Theoretical Foundation

The theoretical underpinning of FPO's ratio estimate stems from the relationship between the weighted denoising loss ($L_w(\theta)$) (which includes CFM loss as a special case) and the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). For monotonic weighting functions in the denoising process, it has been shown that $L_w(\theta) = -ELBO(\theta) + c$, where $c$ is a constant independent of the model parameters $\theta$. This implies that minimizing the denoising loss is equivalent to maximizing the ELBO.

FPO leverages this by defining its ratio based on the ELBOs of the current and old policies:

$r_{FPO}(\theta) = \frac{\exp(ELBO_\theta(a_t|o_t))}{\exp(ELBO_{\theta_{old}}(a_t|o_t))}$

Substituting the ELBO-loss relationship, the constant $c$ cancels out, leading directly to the form based on the difference in CFM losses:

$r_{FPO}(\theta) = \exp(L_w(\theta_{old}) - L_w(\theta))$

This decomposition shows that maximizing this ratio increases the modeled likelihood of high-advantage actions while simultaneously reducing the KL divergence gap between the ELBO and the true log-likelihood, both desirable properties for policy optimization.

#### 3.4. Estimating the FPO Ratio with Flow Matching

The estimated ratio $\hat{r}_{FPO}(\theta)$ is computed using Monte Carlo samples of $(\tau, \epsilon)$. While a single sample ($N_{mc}=1$) introduces an upward bias in the ratio's scale (due to Jensen's inequality), the gradient estimate remains directionally unbiased. The authors find empirically that FPO performs well even with $N_{mc}=1$, though more samples generally improve learning. This makes FPO a practical and efficient algorithm.

#### 3.5. Comparison with Denoising MDP Approaches

FPO offers key distinctions and advantages over previous diffusion-based RL algorithms (e.g., DDPO, DPPO, Flow-GRPO) that reformulate the denoising process as an MDP:
1. **Credit Assignment:** FPO avoids treating each denoising step as a separate action, thereby not multiplying the environment's horizon length. This simplifies credit assignment, as the agent directly optimizes for the end action generated by the flow model.
2. **Observation Space:** FPO does not treat initial noise values as additional observations from the environment, which significantly reduces the dimensionality and complexity of the learning problem compared to denoising MDPs.
3. **Sampler Agnosticism:** Crucially, FPO treats the sampling procedure as a black box during rollouts. This means it is compatible with any flow or diffusion integration method—deterministic or stochastic, first- or higher-order, and with any number of integration steps—at both training and inference time. This flexibility is a significant practical advantage over methods tied to specific stochastic samplers.
4. **Simplicity:** FPO requires only minor modifications to standard PPO implementations (adding noisy action and timestep inputs, replacing Gaussian sampling with flow sampling, and using the FPO ratio), making it simpler to implement.

Algorithm 1 provides a clear, practical outline for FPO's implementation, emphasizing its straightforward integration into existing PPO pipelines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors empirically validate FPO's effectiveness across a diverse set of tasks, demonstrating its robustness and advantages over traditional Gaussian policies and other diffusion-based RL methods.

#### 4.1. GridWorld Experiments: Multimodal Action Distributions

- **Task:** A 25x25 GridWorld environment with sparse rewards and two distinct goal regions, designed to create "saddle points" where multiple optimal actions exist.
- **Findings:** FPO successfully trains a diffusion policy from scratch. When probing the learned policy at saddle points, FPO demonstrates the ability to capture **multimodal action distributions**, evolving an initial Gaussian noise into a bimodal distribution that points towards both goal regions (Figure 1, center). Consequently, trajectories sampled from FPO exhibit **diverse behaviors**, reaching different goals from the same starting state and showing significant variation even when heading towards the same goal (Figure 1, right). In contrast, a Gaussian PPO baseline, while successful, showed more deterministic behavior, consistently favoring the nearest goal with less trajectory diversity. This highlights FPO's superior expressivity in modeling complex action spaces.

#### 4.2. MuJoCo Playground: Continuous Control Benchmarks

- **Task:** 10 continuous control tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite, using the MuJoCo Playground environment. FPO is compared against Gaussian PPO and DPPO.
- **Findings:** FPO-optimized policies **outperform both Gaussian PPO and DPPO in 8 out of 10 tasks** (Figures 2 and 3), demonstrating its strong performance and robustness across various continuous control settings.
- **Analysis (Table 1):**
 - **Effect of $N_{mc}$:** Increasing the number of Monte Carlo samples ($N_{mc}$) for estimating the CFM loss generally improved evaluation rewards, confirming the importance of accurate ratio estimation.
 - **$\epsilon$-MSE vs. $u$-MSE:** The authors found that computing flow matching losses by predicting $\epsilon$ (noise-MSE) was preferable to directly predicting velocity ($u$-MSE) in the Playground tasks. They hypothesize this is due to $\epsilon$ scale invariance, leading to better generalization for clipping choices.
 - **Clipping Parameter:** Similar to standard PPO, the choice of $\epsilon_{\text{clip}}$ in FPO significantly impacts performance, underscoring its role in stable learning.

#### 4.3. Humanoid Control: High-Dimensional and Under-Conditioned Settings

- **Task:** Physics-aware humanoid control in Isaac Gym, training a humanoid policy to track motion-capture (MoCap) trajectories in a goal-conditioned imitation learning setup. This is a high-dimensional and stringent test. The authors specifically explored scenarios with varying levels of goal conditioning: full joint information, root + hands, and root only. The latter two represent significantly more challenging, under-conditioned problems.
- **Findings:**
 - FPO successfully trains high-dimensional humanoid control policies from scratch.
 - With **full joint conditioning**, FPO's performance is comparable to Gaussian PPO (Figure 4a, Table 2).
 - **Crucially, in under-conditioned settings (root + hands, root only), FPO significantly outperforms Gaussian PPO.** For "Root + Hands" conditioning, FPO achieves a 70.6% success rate compared to Gaussian PPO's 46.5%. For "Root" conditioning, FPO reaches 54.3% success rate versus 29.8% for Gaussian PPO. FPO also shows higher "alive duration" and lower "MPJPE" (Mean Per-Joint Position Error) in these challenging scenarios (Table 2, Figure 4b). This demonstrates FPO's ability to "fill in" missing joint specifications in a physically plausible manner, a task that Gaussian policies struggle with in a single-stage training setup.
 - FPO policies trained with terrain randomization enabled stable locomotion across procedurally generated rough terrain (Figure 4c), indicating potential for robust sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Flow Policy Optimization (FPO) represents a significant step forward in integrating the expressive power of flow-based generative models with the robust optimization capabilities of reinforcement learning.

1. **Enabling Expressive Policies in RL:** FPO successfully provides a simple and effective on-policy RL algorithm for training flow-based generative models. This allows RL policies to learn and represent complex, high-dimensional, and **multimodal action distributions**, a capability often lacking in standard Gaussian policies. This is particularly valuable in under-determined or ambiguous situations where multiple optimal actions exist.
2. **Solving the Likelihood Dilemma:** By leveraging the connection between flow matching loss and the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), FPO ingeniously sidesteps the computationally prohibitive problem of exact likelihood computation, which has been a major barrier for using advanced generative models in policy gradient methods.
3. **Sampler Agnosticism:** FPO's design, which treats the flow/diffusion sampling process as a black box during rollouts, provides unprecedented flexibility. This allows practitioners to use any desired sampling method (deterministic, stochastic, higher-order, varying steps) at both training and inference time, optimizing for speed or quality as needed.
4. **Robustness in Challenging Scenarios:** The empirical results, especially in high-dimensional humanoid control with sparse and under-conditioned goals, highlight FPO's superior robustness. Its ability to infer and generate plausible full-body motions from minimal root commands opens new avenues for simplified control interfaces and more generalized robotic skills.
5. **PPO Compatibility and Simplicity:** FPO's design as a "drop-in replacement" for Gaussian policies within the PPO framework makes it highly accessible and easy to integrate into existing RL pipelines, accelerating its adoption.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advanced Robotics and Embodied AI:** FPO can lead to more versatile and robust robotic behaviors, especially in tasks requiring diverse actions or handling under-specified commands (e.g., human-robot interaction with sparse instructions). It could facilitate training complex behaviors directly from demonstrations or high-level goals without extensive reward engineering.
- **Fine-tuning Pre-trained Generative Models:** The authors explicitly note FPO's potential for fine-tuning pre-trained flow/diffusion policies (e.g., those learned via behavior cloning in robotics) with specific task rewards. This could create a powerful pipeline where rich prior knowledge is refined through goal-oriented learning.
- **Beyond Robotics:** The principles of FPO could extend to other domains where expressive, multimodal action distributions are beneficial, such as creative content generation, drug discovery, or complex decision-making systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** FPO establishes a solid foundation for further exploration into the theoretical and practical aspects of combining advanced generative models with online reinforcement learning, potentially inspiring new regularization techniques, adaptive learning rates, and more sophisticated integration strategies.

**Limitations Acknowledged by Authors:**
The paper transparently discusses several limitations:
1. **Computational Cost:** Flow-based policies are generally more computationally intensive than Gaussian policies during both training and deployment.
2. **Missing RL Machinery:** FPO currently lacks established machinery common in PPO, such as KL divergence estimation for adaptive learning rates and entropy regularization, which could further enhance stability and exploration.
3. **Instability in Image Fine-tuning:** The authors report instability when applying FPO to fine-tune pre-trained image diffusion models on their own output, a phenomenon they attribute to broader challenges in recursively generated data and classifier-free guidance (CFG) sensitivity, rather than a limitation of FPO itself. This points to an important area for future research in the generative AI field.

Despite these limitations, FPO provides a flexible and theoretically sound bridge, promising significant advancements in areas where highly expressive and adaptable control policies are crucial.
