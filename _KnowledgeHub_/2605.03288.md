# Neural Control: Adjoint Learning Through Equilibrium Constraints

> [!summary] Summary
> The "Neural Control" framework from the University of Michigan and UCLA addresses control challenges in physical systems with implicit equilibrium constraints, providing an adjoint-based method and receding-horizon control to efficiently manipulate multi-stable deformable objects. This approach achieved up to 127 times faster optimization and 6 orders of magnitude lower loss compared to gradient-free methods in simulations, also demonstrating robust qualitative sim-to-real transfer for robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Controlling physical systems where states are implicitly defined by equilibrium conditions, making direct gradient computation complex and computationally intensive for long horizons.
- The high computational cost and numerical fragility of backpropagating through iterative implicit equilibrium solvers (e.g., Newton-CG) over extended control sequences.
- Managing multi-stability and path dependence, where identical control inputs can lead to different stable configurations based on actuation history, complicating robust long-horizon planning.

## Method

- Developed an adjoint-based method for computing trajectory-dependent gradients that differentiates equilibrium conditions, bypassing the need to unroll internal iterations of iterative solvers.
- Introduced a Receding-Horizon Continuation (RHC) scheme to segment control tasks, re-anchoring optimization to realized equilibria, which enhances robustness against multi-stability and path dependence.
- Employed a neural network controller, optimized using these efficient adjoint gradients within the RHC loop, to generate control signals for system boundary conditions.

## Results

- The Adjoint+RHC method demonstrated significant efficiency, being 19 to 127 times faster and achieving 3 to 6 orders of magnitude lower final loss than gradient-free baselines (SPSA, CEM) in numerical simulations.
- It consistently achieved better performance across tasks including point-to-point targeting, trajectory tracking, and multi-stable shape formation, reliably selecting specified equilibrium branches.
- Qualitative real-world experiments with a 6-DoF robotic arm manipulating an elastic strip validated the framework's practical applicability and robust sim-to-real transfer for compliant object manipulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adjoint-based gradients, derived from implicit differentiation of equilibrium conditions, offer a memory-efficient and computationally tractable way to optimize implicitly-defined physical systems.
> - The 'frozen-tangent' approximation used in the adjoint method is empirically sufficient for achieving first-order accurate gradients, balancing theoretical rigor with practical efficiency.
> - Combining adjoint-based gradient computation with a receding-horizon approach is crucial for reliable control in multi-stable physical systems, preventing error accumulation and unintended state transitions over long control horizons.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses challenges at the intersection of physical AI, robotics, and differentiable simulation, particularly for systems where configurations are determined by implicit equilibrium problems rather than explicit transition dynamics. This domain often arises in the manipulation of deformable objects, graphics, and physical simulation, where states are typically minimizers of an energy functional under various conditions.

Current approaches to learning and control in physical AI often grapple with two primary issues when dealing with implicit computations. First, in multistable physical systems, identical control inputs can lead to multiple stable equilibria. The realized state is history-dependent, influenced by the previous configuration and the actuation path, challenging the single-valued transition assumption common in many model-learning and planning methods. This path dependency makes long-horizon credit assignment difficult. Second, backpropagating gradients through iterative implicit solvers, such as Newton–CG methods, is computationally and memory-intensive due to the need to unroll all internal iterations.

Existing methodologies in differentiable simulation and control include:
- **Long-Horizon, Path-Dependent Control:** Approaches like recurrent reinforcement learning (RL) or latent state-space models attempt to handle history dependence. Model Predictive Control (MPC) with receding-horizon planning improves robustness by repeatedly replanning over short horizons.
- **Black-box and Sampling-Based Optimizers:** Methods such as Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA), the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), and its efficient variant iCEM, or Evolution Strategies (ES) are used to avoid differentiating complex transitions. However, these often require numerous full rollouts per update, making them costly, especially when each rollout step involves an expensive implicit equilibrium solve.
- **Memory-Efficient Gradients Through Implicit Computation:** A line of work focuses on differentiating through optimality, fixed-point, or Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions to avoid solver unrolling. Examples include differentiable optimization layers (e.g., OptNet), Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQs), and Pontryagin Differentiable Programming (PDP), often inspired by adjoint sensitivity methods for continuous-depth models. These methods typically differentiate a single implicit layer or fixed point.
- **Differentiable Models for Physical Learning and Control:** Advances in differentiable simulators (e.g., DiffTaichi, Brax, DiffPD) have made gradient-based optimization practical for various physical systems, including rigid and soft bodies. Learned differentiable simulators and graph-based dynamics models offer fast predictors, but near multistability, they may average outcomes or require explicit modeling of multimodality.

This work specifically distinguishes itself from prior implicit-layer methods by focusing on propagating gradients through a *sequence* of warm-started, branch-dependent equilibrium solves, rather than a single fixed point or optimization layer. It seeks to provide trajectory-level sensitivities that remain aligned with the realized equilibrium branch, a crucial aspect for multistable systems like deformable linear objects (DLOs) where current methods face limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient framework, termed "Neural Control," for long-horizon optimization in physical AI tasks that necessitate sequential implicit computation. This framework aims to address the inherent challenges posed by such systems, particularly those involving deformable objects.

The core motivation stems from several critical limitations observed in the broader research landscape:

1. **Implicit and Nonlinear Control-to-Configuration Relation:** Many physical AI tasks, such as deformable object manipulation, involve systems where the desired configuration is obtained by solving an equilibrium problem. The relationship between applied boundary controls and the resulting configuration is implicit, often high-dimensional, nonlinear, and can be multistable. This makes explicit modeling of transitions difficult and traditional control methods less effective.
2. **History-Dependent and Multistable Equilibria:** In multistable systems, identical boundary conditions or control inputs can lead to multiple valid equilibrium configurations. The specific configuration realized depends on the actuation history and the warm-started solver's basin of attraction. This "branch-dependent" nature makes long-horizon learning and credit assignment brittle, as small control changes can inadvertently switch the system to an undesired equilibrium branch. Existing model-learning and planning methods often assume single-valued transitions, which is violated in such contexts.
3. **Computational and Memory Cost of Gradient Propagation:** When each step in a sequential task requires solving an iterative implicit equilibrium problem (e.g., using Newton or Conjugate Gradient methods), naively backpropagating through all inner solver iterations is prohibitively memory-intensive and computationally expensive. Moreover, such backpropagation can be numerically unstable near system instabilities.
4. **Limitations of Existing Implicit-Layer Methods:** While advancements have been made in differentiating through single implicit layers (e.g., fixed-point iterations or KKT systems), these methods are not directly applicable to sequences of *warm-started, branch-dependent* equilibrium solves, which characterize the types of physical systems considered in this work. Propagating useful, trajectory-level gradients that are consistent with the realized equilibrium branch remains a significant challenge.

To overcome these challenges, the specific objectives of Neural Control are:
- To formulate a sequential implicit optimization approach that inherently supports branch-dependent equilibrium rollouts.
- To introduce a proxy-adjoint gradient method capable of computing trajectory-dependent sensitivities through converged equilibrium solves, thereby avoiding the unrolling of inner iterative solvers and reducing memory/compute demands.
- To integrate a receding-horizon continuation (RHC) scheme to improve robustness in multistable regimes, mitigating basin switching and re-anchoring optimization to the realized equilibrium branch.
- To empirically validate the effectiveness and efficiency of this framework on both simulated and real-world deformable linear object (DLO) manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against modern gradient-free baselines, and demonstrating its applicability to learned implicit equilibrium models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Neural Control framework is designed for learning and control problems where the system state `x(λ)` is implicitly defined by an equilibrium condition `G(x(λ), z(λ)) = 0` along a continuation path indexed by `λ ∈`. Here, `z(λ)` represents controllable quantities (e.g., boundary conditions), and `G` typically represents quasi-static force balance, often derived from the gradient of a potential energy functional.

The core methodology comprises three main components: a formulation for sequential implicit optimization, an adjoint-based method for computing proxy gradients, and a receding-horizon continuation scheme for enhanced robustness.

#### 3.1. Sequential Implicit Optimization Formulation

- **System State and Control:** The system state `x(λ)` is defined as the solution to `G(x(λ), z(λ)) = 0`. For mechanical systems, `G(x, z) = ∇_x E(x, z) - F_ext`, where `E` is total potential energy and `F_ext` are external loads. A neural controller, parameterized by `Θ`, generates a continuation control signal `u(λ) = u_Θ(λ)`. This signal drives `z(λ)` through known continuation dynamics `dz/dλ = f(z(λ), u(λ))`, starting from an initial condition `z(0) = z_0`.
- **Forward Rollout:** A key aspect is that the forward rollout is *solver-consistent*. At each discrete continuation step `k`, the state `x_k` is obtained by solving the equilibrium equations `G(x_k, z_k) = 0` to convergence, warm-started from the previous solution `x_{k-1}`. This ensures that the realized trajectory follows the specific, history-dependent equilibrium branch.

#### 3.2. Adjoint Gradients via Proxy Sensitivity Dynamics

To efficiently compute gradients through the sequence of implicit solves, Neural Control avoids unrolling the internal iterations of the equilibrium solver. Instead, it leverages an adjoint formulation for proxy gradient computation.

- **Local Equilibrium Sensitivity:** At any converged equilibrium `(x(λ), z(λ))`, the local sensitivity `S(λ):= ∂x/∂z(λ)` is derived from implicit differentiation: `G_x(λ) S(λ) = -G_z(λ)`. `G_x` and `G_z` are the partial derivatives of `G` with respect to `x` and `z`, respectively.
- **Matrix-Free Sensitivity Product:** To avoid explicitly forming the potentially large `S(λ)` matrix, the method computes products `S(λ)^⊤ v` by solving an adjoint linear system: `G_x(λ)^⊤ η = v`, then returning `-G_z(λ)^⊤ η`. This requires one linear solve per vector `v`, which is more efficient than explicitly constructing `S`.
- **Continuous Sensitivity Identity:** The total derivative `dx/dλ` along the realized branch is given by `dx/dλ = S(λ) dz/dλ`, assuming no explicit `λ`-dependence in `G`. Combining this with the continuation dynamics gives `dx/dλ = S(λ) f(z(λ), u(λ))`. This equation defines the *proxy sensitivity dynamics* for backward propagation.
- **Frozen-Tangent Approximation:** The main analytical approximation is that `S(λ)` is treated as locally constant during the backward pass over discrete continuation steps. This "frozen-tangent" approximation avoids higher-order derivatives of `G`. The paper formally shows that this approximation leads to first-order accuracy in the gradient error (O(Δλ)) under local regularity assumptions (smooth, non-singular `G_x`).
- **Costate Equations:** For a general objective `L` that can include a terminal loss `ϕ` and a running loss `ℓ` over the trajectory, costates `a(λ):= ∂L/∂x(λ)` and `g(λ):= ∂L/∂z(λ)` are propagated backward in time (from `λ=1` to `λ=0`) via a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). These ODEs incorporate the derivatives of `ℓ` and the contributions from the proxy sensitivity dynamics.
- **Control and Parameter Gradients:** The instantaneous control gradient `dL/du(λ)` is derived from the costates and continuation dynamics. The final policy parameter gradient `dL/dΘ` is obtained by integrating `(∂u_Θ(λ)/∂Θ)^⊤ (dL/du(λ))` over the continuation path.

#### 3.3. Receding-Horizon Continuation Control (RHC)

To enhance robustness, particularly in multistable regimes where errors can accumulate and cause unwanted basin switches, Neural Control integrates RHC.

- **Segmented Optimization:** The overall task is divided into a sequence of short continuation segments. For each segment `m`, starting from a realized state `(x_0^(m), z_0^(m))`, a local control schedule `u(λ) = u_Θ^(m)(λ)` is optimized.
- **Re-anchoring and Localized Optimization:** The controller parameters `Θ^(m)` are reinitialized for each segment and optimized to minimize a short-horizon segment objective `L^(m)`. After optimizing and executing a segment, the system's state `(x_H, z_H)` becomes the starting point `(x_0^(m+1), z_0^(m+1))` for the next segment. This process repeatedly re-anchors the optimization to the *realized* equilibrium branch, localizing proxy-gradient errors and mitigating basin drift.
- **Discrete Implementation:** Appendix A provides the discrete derivation and algorithm for the proxy-adjoint recursions and the RHC scheme, detailing the forward rollout, matrix-free `S^⊤ v` computation, backward pass for costates, and controller parameter updates.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

The methodology is validated on the manipulation of planar elastic strips, which exhibit large, geometrically nonlinear deformations and multistable equilibria.
- **Object Model:** Discretized centerline of N+1=101 nodes, with clamping at both ends. Boundary nodes `q_b` constitute `z`, while free nodes `q_f` constitute `x`.
- **Energy Model:** Total potential energy includes stretching `E_s`, bending `E_b`, and gravitational `E_g` components. The equilibrium residual `G(x, z)` is `∇_{q_f} E_tot(q_f, q_b)`.
- **Equilibrium Solver:** A trust-region Newton-CG method is used to solve for `x_k = SolveEq(z_k)`, warm-started from `x_{k-1}`.
- **Neural Controller:** A lightweight MLP (2-3 hidden layers, width 64) maps scalar `λ` to control vector `u(λ)`, which specifies boundary displacement/rotation rates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates Neural Control on simulated and real-world deformable linear object (DLO) manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against gradient-free baselines and ablating its components.

#### 4.1. Numerical Tasks and Metrics

Three progressively challenging numerical tasks were designed for elastic-strip manipulation:
1. **Task 1: Point-to-Point Node Targeting:** A terminal objective (`L_pos`) to guide a selected strip node to a target position at the end of the continuation.
2. **Task 2: Trajectory Tracking:** A trajectory objective (`L_traj`) to follow a prescribed planar path with the strip's midpoint over the entire continuation.
3. **Task 3: Shape Formation Under Multistability:** A shape-matching objective (`L_κ`) based on curvature to achieve a target shape in regimes where multiple stable equilibria coexist under identical boundary conditions, requiring robust branch selection.

The elastic strip model used N+1=101 nodes with fixed stretching and bending stiffness (k_s = 0.314 N, k_b = 7.85 × 10^-8 N m^2), and gravity was ignored in numerical experiments. The controller output rates for transverse displacement (Tasks 1-2) or (x,y) displacement and rotation (Task 3).

#### 4.2. Main Numerical Results

- **Tasks 1 & 2 (Two-End Boundary Actuation):** Neural Control successfully coordinated transverse actuation of both clamped ends to steer a selected node to target positions (Task 1) and guide the strip midpoint along prescribed planar trajectories (Task 2). The tracked paths closely matched references, confirming the method's ability to optimize objectives dependent on intermediate equilibria.
- **Task 3 (Multistable Shape Formation):** The method demonstrated robust branch selection in multistable scenarios. Despite the coexistence of multiple stable equilibria (e.g., mirror-symmetric C-shapes, distinct U/M-like shapes) for the same boundary conditions, the curvature-based shaping successfully drove the strip to the specified target configuration, indicating effective navigation of competing basins of attraction.

#### 4.3. Baselines and Ablations

Neural Control (Adjoint+RHC) was compared against:
- **Gradient-free baselines:** SPSA (Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation) and iCEM (sample-efficient Cross-Entropy Method). CEM was also reported in Appendix E.
- **Ablation:** Adjoint-only (Neural Control without Receding-Horizon Continuation, running full-horizon).

All methods used the same forward simulator and controller parameterization. The evaluation considered best achieved loss and total wall-clock time under fixed update budgets (200 for Task 1, 500 for Tasks 2-3).

- **Convergence and Robustness (Figure 4):**
 - **Task 1 (Node Targeting):** All methods reduced loss, but gradient-free baselines plateaued. Both adjoint-based variants achieved lower final loss, with Adjoint-only converging fastest due to its direct gradient and the short horizon.
 - **Task 2 (Trajectory Tracking):** This harder task saw SPSA and iCEM stall. Adjoint-only succeeded on smooth references but struggled with non-smooth targets due to long-horizon error accumulation. Adjoint+RHC showed consistent convergence across all trajectory types, highlighting the importance of segment-wise re-anchoring.
 - **Task 3 (Multistable Shape Formation):** SPSA and iCEM stagnated early. Adjoint-only, despite an initial decrease, often became trapped in incorrect branches. Adjoint+RHC consistently achieved the lowest curvature losses and reliably selected the specified equilibrium branch, demonstrating its effectiveness in multistable landscapes.

- **Efficiency Summary (Table 1):**
 - Adjoint+RHC was consistently the most efficient method, achieving significantly lower loss with reduced runtime compared to SPSA and iCEM. For example, in Task 3, Adjoint+RHC was approximately 78 times faster than SPSA and 195 times faster than iCEM, while achieving orders of magnitude lower loss.
 - Gradient-free methods require multiple full equilibrium-defined rollouts per update. Adjoint-based methods use one realized rollout plus additional linear solves for gradients.
 - RHC mitigated long-horizon error accumulation and reduced trajectory-storage memory by propagating gradients through shorter segments, contributing to its superior time-accuracy trade-off.

#### 4.4. Real-World Experiments

Qualitative validation was performed using a 6-DoF robot arm manipulating an elastic strip. The robot actuated one endpoint, with the other passively clamped. Material parameters were identified through a gravito-bending length method (Appendix G). The observed strip motion across all three tasks matched the task requirements, indicating qualitative sim-to-real transfer under gravity and model mismatch.

#### 4.5. Learned DEQ-Style Implicit Equilibrium Model

An additional experiment (Appendix F) demonstrated the framework's applicability beyond analytical models. Neural Control was evaluated on a learned DEQ-style implicit equilibrium model, trained from experimental force–strain data of a slinky. The method successfully optimized a force trajectory to track a prescribed sinusoidal strain, achieving very low tracking losses (10^-8 to 10^-11). This indicates that the proxy-adjoint continuation framework is compatible with learned implicit equilibrium models, provided local sensitivities are available.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Neural Control: Adjoint Learning Through Equilibrium Constraints" presents a significant advancement in addressing the challenges of long-horizon optimization in physical AI tasks involving sequential implicit computations and multistable systems.

#### 5.1. Contribution to Research

The paper addresses a critical gap in the field by providing a robust and efficient framework for optimizing through sequences of warm-started, branch-dependent equilibrium solves. This differentiates it from prior implicit-layer methods, which typically focus on single-step implicit functions. The formulation clarifies the unique challenges of multistability and history dependence in such systems.

#### 5.2. Technical Innovations

- **Memory- and Compute-Efficient Gradient Computation:** The introduction of a proxy-adjoint gradient method is a key technical innovation. By differentiating equilibrium conditions via an adjoint formulation, the framework avoids the computationally and memory-intensive process of unrolling iterative solvers (e.g., Newton-CG iterations). This allows forward rollouts to remain solver-consistent (i.e., operating on converged equilibria) while efficiently obtaining trajectory-dependent sensitivities.
- **Robustness in Multistable Regimes:** The integration of Receding-Horizon Continuation (RHC) specifically addresses the problem of multistability and error accumulation. RHC acts as a continuation and branch-tracking mechanism, repeatedly re-anchoring the optimization to the realized equilibrium branch. This localization of proxy-gradient errors and reduction of basin drift is crucial for robust control in systems where small perturbations could lead to unintended switches between equilibrium branches.

#### 5.3. Empirical Validation

The empirical results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness and efficiency of Neural Control.
- **Superior Performance:** In simulated deformable linear object (DLO) manipulation tasks, the proposed method (Adjoint+RHC) significantly outperforms gradient-free baselines (SPSA, iCEM) in terms of both solution quality (substantially lower loss) and computational efficiency (up to 195x faster wall-clock time).
- **Handling Complexity:** The method demonstrates robust performance across tasks of increasing complexity, from terminal node targeting to trajectory tracking and, critically, shape formation in multistable environments.
- **Real-World Applicability:** Qualitative sim-to-real transfer on a 6-DoF robotic setup further substantiates the practical relevance of the framework, showing its ability to operate under real-world conditions including gravity and model mismatch with experimentally identified parameters.
- **Generalizability to Learned Models:** Validation on a learned DEQ-style implicit equilibrium model indicates that the framework is not restricted to analytical mechanics models but can be applied to a broader class of differentiable implicit models.

#### 5.4. Potential Positive Impacts

- **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation:** The method has the potential to significantly improve the robotic manipulation of flexible and compliant objects, enabling more precise and robust control in tasks involving large, geometrically nonlinear deformations.
- **Efficient Simulation-Based Control:** By offering a more efficient gradient pathway, Neural Control can accelerate the development and deployment of simulation-based control strategies across various physical AI applications.
- **Broader Adoption of Differentiable Implicit Models:** The framework facilitates the use of differentiable implicit models in physical AI, opening avenues for optimizing complex systems where explicit dynamics are difficult to formulate or learn.

#### 5.5. Limitations and Risks

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Quasi-static Assumption:** The method assumes a quasi-static regime, neglecting inertia and fast transient dynamics. This limits its direct applicability to tasks requiring dynamic control or high-speed maneuvers.
- **Assumptions on Equilibrium Branch:** The derivation relies on assumptions of a locally smooth equilibrium branch with a nonsingular and reasonably conditioned `G_x`. Near bifurcations, singular equilibria, or high-curvature branches, the frozen-tangent approximation may become less accurate, potentially leading to instability or reduced performance.
- **Model Quality Dependence:** Performance depends on the quality of the underlying differentiable implicit model. Unmodeled non-conservative effects (e.g., friction, hysteresis, damping, intermittent contact) or significant model mismatch could degrade sim-to-real transfer.
- **Computational Cost Scaling:** While efficient, equilibrium solves remain the dominant computational cost. Scaling to high-dimensional 2D or 3D deformable bodies will require advanced solvers, preconditioning, and adaptive continuation techniques.
- **Deployment Risks:** As with any control system, deploying such controllers on hardware without thorough validation, especially under unmodeled dynamics or safety-critical constraints, poses potential risks. The authors advocate for appropriate hardware limits, task-specific validation, safety constraints, and human oversight.

In conclusion, Neural Control offers a principled and empirically validated approach to a challenging problem in physical AI, providing a practical gradient pathway for optimizing compliant, multistable systems defined by implicit equilibrium solves.
