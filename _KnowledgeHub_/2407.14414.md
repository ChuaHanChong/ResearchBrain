# System-1.x: Learning to Balance Fast and Slow Planning with Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework is introduced that allows large language models to dynamically balance between fast, intuitive System-1 planning and deliberate, explicit System-2 planning for complex tasks. This method achieves improved planning success rates and better generalization, especially under constrained computational budgets, compared to isolated planning approaches.

## Problem

- Existing LLM planners struggle with long-horizon tasks due to a lack of explicit search, leading to poor accuracy and robustness.
- Current approaches are either fast but inaccurate (System-1) or accurate but computationally expensive (System-2), lacking a flexible balance between the two.
- There is no systematic way for users to control the trade-off between computational cost and planning accuracy in LLM-based systems.

## Method

- A framework named ==System-1.x== is introduced, comprising a ==Controller==, a ==System-1 Planner==, and a ==System-2 Planner==, all fine-tuned on a single LLM.
- The Controller decomposes planning problems into sub-goals and classifies each as "easy" or "hard," directing them to the System-1 or System-2 planner respectively.
- Training data is automatically generated from gold search traces, incorporating a user-defined =="hybridization factor" (x)== that controls the balance between fast and slow planning during training.

## Results

- System-**1.x** achieved **70.4%** valid plans in Maze Navigation, outperforming System-1 (**48.7%**) and truncated System-2 (**37.2%**) at matched computational budgets.
- The framework demonstrated improved out-of-distribution generalization in Blocksworld, achieving **25%** validity at low budgets where other methods neared **0%**.
- The "hybridization factor" allowed for controllable trade-offs, with a System-1.75 model achieving **75.7%** validity compared to System-1.5's **70.4%**, demonstrating adaptability to user-defined preferences for accuracy over compute.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human-like dual-process cognition (fast intuition and slow deliberation) can be effectively applied to LLM-based planning by dynamically switching between planning modes.
> - Intelligent decomposition of complex problems into easier and harder sub-goals, coupled with dynamic resource allocation, improves planning efficiency and accuracy.
> - Controllability through a "hybridization factor" allows users to systematically balance the trade-off between computational cost and planning performance at both training and test time.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into long-horizon planning and reasoning tasks represents a rapidly evolving frontier in artificial intelligence. While LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generating coherent text and performing various language-related tasks, their application to complex planning problems, such as robotic control or intricate puzzle-solving, has revealed significant limitations. This paper directly addresses these shortcomings by proposing a novel framework that bridges the gap between the intuitive, fast decision-making often seen in LLMs and the deliberate, systematic search required for effective planning.

Traditionally, planning in AI has relied on symbolic methods and explicit search algorithms (e.g., A*, BFS, DFS) that meticulously explore state spaces to find optimal or near-optimal solutions. With the advent of LLMs, a new paradigm emerged where models directly "generate" plans based on learned patterns from vast text corpora. This direct generation mirrors what is often termed "System-1" thinking in cognitive science—fast, intuitive, and often accurate for familiar problems, but prone to errors for novel or complex scenarios that require explicit reasoning. Indeed, recent critical evaluations (e.g., by Valmeekam et al., Pallagani et al., Momennejad et al.) have consistently highlighted LLMs' struggles with classical planning tasks, attributing these failures to a fundamental lack of explicit search, backtracking, or iterative refinement from mistakes.

To mitigate these issues, the research community has explored two main "System-2" inspired approaches:
1. **Augmenting LLMs with external symbolic tools:** This involves LLMs acting as high-level planners or reasoners that offload sub-problems to external symbolic solvers, verifiers, or tree search algorithms (e.g., Tree-of-Thoughts by Yao et al., ReAct by Yao et al., methods by Sel et al., Ahn et al.). While effective in improving performance, these methods often introduce complexity by relying on external modules, which may not always be available for specific domains, and can complicate end-to-end training due to the non-differentiable nature of symbolic components.
2. **Teaching LLMs to perform search internally:** This approach aims to train LLMs to verbalize and execute search trajectories themselves (e.g., Stream-of-Search by Gandhi et al., Searchformer by Lehnert et al.). This leads to more self-contained, unified models that can potentially discover novel search strategies. However, these methods are computationally intensive, especially for long plans or large action spaces, as Transformers exhibit quadratic complexity with sequence length, leading to high token costs and potential infeasibility for complex tasks.

The "SYSTEM-1.x" paper identifies a critical gap: neither isolated System-1 nor System-2 approaches offer a flexible, controllable balance between speed and accuracy. Pure System-1 is fast but often inaccurate, while pure System-2, though accurate, is computationally expensive and rigid in its resource allocation. The authors draw inspiration from Daniel Kahneman's Dual-Process Theory, which posits that human cognition flexibly switches between fast, intuitive (System-1) and slow, deliberate (System-2) thinking based on task demands and available resources.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by introducing a *hybrid and controllable* planning framework that dynamically allocates computational effort. Instead of forcing an LLM into a single mode, System-1.x learns to discern problem difficulty and invoke the appropriate planning mechanism (fast System-1 or slow System-2) for different sub-problems. This adaptive strategy represents a significant step towards more efficient and robust LLM-based planning, offering a nuanced middle ground that maximizes performance within given computational budgets, a crucial consideration for real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the "SYSTEM-1.x Planner" is to develop a framework for controllable long-horizon planning with language models that intelligently balances between fast, intuitive "System-1" planning and slow, deliberate "System-2" planning. This balance is dynamically adjusted based on the perceived difficulty of the problem or sub-problem at hand, as well as explicit user-specified constraints.

The motivation behind this ambitious objective stems from several critical limitations observed in current LLM-based planning paradigms:

- **Inherent Trade-off between Speed and Accuracy:** Existing LLM planners tend to operate in two distinct, isolated modes. "System-1" style planners (direct generation) are computationally fast but often lack the accuracy and robustness required for complex, long-horizon tasks, frequently failing on out-of-distribution problems. Conversely, "System-2" style planners (those involving explicit search or external tools) achieve higher accuracy but incur significant computational costs, often rendering them infeasible for problems with large state/action spaces or long plan lengths due to high token generation and quadratic complexity. There is a need for a mechanism that can leverage the strengths of both.
- **Lack of Controllability and User-Guidance:** Neither pure System-1 nor pure System-2 planning offers a systematic way for users to control the model's behavior based on external constraints, such as a budget for tokens or computation time. Current System-2 methods might allow for *post-hoc* truncation of search, but this is often ad-hoc and can lead to arbitrary performance drops. The authors aim to provide explicit, systematic control over the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.
- **Inefficient Resource Allocation:** Applying a computationally expensive System-2 approach to every planning problem, regardless of its difficulty, leads to wasted resources. Many sub-problems within a larger planning task might be "easy" and solvable efficiently by a System-1 approach. A smarter allocation of compute, reserving the more expensive System-2 for genuinely "hard" parts, could significantly improve overall efficiency.
- **Enhancing LLMs' Intrinsic Planning Capabilities:** While augmenting LLMs with external symbolic planners (neuro-symbolic approaches) can be effective, the authors also aim to improve LLMs' *intrinsic* ability to perform search and planning by training them directly on search traces. This could lead to more self-contained models that learn more efficient search strategies, though they also propose a neuro-symbolic variant to leverage existing symbolic tools when available.

In essence, the motivation is to create an "intelligent" planner that is not only effective but also efficient and adaptable, much like human cognitive processes that fluidly combine quick intuitions with deliberate reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The System-1.x Planner is a sophisticated framework designed to dynamically balance between fast, intuitive System-1 planning and slow, deliberate System-2 planning. It achieves this balance through the coordinated action of three distinct, yet interconnected, components, all fine-tuned on a single base Large Language Model (LLM). The core innovation lies in its ability to decompose problems and intelligently allocate computational resources based on perceived difficulty and a user-defined "hybridization factor."

**Components of System-1.x:**
1. **Controller:** This component acts as the orchestrator. Its primary roles are:
 - **Problem Decomposition:** Breaking down a long-horizon planning problem into a sequence of smaller, manageable "sub-goals."
 - **Difficulty Classification:** Classifying each sub-goal as either "easy" or "hard."
2. **System-1 Planner:** This component is responsible for solving "easy" sub-goals. It operates in a fast, direct generation mode, producing a plan (sequence of actions) without explicit search or exploration.
3. **System-2 Planner:** This component is invoked for "hard" sub-goals. It performs explicit search over possible actions, verbalizing search trajectories (explored states, validity, etc.) before generating a plan. This process is more computationally intensive but typically leads to higher accuracy.

**Training Data Generation (Key Aspect):**
A critical part of the System-1.x methodology is the automatic generation of training data for each of its components, specifically from gold search traces. This data generation process also incorporates the user's desired "hybridization factor" (`x`).

- **Supervision Source:** The framework assumes access to a training dataset `D` of gold search trajectories (e.g., generated by a symbolic planner like A*). These trajectories include the optimal plan and all intermediate steps/states explored during the search.
- **System-1 Planner Training Data (`D_Sys1`):** Created by extracting only the final plan (a sequence of actions) from the gold search trajectories. The LLM is trained to map (start state, goal state) to this plan.
- **System-2 Planner Training Data (`D_Sys2`):** Created by verbalizing the full search trajectory (including explored valid/invalid states, actions, transitions) for each problem. The LLM is trained to map (start state, goal state) to this detailed verbalized trace. For Blocksworld, due to large state spaces, the number of verbalized explored states is capped.
- **Controller Training Data (`D_c` - The Core Innovation):** This is where the hybridization factor `x` and a "hardness function" come into play, enabling controllable compute allocation.
 1. **Hardness Function:** A domain-specific function `h(s_i, s_j)` is defined to estimate the difficulty of reaching `s_j` from `s_i`. For Maze Navigation, this is the number of obstacles in the sub-maze between `s_i` and `s_j`. For Blocksworld, it's a metric of block mismatches.
 2. **Ranking and Initial Annotation:** All training problems are ranked by their overall hardness `h(s_0, s_g)`. The first `(1 - x) * N` (easiest) samples are entirely labeled as "easy" and designated for System-1 only, meaning the controller for these instances will simply output a single System-1 sub-goal covering the entire problem.
 3. **Hybrid Annotation for Hard Instances:** For the remaining `x * N` (hardest) samples, a sophisticated *sliding window approach* is used to decompose the gold plan into sub-goals. The goal is to find an intermediate segment of the plan (from `s_j` to `s_k`) that represents the *hardest* sub-goal, and is of length proportional to `x` (i.e., `x * n` for an `n`-length plan). This segment is designated for System-2. The two flanking segments (`s_0` to `s_j` and `s_k` to `s_g`) are chosen to be the "easiest" possible sub-goals and are designated for System-1. This results in a three-way decomposition (or two-way if `j=0` or `k=g`). This process ensures that System-2 is only invoked where it's most needed, saving compute.

**Training and Inference:**
A single base LLM (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 with LoRA adapters) is fine-tuned to act as all three components. During inference, the Controller first generates a "meta-plan" (a sequence of sub-goals and their System-1/System-2 assignments). Then, the appropriate planner is invoked for each sub-goal, and their respective sub-plans are concatenated to form the final overall plan.

**Controllability:**
- **Train-time:** The hybridization factor `x` (e.g., System-1.5 for `x=0.5`, System-1.75 for `x=0.75`) directly influences how much of the training data is allocated to "hard" problems/sub-goals, thus controlling the inherent System-1/System-2 bias in the trained model.
- **Test-time:** An already trained controller can be further biased (e.g., by adjusting confidence thresholds) to invoke System-2 more or less frequently, allowing for dynamic compute allocation post-training.

**Neuro-Symbolic Variant:** The framework is flexible enough to replace the neural System-2 Planner with an external symbolic solver (like A*), allowing it to leverage powerful traditional planning tools when available.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments on two diverse classical planning tasks: **Maze Navigation** (in-distribution for training data, but challenging for LLMs) and **Blocksworld** (primarily for testing out-of-distribution generalization to longer plan lengths). Performance was evaluated using two key metrics: **Plan Validity** (fraction of correctly solved problems) and **#States-Explored** (computational cost). Methods were compared at matched `States-Explored` budgets, using truncation for lower budgets and test-time control for higher budgets.

**Key Findings:**

1. **System-1.x Outperforms Baselines (System-1, System-2, System-1.x w/o sub-goals):**
 - **Maze Navigation:** The System-1.x Planner (with sub-goals and `x=0.5`) consistently delivered the best performance across all tested computational budgets. At its default average of 13.6 states explored, System-1.x achieved 70.4% valid plans, significantly outperforming System-1 (48.7% at 3.1 states) and a pure System-2 Planner (37.2% at 13.6 states when truncated, or 93.7% at 24.4 states for full System-2). This demonstrates System-1.x's effective resource allocation.
 - **Blocksworld (Out-of-Distribution):** System-1.x (with sub-goals) showed substantial advantages at lower budgets for OOD generalization. For instance, at 30 states, System-1.x achieved 25% validity, whereas System-2 (truncated) and System-1.x without sub-goal decomposition dropped to near 0%. This highlights the crucial role of the controller in decomposing complex, OOD problems into simpler, potentially in-distribution sub-goals that the System-1 component can handle. At maximum budgets, System-1.x performance was comparable to System-2.
 - **Utility of Sub-goal Decomposition:** Experiments explicitly showed that System-1.x with sub-goal decomposition consistently outperformed its variant without decomposition. This confirms that breaking down problems into smaller, manageable parts and assigning them appropriately is a key driver of the framework's success. Even System-2 benefits from this decomposition.

2. **Neuro-Symbolic System-1.x Performance:**
 - When the neural System-2 component was replaced with a symbolic A\* solver, the neuro-symbolic System-1.x variant still outperformed a pure A\* search at lower state exploration budgets (e.g., 70.5% validity for System-1.x vs. 31.0% for A\* at ~11.6 states). This further underscores the efficiency gains from adaptive System-1 utilization. At maximum budget, System-1.x achieved near-perfect validity (99.2%), matching A\*.

3. **Controllability (Train-time `x`):**
 - Training a System-1.75 Planner (higher `x`, more System-2 bias) demonstrated the framework's controllability. System-1.75 achieved 75.7% valid plans with an average of 16.6 states, a 5% gain in validity compared to System-1.5 (70.4% at 13.6 states), showcasing a clear trade-off between efficiency and accuracy that the user can control. The model converges towards full System-2 performance with increasing `x`.

4. **Generalizability Across Search Algorithms:**
 - System-1.x proved robust to the choice of underlying search algorithm used for generating training traces (A\*, BFS, DFS). In all cases, System-1.x significantly outperformed its System-2 counterparts. The learned System-1.x planners also exhibited exploration behaviors that closely resembled their training algorithm (e.g., BFS-trained System-1.x yielded more optimal plans than DFS-trained, at the cost of more states explored, mirroring the symbolic algorithms).

5. **Ablations and Controller Analysis:**
 - The *sliding window decomposition* strategy significantly outperformed alternatives without it, highlighting its effectiveness in identifying hard sub-goals for System-2.
 - Different *hardness functions* (e.g., number of obstacles vs. Manhattan distance for mazes) yielded comparable strong results, suggesting robustness to the specific choice of heuristic, though `num_obstacles` was slightly more efficient.
 - Comparison with a random controller showed that the trained controller (using the hardness function and `x`) significantly outperformed a random baseline, validating its effectiveness in classifying sub-goals.

In summary, the results demonstrate that System-1.x provides a highly effective, efficient, and controllable solution for LLM-based planning, surpassing previous approaches by intelligently allocating computational resources and adapting to problem complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The System-1.x Planner introduces a paradigm shift in how Large Language Models approach long-horizon planning, offering a compelling blend of efficiency, accuracy, and user control. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

1. **Bridging the Efficiency-Accuracy Divide:** The most significant contribution of System-1.x is its ability to intelligently balance the computational cost of deliberate "System-2" search with the speed of intuitive "System-1" generation. This addresses a critical limitation of previous LLM-based planners, which either sacrificed accuracy for speed (pure System-1) or incurred prohibitive costs for complex problems (pure System-2). By adaptively allocating resources, System-1.x provides a practical solution for real-world planning tasks where both performance and computational budgets are crucial.

2. **Enhanced Controllability for Users:** System-1.x empowers end-users with unprecedented control over the planning process. The "hybridization factor `x`" allows users to define the desired trade-off between speed and accuracy at the training stage, tailoring the model to specific application requirements. Furthermore, test-time control offers flexibility to dynamically adjust compute allocation, providing a systematic way to adapt to varying resource constraints or problem urgencies, unlike ad-hoc truncation methods.

3. **Robustness and Generalizability:** The demonstrated robustness of System-1.x to different underlying search algorithms (A\*, BFS, DFS) for training data is a strong indicator of its broad applicability. This means the framework is not tightly coupled to a single type of search, making it adaptable to diverse planning domains where different search strategies might be optimal or available. Its superior performance on out-of-distribution Blocksworld problems highlights its ability to generalize, particularly by decomposing larger, unfamiliar problems into smaller, more familiar sub-goals.

4. **Advancing LLM Reasoning Capabilities:** Beyond simply using LLMs as "tool users," System-1.x contributes to teaching LLMs *how to search and reason* more intrinsically. By training on verbalized search traces and problem decompositions, the LLM learns a more nuanced understanding of planning dynamics, including identifying difficult sub-problems and allocating appropriate cognitive effort. This moves LLMs closer to exhibiting more human-like, adaptive intelligence.

5. **Flexibility for Neuro-Symbolic Integration:** While primarily a neural planner, the framework's seamless integration with symbolic solvers (as demonstrated by the neuro-symbolic System-1.x variant that outperformed A\* at various budgets) is a significant advantage. This allows developers to leverage existing, highly optimized symbolic planning tools where available, combining their guarantees with the generalization and flexibility of LLMs.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Robotics and Autonomous Systems:** In resource-constrained environments or time-sensitive operations, the ability to balance fast reactive planning with deliberate, accurate planning is invaluable. System-1.x could enable robots to quickly navigate simple environments (System-1) while thoughtfully planning complex maneuvers around obstacles (System-2) only when necessary.
- **Complex Decision-Making and Scheduling:** For domains like logistics, manufacturing, or project management, System-1.x could optimize resource allocation by quickly solving routine sub-tasks and dedicating intensive search to critical, bottleneck-inducing parts of a plan.
- **Human-AI Collaboration:** By offering controllable trade-offs, System-1.x could lead to AI systems that are more understandable and align better with human expectations or computational limits, fostering more effective collaboration.
- **Foundation for Future AI:** This work lays a strong foundation for future research into more sophisticated, adaptive, and resource-aware AI systems. It opens avenues for exploring dynamic difficulty assessment, more complex sub-goal decomposition strategies, and self-improving planning agents that learn optimal resource allocation over time.

In conclusion, "SYSTEM-1.x" represents a significant step forward in making LLM-based planning not just powerful, but also practical and efficient. By intelligently mimicking the "fast and slow" thinking of human cognition, it paves the way for LLMs to tackle real-world planning challenges with greater robustness and controllability.
