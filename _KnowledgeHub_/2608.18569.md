# The Role of Grid Cells in Reducing Spatial Aliasing in Hippocampal Place Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> Adds analytically-constructed grid cell modules to a hippocampal place-cell model driven by boundary vector cells (BVCs), to fix spatial aliasing where symmetric or repeated geometry makes BVC-only place fields indistinguishable across locations. Grid cell phase signals, combined with BVC input via a weighted Hebbian (Oja) place-cell layer, cut Mean Spatial Aliasing Index by 94-99% across three simulated environments.

## Problem

- BVC-only place representations rely solely on boundary geometry, so symmetric or repetitive environmental structures produce near-identical sensory patterns at multiple distinct locations.
- Prior hippocampal models attribute place-field formation mainly to boundary-vector input, leaving no internally generated signal to disambiguate perceptually identical regions.
- Existing aliasing mitigation (e.g. 3D LiDAR structure) is environment-specific and not a general, universally applicable solution to spatial aliasing.

## Method

- ==Analytical Grid Cell Construction==: hexagonal firing fields built directly from three 60-degree-offset cosine gratings per module, with power-law sharpening, min-max normalization, and soft thresholding for sparsity.
- ==Obstacle-Aware Masking==: blob-detection procedure that fragments grid activations at obstacles covering over 20% of a blob's diameter, then Gaussian-smooths edges for biologically plausible boundary fragmentation.
- ==Weighted BVC-GC Place Cell Layer==: leaky-integrator place cells combine BVC and grid cell afferents via weighting parameter eta, trained through competitive ==Oja's-rule Hebbian learning== with global inhibition.

## Results

- Mean Spatial Aliasing Index dropped **94.73%** in the Open environment, **99.29%** in the Cross environment, and **96.65%** in the Maze environment when grid cells were added versus BVC-only baseline.
- The Cross environment, with four rotationally symmetric quadrants, showed the largest absolute aliasing reduction, MSAI falling from **42.4e-4 to 0.3e-4** across five trials.
- Maze environment retained higher and more variable MSAI (**1.3 plus/minus 2.2e-4**) than Open or Cross even with grid cells, attributed to repeated corridor geometry.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Internally generated periodic signals (grid cells) can disambiguate locations that look identical to boundary-based sensing alone, a general principle for any perception system facing symmetric environments.
> - The degree of environmental visual symmetry predicts how much a secondary, geometry-independent signal source helps, greatest gains appear where baseline aliasing is worst.
> - Repetitive local structure (like maze corridors) can still cause residual aliasing even with a path-integration-like signal, since periodicity itself can alias against periodic terrain.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of spatial cognition aims to develop biologically-inspired computational models that replicate the navigational capabilities observed in animals, frequently drawing inspiration from the mammalian hippocampal formation. Within this framework, place cells in the hippocampus are understood to generate spatially localized representations of an environment, often influenced by boundary vector cells (BVCs) that encode information about distances and directions to environmental boundaries. However, a known challenge in models relying primarily on BVC inputs is spatial aliasing. This phenomenon occurs when geometrically similar or repetitive environmental structures lead to identical or highly similar place cell representations for distinct physical locations. While some spatial aliasing has been observed in biological systems, robust spatial discrimination is generally maintained.

Grid cells, located in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC), provide a distinct form of spatial information through their periodic, hexagonally patterned firing fields. These cells are understood to contribute to path integration and vector navigation, offering an internally generated spatial metric that is largely independent of external landmarks or environmental geometry. Prior research has explored various computational models for grid cell generation, ranging from dynamical systems like oscillatory interference models and continuous attractor networks to learning-based deep neural networks. The integration of grid cell signals with place cell activity has been proposed to enhance the accuracy and robustness of spatial representations, particularly in large environments or during path integration.

This work specifically addresses the issue of spatial aliasing by proposing that grid cells provide a critical mechanism for disambiguating perceptually identical locations that might otherwise confuse BVC-driven place representations. Previous attempts to mitigate spatial aliasing have included incorporating three-dimensional environmental structure. This paper positions itself by presenting grid cells as a more universally applicable solution to this specific problem, focusing on their functional property of introducing independent, periodic spatial signals to enrich and de-alias place cell activity, rather than exploring their precise biological origins or their role in other navigational tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate and demonstrate the capacity of grid cell signals to mitigate spatial aliasing in hippocampal place representations. This mitigation was specifically examined in computational models where place cell activity is largely influenced by boundary vector cell (BVC) inputs.

The motivation for this work stems from a recognized limitation in current biologically-inspired spatial cognition models: spatial aliasing. When place representations are constructed primarily from BVC inputs, environments featuring symmetries or repetitive structures can cause multiple distinct spatial locations to generate nearly identical sensory patterns. This leads to the activation of the same place cells at different locations, resulting in an ambiguous and unreliable spatial map. For example, a square room with four identical walls could activate the same "corner" place cell for each of the four corners, creating a lack of distinct positional encoding. While the brain's hippocampus is generally capable of disambiguating locations, even in perceptually similar environments, models often struggle with this.

The researchers hypothesized that grid cells could offer a solution due to their unique properties. Grid cells generate intrinsic, periodic spatial firing patterns that cover an environment with a hexagonal lattice. Crucially, these patterns are internally generated and vary independently of external environmental geometry. This independence implies that even if two locations appear identical from a boundary perspective (as sensed by BVCs), the overlaying grid cell pattern would likely be different, providing a unique "spatial signature" for each location. By integrating these unique, internally generated spatial signals from grid cells with the boundary-based information from BVCs, the model could potentially disambiguate locations that would otherwise be aliased. Therefore, the key objective was to quantitatively demonstrate that the complementary information provided by grid cells could lead to a substantial reduction in spatial aliasing, thereby creating more robust and distinct place representations. The approach focused on the *functional utility* of grid cells in providing a consistent metric spatial coordinate system rather than replicating their exact biological generation mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a computational model of spatial representation comprising four interconnected neural layers: Head Direction Cells (HDCs), Boundary Vector Cells (BVCs), Grid Cells (GCs), and Place Cells (PCs). The model was implemented and evaluated within the Webots R2025a simulation environment, using an agent equipped with a compass for head direction and a 720-beam rangefinder for boundary detection.

**Model Architecture:**

- **Head Direction Cell (HDC) Layer:** Consisted of 8 cells, each tuned to a specific allocentric direction (0°, 45°..., 315°). Their firing rate was modeled using a cosine tuning function, activating maximally when the agent's global heading matched the cell's preferred direction.
- **Boundary Vector Cell (BVC) Layer:** Comprised 400 cells that encoded the agent's spatial relationship to environmental boundaries. Each BVC was characterized by a preferred distance (d_i) and allocentric bearing (ϕ_i) to a boundary. BVC firing rates were calculated by integrating sensory input from the LiDAR system using combined radial and angular Gaussian tuning functions. Preferred displacements were uniformly distributed to cover the boundary coding space.
- **Grid Cell (GC) Layer:** This layer constituted the primary novel addition to address spatial aliasing. Instead of simulating underlying neural mechanisms, an analytical approach was used to construct grid cell firing patterns.
 - **Analytical Construction:** Grid patterns were generated as the mean of three cosine gratings oriented 60° apart, operating on a position vector rotated into the grid cell's reference frame and translated by its phase offset.
 - **Post-processing:** To achieve sharp firing fields, three steps were applied: a power-law transformation for sharpness (using β ∈ [1.2, 1.8]), per-cell min-max normalization, and soft thresholding for sparsity.
 - **Obstacle-Aware Masking:** To ensure biological plausibility (i.e., grid fields not extending across walls), a masking procedure was applied. This involved identifying connected activation components, determining those intersecting obstacles, and applying masks to split or suppress activations. For split components, only the largest was retained. Dilated obstacle boundaries were used to force fragmentation where necessary, followed by Gaussian smoothing to create curved field edges.
 - **Modules:** The GC layer was organized into 8 modules, each aligned with an HDC orientation (α_m ∈ {0°, 45°..., 315°}). Each module contained 50 cells, sharing a common spatial scale (λ = 5.5) but differing in spatial phase offset. This resulted in a total of 400 grid cells.
- **Place Cell (PC) Layer:** Composed of 1000 cells, this layer formed the core spatial representation. Place cells received weighted input from both BVCs and GCs, with a weighting parameter η ∈ controlling the relative contribution (BVC input weighted by (1 − η), GC input by η).
 - **Membrane Dynamics:** The membrane potential of a place cell evolved as a leaky integrator, incorporating excitatory input from BVCs and GCs, along with global inhibition (afferent from BVCs/GCs and recurrent from PCs).
 - **Activation:** Firing rates were derived through rectification and saturation using a hyperbolic tangent function, promoting sparse and localized place fields.
 - **Self-Organization:** Place fields emerged through competitive learning during exploration, governed by Oja's rule. Synaptic weights from BVCs and GCs to PCs were initialized sparsely and updated based on Hebbian learning (co-activity) and a normalization term that enforced competition and weight decay.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Environments:** Experiments were conducted in three 20m x 20m environments: an "Open" environment (no obstacles), a "Cross" environment (central cross-shaped obstacle creating high visual symmetry), and a "Maze" environment (complex wall structure).
- **Trials:** Five independent trials were conducted for each environment with grid cells (η = 0.35) and five trials without grid cells (η = 0). This totaled 30 trials.
- **Data Collection:** During a training phase, the agent explored until 95% of the environment (0.5m bins) was covered. An evaluation phase followed, where the agent achieved 99% coverage (0.2m bins).
- **Parameters:** Specific values were defined for BVC tuning (radial width σ_r = 1.0m, angular width σ_θ = 3.0°), GC spatial scale (λ = 5.5), and PC learning and inhibition parameters (e.g., normalization factors α_pb, α_pg = √0.4; recurrent inhibition gain Γ_pp = 0.70; learning time constant τ_w = 10 timesteps).

**Metrics for Spatial Aliasing:**

Spatial aliasing was quantified using two metrics:

- **Spatial Aliasing Index (SAI):** For each spatial bin (i), SAI_i measured the average cosine similarity between its place cell activation vector (a^(i)) and those of other spatially distant bins (a^(j)), excluding nearby bins (distance threshold d_th). A higher SAI_i indicates greater aliasing for that location.
- **Mean Spatial Aliasing Index (MSAI):** This was the average of the SAI across all spatial bins in the environment. A higher MSAI value signified more overall aliasing across the environment, while lower values indicated improved spatial discrimination.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated a consistent and substantial reduction in spatial aliasing when grid cell signals were incorporated into the place cell model, across all tested environments.

**Quantitative Results (Mean Spatial Aliasing Index - MSAI):**
The MSAI values (mean ± standard deviation over five trials), reported as ×10⁻⁴, are summarized in the table below:

| Environment | With Grid Cells (GC) | Without Grid Cells (No GC) | Percentage Improvement |
| :---------- | :------------------- | :------------------------- | :--------------------- |
| Open | 0.3 ± 0.1 | 5.7 ± 0.3 | 94.73% |
| Cross | 0.3 ± 0.1 | 42.4 ± 1.0 | 99.29% |
| Maze | 1.3 ± 2.2 | 38.9 ± 2.7 | 96.65% |

- **Open Environment:** With grid cells, the MSAI was 0.3, compared to 5.7 without grid cells, indicating a 94.73% reduction in spatial aliasing. This environment exhibited a moderate baseline aliasing without grid cells due to fewer repeated boundary configurations.
- **Cross Environment:** This environment showed the most significant reduction in aliasing. The MSAI with grid cells was 0.3, drastically lower than the 42.4 observed without grid cells, representing a 99.29% improvement. The high rotational symmetry of the Cross environment, which divides the map into four perceptually similar quadrants, caused severe aliasing in the BVC-only model. The introduction of grid cells effectively disambiguated these symmetric regions.
- **Maze Environment:** In the maze, the MSAI was 1.3 with grid cells, versus 38.9 without, leading to a 96.65% reduction in aliasing. While substantial, this improvement was slightly more variable than in the Cross environment. The presence of corridors introduces repeated spatial patterns that, even with grid cell input, can cause distant locations to retain some degree of similarity in their place-cell representations.

**Qualitative Results (Spatial Aliasing Index Heatmaps):**
Visualizations of the Spatial Aliasing Index (SAI) across the environments provided qualitative support for these findings. Heatmaps generated from single trials consistently showed lower SAI values (represented by cooler colors on a logarithmic scale) across most spatial bins when grid cell input was included. The most pronounced visual reduction in aliasing was evident in the Cross environment, where the high aliasing observed in the BVC-only condition was largely mitigated. These heatmaps localized where aliasing was most pronounced without grid cells and demonstrated the extent of its reduction with grid cell input.

**Discussion of Environmental Factors:**
The results underscore that environments with higher degrees of visual symmetry or repetitive geometric structures (such as the Cross environment) are particularly susceptible to spatial aliasing in models based purely on boundary information. In such scenarios, grid cells, by providing an internally generated, periodic spatial code that is largely independent of external geometry, offer critical complementary information. This additional information allows the place cell network to form more distinct and reliable representations, even when sensory inputs from boundaries are ambiguous across different locations. The mechanism appears to be that grid cells introduce a unique "phase" signature to each location, enabling differentiation even if boundary inputs are identical.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates a significant functional role for grid cells in mitigating a critical limitation of biologically-inspired spatial representation models: spatial aliasing. The findings indicate that grid cells provide information that is complementary to boundary-based inputs, leading to more reliable and distinct place representations, particularly in environments characterized by geometric ambiguity, symmetry, or repetitive structures.

**Theoretical Significance:**
The study contributes to the theoretical understanding of how the brain's spatial navigation system achieves robust and unambiguous spatial mapping. It explicitly highlights that while boundary vector cells offer crucial information about environmental geometry, they are inherently susceptible to aliasing in symmetrical or repetitive spaces. By demonstrating the efficacy of grid cells in resolving this issue, the work supports the hypothesis that grid cells provide a fundamental "metric" or "disambiguation" signal that underpins the hippocampus's ability to localize an animal's position reliably. This extends the understanding of grid cell function beyond their roles in path integration or vector navigation to include their contribution to the specificity and uniqueness of place cell firing.

**Practical and Applied Impact:**
The insights derived from this model have several potential practical implications:

- **Robust Robotic Navigation:** The demonstrated ability of grid cells to reduce spatial aliasing could inform the development of more robust and accurate autonomous navigation systems for robots or other artificial agents. In complex or man-made environments often characterized by repeating patterns, symmetries, and structural similarities (e.g., corridors, identical rooms in a building), traditional boundary-based localization systems can struggle with ambiguity. Incorporating grid-cell-like periodic internal spatial signals could enhance the agent's ability to maintain a unique and unambiguous internal map of its location.
- **Artificial Intelligence for Spatial Cognition:** The work contributes to the broader field of artificial intelligence, particularly in spatial cognition. By providing a biologically plausible mechanism for disambiguating locations, it could inspire novel architectures for neural networks designed to solve complex spatial reasoning tasks in challenging environments.
- **Understanding Neurological Disorders:** While not directly addressed, a better understanding of how these neural systems work together to form coherent spatial maps could indirectly inform research into neurological conditions that affect spatial memory and navigation.

**Future Directions:**
The authors propose future work that includes investigating attractor-based grid cell models, which offer higher biological plausibility, to assess potential trade-offs in aliasing mitigation and stability. Furthermore, extending the model to incorporate 3D attractor-based grid cells and other 3D-aware cell types (BVCs, place cells, head direction cells) and testing them in varied, real-world 3D environments is suggested. Such extensions aim to address challenges related to scalable and biologically grounded spatial representations, moving towards greater robustness in more realistic navigation settings.
