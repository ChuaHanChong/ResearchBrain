# Towards Standardized Disturbance Rejection Testing of Legged Robot Locomotion with Linear Impactor: A Preliminary Study, Observations, and Implications

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers propose and evaluate a pneumatic linear impactor as a standardized tool for testing legged robot locomotion against external disturbances. Their preliminary study on a humanoid robot demonstrates the impactor's repeatability and reveals that a robot's response to impact is not always monotonically related to the severity of the disturbance, with specific controllers tolerating up to 26.376 kg·m/s of impact momentum.

## Problem

- The absence of formal, repeatable, unbiased testing standards impedes objective comparison and certification of legged robot disturbance rejection, acting as a major bottleneck for widespread industrial and commercial deployment.
- Current evaluation methods rely on informal, subjective techniques (e.g., hand-pushing) or simulation tests that often apply unrealistic or unfairly characterized external forces.
- Existing physical infrastructure and equipment for standardized testing have been largely overlooked in the robotics field.

## Method

- Proposes a ==linear impactor==, a device well-established in other testing disciplines, as suitable equipment for adaptive, repeatable, and fair ==disturbance rejection testing== in legged robots.
- Conducts a preliminary case study using a ==pneumatic linear impactor== on a **Digit** humanoid robot, evaluating three different state-of-the-art locomotion controllers during walking-in-place against frontal impacts.
- Characterizes external disturbances by ==impact momentum== rather than impact force, arguing that momentum provides a fairer, robot-independent metric for the testing action.

## Results

- The pneumatic impactor exhibited high repeatability, with a measurement error of **less than 0.1 m/s** in impact velocity within the tested range.
- The Template-Model controller demonstrated the highest disturbance rejection capability, successfully withstanding impacts up to **26.376 kg·m/s**, which is comparable to the effective momentum of an Olympic boxer's punch.
- Real-world tests revealed anti-intuitive instances where robots recovered from higher impact velocities but failed at slightly lower or similar impact velocities, confirming observations previously mostly confined to simulations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The linear impactor effectively generates repeatable and controlled external disturbances, demonstrating its potential as a standardized testing apparatus.
> - Impact duration is influenced by the robot's dynamic response, making impact momentum a more appropriate and fair metric than impact force for characterizing external disturbances in legged robot testing.
> - Robot success or failure against external impacts does not always correlate monotonically with impact severity, suggesting complex interactions between the impact, the robot's internal state, and its control strategy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has seen substantial progress in developing dynamic locomotion capabilities, enabling robots to achieve stable and adaptive gaits that can withstand external disturbances. Researchers have explored diverse approaches, including model-based control strategies, machine learning techniques, and hybrid methods, often demonstrating their effectiveness in real-world scenarios. Despite these advancements, a significant impediment to the widespread industrial and commercial deployment of legged robots is the absence of formal and unbiased performance testing standards and certifications.

Historically, the evaluation of disturbance rejection in legged robots has relied on informal, non-rigorous, and often non-repeatable methods such as hand-pushing, foot-kicking, rope-dragging, stick-poking, and ball-swinging. While these methods offer intuitive demonstrations, they lack the consistency, standardization, and rigorous flexibility required for objective performance assessment. Similarly, simulation-based tests, though offering controlled environments, frequently apply external forces in unrealistic manners, assuming "ideal" patterns or static force directions that do not account for the robot's dynamic response during contact. These limitations prevent a clear determination of whether a robot "works well" or "well enough" for practical applications.

This research addresses a critical and often overlooked aspect of scaling up legged robot deployment: the development of standardized testing equipment and infrastructure. It explicitly bridges a gap by proposing a well-established tool from other standardized testing disciplines—the linear impactor—as a suitable apparatus for adaptive, repeatable, and fair disturbance rejection testing in legged robots. By focusing on the physical equipment rather than solely on theoretical or algorithmic advancements, this work contributes to the foundational requirements for developing robust performance standards, moving the field closer to industrial applicability and commercialization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose and investigate the use of a linear impactor as a standardized piece of equipment for testing the disturbance rejection capabilities of legged robots during locomotion. This overarching goal is motivated by several critical needs within the legged robotics community:

**Motivation for Standardization:**
1. **Lack of Formal Testing Standards:** Current methods for evaluating legged robot performance against external disturbances are largely informal, relying on subjective and inconsistent techniques (e.g., hand-pushing, kicking, ball-swinging). This lack of standardization makes it difficult to objectively compare different robot designs or control algorithms, assess their true capabilities, or certify them for industrial or commercial use. Without clear metrics and repeatable test procedures, determining if a robot is "safe enough" or "robust enough" remains largely subjective.
2. **Obstruction to Commercialization:** The absence of standardized performance testing is identified as a major bottleneck preventing the scalable deployment of legged robots alongside humans in various environments. Commercialization and widespread adoption necessitate verifiable performance and safety guarantees, which current informal testing methodologies cannot provide.
3. **Limitations of Existing Testing Approaches:**
 - **Informal Physical Tests:** Methods like hand-pushing or stick-poking are non-rigorous, non-repeatable, and potentially unfair because the magnitude, duration, and direction of the disturbance cannot be precisely controlled or reproduced.
 - **Simulation-Oriented Tests:** While offering control, these often apply external forces unrealistically (e.g., static force direction regardless of robot movement, "ideal" force patterns) or require accurate control of impact time and duration that is not feasible in real-world interactions with dynamic robots, leading to potentially unfair assessments.
4. **Overlooked Aspect of Physical Infrastructure:** The paper highlights that while theoretical and algorithmic aspects of legged robot control have received significant attention, the physical infrastructure and equipment necessary for standardized testing have been largely overlooked and understudied. Addressing this physical component is deemed crucial for progress.

**Key Objectives of the Research:**
1. **Propose the Linear Impactor:** Introduce the linear impactor, an established tool in other engineering and biomechanics disciplines, as a suitable candidate for standardized, adaptive, repeatable, and fair disturbance rejection testing of legged robots.
2. **Conduct a Preliminary Case Study:** Utilize a pneumatic linear impactor to perform real-world, in-lab experiments on a humanoid robot (Digit) equipped with state-of-the-art locomotion controllers. This case study aims to assess the impactor's practical utility, gather preliminary performance data, and uncover unique insights into robot-impactor interactions.
3. **Identify and Analyze Novel Observations:** Document anti-intuitive observations and implications revealed during real-world testing that might challenge conventional assumptions about disturbance rejection in legged robots, particularly concerning the relationship between impact severity and outcome.
4. **Establish a Basis for Future Standardization:** Lay the groundwork for developing objective performance tests that can enable robust benchmarking, characterization, and certification of legged robot locomotion capabilities against physical disturbances.

By addressing these motivations and objectives, the research aims to fill a critical void in the legged robotics field, facilitating a transition from experimental demonstrations to verifiable, industrially applicable performance standards.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This study proposes the linear impactor as a standardized tool for disturbance rejection testing in legged robots and validates its utility through a preliminary case study involving a humanoid robot.

**A. Problem Formulation and Desired Equipment Properties:**
The research frames the legged robot system as a discrete-time dynamic system where the robot's state evolution `s(t+1) = h(s(t), u(t); ω(t))` is influenced by internal control actions `u_i` and environmental factors/external disturbances `u_e`. The authors emphasize that in the context of testing, `u_e` (specifically physical impact) is controlled with precision, unlike typical stochastic system formulations. The internal control `u_i` is determined by the robot's locomotion policy `π(s(t))`. The testing equipment is responsible for generating `u_e` based on its state `x`, operator actions `a`, and internal uncertainties `ω_g`.

The paper specifies three critical properties for ideal testing equipment:
1. **Adaptive:** The equipment should be compatible with various robot systems and designs (`f ∈ F`).
2. **Repeatable:** The equipment should consistently generate the same `u_e` for given `x` and `a`, implying minimal internal uncertainty (`W_g = ∅`). Small changes in `a` should result in proportionally small changes in `u_e`.
3. **Fair:** The generated `u_e` must be strictly determined by the testing equipment, independent of the robot's internal dynamics or control policy.

**B. The Linear Impactor:**
A linear impactor generates impact by driving an object (the impactor) along a linear path to strike the target. The study utilizes a **pneumatic linear impactor**, which employs compressed air to propel the impactor. This type of impactor is well-established in other fields such as injury biomechanics and sports biomechanics.

**Advantages of Linear Impactor:**
- **Adaptivity:** The setup allows for testing in open spaces, accommodating extended robot motion. The impactor face is free to deflect and rebound naturally. Various ram shafts, components, and impactor faces can be configured to achieve desired impact energies and simulate different contact surfaces. It can also be mounted on mobile platforms for higher degrees of freedom.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Gravity-Dependent Systems:** Unlike pendulum or swing-ball tests, which are limited by gravity in terms of achievable momentum and speed, the linear impactor offers greater flexibility and relevance for large-scale or moving subjects.
- **Repeatability Demonstration:** The pneumatic impactor used in the study empirically demonstrates a linear relationship between the configured air pressure (operator control `a`) and the peak velocity achieved by the impactor (a key component of `u_e`). Within a velocity range of 1.5 m/s to 4.4 m/s, the error in repeatability was measured to be less than 0.1 m/s. The device is capable of higher velocities (~10 m/s), but these were beyond the scope of the current robot's capabilities.

**C. Case Study Configuration and Procedure:**
- **Subject Robot:** A single Digit humanoid robot was used for all tests.
- **Locomotion Controllers:** Three different controllers were tested:
 1. **Template-Model (TM) Controller:** Based on Task Space Inverse Dynamics (TSID) with a high-level step planner derived from Raibert's regulations.
 2. **Template-Model based Task Space Learning (TL) Policy:** A hierarchical framework combining a reinforcement learning-based high-level planner with a low-level TSID controller.
 3. **Agility Robotics (AR) Default Controller:** A commercial, proprietary controller supplied by the robot's manufacturer.
- **Testing Task:** Walking-in-place against frontal impacts.
- **Physical Setup:**
 - The linear pneumatic impactor was positioned with its face center 99.2 cm from the ground. The impactor weighed 6.4 kg and had a rectangular face of 15.24 cm × 10.16 cm.
 - To protect the robot (especially sensors), a 5.4 cm thick Polyethylene foam block was installed at the expected impact point on the robot, and an Ethafoam block was placed on the impactor face.
 - For safety against mechanical damage, the robot was loosely connected to an overhead sliding rail. This prevented actual physical fallovers but allowed for "fallover" judgment based on dynamic states.
- **Testing Procedure:**
 - The robot was controlled to perform walking-in-place at a fixed point near the impactor. Initialization methods varied slightly per controller due to their inherent capabilities (e.g., AR required continuous small speed signals).
 - Impacts were initiated by configuring the compressed air pressure for the pneumatic impactor, with the "impact action" defined as the maximum velocity achieved by the impactor just before contact (peak velocity).
 - Tests started at approximately 50 PSI and progressively increased to 95 PSI.
 - Testing for a given controller ceased if it failed (fell over, based on operator observation and post-analysis of dynamic states) in two consecutive trials. This resulted in 9 tests for TL, 13 for TM, and 14 for AR.
- **Data Collection:** Impactor velocity profiles, robot center of mass (CoM) positions and velocities, torso roll, pitch, and yaw angles and rates, and the robot's stepping phase at the moment of impact were recorded and analyzed.
- **Key Consideration:** The study emphasized characterizing the impact as *momentum* (mass × velocity) rather than *force*. This is because impact duration is influenced by the robot's dynamic response, making force an unfair metric as it is jointly determined by both the impactor and the robot. Momentum, as generated by the impactor, is considered a fairer representation of the external disturbance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The preliminary study conducted using the pneumatic linear impactor on the Digit humanoid robot yielded several key findings and observations regarding disturbance rejection testing.

1. **Demonstrated Repeatability of the Linear Impactor:** The pneumatic impactor exhibited a linear relationship between the configured air pressure (operator control `a`) and the peak velocity of the impactor. The empirical demonstration showed a measurement error of less than 0.1 m/s within the tested velocity range (1.5 m/s to 4.4 m/s). This directly supports the claim that the linear impactor can provide repeatable and controlled external disturbances, a fundamental requirement for standardized testing.

2. **Impact Characterization: Momentum vs. Force:** The study highlighted the necessity of characterizing the external disturbance as *impact momentum* rather than *impact force* to ensure fairness in testing. Observations of impactor velocity profiles revealed that the impact duration varied significantly depending on the locomotion controller being tested. For example, the impactor decelerated faster against the AR controller compared to the TM controller for similar impact velocities. This variation in impact duration is largely attributed to the robot's internal control design and its dynamic response, meaning that impact force (which depends on duration) is not solely determined by the testing equipment. Therefore, impact momentum, which reflects the energy generated by the impactor at the moment of contact, was identified as a fairer, robot-independent metric for the testing action.

3. **Controller Performance and Human Biomechanics Comparison:**
 - Among the three locomotion controllers tested (TM, TL, AR), the **TM controller demonstrated the highest disturbance rejection capability**, successfully withstanding an impact momentum of 26.376 kg·m/s.
 - Intriguingly, this sustained impact momentum was reported to be roughly on par with the average effective momentum from straight punches by Olympic boxers (26.506 kg·m/s). While acknowledging that detailed comparative conditions (e.g., impact face materials) were not rigorously controlled, this comparison introduces a novel perspective for evaluating robot performance against human biomechanical standards, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of "how safe is safe enough" for robots operating in human environments.

4. **Anti-intuitive Observation: Non-monotonic Relationship Between Impact Severity and Outcome:**
 - A significant and anti-intuitive observation was that a more severe impact (higher velocity/momentum) did not consistently translate into a worse outcome (fallover). The paper presents instances where robots with the TL and AR controllers recovered from higher impact velocities but failed at slightly lower or similar impact velocities.
 - Specifically, for the AR-equipped Digit, the robot fell over against frontal impacts at 3.89 m/s and 4.26 m/s, but recovered from a 4.23 m/s impact. This suggests that the robot's success or failure is not solely dependent on the magnitude of the impact but also on other complex factors such as the robot's internal state (e.g., stepping phase, balance configuration) at the precise moment of impact, its control strategy, and its real-time reactive capabilities.
 - This finding, previously observed mainly in simulation-based studies, was here confirmed in real-world testing. It underscores the complexity and "intelligent feedback mechanisms" inherent in legged robot locomotion control, posing significant challenges for designing fair and effective safety performance testing algorithms.

5. **Operational Insights from Real-World Testing:**
 - The study acknowledged the inherent complexities of conducting real-world tests, including the need for careful initialization, calibration, and human operator assistance to bring the robot to a desired starting state (e.g., maintaining walking-in-place, especially for the AR controller which otherwise stops).
 - Minor robot body drifts and torso shaking were observed even during "walking-in-place," indicating inherent uncertainties (`ω` in the problem formulation) that are part of the robot's nature and influence the precise impact conditions.
 - Safety measures, such as protective foam blocks and an overhead sliding rail, were deemed essential for preventing mechanical damage and ensuring the feasibility of repetitive testing.

In summary, the case study confirmed the practical utility and repeatability of the linear impactor for legged robot testing, provided insights into the appropriate characterization of impact disturbances, established a preliminary benchmark for disturbance rejection, and most notably, revealed complex, non-linear robot responses to impacts in real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the advancement and commercialization of legged robotics, addressing fundamental challenges in performance evaluation and safety assurance.

1. **Enabling Standardization for Commercialization:** The most substantial impact of this work is its direct contribution towards standardizing disturbance rejection testing for legged robots. By proposing the linear impactor as a robust, repeatable, and fair testing apparatus, the paper offers a concrete pathway to move beyond informal, subjective evaluation methods. This standardization is critical for establishing clear performance benchmarks, developing industry-wide certifications, and ultimately facilitating the safe and reliable deployment of legged robots in industrial, commercial, and human-centric environments. It addresses a key bottleneck hindering the scaling up of legged robot applications.

2. **Objective Benchmarking of Locomotion Controllers:** The proposed methodology provides a framework for objectively comparing the disturbance rejection capabilities of different locomotion controllers and robot designs. The ability to generate precisely controlled and repeatable impacts allows researchers and developers to quantitatively assess the robustness of their systems. This enables systematic improvements in robot control algorithms and mechanical designs, fostering innovation based on empirical, verifiable data. The preliminary benchmark established (e.g., TM controller withstanding 26.376 kg·m/s) provides a starting point for future comparisons.

3. **Deeper Understanding of Robot Dynamics and Control:** The anti-intuitive observation—that a more severe impact does not necessarily lead to a worse outcome—is highly significant. This finding, previously observed mainly in simulations, is now validated in real-world testing. It underscores the complex, non-linear interplay between external disturbances, the robot's internal state (e.g., stepping phase), and its intelligent feedback control mechanisms. This insight is crucial for developing more sophisticated and robust control strategies that can intelligently adapt to varied impact conditions, rather than simply scaling responses with impact magnitude. It also highlights the challenges in designing fair and comprehensive testing protocols that account for these intricate dynamics.

4. **Cross-Disciplinary Learning and Safety Standards:** By adopting a tool (linear impactor) and methodology well-established in fields like injury biomechanics and materials science, this research promotes cross-disciplinary knowledge transfer. The comparison of robot performance to human biomechanical metrics (e.g., Olympic boxer punch momentum) offers a novel perspective for defining acceptable safety thresholds for robot interaction with humans. This approach can inform the development of regulatory frameworks and safety guidelines for legged robots, similar to how standardized impact testing has shaped safety in other industries (e.g., automotive safety, helmet design).

5. **Foundation for Future Research and Development:** This preliminary study lays a crucial foundation for extensive future work. It opens avenues for:
 - More rigorous statistical analyses with larger datasets to fully characterize robot responses.
 - Expansion of testing to include other locomotion functions (e.g., speed-tracking, complex terrains) and a wider variety of legged robot platforms (e.g., quadrupeds, different humanoid designs).
 - Integration of advanced testing algorithms with cyber-physical systems to create highly dynamic and adaptive testing scenarios.
 - Physical enhancements to linear impactors to better suit the specific requirements and conditions of legged robot testing.

In conclusion, this research provides a vital step forward for the legged robotics community by proposing and validating a standardized, objective, and repeatable method for evaluating disturbance rejection. Its findings offer both practical tools and profound insights into robot behavior, ultimately accelerating the development of safer, more reliable, and commercially viable legged robots.
