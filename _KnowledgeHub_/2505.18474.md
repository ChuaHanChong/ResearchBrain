# Canonical Policy: Learning Canonical 3D Representation for SE(3)-Equivariant Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Purdue University introduced Canonical Policy, a framework for SE(3)-equivariant robotic imitation learning that maps 3D point clouds, robot states, and actions to a consistent canonical frame. The approach showed enhanced generalization to novel conditions and improved data efficiency, achieving successful task completion with as few as 50 demonstrations in real-world settings.

## Problem

- Visual imitation learning policies often struggle to generalize to unseen object types, novel scene layouts, or varied camera viewpoints.
- Existing equivariant neural network approaches for robotics integrate components in an unstructured manner, leading to degraded geometric symmetry and lack of interpretability.
- Current imitation learning methods typically require extensive human demonstration data, limiting their sample efficiency and practical deployability.

## Method

- Developed a principled framework that rigorously unifies 3D point cloud observations under a canonical representation, making the policy SE(3)-equivariant (translation and rotation invariant).
- Implemented a data canonicalization procedure that first de-centers the point cloud and then uses an SO(3)-equivariant network to estimate and apply a rotation matrix, transforming it into a unique canonical form.
- Designed a flexible policy pipeline that operates entirely on these canonicalized inputs (point clouds, robot proprioception, and actions), compatible with generative models like diffusion policies, for end-to-end learning.

## Results

- The Canonical Policy (CP-SO2 and CP-SO3) consistently outperformed state-of-the-art baselines across 12 simulated and 4 real-world manipulation tasks, showing an average task success improvement of 13.4% over DP3 in simulation.
- Demonstrated robust generalization to unseen object appearances (color, shape), novel scene layouts, and varying camera viewpoints, maintaining high success rates where baselines exhibited significant performance degradation.
- Achieved non-zero success rates with as few as 50 demonstrations on a real robot, making it the only method to perform successfully in low-data regimes, and maintained strong performance under moderate camera viewpoint shifts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly mapping diverse 3D observations to a unique, standardized canonical representation effectively factors out geometric variability, simplifying policy learning and enabling robust generalization to arbitrary SE(3) transformations.
> - A theoretically grounded canonicalization process, involving translation invariance and SO(3)-equivariant rotation estimation, provides a principled mechanism for achieving spatial symmetry in visuomotor policies.
> - The integration of a specialized point cloud aggregation encoder with the canonicalization step is crucial for robust feature extraction, significantly enhancing the policy's performance and noise resilience.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "Canonical Policy" is situated at the nexus of several critical and rapidly evolving areas within robotic artificial intelligence: visual imitation learning, 3D scene representation, and equivariant neural networks.

**Visual Imitation Learning (IL)** has made significant strides in enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks by learning from human demonstrations. However, a pervasive challenge remains: generalization. Policies trained on a fixed set of demonstrations often struggle when confronted with unseen objects, novel scene layouts, or varied camera viewpoints. This limitation severely hampers the practical deployability of IL systems in real-world, dynamic environments.

To address generalization, two main avenues have been extensively explored:

1. **3D Point Cloud Representations:** Moving beyond 2D RGB images, researchers have increasingly adopted 3D point clouds as input observations. Point clouds inherently capture the geometric structure of an environment, making them invariant to visual distractors such as object color, background textures, or lighting conditions. This focus on task-relevant geometry allows policies to learn more robust mappings from observation to action, thereby improving generalization and sample efficiency. Papers like DP3 and iDP3 exemplify this direction.

2. **Equivariant Neural Networks:** Robotic manipulation tasks naturally exhibit spatial symmetries (e.g., rotation, translation) because they occur in 3D Euclidean space. Incorporating these symmetry priors into the policy architecture, known as equivariance, has been shown to significantly enhance sample efficiency and generalization. Equivariant networks ensure that if an input is transformed, the output transforms consistently, reducing the need for the network to "re-learn" the same task from different viewpoints or poses. Approaches like EquiBot and EquiDiff integrate equivariance into neural modules.

The "Canonical Policy" paper critiques existing equivariant approaches, noting their lack of rigor and interpretability due to unstructured integration of equivariant components within complex, black-box neural networks. This often leads to degraded geometric symmetry in the learned policy. The paper's core contribution is to provide a *principled framework* that rigorously unifies 3D point cloud observations under a *canonical representation*, thereby enabling truly SE(3)-equivariant (translation and rotation equivariant) policies. By explicitly normalizing observations into a consistent canonical frame before policy inference, the proposed method bypasses the limitations of implicitly learning equivariance through data augmentation or by re-designing every network layer. This approach aims to maximize the generalization benefits of both 3D representations and spatial symmetries, representing a significant advancement in building more robust and data-efficient robotic manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the "Canonical Policy" research are multi-fold, aiming to address critical limitations in current visual imitation learning for robotic manipulation:

1. **Improve Generalization:** The overarching goal is to significantly enhance the generalization capabilities of visual imitation learning policies. Current methods often struggle to generalize to unseen object types, scene configurations, geometric layouts, or camera viewpoints, limiting their real-world applicability.
2. **Enhance Sample Efficiency:** By leveraging inherent geometric symmetries, the authors aim to reduce the amount of human demonstration data required to train effective policies. If a policy can inherently adapt to pose variations, less data is needed to cover the diversity of possible task configurations.
3. **Introduce a Principled Equivariant Framework:** The authors identify a gap in existing equivariant approaches, which often integrate equivariant components in an unstructured and ad-hoc manner. This lack of rigor can lead to policies that are not truly equivariant, lacking interpretability, and failing to fully exploit spatial symmetries. The key motivation is to develop a *principled, rigorous framework* for 3D equivariant imitation learning.
4. **Unify 3D Point Cloud Observations under Canonical Representation:** A central objective is to establish a robust theoretical foundation for 3D canonical representations. This involves demonstrating how both seen and novel point clouds, related by SE(3) transformations, can be consistently mapped to a unified, standardized representation. This canonical form is expected to simplify the policy learning problem by normalizing geometric variability.
5. **Enable End-to-End SE(3)-Equivariant Policy Learning:** The research seeks to design a flexible policy learning pipeline that can leverage geometric symmetries from the canonical representation while benefiting from the expressiveness of modern generative models (e e.g., diffusion models, flow matching). This integration aims to achieve strong spatial generalization from demonstrations in an end-to-end manner.
6. **Validate on Diverse Real-World and Simulated Tasks:** The authors are motivated to empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach across a broad spectrum of manipulation tasks, varying in complexity, object types, camera viewpoints, and robot platforms, to showcase superior generalization and data efficiency compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

In essence, the paper strives to overcome the inherent limitations of black-box neural networks in maintaining geometric equivariance by introducing an explicit, theoretically-grounded canonicalization step, thereby making imitation learning policies more robust, data-efficient, and generalizable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Canonical Policy" introduces a principled framework for SE(3)-equivariant imitation learning by operating on a canonical 3D representation. The methodology can be broken down into three key components: the theory of canonical representations, the data canonicalization procedure, and the canonical policy pipeline incorporating a specialized point cloud encoder.

**A. Theory of Canonical Representations**
The core theoretical foundation rests on two assumptions:
1. **Group-Based Partition (Assumption 1):** The set of all 3D point clouds for a given task can be partitioned into a finite number of mutually exclusive equivariant groups ($G_i$). Within each group, any two point clouds are related by an SE(3) transformation (rotation $R$ and translation $b$). Crucially, point clouds from different groups *cannot* be made to coincide via any SE(3) transformation. This formalizes the idea that different instances of the "same" object or scene configuration (e.g., a cup at different poses) belong to one group, while entirely different objects or scene types belong to separate groups.
2. **SE(3) Reachability (Assumption 2):** Any unseen point cloud observation from the *same task* (but not present in the training data $D$) can be mapped to a point cloud within $D$ via an SE(3) transformation. This implies that the training data is sufficiently diverse to represent the fundamental geometries of the task.

Under these assumptions, the authors define a **canonical representation** ($x_{cn}$) as a unique, standardized form for all point clouds within the same equivariant group. If an SE(3)-equivariant function $f$ is applied to any point cloud $x$ in a group, its output $f(x)$ will be an SE(3) transformed version of $f(x_{cn})$. This means the policy can learn on a single canonical representation, and generalize to any pose-variant input by first canonicalizing it. This contrasts with data augmentation, which exposes the network to diverse poses but doesn't guarantee generalization to *unseen* transformations.

**B. SE(3) Equivariance via Data Canonicalization**
The practical realization of canonical representations involves an SE(3)-equivariant canonicalization procedure:
1. **Translation Invariance:** First, any input point cloud $x$ is converted to its **decentered form** ($x_{de}$) by subtracting its mean ($x_{mn}$). This step eliminates translational variance, ensuring that any two decentered point clouds from the same group are related solely by a rotation.
2. **Rotation Estimation (SO(3)-equivariant network):** An SO(3)-equivariant network (e.g., built on the Vector Neuron framework) takes the decentered point cloud $x_{de}$ as input. This network is designed to output SO(3)-equivariant features. Specifically, it outputs two 3D vectors ($r^1_x, r^2_x$) that transform consistently under rotation.
3. **Rotation Matrix Construction (Schmidt Orthogonalization):** These two vectors ($r^1_x, r^2_x$) are used to construct an orthonormal basis, and thus a rotation matrix $R_x \in SO(3)$, via a process similar to Schmidt orthogonalization (normalizing $r^1_x$, orthogonalizing $r^2_x$ with respect to $r^1_x$, and computing their cross product). This procedure is itself SO(3)-equivariant.
4. **Canonicalization:** The estimated rotation matrix $R_x$ is then used to transform the decentered point cloud into its canonical form: $x_{cn} = R^{-1}_x x_{de}$. The theory proves that any $y_{de}$ from the same equivariant group will map to the *identical* $x_{cn}$.
5. **SO(2) Variant:** In many real-world scenarios, the z-axis is consistently aligned vertically. This limits rotational variations primarily to in-plane SO(2) rotations around the z-axis. The framework offers an SO(2) variant (CP-SO2) where the SO(3)-equivariant network extracts only the in-plane rotational component, simplifying the process and potentially improving robustness for such tasks.

**C. Canonical Policy Pipeline**
The canonical policy framework (Figure 4) maps all policy inputs and outputs into this shared canonical frame:
1. **Point Cloud Canonicalization:** The observed point cloud $x$ is first canonicalized into $x_{cn}$ using the procedure described above, yielding a transformation matrix $T_t$ (combining $R_t$ and $x_{mn_t}$).
2. **Robot Proprioception Canonicalization:** The robot's proprioceptive state (end-effector position $s^{pos}_t$ and orientation $s^{ori}_t$) is also transformed into the canonical frame using the *inverse* of $T_t$: $S^{cn}_t = T^{-1}_t S_t$. The gripper width $s^{grip}_t$ is transformation-invariant.
3. **Action Canonicalization:** The action (e.g., target end-effector pose $A_{k,t}$ in diffusion models) is likewise mapped to a canonical action $A^{cn}_{k,t} = T^{-1}_t A_{k,t}$. The paper details this for both absolute and relative control modes, noting that for relative control, the transformation is $T^{-1}_t \Delta A_{k,t} T_t$.
4. **Policy Inference and De-canonicalization:** The policy (implemented using a generative model like diffusion or flow matching) operates entirely on these canonicalized inputs (point cloud features, canonical proprioception, noisy canonical actions). After predicting the denoised canonical action $A^{cn}_{k-1,t}$, it is mapped back to the world frame using the *forward* transformation $T_t$ (e.g., $A_{k-1,t} = T_t A^{cn}_{k-1,t}$).

**D. Point Cloud Aggregation Encoder**
For extracting features from the canonicalized point clouds, the paper uses an MLP-based encoder with residual connections, inspired by PointMLP. This encoder incorporates a **neighborhood aggregation module** (Figure 5) that identifies neighbors for each point, normalizes their features based on local mean and standard deviation, and applies learned affine transformation parameters ($\alpha, \beta$). This design enhances robustness to noise by leveraging local contextual cues, similar to convolutions in image processing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents extensive experimental validation across both simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, demonstrating the "Canonical Policy's" (CP) superior generalization and sample efficiency.

**A. Validation of SE(3) Equivariance (Simulation)**
- **Qualitative Visualization (Figure 7):** The paper qualitatively shows that CP's data canonicalization successfully aligns both "seen" (training set) and "novel" (randomly transformed) scenes to a consistent canonical frame. This visual evidence directly supports the theoretical claim of mapping diverse poses to a unified representation.
- **Quantitative Evaluation (Figure 8):** Policies were evaluated under four conditions: seen scenes, seen scenes with random SE(3) transforms, novel scenes, and novel scenes with random SE(3) transforms. CP consistently maintained high and stable success rates across all conditions. In contrast, baseline policies (DP3, iDP3, EquiBot) exhibited significant performance degradation (often near zero success) when random SE(3) transformations were applied, especially to novel scenes. This validates that CP's explicit canonicalization effectively removes pose variability and enables robust generalization to arbitrary rigid transforms.

**B. Benchmark with Point Cloud Policies (Simulation)**
- **Superior Performance (Table II):** Both CP-SO3 (full SO(3) equivariance) and CP-SO2 (SO(2) equivariance around z-axis) significantly outperformed baseline point cloud policies (DP3, iDP3, EquiBot) across 12 diverse simulated tasks (MimicGen, Push T, Bimanual).
- **CP-SO2 Advantage:** CP-SO2 achieved the highest success rates in 6 out of 9 single-arm tasks and the highest average overall success rate (58.3%), followed by CP-SO3 (56.0%). This suggests that for tasks with an upright z-axis, the simplified SO(2) equivariance is often more practical and effective.
- **Quantified Improvement:** CP-SO2 showed an average task success improvement of 13.4% over DP3, 15.4% over iDP3, and 25.1% over EquiBot.
- **EquiBot Performance Discrepancy:** EquiBot performed well on bimanual tasks but poorly on MimicGen tasks. Ablation studies (discussed below) indicated this was likely due to its point cloud encoder.

**C. Ablation Studies (Simulation)**
- **Module Contributions (Table III):** The combination of the Point Cloud Aggregation Encoder Block and canonicalization (translation invariance + SO(2)/SO(3) equivariance) was crucial. Compared to a basic DP3 setup, these components collectively led to substantial improvements in success rates.
- **Point Cloud Encoders (Table IV):** The DP3 encoder (simple MLP) consistently performed best. Replacing it with PointNet++, DGCNN, or even EquiBot's original SO(3)-equivariant encoder resulted in near-zero success rates for most MimicGen tasks. Interestingly, when EquiBot adopted the DP3 encoder, its performance drastically improved, achieving the highest success rate (90%) on Push T and competitive results on MimicGen. This highlights the importance of the encoder choice, even within equivariant frameworks.
- **Scaling (SIM(3) vs. SE(3)) (Table V):** Adding scaling invariance (SIM(3)) to SE(3) (as in EquiBot) yielded mixed results, improving performance on some tasks but degrading it on others. Thus, the authors opted for pure SE(3) canonicalization.
- **SO(3)-Equivariant Network Parameters (Table VI):** The SO(3)-equivariant branch is lightweight (~3.2% of total parameters). Increasing its capacity (feature dimensions and repeat layers) generally improved success rates, indicating that a more capable equivariant estimator is beneficial for handling noisy inputs.
- **Action Heads (Table VII):** CP was compatible with both Diffusion and Flow Matching action heads, consistently outperforming baselines regardless of the chosen generative model. Task-specific preferences were observed (diffusion better for Stack D1, flow matching for Push T).

**D. Reliability Analysis of the Rotation Estimator (Simulation)**
- **Noise Robustness (Figure 9 & Table VIII):** An experiment with varying noise levels (random rotation, Gaussian jitter, dropout, cropping, insertion) demonstrated the robustness of the SO(3)-equivariant network. With frozen parameters, the network quickly lost coherence under noise. However, a *trained* network maintained tight feature clusters at low-to-moderate noise levels, showing an improved ability to map approximately rigid point clouds to a shared canonical representation. Performance degraded at very high noise levels.

**E. Real Robot Experiments (Franka Emika Panda)**
- **Benchmarking Across Diverse Tasks (Table IX):** On four real-world manipulation tasks of increasing difficulty, CP-SO2 consistently achieved the highest success rates. For example, in Block Stacking, it achieved 70% success (40% higher than the next best). In the most challenging Table Organization task, CP-SO2 (30%) was the only policy with a non-zero success rate alongside EquiDiff (20%). This validates CP-SO2's effectiveness in complex, real-world scenarios. Point cloud policies generally outperformed image-based policies.
- **Generalization to Unseen Object Appearances (Table X & XI):**
 - **Color Variations:** CP-SO2 maintained strong performance under significant color shifts (e.g., blue blocks, white/green cans), outperforming image-based policies (which often dropped to 0% success) and other point cloud baselines. This highlights the appearance-invariant nature of point clouds and CP's robustness.
 - **Shape & Color Variations:** Under simultaneous changes in object shape and color (e.g., leather or hiking boots), CP-SO2 continued to achieve the highest success rates, demonstrating robust generalization beyond appearance.

**F. Data Efficiency Analysis (UR5 Platform)**
- **Low-Data Regimes (Table XII):** CP-SO2 was the *only* method to achieve non-zero success rates with only 50 demonstrations (40% on trained black shoes, 60% on unseen white shoes). All other baselines failed completely. With 100 demonstrations, CP-SO2 further improved (60% on black, 70% on white), significantly outperforming DP3 (30% on black, 20% on white) and other baselines (which remained low).
- **Unseen Color Performance:** CP-SO2 achieved *higher* success rates on unseen white shoes compared to trained black shoes in low-data regimes, suggesting that slight observation randomness can enhance policy robustness.

**G. Robustness to Viewpoint Shifts (UR5 Platform)**
- **Viewpoint Generalization (Table XIII):** CP-SO2 maintained strong performance under moderate camera viewpoint shifts (Frontal View, 15° rotation) with 40-60% success rates, while all baselines failed. Larger viewpoint shifts (Right-Angled View, ~30° rotation) caused CP-SO2's performance to drop (to 30%), consistent with the noise robustness analysis where very high noise levels degrade the estimator's ability to reliably align point clouds.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Canonical Policy" presents a significant step forward in visual imitation learning for robotic manipulation, offering a principled solution to long-standing challenges of generalization and sample efficiency.

**Significance:**
- **Principled Equivariance:** The most critical contribution is its rigorous, theoretically-grounded framework for SE(3)-equivariant policy learning. By explicitly canonicalizing 3D point cloud observations, robot states, and actions into a standardized frame, it overcomes the limitations of previous ad-hoc or implicitly learned equivariant approaches. This provides a clear, interpretable mechanism for ensuring spatial symmetry.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The canonical representation allows policies to robustly generalize across a wide range of distribution shifts—unseen object appearances (color, shape), novel scene layouts, and varying camera viewpoints—without requiring extensive data augmentation or retraining. This is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured, real-world environments.
- **Superior Data Efficiency:** By factoring out geometric variations, the policy can learn task-relevant behaviors from fewer demonstrations. The real-world experiments on the UR5 platform demonstrated that Canonical Policy was the *only* method to achieve successful task completion with as few as 50 demonstrations, a critical advantage for practical robotic systems where data collection is often costly and time-consuming.
- **Compatibility with Modern Generative Models:** The framework is flexible, seamlessly integrating with powerful generative models like diffusion policies and flow matching, leveraging their representational capacity for learning complex action distributions.
- **Strong Empirical Validation:** The comprehensive evaluation across 12 simulated tasks and 4 real-world tasks on two different robot platforms, covering 16 distinct configurations, provides compelling evidence of the method's effectiveness and robustness, consistently outperforming state-of-the-art baselines.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Broader Deployment of IL:** By making imitation learning policies more generalizable and data-efficient, Canonical Policy could accelerate the deployment of robots in diverse manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics applications, where object and scene variations are common.
- **Reduced Data Collection Burden:** The improved sample efficiency directly translates to lower costs and faster development cycles for new robotic skills.
- **Foundation for Future Equivariant Learning:** The rigorous theoretical framework for 3D canonical representations could serve as a foundational building block for future research in equivariant learning, inspiring new designs for perception and control systems that inherently respect physical symmetries.
- **Advancements in 3D Perception for Robotics:** The success of 3D point cloud inputs within this framework reinforces the importance of geometric representations over pure 2D visual inputs for robust manipulation.

**Limitations:**
Despite its strengths, the paper acknowledges several limitations:
1. **Computational Cost:** The neighborhood processing in the Point Cloud Aggregation Encoder Block and the SO(3)-equivariant networks can be computationally intensive, potentially limiting scalability in resource-constrained applications or for real-time performance at very high rates.
2. **Sensitivity to Point Cloud Structure under Occlusion/Viewpoint Shifts:** While theoretically robust to global rotations, the SO(3)-equivariant network remains sensitive to significant structural changes in the input point cloud due to occlusions or drastic viewpoint shifts. Different views of the same object might yield substantially different point cloud geometries, undermining the estimator's ability to reliably map them to a single canonical space. This could lead to performance degradation in highly occluded or drastically altered viewing conditions. Future work might need to explore viewpoint-invariant encoders or multi-view representations.

Overall, "Canonical Policy" represents a significant contribution to making robotic imitation learning more practical and robust by providing a principled method for incorporating 3D geometric symmetries into visuomotor policies.
