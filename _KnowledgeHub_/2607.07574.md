# Context-Aware Force Estimation for Deformable Tool Manipulation in Robotic Environmental Swabbing via Few-Shot Continual Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> A data-driven framework estimates tip-level contact forces during robotic deformable tool manipulation (DTM) for environmental swabbing using only proprioceptive sensing. It employs a context-aware, few-shot continual adaptation strategy that achieved 18-63% error reduction on new surfaces/tools while preventing catastrophic forgetting.

## Problem

- Accurate contact force estimation at the deformable tool-environment interface is difficult due to the tool's compliant nature, decoupling forces measured at the robot's wrist.
- Integrating permanent sensors into deformable tools (e.g., swabs) is often impractical due to sterility, disposability, limited space, and cost constraints.
- Existing learning-based force estimation methods require extensive data, struggle with generalization to new interaction conditions (domain shifts), and are prone to catastrophic forgetting during adaptation.

## Method

- The research formulates contact force estimation as a supervised sequence-to-scalar regression problem, predicting tip forces from robot proprioceptive measurements (joint states, wrist F/T data).
- A ==Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)== recurrent neural network is employed as a contact dynamics encoder to capture complex, history-dependent dynamics like viscoelastic hysteresis and stress relaxation.
- A **parameter-isolated few-shot continual adaptation** mechanism uses ==Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)== with low-dimensional latent context vectors, allowing rapid adaptation to new interaction conditions (e.g., surfaces, tool compliance) by only optimizing the context vectors while freezing the shared backbone.

## Results

- The LSTM model achieved the lowest estimation error (**RMSE = 26.99 ADC units**) and highest coefficient of determination (**R^2 = 0.992**) on the baseline wood surface, outperforming Transformer (**12.4%** higher RMSE) and TCN (**23.5%** higher RMSE).
- Few-shot adaptation (using 5 trials) across nine unseen interaction regimes (varying surfaces and tool compliance) reduced RMSE by **18% to 63%**, restoring performance to near-baseline levels.
- The parameter-isolated adaptation mechanism successfully prevented catastrophic forgetting; performance on the original Wood domain remained statistically unchanged (**R^2 = 0.991**) after all adaptation cycles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recurrent architectures like LSTMs are highly effective for modeling the non-Markovian, history-dependent dynamics of deformable tool-environment interactions, outperforming Transformer and TCN models.
> - Low-dimensional latent context vectors, modulated via FiLM, can effectively encode task-specific dynamics for rapid and data-efficient adaptation to novel interaction conditions.
> - Freezing the shared recurrent backbone and exclusively optimizing context embeddings during adaptation successfully prevents catastrophic forgetting, enabling lifelong learning across diverse interaction regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has extensively studied deformable object manipulation (DOM), which involves a rigid robot interacting with and controlling the deformation of an object. This work, however, addresses a distinct and often more complex challenge: Deformable Tool Manipulation (DTM). In DTM, a robot interacts with its environment indirectly through a compliant, deformable tool, rather than directly manipulating a deformable object. Examples of DTM include robotic environmental swabbing, wiping, brushing, and polishing, where sustained contact and precise force regulation are critical.

A fundamental difficulty in DTM arises because the deformable nature of the tool decouples the forces measured at the robot's rigid wrist from the actual contact forces at the tool-environment interface. This decoupling is attributed to the tool's internal deformation, stress distribution, and material properties, which introduce nonlinear viscoelastic hysteresis, rate-dependent stiffness, and geometric nonlinearities. Traditional physics-based models struggle with these complex, high-dimensional, history-dependent dynamics, as they often rely on linear constitutive assumptions.

Direct measurement using tool-integrated sensors at the contact interface is a potential solution, but practical constraints such as sterility requirements, disposability, limited space for electronics, and sensor drift often render this approach impractical, especially in applications like environmental swabbing.

Learning-based approaches offer an alternative by approximating complex force-motion mappings from raw sensor inputs. However, existing methods typically require extensive data collection for training and exhibit reduced performance when faced with distribution shifts, such as changes in surface material or tool compliance. Furthermore, adapting these pre-trained models to new interaction regimes can lead to catastrophic forgetting, where the model's performance on previously learned tasks degrades.

This research positions itself within this landscape by developing a data-driven framework that addresses the challenges of accurate force estimation in DTM, specifically for robotic environmental swabbing. It leverages proprioceptive sensing without requiring explicit physical models or permanent tool-integrated sensors. The work aims to overcome the limitations of data efficiency and generalization in non-stationary environments by employing a novel context-aware, few-shot continual adaptation strategy. This approach draws inspiration from parameter-efficient transfer learning and continual learning techniques from vision and language domains, such as Visual Prompt Tuning and Learning to Prompt, but applies them to the unique problem of robotic force estimation in DTM. By doing so, the authors aim to enable robust adaptation to new interaction conditions while preserving performance on previously learned tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-driven framework for accurate contact force estimation during Deformable Tool Manipulation (DTM) in robotic environmental swabbing. This estimation is intended to rely solely on proprioceptive sensing from the robot, eliminating the need for permanent, integrated sensors within the disposable or sterile deformable tool.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in DTM, particularly in applications like environmental swabbing:

1. **Complex Deformable Tool Dynamics:** Deformable tools exhibit complex interaction dynamics that make accurate force estimation difficult. These include:
 - **Geometric Nonlinearity:** The contact area and pressure distribution change as the tool compresses, splays, or reorients.
 - **Hysteretic Behavior:** Internal energy dissipation causes force responses during loading to differ from those during unloading at identical configurations.
 - **Rate-Dependent Stiffness:** Apparent material properties vary with interaction velocity due to limited internal relaxation during rapid movements.
 These factors mean the tool's effective state is high-dimensional, encompassing not just its pose but also its internal stress distribution and deformation history, which precludes simple, closed-form force-displacement models.

2. **Decoupling of Sensing:** The continuous internal deformation of the compliant tool during contact causes interaction forces at the tool-environment interface to be filtered, delayed, and decoupled from the forces measured at the robot's wrist. This disparity limits the reliability of conventional wrist-mounted force-torque sensors for directly inferring contact forces.

3. **Impracticality of Tool-Integrated Sensors:** While direct measurement at the tool tip could provide ground truth, tool-integrated sensors are often impractical for deployment in applications like environmental swabbing due to:
 - **Sterility and Disposability Constraints:** Tools used for sampling must be sterile and are often disposable, making permanent electronic integration problematic.
 - **Limited Space and Cost:** Small, compliant tools offer limited space for embedded electronics, and the cost of embedding sensors in frequently replaced tools can be prohibitive.
 - **Sensor Drift and Accessibility:** Tool-integrated sensors can be prone to drift and may be difficult to access or recalibrate.

4. **Limitations of Existing Learning-Based Methods:** Although learning-based approaches can capture complex dynamics without explicit physical models, they face significant hurdles:
 - **Data Efficiency:** They typically require extensive data collection (e.g., over 1000 trials per configuration).
 - **Generalization:** Models often exhibit reduced performance (brittleness) when faced with domain shifts, such as variations in surface material or tool compliance.
 - **Catastrophic Forgetting:** Standard fine-tuning to adapt to new conditions often leads to the loss of proficiency on previously learned tasks.

To address these motivations, the research aims to develop an estimator that fulfills three specific requirements:
- **Capture Long-Range Temporal Dependencies:** The model must account for stress relaxation and other history-dependent effects, requiring memory over several seconds.
- **Support Real-Time Inference:** The solution must be compatible with high-frequency control loops, necessitating sub-millisecond latency.
- **Enable Parameter-Isolated Adaptation:** The framework should allow rapid adaptation to new interaction conditions (e.g., different surfaces or tool compliances) with minimal new data, while preventing catastrophic forgetting of previously learned dynamics.

By addressing these points, the authors seek to provide a robust and practical solution for force estimation in DTM, thereby enhancing the performance and applicability of robotic systems in tasks requiring compliant interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates contact force estimation in deformable swab tool manipulation as a supervised sequence-to-scalar regression problem. The objective is to predict tip-level contact forces from proprioceptive measurements, without relying on explicit constitutive models or permanent sensors integrated into the tool.

**A. System Overview and Data Collection:**
An experimental system comprising a 6-DOF UR5e manipulator equipped with a custom gripper holding a disposable cellulose sponge swab is used. During training, a single-axis force-sensitive resistor (FSR) embedded within the sponge provides ground-truth force labels. This FSR was calibrated to provide accurate force readings (RMSE < 0.08 N over 0-5 N range). Training data is collected using a controlled swabbing trajectory designed to induce coupled normal compression, shear, and bending, thereby exciting viscoelastic and hysteretic deformation modes representative of realistic surface sampling.

At each timestep *t*, the robot provides a 24-dimensional proprioceptive measurement vector x*t*, which includes:
- Joint positions (q*t* ∈ R^6^)
- Joint velocities (q̇*t* ∈ R^6^)
- Commanded joint torques (τ*t* ∈ R^6^)
- Wrist-mounted force-torque sensor measurements (f*FT*t* ∈ R^6^, comprising 3D forces and 3D torques).
All signals are sampled synchronously at 300 Hz and time-aligned with the FSR supervision signal.

**B. Recurrent Modeling of Contact Dynamics:**
Given the viscoelastic hysteresis and stress relaxation, contact force at time *t* cannot be inferred from instantaneous measurements alone. It is modeled as a nonlinear function of temporal interaction history: y*t* = fθ(x*t-T:t*, z*k*), where x*t-T:t* is a sliding temporal window of robot states, θ denotes learnable model parameters, and z*k* is a task-specific latent context embedding.

To capture the non-Markovian, history-dependent dynamics, a recurrent neural network architecture was selected after a comparative evaluation of temporal models. A Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was adopted as the contact dynamics encoder. The LSTM, chosen for its ability to maintain long-range temporal dependencies through gated memory mechanisms, comprises two layers, each with 64 hidden units. The final hidden state h*t* from the LSTM, representing a latent encoding of accumulated deformation energy and delayed stress responses, is then passed through a two-layer feedforward regression head (32 units with ReLU activation to a scalar output) to predict the contact force ŷ*t*.

The model, with a compact parameter set of 60,545, is trained on the source domain (baseline wood surface) by minimizing the mean squared error between predicted and ground-truth contact forces. This architecture achieves sub-millisecond inference latency (0.37 ms), which is suitable for real-time control applications.

**C. Parameter-Isolated Few-Shot Adaptation via Context Modulation:**
To address generalization across unseen surfaces and tool compliance conditions, a parameter-isolated few-shot adaptation mechanism is introduced. This mechanism modulates latent dynamics through low-dimensional conditioning while preserving shared temporal representations.

After pretraining on the source domain, the parameters of the recurrent LSTM encoder (θ*enc*), FiLM projector parameters, and the regression head parameters (ϕ) are permanently frozen. Adaptation to a new interaction condition *k* is achieved by learning a low-dimensional latent context vector z*k* (where the dimensionality of z*k* is much smaller than that of the hidden state h*t*). This context vector modulates the LSTM's latent representation h*t* using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM):
˜h*t* = γ(z*k*) ⊙ h*t* + β(z*k*)
Here, ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication, and γ(·) and β(·) are lightweight MLP projectors (two-layer network: d*z* → 64 → d*h*) that map z*k* to scale and bias vectors. The final layer of the γ projector is initialized with a bias of +1, ensuring an identity transform at the start of adaptation.

For adaptation to a novel condition, only the latent context vector z*k* is optimized using a small support set (K swabbing trials) from the new domain, while all other shared network parameters remain fixed. Learned context vectors are stored in a domain-indexed context bank. This approach prevents catastrophic forgetting, as the shared backbone is never modified, and ensures that previously learned domain mappings remain invariant. The framework supports continual adaptation with minimal memory overhead, proportional only to the dimensionality of the context vectors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation assessed the framework's modeling accuracy, data efficiency, sensing sensitivity, and robustness to domain shifts across a UR5e platform.

**A. Comparative Analysis of Temporal Architectures:**
Five temporal modeling architectures were compared for force estimation on the baseline wood surface.
- The **LSTM** model achieved the lowest estimation error (RMSE = 26.99 ADC units) and the highest coefficient of determination (R^2 = 0.992).
- It outperformed the **Transformer** (RMSE = 30.82) by approximately 12.4% and the **TCN** (RMSE = 35.31) by over 23.5%. This performance improvement over the Transformer was statistically significant (p = 0.049, α = 0.05).
- The LSTM also demonstrated sub-millisecond inference latency (0.37 ms) and a compact parameter footprint (60,545), balancing accuracy with computational efficiency suitable for real-time control.
- Error residual analysis showed a symmetric distribution centered near zero (μ = 1.48 ADC units), indicating unbiased estimates, with most errors being small in magnitude. The LSTM tracked ground truth closely during both impact onset and non-linear relaxation phases.

**B. Data Efficiency and Learning Dynamics:**
The LSTM's performance was evaluated with varying fractions of the baseline wood dataset.
- The model achieved a high R^2 (0.9763) with only 10% of the training data (5 trials), indicating rapid capture of dominant quasi-static force components.
- However, the RMSE initially increased to 50.23 with 15% data before consistently decreasing as more data was introduced. Full-data training (100%, 56 trials) reduced the RMSE by approximately 38% compared to the 10% training condition. This suggests that while elastic responses are learned quickly, a broader dataset is required to capture the subtler, history-dependent viscoelastic effects.

**C. Sensitivity to Proprioceptive Modalities (Ablation Study):**
Ablation experiments identified the contribution of different proprioceptive inputs from the 24-dimensional state vector.
- Removing **wrist force-torque measurements** increased RMSE by over 143% (from 26.99 to 65.63), confirming their essential role in force reconstruction.
- Excluding **joint velocities** resulted in a moderate degradation (RMSE increased to 30.40), indicating their contribution to modeling rate-dependent stiffness and damping.
- Removing **commanded joint torques** yielded a marginal improvement (RMSE decreased to 26.39), suggesting that these signals might introduce noise or redundancy in this context.

**D. Robustness to Material- and Tool-Induced Domain Shift:**
The framework's generalization capabilities were tested across nine interaction regimes, involving three swab tools with distinct compliance levels (Standard-3M, Hard PP, Soft TPU) on three surfaces (Wood, Stainless Steel, Viscoelastic Composite). The LSTM backbone was trained solely on Standard-3M interacting with Wood.

- **Zero-Shot Transfer:**
 - Significant performance degradation was observed when transferring to unseen domains. On Stainless Steel, RMSE increased by approximately 45%.
 - On the Viscoelastic Composite, RMSE increased by over 200%, highlighting the inability to capture damping-induced phase lag and hysteresis without adaptation.
 - Tool compliance also introduced shifts; both Hard and Soft stems increased zero-shot error on Wood.
 - The most severe degradation occurred with the Hard (PP) tool on Stainless Steel, where rigid-rigid interaction amplified high-frequency impact transients. Conversely, the Soft (TPU) tool generally exhibited lower zero-shot error on rigid surfaces due to passive filtering of impact spikes.

- **Parameter-Isolated Few-Shot Adaptation:**
 - Across all nine regimes, few-shot adaptation (using 5 trials to update only the latent context vector) reduced RMSE by 18% to 63%, restoring performance to near-baseline levels.
 - For the Viscoelastic Composite surface, error reductions exceeded 60%, demonstrating that the latent context vector effectively compensates for changes in environmental stiffness and damping.
 - Crucially, performance on the original Wood domain remained statistically unchanged after all adaptation cycles (27.1 ± 1.9 ADC, R^2 = 0.991), confirming that the parameter isolation mechanism successfully prevented catastrophic forgetting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for contact force estimation in Deformable Tool Manipulation (DTM) that addresses several long-standing challenges in robotics. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Force Estimation Without Tool-Integrated Sensors:** The framework demonstrates a viable method for estimating tip-level contact forces using only proprioceptive robot sensing. This eliminates the need for expensive, non-sterile, or non-disposable sensors integrated into the deformable tool, which is a critical practical advantage for applications like robotic environmental swabbing, surgical interventions, and certain manufacturing processes.

2. **Robustness to Complex Deformable Dynamics:** By employing a recurrent architecture (LSTM), the method effectively captures the history-dependent, nonlinear, viscoelastic, and rate-dependent dynamics inherent in deformable tool-environment interactions. This is a notable improvement over traditional models that struggle with such complex, non-Markovian behaviors.

3. **Generalization and Continual Adaptation:** The introduction of a parameter-isolated few-shot continual adaptation strategy is a key contribution. It allows the force estimation model to adapt rapidly and effectively to unseen surface materials and tool compliance conditions with minimal new data (e.g., 5 trials). This adaptability is crucial for deployment in heterogeneous and non-stationary environments.

4. **Prevention of Catastrophic Forgetting:** The design of the adaptation mechanism, which freezes the shared recurrent backbone and only optimizes low-dimensional context embeddings, directly addresses the problem of catastrophic forgetting. This ensures that the model retains its proficiency on previously learned tasks while adapting to new ones, enabling scalable and lifelong learning for robotic systems.

5. **Real-Time Applicability:** The chosen LSTM architecture is compact and achieves sub-millisecond inference latency. This characteristic is essential for integrating the force estimation capabilities into high-frequency feedback control loops, allowing for precise force regulation in contact-rich tasks.

6. **Broad Applicability in Robotic Interaction Tasks:** While demonstrated on environmental swabbing, the proposed framework has potential applications across a wider range of DTM tasks. These include robotic wiping, polishing, deburring, and even surgical procedures where compliant tools are used and precise, adaptive force control is paramount for task effectiveness, safety, and preventing damage to the environment or the tool.

7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a scalable foundation for future work, including the integration with advanced adaptive control strategies, exploration of automatic context inference mechanisms, and deployment in more unstructured and dynamic environments.

Overall, this research contributes to advancing the state-of-the-art in robotic manipulation by providing a practical, robust, and adaptable solution for a challenging class of contact-rich tasks involving deformable tools.
