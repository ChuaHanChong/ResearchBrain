# Dreaming in Flow: Generative Grounding Feedback for Self-Evolving Unified Multimodal Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Generative Grounding Feedback (GGF) is a self-evolving post-training framework for unified multimodal models that needs only text prompts. The model generates a visual "dream", then flow-level feedback distills image- and repair-conditioned velocity guidance into the prompt condition, while dream replay grounding enforces claim-level evidence consistency across captioning and re-imagination. Across three backbones it lifts GenEval, DPG-Bench, MME and HallusionBench without paired image-text data.

## Problem

- Unified models place understanding and generation in one network, yet the two capabilities are typically optimized as separate tasks with no reciprocal supervision.
- Cross-capability transfer methods stay one-way: understanding scores or features steer generation, or generated images aid understanding, never forming a closed reciprocal loop.
- Existing self-improvement schemes need curated task rewards, predefined question-answer supervision, external unlabeled images, or offline scoring stages, and naive training on self-generated images amplifies their own errors.

## Method

- **Flow-level feedback** evaluates ==text-, image-, image+prompt-, and repair-conditioned velocities== at one shared noisy latent, then aligns prompt guidance with a ==repair-enhanced teacher== built from the image residual plus the edit direction.
- **Dream replay grounding** captions and re-imagines the dream twice, scoring bias-removed claim-level visual evidence into per-image distributions trained toward a consensus via ==alignment, cycle, sharpening and margin-based separation== from an unrelated dream.
- ==Anchor losses== to the frozen initial model (cosine on prompt velocity, KL on understanding tokens) preserve the pretrained prior; both objectives are optimized jointly with ==rank-16 LoRA adapters==.

## Results

- On BAGEL, GenEval overall rises **84.30** to **86.97** and DPG-Bench **84.04** to **85.43**, with the largest gains on position (**68.50** to **75.00**) and counting (**78.13** to **82.50**).
- Understanding improves by over **20** MME points on BAGEL (**2369.2** to **2390.4**) and Show-o2 (**1691.8** to **1714.4**); HallusionBench aAcc/fAcc go **51.8/24.3** to **59.4/31.2**, while SEED-Bench stays flat.
- Ablations show semantic separation matters most, dropping MME nearly **19** points when removed; SRUM by contrast lowers GenEval to **81.12**, and GGF trains in **4.1 hours** on two H100s versus SRUM's ~**19 hours** on eight.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Comparing conditional velocity predictions at one shared noisy latent turns internal generation directions into a supervision signal, richer than scoring only the finished image.
> - Self-generated experience is usable only when grounded first; consistency across a replay cycle plus an unrelated negative sample prevents both error amplification and distribution collapse.
> - Anchoring to the frozen pretrained model is the single most important guard against drift during self-training; gains concentrate in composition and hallucination consistency, not broad comprehension.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the development of **unified multimodal models**, which combine visual understanding and visual generation in a common system. Earlier multimodal systems frequently treated these functions as separate components: a vision-language model interpreted images, while an independent diffusion or autoregressive model generated them. More recent architectures, including Chameleon, Emu3, Janus, Show-o, JanusFlow, BAGEL, and OpenUni, place language modeling, visual representation, and image synthesis within a more integrated framework.

Architectural integration creates the possibility of **cross-capability supervision**. Visual understanding can provide semantic information to improve image generation, while image generation can produce visual examples that help train or refine visual understanding. Existing research has explored both directions, but many methods remain one-way. For example, some approaches use visual-language model scores, question-answering systems, or rewards to improve generation, whereas others use generated images as auxiliary data for understanding. Related self-improvement methods also commonly depend on external images, curated question-answer pairs, pretrained evaluators, or task-specific reward functions.

The paper’s contribution is to formulate a reciprocal self-training process that uses **text prompts and the model’s own generated images**, rather than paired image-text data or an external visual dataset. Its proposed framework, Generative Grounding Feedback (GGF), is designed to close the loop between generation and understanding. It combines two mechanisms:

1. **Flow-level feedback**, which uses image-conditioned and repair-conditioned predictions to refine prompt-conditioned image generation.
2. **Dream replay grounding**, which uses captioning and re-generation to identify semantic content that remains stable across repeated transformations of a generated image.

The work therefore sits at the intersection of unified multimodal architecture design, self-supervised post-training, visual grounding, and self-improving generative models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to enable a unified multimodal model to improve both its visual generation and visual understanding capabilities from its own generated experience. The authors refer to a generated image as a “visual dream.” The source prompt provides the intended semantics, while the generated image provides a concrete visual realization of those semantics. The model can then inspect, describe, and regenerate that image.

The motivation arises from a limitation of current unified models: although their understanding and generation pathways may share parameters or representations, they are usually optimized as separate tasks. Such models do not automatically use generated visual content as supervision for understanding, nor do they necessarily use understanding-derived visual evidence to improve future image synthesis.

However, self-generated images are unreliable training signals. A model may omit requested objects, produce incorrect counts, confuse attributes, violate spatial relationships, or generate malformed local structures. Directly imitating these images could reinforce existing errors. GGF therefore attempts to determine which aspects of a generated image are semantically reliable before using them for optimization.

For generation, the paper aims to move beyond evaluating only the final image. It compares multiple predictions at the same noisy latent state in a flow-based generator. Differences between text-conditioned, image-conditioned, and repair-conditioned predictions are interpreted as directions showing how visual evidence and quality instructions influence the generation trajectory.

For understanding, the objective is to retain semantic claims that survive a replay cycle. The model captions its own image, regenerates an image from that caption, captions the regenerated image, and compares the semantic evidence across the sequence. Consistent claims are reinforced, while unrelated visual experiences are separated. The intended result is a grounded, reciprocal learning process in which better generation supplies clearer visual evidence and improved understanding provides more useful guidance for subsequent generation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Self-Generated Visual Experience

For each text prompt \(p\), the current model generates an image \(x_1\) without gradient computation. This image is treated as a fixed self-generated training sample. The image is encoded into a VAE latent \(z_1\), after which a flow-matching training state is constructed by interpolating between the image latent and Gaussian noise:

\[
z_t=(1-t)z_1+t\epsilon.
\]

The target flow velocity is \(v^*=\epsilon-z_1\). The same generated image is also passed through the model’s visual understanding encoder to produce semantic visual tokens.

The authors evaluate the generation branch under four conditions: image only (\(I\)), prompt only (\(P\)), image plus prompt (\(I+P\)), and image plus prompt plus a quality-repair instruction (\(I+P+Q\)). An unconditional prediction is also computed. All predictions use the same noisy latent and timestep, allowing their differences to represent the effect of conditioning rather than differences in sampling state.

#### 3.2. Flow-Level Feedback

The flow-level objective contains four main elements.

First, an image-conditioned reconstruction loss trains the image condition to predict the target velocity of the generated image. Because the image condition describes the concrete visual content, it is treated as a more direct representation of the realized sample.

Second, a cross-condition loss aligns the prompt-conditioned velocity with the image-conditioned velocity. This transfers information from the model’s visual interpretation of the generated image to its prompt-based generation pathway.

Third, the method constructs a repair direction by subtracting the prediction under \(I+P\) from the prediction under \(I+P+Q\). This difference represents how a quality-repair instruction changes the generation trajectory. The repair-enhanced teacher combines the image-conditioned guidance with a weighted repair direction, and the prompt-conditioned guidance is aligned with this teacher. The purpose is to avoid merely reproducing defects in the self-generated image.

Fourth, an anchor loss keeps the updated prompt-conditioned generation direction close to the original pretrained model. This is intended to reduce drift caused by repeated optimization on self-generated samples. The complete flow objective combines reconstruction, cross-condition alignment, repair alignment, and generation anchoring.

#### 3.3. Dream Replay Grounding

The reverse direction uses the generated image to improve visual understanding. The model produces a caption \(c_1\) from \(x_1\), generates a second image \(x_2\) from \(c_1\), captions \(x_2\) as \(c_2\), and generates a third image \(x_3\) from \(c_2\). These operations are performed without gradients. The sequence is intended to reveal which semantic content remains stable through captioning and re-imagination.

An unrelated prompt \(p_b\) is sampled from the training corpus, and an unrelated dream \(x_b\) is generated. The set of statements used for comparison is:

\[
A=\{p,c_1,c_2,p_b\}.
\]

For each statement and image, the understanding branch calculates the difference between the logits for affirmative and negative answers. A text-only score is subtracted to reduce the influence of language priors. These residual evidence scores are normalized into semantic distributions over the shared statement set.

Several losses are applied:

- **Replay alignment:** the semantic distributions for \(x_1\), \(x_2\), and \(x_3\) are aligned to their detached consensus distribution.
- **Cycle consistency:** the final replay state is encouraged to return to the semantic distribution and score geometry of the initial dream.
- **Semantic separation:** the unrelated dream \(x_b\) is pushed away from the replay manifold.
- **Distribution sharpening:** the consensus distribution is encouraged to concentrate on a compact set of supported semantic statements.
- **Source recovery:** the understanding branch is trained to recover the original prompt from the initial dream.
- **Understanding anchoring:** the updated understanding distribution is regularized toward that of the original frozen model.

The full GGF objective is the sum of the flow-level and grounding objectives. Both are derived from the same self-generated image and optimized jointly.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

GGF is evaluated on three unified multimodal backbones:

- **BAGEL**, a decoder-only mixture-of-transformers model with interleaved understanding and rectified-flow generation.
- **Show-o2-1.5B-HQ**, which uses a shared transformer for autoregressive text modeling and flow-matching image generation.
- **OpenUni-B-512**, which combines an InternVL3-based understanding model with a SANA diffusion generator connected through learnable queries.

The method uses 1,000 text prompts for visual dreaming and a separate set of 1,000 prompts for unrelated replay grounding. Each model is trained for 1,000 steps using rank-16 LoRA adapters while the original model weights remain frozen. Training is conducted on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Generation is evaluated with GenEval and DPG-Bench. Understanding is evaluated with MME, POPE, HallusionBench, and SEED-Bench.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Image Generation

GGF improves text-to-image generation across all three backbones. On GenEval, the overall score changes as follows:

- **OpenUni:** 56.38 to 61.92.
- **Show-o2:** 69.27 to 73.22.
- **BAGEL:** 84.30 to 86.97.

DPG-Bench also improves:

- **OpenUni:** 76.27 to 78.49.
- **Show-o2:** 83.26 to 84.52.
- **BAGEL:** 84.04 to 85.43.

The largest gains occur in compositional categories, including object counting, spatial position, and color or attribute binding. Single-object accuracy changes only slightly, which the authors attribute to the relatively high baseline performance of the pretrained models in that category. These results support the claim that GGF improves adherence to prompts containing multiple semantic constraints.

Qualitative examples show fewer malformed hands, distorted faces, and locally inconsistent objects. The examples also indicate improved object counts and spatial relationships. These observations are consistent with the repair-conditioned flow feedback and the prompt-to-image alignment objectives.

#### 4.2. Visual Understanding

Understanding improvements are more modest but generally positive. For BAGEL, MME rises from 2369.2 to 2390.4. Show-o2 increases from 1691.8 to 1714.4. POPE changes only slightly, with both accuracy and F1 increasing for BAGEL and Show-o2.

The clearest changes occur on HallusionBench. BAGEL improves from 51.8 to 59.4 in aAcc, from 32.1 to 38.9 in qAcc, and from 24.3 to 31.2 in fAcc. Show-o2 also improves across all three HallusionBench measures. Because the stricter grouped metrics require consistency across related questions or contexts, these results are compatible with the replay grounding objective, which explicitly encourages semantic stability across re-imagination.

SEED-Bench remains essentially unchanged for both reported models. OpenUni is evaluated for generation, but corresponding understanding results are not included in the reported Table 2.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Comparisons

The full objective performs better than versions that remove any individual component. Within the flow-level feedback group, removing the generation anchor produces the largest GenEval decline, indicating that preserving the pretrained generation prior is important. Removing cross-condition alignment has the largest MME effect among the flow losses.

Within dream replay grounding, removing semantic separation decreases MME from 2390.41 to 2371.70, nearly a 19-point decline. This suggests that preventing semantic collapse is important. Replay alignment and cycle consistency also contribute to both metrics. Source recovery, sharpening, and the understanding anchor provide smaller but consistent benefits.

The paper compares GGF with SRUM, a reward-weighted flow-matching post-training method. On BAGEL, SRUM improves DPG-Bench slightly but reduces GenEval and MME relative to the baseline. GGF reaches 86.97 GenEval, 85.43 DPG-Bench, 2390.41 MME, and 88.4 POPE accuracy. The authors also report lower training cost: GGF completes 1,000 steps in approximately 4.1 hours on two H100 GPUs, whereas their SRUM reproduction requires separate generation and scoring stages totaling approximately 18 hours on eight GPUs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GGF provides a method for post-training unified multimodal models without paired image-text supervision or an external visual evaluator. Its main conceptual significance is the use of a single self-generated visual experience as a shared source of supervision for both capabilities. The flow-level mechanism operates on internal generation trajectories, while dream replay grounding evaluates semantic persistence across transformations rather than relying solely on final-image quality.

If validated more broadly, this approach could reduce the dependence of unified multimodal models on curated multimodal post-training data and enable efficient adaptation using text prompts alone. It may also offer a framework for improving compositional image generation while maintaining or modestly improving visual reasoning.

Several limitations remain. Self-generated images can contain systematic errors, and internal grounding signals may reproduce rather than correct the model’s biases. The experiments use relatively small prompt sets, short training schedules, and LoRA adapters, so the behavior of full-parameter or longer self-training is not established. Understanding results are reported for only two of the three backbones, and the study does not provide extensive human evaluation or comparisons with a wider range of self-improvement methods. The paper also contains an internal parameter-count inconsistency: BAGEL is described as a 7B model in the experimental setup, while the results table lists 14B parameters. These issues do not invalidate the reported trends, but they indicate areas requiring clarification and further testing.
