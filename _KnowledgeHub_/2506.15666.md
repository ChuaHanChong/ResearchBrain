# Vision in Action: Learning Active Perception from Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University researchers developed Vision in Action (ViA), a system that enables bimanual robots to learn task-relevant active perception strategies from human demonstrations. This approach improved average task performance by 45% in complex, visually occluded manipulation tasks by integrating a flexible head camera and a novel VR teleoperation interface that significantly reduced motion sickness.

## Problem

- Current robot imitation learning systems primarily rely on passive vision from fixed or wrist-mounted cameras, leading to frequent visual occlusions and insufficient information for complex tasks.
- A fundamental observation mismatch exists during imitation learning: human operators naturally employ active gaze, while robots are trained on or attempt to act with passive visual inputs.
- Significant system-level engineering challenges, such as designing flexible gaze hardware and mitigating motion sickness in VR teleoperation, have hindered the practical application of active perception in robotics.

## Method

- The ViA system uses an off-the-shelf 6-DoF robot arm as a flexible 'robot neck' with an iPhone serving as the active head camera to mimic human-like gaze movements.
- A VR-based teleoperation interface mitigates motion sickness by rendering real-time views from an intermediate 3D point cloud of the scene, decoupling the user's immediate visual feedback from the robot's physical motion updates.
- Active perception strategies are learned by training a Diffusion Policy-based visuomotor policy with an active head camera view and proprioceptive states directly from human demonstrations via behavior cloning.

## Results

- ViA's active head camera setup consistently outperformed passive camera baselines, improving average task performance by 45% compared to setups with chest and wrist cameras.
- The novel VR teleoperation interface significantly reduced motion sickness among participants (average sickness level of 2.0 for ViA vs. 3.375 for RGB streaming), with 6 out of 8 participants preferring ViA's system.
- Using a DINOv2 pre-trained Vision Transformer for image encoding yielded the highest success rates, demonstrating the benefits of strong semantic visual priors for learning robust active perception strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning active perception strategies directly from human demonstrations is crucial for robots to handle visual occlusions and improve robustness in complex manipulation tasks.
> - A novel VR teleoperation method that decouples real-time view rendering from asynchronous robot motion effectively mitigates motion sickness, enabling practical collection of human demonstrations that include active gaze behaviors.
> - Utilizing powerful pre-trained visual encoders like DINOv2 is essential for effectively leveraging active sensory input and achieving strong performance in real-world robot manipulation tasks.
