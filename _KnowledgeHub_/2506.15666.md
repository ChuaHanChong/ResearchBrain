# Vision in Action: Learning Active Perception from Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University researchers developed Vision in Action (ViA), a system that enables bimanual robots to learn task-relevant active perception strategies from human demonstrations. This approach improved average task performance by 45% in complex, visually occluded manipulation tasks by integrating a flexible head camera and a novel VR teleoperation interface that significantly reduced motion sickness.

## Problem

- Current robot imitation learning systems primarily rely on passive vision from fixed or wrist-mounted cameras, leading to frequent visual occlusions and insufficient information for complex tasks.
- A fundamental observation mismatch exists during imitation learning: human operators naturally employ active gaze, while robots are trained on or attempt to act with passive visual inputs.
- Significant system-level engineering challenges, such as designing flexible gaze hardware and mitigating motion sickness in VR teleoperation, have hindered the practical application of active perception in robotics.

## Method

- The ViA system uses an off-the-shelf 6-DoF robot arm as a flexible 'robot neck' with an iPhone serving as the active head camera to mimic human-like gaze movements.
- A VR-based teleoperation interface mitigates motion sickness by rendering real-time views from an intermediate 3D point cloud of the scene, decoupling the user's immediate visual feedback from the robot's physical motion updates.
- Active perception strategies are learned by training a Diffusion Policy-based visuomotor policy with an active head camera view and proprioceptive states directly from human demonstrations via behavior cloning.

## Results

- ViA's active head camera setup consistently outperformed passive camera baselines, improving average task performance by 45% compared to setups with chest and wrist cameras.
- The novel VR teleoperation interface significantly reduced motion sickness among participants (average sickness level of 2.0 for ViA vs. 3.375 for RGB streaming), with 6 out of 8 participants preferring ViA's system.
- Using a DINOv2 pre-trained Vision Transformer for image encoding yielded the highest success rates, demonstrating the benefits of strong semantic visual priors for learning robust active perception strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning active perception strategies directly from human demonstrations is crucial for robots to handle visual occlusions and improve robustness in complex manipulation tasks.
> - A novel VR teleoperation method that decouples real-time view rendering from asynchronous robot motion effectively mitigates motion sickness, enabling practical collection of human demonstrations that include active gaze behaviors.
> - Utilizing powerful pre-trained visual encoders like DINOv2 is essential for effectively leveraging active sensory input and achieving strong performance in real-world robot manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "Vision in Action" addresses a critical gap in the field of robot manipulation: the lack of robust, learned active perception strategies, particularly in the context of imitation learning for complex, bimanual tasks.

**Historical Context of Active Perception:** Active perception, where a system dynamically changes its viewpoint or sensing parameters to gather task-relevant information, has a long history in robotics and computer vision, dating back to seminal works by Bajcsy (1988) and Itti & Koch (2001). Early approaches often relied on hand-crafted heuristics or saliency maps to guide gaze, or formulated next-best-view problems for scene reconstruction. While effective for specific perceptual goals, these methods often did not fully integrate perception with complex manipulation actions, nor did they scale well across diverse tasks without significant re-engineering. Reinforcement learning (RL) has recently explored active vision, but its application to real-world, complex manipulation remains challenging due to sim-to-real gaps and sample efficiency.

**Current Landscape of Robot Imitation Learning:** In recent years, imitation learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for teaching robots complex behaviors from human demonstrations. Breakthroughs like ALOHA and Diffusion Policy have shown impressive results in bimanual manipulation, often leveraging low-cost hardware and large datasets. However, a significant limitation of most existing imitation learning systems is their reliance on *passive vision*, typically from wrist-mounted cameras or fixed third-person cameras.
- **Limitations of Passive Vision:** Wrist cameras, while providing close-up views, are constrained by arm movements and frequently suffer from occlusions, especially in cluttered environments or during interactive tasks (e.g., peeking inside a bag). Fixed third-person cameras offer a broader view but lack the flexibility to focus on task-critical regions or resolve occlusions.
- **Human-Robot Observation Mismatch:** Critically, humans performing manipulation tasks naturally employ active gaze, shifting their attention and viewpoint to search, track, and focus. When robots learn from such human demonstrations but perceive the world passively, a fundamental observation mismatch arises, hindering the learning of truly effective visuomotor policies.

**ViA's Position and Contribution:** "Vision in Action" directly tackles this observation mismatch and the limitations of passive vision by integrating *learned active perception* into an imitation learning framework. It differentiates itself by:
- **Focusing on Real-World Application:** Unlike many active vision works that are simulation-based or use highly specialized hardware, ViA proposes a practical, real-world system using off-the-shelf components.
- **Learning from Human Demonstrations:** Instead of hand-designing gaze strategies or relying on complex RL, ViA learns active perceptual behaviors directly from human operators who are also employing active vision. This directly captures human intent and strategies in context.
- **Addressing System-Level Challenges:** The paper specifically highlights and overcomes significant engineering hurdles:
 - **Flexible Gaze Hardware:** Moving beyond limited 2-DoF necks by employing a 6-DoF robot arm as an "active neck."
 - **Teleoperation Latency:** Introducing a novel VR teleoperation interface that mitigates motion sickness by decoupling human view rendering from robot motion via an intermediate 3D scene representation. This is a significant improvement over prior VR teleoperation systems that stream raw RGB video and suffer from motion-to-photon latency.
 - **Scalable Learning:** Demonstrating that a Diffusion Policy, combined with a powerful pre-trained vision encoder (DINOv2) and active head camera inputs, can learn robust active perception without task-specific assumptions.

In essence, ViA pushes the boundaries of robot manipulation by demonstrating that equipping robots with learned, human-like active perception is crucial for handling visual occlusions and achieving high success rates in complex, multi-stage tasks. It offers a scalable and practical solution that bridges the gap between sophisticated human perceptual abilities and current robot learning capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the "Vision in Action" (ViA) system is to enable bimanual robot manipulation systems to learn and execute **task-relevant active perceptual strategies**—such as searching, tracking, and focusing—directly from human demonstrations.

This primary objective is motivated by several critical observations and limitations in contemporary robot manipulation:

1. **Inherent Activity of Human Perception:** Humans do not perceive the world passively. When performing complex tasks, especially those involving physical interaction or visual occlusions, they actively move their heads and eyes to scan the environment, resolve ambiguities, reduce occlusions, and focus attention on critical areas. For instance, retrieving an object from a bag involves scanning to locate the bag, peeking inside to find the object, and focusing for precise grasping.

2. **Deficiencies of Passive Vision in Current Robot Systems:** Most modern robot imitation learning systems predominantly rely on static viewpoints (fixed third-person cameras) or wrist-mounted cameras.
 - **Wrist Camera Limitations:** Wrist cameras are constrained by the robot's arm movements, meaning their viewpoint is dictated by manipulation requirements rather than perceptual needs. This often leads to severe visual occlusions, rendering them ineffective in scenarios where critical information is blocked.
 - **Fixed Camera Limitations:** Fixed cameras provide a static perspective, unable to adapt to dynamic scene changes or occlusions, and failing to capture the rich, adaptable perceptual behaviors that humans naturally exhibit.
 - **Observation Mismatch:** This creates a fundamental "observation mismatch" during imitation learning: the human operator sees the world with active perception, while the robot is trained on or attempts to act with passive, often insufficient, visual inputs. This discrepancy ultimately hinders the robot's ability to learn robust and generalizable policies.

3. **Significant System-Level Challenges for Active Perception:** Despite its recognized importance, active perception has been largely neglected in practical robotic systems due to several engineering complexities:
 - **Hardware for Flexible Gaze Control:** Replicating human-like eye, neck, and torso coordination in robots is challenging. Most robot "necks" are limited to 2-degrees of freedom (DoF), restricting their ability to achieve diverse viewpoints.
 - **Synchronized Camera-Gaze Movements (Teleoperation Latency):** For human demonstrations, VR teleoperation is a powerful tool to capture human active perception. However, achieving precise synchronization between human head movements and robot camera movements, with low latency, is difficult. High motion-to-photon latency (delay between user movement and visual feedback) causes severe motion sickness in VR, making data collection impractical.
 - **Scalability of Active Perception Strategies:** Prior attempts to implement active perception often relied on hand-crafted heuristics (e.g., image filters, object detectors for saliency) or model-based uncertainty reduction. Such methods are difficult to generalize across diverse tasks and lack the scalability needed for real-world complexity.

To address these motivations, the **key objectives** of the ViA system are embodied in its design choices:
- To design a **flexible robot neck** using an off-the-shelf 6-DoF arm to mimic the full range of human upper-body gaze movements.
- To develop an innovative **VR-based teleoperation interface** that mitigates motion sickness by decoupling real-time view rendering from asynchronous robot physical movement updates, thereby creating a shared observation space for effective human data collection.
- To demonstrate that **learning active perception strategies directly from human demonstrations** (via behavior cloning with a robust visuomotor policy) is a scalable and effective approach, eliminating the need for hand-crafted heuristics or complex reinforcement learning in real-world scenarios.

By achieving these objectives, ViA aims to significantly enhance robot manipulation capabilities in complex, visually occluded, and multi-stage tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Vision in Action" (ViA) system integrates novel hardware design, an innovative teleoperation interface, and a robust policy learning framework to enable robots to learn active perception from human demonstrations.

#### 3.1. Hardware Design

ViA employs a pragmatic and effective hardware setup designed to facilitate bimanual manipulation with human-like active perception:
- **Robot Neck:** Instead of a custom-designed anthropomorphic neck, ViA uses an **off-the-shelf 6-DoF ARX5 robot arm** as the robot's neck. This provides a high degree of flexibility, allowing the camera to mimic the full range of motion achieved by coordinated human head and torso movements.
- **Active Head Camera:** An **iPhone 15 Pro** is mounted on the end-effector of the robot neck. The iPhone serves as the primary visual sensor, streaming real-time RGB-D data and synchronized camera pose information. Its built-in depth sensor and high-quality camera are suitable for this purpose.
- **Bimanual Manipulators:** For manipulation, the system utilizes **two additional 6-DoF ARX5 robot arms**, each equipped with a fin-ray parallel-jaw gripper. These arms are mounted on custom 3D-printed shoulder structures, enabling versatile bimanual interaction with the environment.

#### 3.2. Teleoperation Interface (Key Innovation)

The teleoperation interface is central to ViA's ability to capture human active perception strategies while ensuring operator comfort. It addresses the critical issue of "motion-to-photon latency" common in VR teleoperation.
- **Bimanual Exoskeleton for Arms:** For controlling the two robot arms, a full-scale bimanual exoskeleton (inspired by GELLO) is used, providing a direct joint-to-joint mapping between human and robot.
- **VR Interface for Head (Active Perception):** A VR headset allows the user to control the active head camera's pose and receive visual feedback. The core innovation lies in its ability to decouple the user's instantaneous viewpoint from the robot's physical movement.
 - **Challenge: Motion-to-Photon Latency:** Traditional VR teleoperation directly streams live RGB video from the robot, leading to latency from both video transmission and robot control execution. This delay causes a mismatch between the user's head motion and the visual feedback, inducing motion sickness.
 - **Solution: Intermediate 3D Scene Representation and View Decoupling:**
 1. **Point Cloud Construction:** The robot's active head camera continuously captures RGB-D frames. These frames are transformed into a **world-frame point cloud**, which serves as an intermediate 3D representation of the scene. The robot's head pose (camera extrinsics) is used for this transformation.
 2. **Low-Latency View Rendering (for User):** The VR display renders stereo RGB views for the human operator *in real-time* (at ~150 Hz) based on the user's *latest head pose* in the world frame. This rendering is performed *from the point cloud*, not from live robot camera feeds. This ensures instant visual feedback aligned with the user's physical head movements, significantly reducing perceived latency and motion sickness. Minor missing information in the rendered view (due to robot not physically being at that exact viewpoint yet) is deemed acceptable for perceptual continuity.
 3. **Asynchronous Robot Head Update:** The robot's physical head pose is updated asynchronously and at a lower frequency (10 Hz) based on *aggregated* user head movements over a longer time interval. This means the robot doesn't try to mirror every subtle head twitch, but rather follows the general movement, allowing for smoother physical control and less demanding real-time synchronization requirements.
This decoupled approach allows ViA to balance low visual latency for the user (<7ms) with a practical control frequency for the robot (<10Hz), enabling effective data collection for complex tasks.

#### 3.3. Learning Active Perception for Bimanual Manipulation

The collected human demonstrations, which inherently include active perception strategies, are used to train a visuomotor policy:
- **Policy Architecture:** The policy network is based on **Diffusion Policy**, a state-of-the-art framework for visuomotor control via action diffusion.
- **Observations:** At each time step, the policy receives:
 - An RGB image (`I_t`) from the active head camera.
 - A proprioceptive state (`P_t`) including the end-effector poses (position and quaternion) of the neck, left arm, and right arm, as well as the two gripper widths.
- **Visual Encoder:** A **DINOv2 pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT)** is used as the visual encoder for the active head camera's RGB image. The 384-dimensional classification token provides a compact, semantically rich representation of the visual scene. The choice of DINOv2 is critical as it provides strong visual priors and semantic understanding without task-specific fine-tuning.
- **Actions:** The policy outputs a sequence of future actions (`A_t`) for a prediction horizon (`n_p=16`). These actions comprise the future end-effector poses of the neck and arms in the world frame, along with gripper widths. An execution horizon (`n_a=8`) dictates how many of the predicted actions are executed on the physical robot (via inverse kinematics). The policy operates at 10 Hz.
- **Learning Paradigm:** The policy learns through **behavior cloning** on the collected human demonstrations, directly mapping observed states (active head camera view + proprioception) to demonstrated actions (bimanual arm movements + active neck movements). This allows the robot to implicitly learn the task-relevant active perceptual strategies that the human operator naturally employed.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Tasks

Three challenging multi-stage bimanual manipulation tasks involving significant visual occlusions were designed to evaluate the system:
1. **Bag Task:** Object retrieval from a bag, requiring interactive perception (opening the bag, peeking inside, taking out the object).
2. **Cup Task:** Arranging cups, involving active viewpoint switching (picking a cup from a cluttered shelf, handing over, placing it under another shelf).
3. **Lime & Pot Task:** Bimanual coordination and precise alignment (finding a lime, placing it in a pot, bimanually lifting the pot, precisely aligning it on a trivet).
These tasks highlight the necessity of active perception for success, as traditional fixed or wrist-mounted cameras would frequently suffer from occlusions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the ViA system yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the critical role of active perception and validating the proposed hardware and teleoperation interface designs.

#### 4.1. Policy Learning Camera Setup Comparison

This experiment compared ViA's active head camera setup against two common baselines: [Active Head & Wrist Cameras] and [Chest & Wrist Cameras]. All policies were trained using views recorded from the same human demonstrations.

- **ViA (Ours) vs. Baselines:** ViA, utilizing only a single active head camera as visual input, **consistently outperformed both alternative camera setups** across all three complex, multi-stage manipulation tasks.
- **Impact of Active Perception:** The results clearly highlight the importance of active perception. ViA improved average task performance by **45%** compared to the [Chest & Wrist Cameras] setup. The latter often failed due to inherent visual occlusions, as wrist cameras were blocked by objects (e.g., upper shelf tier in the cup task) and the fixed chest camera lacked the necessary detailed views.
- **Unexpected Result: Adding Wrist Cameras Decreases Performance:** Surprisingly, augmenting ViA with additional wrist camera observations ([Active Head & Wrist Cameras]) led to an average **18.33% decrease in success rate**. The authors hypothesize two main reasons:
 1. The active head camera alone provides **sufficient task-relevant information**, as the human teleoperator successfully completes the tasks relying solely on this view.
 2. Adding wrist cameras, despite providing more input dimensions, often introduces **redundant or noisy observations**, especially due to frequent occlusions experienced by wrist cameras during manipulation. In a data-constrained imitation learning setting, this added complexity can hinder learning by increasing the risk of overfitting or distracting the model with less informative inputs.

#### 4.2. Policy Learning Visual Representation Comparison

This section evaluated the effectiveness of different visual encoders for the active head camera input, keeping the active perception mechanism constant.

- **DINOv2 (ViA) Superiority:** ViA, leveraging a **DINOv2 pretrained ViT backbone** for image encoding, achieved the highest final-stage success rate across all three tasks.
- **Benefits of Semantic Understanding:** The DINOv2 backbone provides superior semantic understanding, which proved crucial for tasks requiring active search, such as the "Lime & Pot" task where the robot needed to actively search for the lime.
- **Baseline Limitations:**
 - **[ResNet-DP]** (ResNet-18 pretrained on ImageNet) showed lower performance, indicating that a more powerful and semantically rich visual representation like DINOv2 is beneficial for learning robust active perception strategies.
 - **[DP3]** (trained from scratch using world-frame point clouds) performed the worst, often exhibiting "hallucination" by misinterpreting the scene or failing on precise tasks (e.g., imprecise grasping of the bag handle). This suggests that training visual representations from scratch, especially for 3D data, lacks the strong pretrained visual priors necessary for robust performance in these complex, real-world tasks, particularly in a low-data regime.

#### 4.3. Teleoperation Interface Comparison (User Study)

A user study with 8 participants compared ViA's point-cloud rendering teleoperation method with a conventional stereo-RGB streaming approach.

- **Reduced Motion Sickness:** ViA's point-cloud rendering method **significantly reduced motion sickness** among participants (average sickness level of 2.0 vs. 3.375 for RGB streaming). This validates the core innovation of decoupling view rendering from robot physical movement.
- **User Preference:** As a direct consequence of reduced discomfort, **6 out of 8 participants preferred ViA's system**.
- **Data Collection Time:** ViA's method resulted in slightly longer data collection times, indicating that users might require a bit more practice or effort to adapt to the point-cloud visualization compared to direct video streaming. However, the trade-off for reduced motion sickness and increased comfort makes it a more practical solution for extended data collection.

In summary, the results unequivocally demonstrate that integrating active perception, enabled by a flexible head camera and a low-latency VR teleoperation system, dramatically enhances the performance and robustness of robot manipulation policies learned via imitation. The choice of a powerful, pre-trained visual encoder is also critical for leveraging this active sensory input effectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Vision in Action" (ViA) system represents a significant step forward in robotic manipulation, with profound implications for the development of more capable and autonomous robots. Its significance stems from addressing a fundamental limitation in current robot learning paradigms and offering practical, scalable solutions.

#### 5.1. Bridging the Active Perception Gap in Robot Learning

The most critical impact of ViA is its direct and effective integration of active perception into imitation learning. By demonstrating that robots can learn sophisticated active perceptual strategies (like searching, tracking, and focusing) directly from human demonstrations, the work closes a long-standing gap. Most prior robot learning systems treat perception as a passive input, leading to fragility in complex, occluded, or dynamic environments. ViA shows that aligning "what the human sees" with "what the robot learns from" is paramount for robust task execution, especially in situations where critical visual information is initially hidden or ambiguous.

#### 5.2. Practical and Accessible Hardware Design

The decision to use an off-the-shelf 6-DoF robot arm as a "robot neck" is a highly practical and impactful contribution. It bypasses the need for complex, custom-engineered anthropomorphic heads or limited 2-DoF pan-tilt units, making the concept of a flexible, active camera more accessible for researchers and practitioners. This design choice simplifies hardware development while achieving human-like range of motion, thus accelerating the adoption of active perception in robot systems.

#### 5.3. Novel Teleoperation Paradigm for Data Collection

The innovative VR-based teleoperation interface, particularly its strategy of decoupling real-time view rendering (from a 3D point cloud) from asynchronous robot motion updates, is a game-changer for human-in-the-loop robot learning. Motion sickness is a significant barrier to collecting high-quality, long-duration human demonstrations in VR for robotics. ViA's solution, validated by user studies, effectively mitigates this discomfort, enabling more comfortable and productive data collection. This approach has potential broad applicability beyond active perception, for any teleoperation scenario where low latency and user comfort are crucial. It paves the way for richer, more natural human teaching signals for robots.

#### 5.4. Enhanced Robustness in Challenging Environments

The experimental results vividly demonstrate that ViA significantly outperforms passive camera setups (wrist and chest cameras) in complex, multi-stage bimanual manipulation tasks involving considerable visual occlusions. The ability of the robot to actively adjust its viewpoint to find objects inside bags, peek under shelves, or precisely align components makes it far more robust and adaptable to real-world clutter and uncertainties. This is a crucial step towards deploying robots in unstructured human environments.

#### 5.5. Scalable Learning through Pre-trained Models

The adoption of a powerful, pre-trained visual encoder like DINOv2 highlights a growing trend in robot learning: leveraging large-scale visual models to provide strong semantic understanding and visual priors. This approach allows the policy to learn complex behaviors from relatively fewer demonstrations, demonstrating a scalable path for learning active perception without resorting to computationally expensive reinforcement learning or task-specific manual engineering.

#### 5.6. Paving the Way for Future Research

ViA opens up several exciting avenues for future research:
- **Improved Teleoperation Fidelity:** Further enhancing the visual fidelity of the 3D scene representation (e.g., using dynamic scene fusion or radiance fields) and exploring alternative interfaces like AR glasses to capture human intent.
- **Advanced Hardware Designs:** Optimizing robot hardware to more closely mimic human whole-body active perception, potentially integrating with mobile manipulation platforms for even broader applications.
- **Sophisticated Policy Learning:** Developing methods for fusing multi-camera observations more intelligently, incorporating language conditioning for higher-level reasoning, and endowing policies with memory for long-horizon search tasks.

In conclusion, "Vision in Action" is a landmark paper that not only underscores the fundamental importance of active perception for robust robot manipulation but also provides a comprehensive, practical, and effective system-level solution. By enabling robots to learn how to "see in action" directly from human expertise, it brings us closer to a future where robots can operate more intelligently and autonomously in the complex, dynamic environments of our everyday lives.
