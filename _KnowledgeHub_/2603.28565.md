# StreamingVLA: Streaming Vision-Language-Action Model with Action Flow Matching and Adaptive Early Observation

> [!summary] Summary
> The StreamingVLA framework introduces a method to reduce latency and execution halting in large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, making them more suitable for resource-constrained edge platforms. It achieves this through action flow matching and adaptive early observation, resulting in a 1.57x speedup and a 6.45x reduction in halting time with minimal impact on task success rates.

## Problem

- Large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models suffer from high latency due to sequential processing stages (observation, action generation, execution).
- Robotic systems exhibit stuttered, non-fluent movements and significant idle periods (halting) between VLA stages.
- Prior efficiency methods like action chunking or early observation either partially address the problem or lead to performance degradation.

## Method

- A ==streaming paradigm== that enables asynchronous and parallelized execution across all Vision-Language-Action (VLA) stages.
- ==State-based Modeling== for ==Action Flow Matching (AFM)== reformulates action prediction to allow single-action generation and execution overlap.
- An ==Action Saliency-aware Adaptive Early Observation (AEO)== mechanism selectively delays observation for highly salient actions, preserving performance during parallel execution.

## Results

- **StreamingVLA** achieved a **1.57x** speedup in time per action and a **6.45x** reduction in halting time, maintaining a success rate comparable to baseline models (**94.9%** vs **95.1%**).
- The Action Flow Matching (AFM) component alone delivered a **2.21x** speedup and a **3.05x** reduction in halting time, indicating its significant contribution to efficiency and performance.
- The Adaptive Early Observation (AEO) scheme successfully preserved task performance (**94.9%** success rate) while enabling substantial speedup, outperforming both naive and action-norm based early observation strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action trajectories can be effectively modeled as continuous flows, enabling individual actions to be streamed and executed sequentially rather than in large, discrete chunks.
> - Architectural adjustments like shared normalization statistics and offset term removal are necessary to preserve additivity in large VLA models when using state-based action modeling.
> - Introducing an action saliency metric, based on an action's predicted impact on VLM feature representations, allows for adaptive early observation without sacrificing task performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant advancement in embodied artificial intelligence, leveraging the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) to enable natural language-driven perception and control in robotic systems. These models are designed to interpret complex instructions, understand environmental contexts through visual input, and generate corresponding physical actions. Examples such as RT-1, RT-2, and the $\pi_0$ model series have demonstrated robust performance in various robotic tasks, indicating a promising direction for creating generalized foundation models for embodied agents.

Despite their capabilities, large-scale VLA models encounter substantial efficiency challenges, particularly when deployed on resource-constrained edge platforms. The computational overhead of these models often results in high latency and stuttered execution, limiting their practical applicability in real-world scenarios.

Existing research efforts to address VLA efficiency can be broadly categorized:
- **Action Chunking**: Techniques like action chunking aim to improve efficiency by generating multiple actions in parallel for each observation. While this reduces the overall latency by processing actions in batches, it does not fully resolve the sequential dependencies between observation, action generation, and execution stages.
- **Model Compression**: Approaches involving pruning, quantization, or token compression for VLM components target the reduction of computational costs within individual stages of the VLA pipeline. These methods can decrease the latency of specific operations (e.g., observation processing or action generation) but do not inherently minimize the idle periods or "halting" that occurs between the completion of one stage and the initiation of the next.
- **Parallelized Execution with Early Observation**: Recent studies, including RTC, VLASH, and SmolVLA, explore overlapping action execution with VLM-based observation processing. These methods typically initiate VLM observation midway through an ongoing action sequence, aiming to reduce the total waiting time. VLASH, for instance, fine-tunes models to mitigate performance degradation caused by early observation.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a systematic approach to enable asynchronous and parallelized execution across *all* VLA stages, specifically targeting both overall latency and execution fluency (halting time). It introduces a novel dimension of parallelism by overlapping action generation with action execution, in addition to improving the overlap between action execution and subsequent observation processing. This comprehensive "streaming" approach aims to overcome the limitations of synchronous pipelines and existing partial parallelization strategies by fundamentally restructuring how VLA stages interact.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to mitigate the high computational cost, significant latency, and inconsecutive execution (halting) commonly observed in large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly for deployment on resource-constrained edge platforms.

The motivation stems from several identified challenges in current VLA model execution:

1. **High Latency**: The standard VLA pipeline operates sequentially through three main stages: "observation" (VLM processing), "gen-action" (action expert generating actions), and "execution" (robot arm performing actions). Each stage must complete before the next can begin. This synchronous processing accumulates latency, leading to a high "time per action" ($T_{action}$), which is defined as the sum of latencies of all stages divided by the number of valid actions (horizon $h$). This high $T_{action}$ directly impacts the responsiveness and speed of embodied agents.

2. **Stuttered and Inconsecutive Execution (Halting)**: A significant portion of the total latency manifests as idle periods, referred to as "halting gap" ($T_{halt}$). In a synchronous pipeline, $T_{halt}$ is equivalent to the combined latency of the observation and action generation stages ($T_{observe} + T_{gen-action}$). During these periods, the robotic arm stalls, resulting in jerky and non-fluent movements. This lack of fluency degrades the user experience and can hinder task success, especially in dynamic or time-sensitive environments.

3. **Limitations of Existing Solutions**:
 - While action chunking improves overall efficiency by generating multiple actions concurrently, the entire chunk must be generated before any action can be executed, still introducing delays.
 - Model compression techniques reduce the latency of individual stages ($T_{observe}$ or $T_{gen-action}$) but cannot inherently eliminate the $T_{halt}$ gap, as the sequential dependency remains.
 - Prior parallelized execution methods, primarily focusing on "early observation," overlap VLM processing with action execution. However, these methods often face a trade-off between speedup and performance degradation, as uniformly premature observation might discard critical information or misinterpret environmental changes.

To address these issues, the research proposes a "streaming" paradigm that allows VLA stages to operate asynchronously and in parallel. The overarching goal is to minimize both $T_{action}$ (for overall speed) and $T_{halt}$ (for fluency) without sacrificing task performance. This is formulated by introducing concepts of overlapped latency ($O_{ge}$ for generation-execution and $O_{oe}$ for observation-execution) into the latency equations, aiming to drive $T_{halt}$ towards zero and substantially reduce $T_{action}$.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The StreamingVLA framework is designed to enable asynchronous and parallelized processing across the VLA model's stages, thereby reducing latency and improving execution fluency. This is achieved through two primary technical contributions: State-based Modeling for Action Flow Matching and Action Saliency-aware Adaptive Early Observation.

#### 3.1. State-based Modeling for Action Flow Matching

This component aims to overlap action generation and execution, addressing the $O_{ge}$ term. Current action chunking methods generate a batch of actions that can only be used once the entire chunk is denoised, leading to waiting periods. StreamingVLA reformulates action modeling to allow actions to be generated and executed one by one.

**Primary Formulation of Action Flow Matching:**
The approach adapts the Streaming Flow Policy (SFP) which models action trajectories as continuous flows rather than discrete, denoised chunks.
- **Action Flow Definition**: Instead of predicting absolute action values from noise, the model learns the velocity field $v_{\theta}(x, t|o)$ of an action trajectory $\xi(t)$. Here, $x$ represents the state (initially noisy) and $t$ is the normalized action index within the horizon. The flow transitions from a noise distribution $p(A_0^h)$ to a data distribution $p(A_1^h)$.
- **Training Objective**: The model is trained by minimizing a conditional flow matching loss, which measures the difference between the predicted velocity field $v_{\theta}(x, t|o)$ and the ground-truth velocity field $v_{\xi}(x, t)$ along sampled trajectories $\xi$.
- **Inference**: Once trained, the learned velocity field can be integrated to iteratively obtain the state $x_t$. Each action $a_t$ is then derived as $a_t = x_{t+1} - x_t = v_{\theta}(x_t, t|o)\Delta t$. This allows immediate conversion of velocity estimations into individual action tokens for execution, enabling streaming generation.

**Challenges for Large-Scale VLA Models and Solutions:**
While effective for simpler tasks and smaller models, adapting this formulation to large-scale VLA models and complex benchmarks like LIBERO introduces two non-trivial challenges:

1. **Extended Formulation of State-based Action Modeling for Complex Control Schemes**:
 - **Problem**: In many mainstream benchmarks, generated actions are not direct physical states but inputs to an independent controller (e.g., robosuite's OSC\_POSE) that indirectly affects the robot's physical state. This non-linearity makes the direct physical state assumption of the original SFP formulation inapplicable.
 - **Solution**: The paper introduces an "action-space state" that is updated by the generated actions. To maintain alignment with the physical-space state, the training procedure is modified: complete action-space states are precomputed along the trajectory, and the initial action-space state corresponding to the physical state is provided during training of each sub-trajectory. During inference, the action-space state is continually updated based on the executed actions.

2. **Architectural Adjustment for Additivity Preservation**:
 - **Problem**: State-based action modeling relies on the additivity property ($x_t + a_t = x_{t+1}$), where states are updated cumulatively by historical actions. Normalization layers common in large VLA architectures, designed to stabilize training, can break this additivity.
 - **Solution**: Two modifications are proposed:
 - **Shared Normalization Statistics**: Normalization statistics (e.g., $q_{max}$, $q_{min}$) are shared across all action-space states and actions to ensure consistent scaling.
 - **Offset Term Removal**: The offset term is removed from the normalization layer, resulting in a simpler scaling ($a'_t = a_t / scale$). This modification empirically helps stabilize training while preserving additivity.

#### 3.2. Action Saliency-aware Adaptive Early Observation

This component aims to overlap action execution with VLM observation processing, addressing the $O_{oe}$ term, while preserving performance. Prior early observation methods typically use uniform strategies, leading to performance degradation when "highly salient" actions (those causing significant environmental changes) are prematurely skipped.

**Concept of Action Saliency**:
The core idea is that the "saliency" of an action (its influence on subsequent observations) varies. Skipping a highly salient action can lead to erroneous VLM observations and poor decision-making, whereas skipping a low-saliency action has negligible impact. A uniform early observation strategy fails to differentiate these cases.

**Action Saliency-aware Adaptive Early Observation Scheme**:
The proposed scheme adaptively decides when to perform early observation, avoiding it for high-saliency actions.
- **Measuring Action Saliency**: Instead of using the norm of action tokens, which showed limited effectiveness, saliency is measured by the influence of an action on the *observation processing result* (i.e., the VLM's feature representation of the environment). This is more directly relevant to future action generation.
- **Lightweight Predictor**:
 - **Architecture**: A transformer-based model.
 - **Inputs**: Image embedding (from the VLM's Vision Transformer, ViT) representing the current environment, and the sequence of remaining actions as conditional control signals.
 - **Training**: Trained to estimate the *change* in image embeddings (∆embedding) after executing the remaining actions, using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss.
 - **Inference**: During inference, the L2-norm of the predicted ∆embedding serves as a visual variance indicator. If this value exceeds a predefined threshold, early observation is inhibited for that step.
- **Efficiency**: The predictor introduces minimal overhead (approximately 5% of model inference time) and has a significantly lower training cost compared to fine-tuning the entire VLA model. This allows it to augment existing early observation techniques with adaptive capabilities efficiently.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The StreamingVLA framework was evaluated using the $\pi_{0.5}$-Libero model, a state-of-the-art VLA, across four task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long) from the LIBERO simulation environment. Performance was measured by Success Rate (%), Time per Action ($T_{action}$, ms), and Halting time ($T_{halt}$, ms).

**Overall Performance and Efficiency (Table 1):**

- **Speedup and Fluency**: StreamingVLA (combining Action Flow Matching and Adaptive Early Observation, AFM+AEO) achieved an average success rate of 94.9%, which is comparable to the baseline $\pi_{0.5}$ (h=10) model's 95.1%, indicating minimal performance degradation (less than 0.5%). Concurrently, StreamingVLA demonstrated a 1.57× speedup over $\pi_{0.5}$ (h=10) and a 2.35× speedup over $\pi_{0.5}$ (h=5, replan=5). The $T_{action}$ was reduced to 31.625 ms from 49.9 ms ($\pi_{0.5}$, h=10).
- **Halting Reduction**: A significant improvement in execution fluency was observed, with the halting time ($T_{halt}$) reduced from approximately 232.3 ms to 36.0 ms, representing a 6.45× reduction.
- **AFM Contribution**: When Action Flow Matching (AFM) was applied alone, the model achieved a 97.1% success rate, 33.7 ms $T_{action}$ (2.21× speedup), and 76.1 ms $T_{halt}$ (3.05× halting reduction) compared to $\pi_{0.5}$ (h=5/10), demonstrating that AFM itself provides substantial benefits in both performance and efficiency.

**Comparison with Baseline Methods (Table 1):**

- **RTC (Real-Time Chunking)**: Showed some halting reduction but incurred a substantial drop in success rate (down to 58.55%), particularly for long-horizon tasks, indicating insufficient compensation for information loss due to premature observation.
- **SmolVLA**: Achieved moderate halting reduction but minimal improvement in $T_{action}$.
- **VLASH**: While effective in maintaining success rates under acceleration through training-based rectification, StreamingVLA demonstrated a superior speed-performance trade-off by enabling parallel action generation and execution.
- **Naive Early Observation (NEO)**: Significantly reduced latency and halting but at the cost of severe performance degradation (success rate dropped from 97.1% to 86.2%).
- **Action-Norm Based Adaptive Observation (ANAO)**: Improved performance over NEO (90.0% success) but still showed noticeable degradation compared to the full StreamingVLA.
- **Adaptive Early Observation (AEO)**: Successfully preserved performance (94.9%) while delivering substantial speedup, outperforming both NEO and ANAO by intelligently avoiding early observation for salient actions.

**Ablation Studies (Table 2):**

- **Effectiveness of Action Flow Matching (AFM)**: While not reported in isolation, AFM's presence as a foundational component in combinations consistently led to improved stability and performance, confirming its role in providing a structured optimization objective for continuous action flow.
- **Critical Role of Normalization Modification (NM)**: NM was found to be essential for the effective training of the AFM formulation. Without NM, the model failed to train stably. Though NM alone with SA led to a 61.8% success rate, it was a necessary enabler for subsequent improvements.
- **Importance of State-based Alignment (SA)**: Adding SA on top of AFM and NM dramatically boosted the success rate to 97.1%, surpassing the baseline. It also significantly reduced $T_{action}$ (from 49.9 ms to 33.7 ms) and $T_{halt}$ (from 230.78 ms to 76.1 ms), confirming its crucial role in maintaining consistency between action-space and physical-space states for both accuracy and efficiency.
- **Impact of Early Observation (EO) Schemes**: Naive EO achieved the lowest latency and halting but at a significant performance cost (86.2% success). Random EO provided a better trade-off, but Adaptive EO, with the same average number of early observed actions, further improved the success rate to 94.9% (from 90.9%), demonstrating the advantage of informed scheduling.

**Real-World Deployment (Appendix A):**

- A pick-and-place task on a Franka Panda robot showed that StreamingVLA achieved an average latency of 170.88 ms per action, compared to 271.49 ms per action for the original $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline. This indicates that StreamingVLA's efficiency gains translate to practical robotic control.

**Runtime Breakdown (Appendix C.1):**

- Detailed profiling confirmed calculated improvements: a 2.40× latency reduction and a 6.71× halting gap improvement, validating the analytical framework.

**Visualization (Appendix C.3):**

- Video simulations of various tasks (e.g., "open the top drawer and put the bowl inside") illustrated StreamingVLA's ability to complete tasks much faster and without noticeable pauses, in contrast to $\pi_{0.5}$ which exhibited numerous interruptions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The StreamingVLA framework offers several significant contributions and has the potential to impact the field of embodied AI in various ways:

1. **Enhanced Real-World Applicability**: By substantially reducing both latency ($T_{action}$) and execution halting ($T_{halt}$) without compromising task performance, StreamingVLA makes VLA models more viable for deployment on resource-constrained edge platforms. This improved efficiency is critical for enabling real-time, responsive, and natural interactions between robots and their environments, moving beyond laboratory settings to practical applications.

2. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction**: The reduction in execution halting leads to smoother and more fluent robot movements. This can significantly improve the perceived intelligence and reliability of robotic systems, fostering more intuitive and safer human-robot collaboration. Robots that operate fluently are less likely to be perceived as unreliable or frustrating, which is a key factor in their adoption.

3. **Methodological Advancements in VLA Design**:
 - The introduction of **state-based modeling for action flow matching** to enable asynchronous action generation and execution is a novel approach. It addresses a fundamental limitation of synchronous action chunking by allowing actions to be processed and executed one by one, thereby overlapping computation and physical movement.
 - The development of an **action saliency-aware adaptive early observation mechanism** provides a more robust and performance-preserving strategy for overlapping VLM observation and action execution. By intelligently identifying and prioritizing salient actions, the framework avoids the performance degradation often associated with uniform early observation techniques. This mechanism is also lightweight and compatible with existing VLA architectures.

4. **Complementary to Existing Efficiency Techniques**: The "streaming" optimization achieved by StreamingVLA can be applied in parallel with other efficiency-enhancing methods, such as model compression (e.g., pruning, quantization). This synergistic potential suggests that even greater efficiency gains could be realized by combining these approaches, further extending the operational capabilities of VLA models.

5. **Foundation for Future Research**: The systematic runtime analysis and the proposed two-pronged approach (action flow matching and adaptive early observation) provide a comprehensive framework for understanding and optimizing VLA execution. This work can serve as a foundation for future research in areas such as predictive control, dynamic task replanning, and energy-efficient robotic operations, where timely and fluent action execution is paramount.

In summary, StreamingVLA addresses critical efficiency bottlenecks in VLA models, enabling them to operate with reduced latency and enhanced fluency. This represents a step towards making advanced VLA capabilities more practical and deployable for a wide range of real-world robotic applications.
