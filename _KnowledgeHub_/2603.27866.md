# Wan-R1: Verifiable-Reinforcement Learning for Video Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Iowa State University and collaborating institutions introduce a reinforcement learning framework for video reasoning, utilizing an adapted Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with novel verifiable reward functions to overcome generalization limitations and reward hacking in video generation models. The method significantly improved exact match rates on maze-solving tasks (e.g., from 38.9% to 90.3% on Trapfield mazes) and enhanced generalization to unseen difficulties, textures, and maze types, while also outperforming baselines in robotic navigation by reducing displacement errors.

## Problem

- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) based video models for reasoning tasks, such as maze-solving, exhibit poor generalization to out-of-distribution scenarios (e.g., harder mazes, irregular layouts, novel textures), tending to memorize patterns rather than learn robust reasoning skills.
- Applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to video generation for reasoning is challenged by reward design; multimodal reward models are susceptible to "reward hacking," where models generate visually convincing but task-incorrect outputs, while sparse binary rewards provide insufficient learning signals.
- The computational cost associated with video generation, particularly during online RL training, can be prohibitive due to the high number of denoising steps required.

## Method

- The authors adapted ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== for ==flow-based video models== by converting the deterministic ODE sampling to a ==stochastic differential equation (SDE)== and computing group relative advantages for policy optimization.
- They designed ==verifiable reward functions== grounded in objective task metrics: for maze-solving, rewards include ==Exact Match==, ==Precision==, and ==Fidelity== (structural consistency); for robotic navigation, rewards use embedding-level metrics like ==mean frame cosine similarity==, ==temporal-order consistency==, and ==endpoint fidelity==.
- To accelerate training, the method employs reduced denoising steps during RL data collection, while maintaining higher steps for evaluation, enabling faster online learning without significant performance degradation.

## Results

- On VR-Bench maze-solving tasks, **Wan-R1** achieved substantial absolute gains in Exact Match (EM) over SFT baselines, for example, improving EM from **38.9%** to **90.3%** on Trapfield mazes and from **65.3%** to **94.4%** on 3D Mazes.
- The method demonstrated superior generalization, maintaining high precision (e.g., **68.4%** on Irregular Maze Hard vs. **5.8%** for SFT) when trained on easier difficulties and achieving robust performance on unseen visual textures (**79.2%** EM on 3D Maze with novel textures vs. **43.1%** for SFT).
- On the Target-Bench robotic navigation task, **Wan-R1** achieved the highest overall score, reducing Average Displacement Error (ADE) and Final Displacement Error (FDE) by approximately half and significantly lowering the Miss Rate compared to base models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Verifiable reward functions, relying on objective task metrics (e.g., trajectory matching or embedding similarity) rather than learned reward models, are essential to prevent "reward hacking" and provide effective learning signals for RL-driven video reasoning.
> - Dense reward components, such as the Precision Reward (R_PR) for partial progress in maze-solving, are crucial for stable and efficient learning, offering more granular guidance than sparse binary rewards.
> - Reinforcement Learning, particularly with moderate KL regularization and a 'cold start' from an SFT-trained model, significantly enhances the generalization capabilities of video generation models to unseen difficulties, textures, and task types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of video generation has progressed significantly with models such as Veo and Sora, which are capable of producing visually coherent content. This advancement has opened avenues for a "reasoning via video" paradigm, where models generate temporal visual sequences to capture spatial relationships, physical dynamics, and temporal causality, analogous to text-based "chains of thought." Recent studies have explored the application of video models to reasoning tasks like maze-solving and semantic navigation, which require spatial perception, path planning, and multi-step decision-making.

However, existing approaches, particularly those based on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using demonstration videos, exhibit limitations. SFT models tend to memorize training patterns, leading to poor generalization when confronted with out-of-distribution scenarios, such as mazes of higher difficulty, irregular layouts, or altered visual textures. This brittleness suggests that SFT primarily encourages surface-level heuristics rather than robust, transferable reasoning skills.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a method to improve generalization in generative models, particularly for aligning large language models with human preferences. In the visual domain, RL techniques like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have been adapted for generative tasks such as image-to-video synthesis. However, applying RL to video reasoning introduces a critical challenge: reward design. Multimodal reward models, which leverage vision-language understanding to assess video quality and task success, have shown susceptibility to "reward hacking." In this phenomenon, the generative model produces visually convincing outputs that satisfy the learned judge without genuinely completing the underlying task, leading to degenerate solutions.

This work addresses the generalization gap in video reasoning by integrating RL, specifically an adaptation of Flow-GRPO, with flow-based video models. It systematically investigates the reward design problem, proposing and evaluating verifiable reward functions designed to counteract reward hacking and provide effective learning signals for complex visual reasoning tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the generalization capabilities of video generation models for reasoning tasks by integrating reinforcement learning. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing Generalization Limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Current video generation models, when fine-tuned using SFT on demonstration videos, often fail to generalize effectively. For instance, a model trained on easy mazes struggles with harder configurations, and training on regular grids does not transfer to irregular or 3D layouts. This suggests SFT encourages memorization of training patterns rather than the acquisition of robust, transferable reasoning skills. The research aims to overcome this brittleness.

2. **Leveraging Reinforcement Learning for Generalization:** RL, through its ability to explore diverse solution strategies during training, is hypothesized to encourage models to learn more generalizable reasoning skills. The researchers motivate the adaptation of Flow-GRPO, an RL technique for generative models, to flow-based video generation to enable this exploration.

3. **Overcoming Challenges in Reward Design for Video RL:** A critical, yet underexplored, challenge in applying RL to video generation is designing effective reward functions.
 - **Reward Hacking:** Multimodal reward models, which might seem intuitive for evaluating video outputs, are susceptible to "reward hacking." The generator can learn to produce visually convincing but task-incorrect videos that satisfy the learned judge, as the output space is high-dimensional and continuous. This constitutes a major barrier to reliable RL training.
 - **Insufficient Learning Signal from Sparse Rewards:** Simple binary rewards (e.g., success/failure) are often too sparse in complex reasoning tasks, providing insufficient gradient information to guide effective learning, especially during early training stages.

4. **Developing Verifiable Reward Functions:** To address the reward design challenges, the central motivation is to design "verifiable reward functions" that are grounded in objective task metrics rather than subjective or learned evaluators. This involves:
 - For structured game environments (like mazes), extracting an objective trajectory and comparing it against a ground-truth solution to ensure the reward reflects true performance.
 - For robotic navigation tasks where ground truth is a reference video rollout, designing an embedding-level reward that measures frame-wise visual similarity, temporal consistency, and endpoint fidelity between generated and reference videos.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to establish verifiable reward design as a key enabler for robust video reasoning, allowing models to learn genuinely transferable reasoning abilities that go beyond mere visual imitation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes an approach to improve video generation reasoning through reinforcement learning, specifically by adapting Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to flow-based video models and designing verifiable reward functions.

#### 3.1. Task Formulation: Visual Trace Reasoning (VTR)

The primary task involves visual trace reasoning, where a model receives a prompt and an initial image (e.g., of a maze) and must generate a video depicting an agent navigating from a start to a goal position. This requires perceiving structure, planning a valid path, and generating temporally consistent motion.

#### 3.2. Background: Flow Matching

The base video generation models used are flow matching models. These models define a continuous transformation between noise and data through a learned velocity field. Training involves minimizing the difference between the predicted velocity field and the true velocity field along linear interpolations between data and noise. Inference generates samples by solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE) derived from the velocity field.

#### 3.3. RL for Flow Matching: Adaptation of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO is adopted as a scalable alternative to PPO. Key adaptations for flow models include:
- **ODE to SDE Conversion:** Standard flow models use deterministic ODE sampling, which lacks the stochasticity required for RL exploration. This work converts the ODE to an equivalent stochastic differential equation (SDE) by adding a noise term, preserving the marginal distribution while introducing randomness.
- **Group Relative Advantage:** To compute effective advantage estimates without a separate value network, GRPO samples a group of *G* videos for a given prompt-image pair. The advantage for each video is computed by normalizing its reward against the mean and standard deviation of rewards within that group. This helps in controlling for maze-specific factors and learning transferable skills.
- **Policy Optimization:** The policy is updated using a GRPO objective that maximizes a clipped surrogate objective, similar to PPO, with a KL regularization term to prevent large policy shifts from a reference policy.
- **Denoising Reduction:** To address the computational expense of video generation, the model uses fewer denoising steps during training data collection (e.g., `S_train = 30`) compared to evaluation (`S_infer = 50`). This accelerates online RL training without significantly sacrificing final performance.

#### 3.4. Verifiable Reward Design for Game Environments (Maze-solving)

The design of rewards for maze-solving tasks focuses on three desiderata: verifiability, density, and decomposability.
- **Trajectory Extraction:** For a generated video, the agent's trajectory (`τ_pred`) is extracted using object tracking, by following the agent's center position across frames.
- **Reward Components:**
 - **Exact Match Reward (R_EM):** A strict binary reward (0 or 1) indicating whether the generated trajectory precisely matches the ground-truth optimal path.
 - **Precision Reward (R_PR):** A softer metric that quantifies the proportion of consecutively correct steps along the optimal path, providing a denser signal for partial progress.
 - **Fidelity Reward (R_MF):** Measures the structural consistency of the maze layout across video frames. It penalizes unrealistic changes like disappearing walls, ensuring physical plausibility by comparing background regions of frames.
- **Combined Reward:** A weighted sum `R = αR_EM + βR_PR + γR_MF`. The coefficients (α=0.3, β=0.5, γ=0.2) prioritize the precision reward for denser learning signals while encouraging exact solutions and physical plausibility.

#### 3.5. Verifiable Reward Design for Robotic Reasoning (Target-Bench)

For robotic navigation, where supervision is a reference rollout video rather than discrete actions, an embedding-level reward is designed:
- **Problem Setting:** Given an initial observation, a semantic goal prompt, and a reference video, the model generates a video rollout depicting motion toward the target.
- **Frame Embedding Reward:** Per-frame embeddings are computed using a frozen vision encoder (DINOv2 or CLIP). These embeddings are normalized, and sequences are interpolated to a common length.
- **Reward Components:**
 - **Mean Frame Cosine Similarity (R_cos):** Average cosine similarity between generated and reference frame embeddings.
 - **Temporal-Order Consistency (R_temp):** Measures the similarity of cumulative displacement in embedding space between generated and reference videos, reflecting the consistency of motion progression.
 - **Endpoint Fidelity (R_end):** Averages cosine similarity of the initial and final frame embeddings, ensuring correct start and end states.
- **Combined Embedding Reward:** `R_emb = α_emb R_cos + β_emb R_temp + γ_emb R_end`, with coefficients (0.5, 0.2, 0.3).

#### 3.6. Training Algorithm

The training procedure involves iterating through batches of data. For each sample, *G* videos are generated using SDE sampling with reduced denoising steps. Trajectories are extracted, and verifiable rewards are computed. Group relative advantages are calculated, and the model's parameters are updated by maximizing the GRPO objective.

#### 3.7. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** VR-Bench (procedurally generated mazes: Regular, Irregular, 3D, Trapfield, Sokoban, with varying difficulty and visual textures) and Target-Bench (real-world mapless path planning with a quadruped robot, using egocentric RGB observations and semantic goals).
- **Base Model:** Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, initialized from an SFT checkpoint (Wan-SFT).
- **Baselines:** A range of closed-source (Veo, Sora, Kling, Seedance, MiniMax-Hailuo) and open-source (Wan2.5-i2v-preview, Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, Wan-SFT) video models.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** For VR-Bench: Exact Match (EM), Success Rate (SR), Precision Rate (PR), Step Deviation (SD). For Target-Bench: Average Displacement Error (ADE), Final Displacement Error (FDE), Miss Rate (MR), Soft Endpoint score (SE), Approach Consistency (AC), and a weighted overall score (WO).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings across maze-solving tasks, robotic navigation, and an in-depth analysis of methodological choices.

#### 4.1. Main Results on VR-Bench (Maze-solving)

The RL-tuned model (Wan-R1) consistently outperformed the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) baseline (Wan-SFT) across all maze types and evaluation metrics.
- **Exact Match (EM):** Wan-R1 demonstrated significant absolute gains, for example, achieving 61.1% EM on Regular Maze compared to 33.3% for Wan-SFT, and a rise from 65.3% to 94.4% on 3D Maze. Trapfield tasks showed the most substantial improvement, with EM increasing from 38.9% to 90.3%.
- **Precision Rate (PR):** Improvements in PR were observed across tasks, with Trapfield reaching near-perfect precision at 97.7%.
- **Step Deviation (SD):** Wan-R1 produced more efficient trajectories, indicated by substantial decreases in step deviation (e.g., from 10.3 to 5.2 on Regular Maze and 3.9 to 1.9 on 3D Maze), suggesting closer adherence to optimal solutions.
- **Success Rate (SR):** While both models achieved high success rates on most tasks, Wan-R1’s performance indicated more optimal and precise pathfinding.

#### 4.2. Generalization Results

- **Difficulty Generalization:** When trained solely on Easy mazes, Wan-R1 exhibited significantly better generalization to Medium and Hard difficulty levels. For instance, on Irregular Maze Hard, Wan-R1 maintained 68.4% precision compared to 5.8% for the baseline. This suggests RL fosters more generalizable reasoning strategies rather than mere memorization.
- **Texture Generalization:** Wan-R1 demonstrated improved invariance to unseen visual textures, particularly for styles that differed significantly from training data. For 3D Maze, it maintained 79.2% EM on unseen textures, surpassing the SFT baseline (43.1% and 50.0%). This indicates a focus on structural maze features over superficial visual patterns.
- **Maze Type Generalization:** Training with the proposed method on a single maze type not only enhanced in-domain accuracy but also improved cross-task transfer. For example, in 3D Maze, EM reached 94.4% (vs. 65.3% for SFT), with near-perfect PR (98.0%). Trapfield with Wan-R1 achieved perfect SR (100.0%) across Base, Irregular, and 3D domains, showing robust transfer to visually distinct maze types.

#### 4.3. Target-Bench: Mapless Path Planning

On the Target-Bench robotic navigation task, Wan-R1 achieved the highest overall score among evaluated models. It reduced Average Displacement Error (ADE) and Final Displacement Error (FDE) by approximately half compared to the base model and significantly lowered the Miss Rate. Improvements were also observed in Soft Endpoint (SE) and Approach Consistency (AC), indicating that RL training, guided by the embedding-level verifiable reward, encourages generated rollouts that better encode goal-directed motion.

#### 4.4. Analysis

- **Effect of KL Regularization:** Moderate KL regularization (β_KL = 0.1) yielded optimal performance, producing higher EM and PR and lower SD, suggesting it helps in generating efficient and structurally consistent trajectories by keeping the policy close to the reference distribution.
- **Denoising Reduction:** Using `S_train = 30` denoising steps during training collection recovered almost all performance of the full `S_infer = 50` steps, significantly accelerating training.
- **Cold Start:** Applying Flow-GRPO directly to a base model without prior SFT resulted in poor performance (near-zero EM). This indicates that SFT provides a necessary "cold start," establishing a foundational task understanding before RL can effectively guide exploration with its typically sparser, verifiable rewards.
- **Test-Time Scaling:** Performance consistently improved with an increasing number of samples (K) during inference, even at K=1, Wan-R1 outperformed SFT, confirming that RL enhances the underlying policy rather than merely benefiting from increased sampling variance. Diminishing returns were observed beyond K=8.
- **Reward Design Analysis:**
 - **Reward Models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL):** Multimodal reward models were found to fail due to reward hacking. They assigned high scores to visually plausible but logically incorrect or glitched videos, responding to high-level semantic cues rather than verifying actual task correctness.
 - **Sparse Rewards:** Using only binary success (SR-Only) or Exact Match (R_EM) rewards provided insufficient learning signals, leading to suboptimal or minimal improvements over SFT.
 - **Dense Verifiable Rewards:** The precision reward (R_PR) was identified as crucial for providing a dense learning signal by rewarding partial progress. Combining R_PR with R_EM (for full solutions) and R_MF (for visual fidelity) enabled stable learning and prevented "visual cheating" where the model altered the maze structure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of video generation and reinforcement learning by demonstrating a method to enhance the reasoning capabilities of video generation models. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Generalization in Video Reasoning:** The work establishes that reinforcement learning, when applied with carefully designed verifiable rewards, can substantially improve the generalization abilities of video models. This addresses a critical limitation of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) methods, which tend to lead to memorization and poor performance on out-of-distribution tasks, such as mazes of varying difficulty or textures. This impact is crucial for developing robust AI systems capable of operating in diverse real-world environments.

2. **Validation of Verifiable Reward Design:** The research highlights the critical importance of reward design in applying RL to generative models. It systematically demonstrates that verifiable rewards, grounded in objective task metrics (e.g., trajectory matching, embedding similarity), are essential for stable training and lead to genuine capability improvements. This contrasts with multimodal reward models, which are shown to be susceptible to reward hacking, generating visually plausible but logically incorrect solutions. This finding provides a principled direction for future RL research in high-dimensional output spaces like video.

3. **Applicability to Diverse Reasoning Tasks:** The methodology is validated across both synthetic structured environments (maze-solving) and more complex real-world robotic navigation tasks (Target-Bench). This broad applicability suggests the potential for developing video reasoning models that can assist in diverse applications requiring sequential visual planning, such as autonomous systems, robotics, and interactive AI agents.

4. **Practical Insights for RL in Video Generation:** The study offers practical guidance for practitioners, analyzing the effects of key hyperparameters and techniques such as KL regularization, denoising reduction, cold-start requirements, and test-time scaling. These insights can inform more efficient and effective deployment of RL for video generation models.

5. **Benchmarking and Comparison with State-of-the-Art:** By comparing the proposed approach against a range of both closed-source (e.g., Sora, Veo) and open-source video models, the research provides a clear benchmark of current capabilities and demonstrates the competitive advantage offered by RL-tuned models with verifiable rewards.

In summary, this work advances the state of video reasoning by offering a robust RL-based framework that addresses the generalization and reward design challenges inherent in applying generative models to complex visual tasks. It lays a foundation for future development of AI systems that can reason more reliably and transfer their skills across varied visual scenarios.
