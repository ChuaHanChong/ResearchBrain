# Instinct vs. Reflection: Unifying Token and Verbalized Confidence in Multimodal Large Models

> [!summary] Summary
> MLLM confidence estimation from a single forward pass: extract normalized option-label token probabilities (instinct) and prompted verbalized per-option probabilities (reflection), plus an RBF cross-channel agreement score, then fuse the signals through a monotone logistic head and an order-preserving mean shift. Across six multiple-choice benchmarks and seven MLLMs this cuts calibration error sharply, from 41.91 to 2.41 ECE on MMMU-Pro.

## Problem

- Prior confidence work targets text-only LLMs and relies on self-consistency sampling that repeats inference or API calls, which is cost-prohibitive and does not transfer to multimodal models.
- Logit-based estimators require open-weight internal access, so they exclude proprietary MLLMs, leaving no unified black-box protocol spanning both open-source and closed-source systems.
- Existing multimodal studies examine one confidence channel only, and prior verbalized-confidence analyses cover few model families and downstream settings, so systematic evaluation is missing.

## Method

- ==Dual-channel extraction==: normalized option-label token probabilities give ==instinct confidence==; a structured prompt template yields verbalized per-option probabilities in the hypercube, allowing ambiguity or blanket ignorance.
- ==Cross-channel consistency== via an ==RBF kernel== over per-option token-verbal gaps, rewarding agreement at both high and low confidence while penalizing hallucinated certainty.
- **Monotone Confidence Fusion**: three log-odds features (token, verbal, consistency) plus top-2 margin and negative entropy enter a logistic head with softplus-nonnegative weights, then ==order-preserving mean alignment== shifts bias to match accuracy.

## Results

- Fusion cuts ECE most on hard reasoning: InternVL3.5-8B drops **39.50** to **2.41** on MMMU-Pro; on MMStar GPT-4o drops **13.94** and Phi-3.5-Vision **10.61**.
- Channel superiority flips with task: InternVL2.5-4B token ECE **0.88** versus verbal **7.64** on ScienceQA, while GPT-4o-mini's token ECE hits **43.66** on MMStar against verbal **19.06**.
- Ranking gains are inconsistent: GPT-4o gains **8.99** AUPRC-N on MMMU-Pro, MiniCPM-V-2.6 loses **11.55** AUROC there, and easy ScienceQA sees slight ECE inflation of **2.10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit token support and verbal self-report are decoupled channels; their disagreement is itself a usable reliability feature, not noise to discard.
> - Nonnegative monotone fusion plus an order-preserving bias shift improves calibration without disturbing ranking, so selective-prediction risk-coverage behaviour stays intact.
> - Fusion helps where a single channel is already miscalibrated; on near-perfect easy tasks the second channel injects noise and can worsen calibration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The development of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has led to advancements in various perception and reasoning tasks. A critical aspect of developing reliable AI systems, similar to human intelligence, involves not only providing correct answers but also accurately quantifying the certainty of these responses. This capability, known as confidence estimation or calibration, is essential for risk assessment and error mitigation in real-world deployments.

Previous research on confidence estimation has predominantly focused on text-only Large Language Models (LLMs). These methods generally fall into two categories:
1. **Logits-based methods**: These approaches derive confidence from the internal probability scores (logits) of open-source models, often by aggregating token-level likelihoods or entropy into a sequence-level uncertainty signal.
2. **Self-consistency methods**: Primarily used for closed-source LLMs, these methods estimate confidence by measuring agreement among multiple reasoning paths, typically generated through varied prompts or sampling parameters. However, these methods often require multiple inferences, leading to high computational costs, particularly for MLLMs which involve more complex computations.

Existing studies in MLLM confidence evaluation are less standardized. Some work has analyzed verbalized confidence in MLLMs, but a systematic analysis across diverse model families and downstream scenarios, or a unified framework compatible with both open-source and proprietary MLLMs, remains an underexplored area. Furthermore, MLLMs are known to suffer from poor confidence calibration, frequently exhibiting overconfidence or underconfidence, which existing calibration techniques have not fully addressed in a multimodal context. This paper aims to bridge these gaps by introducing a systematic evaluation and calibration framework specifically for MLLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to evaluate and calibrate the confidence estimation of MLLMs comprehensively. The authors identify a significant phenomenon they term "instinct-reflection misalignment," where an MLLM's implicit, token-level probabilistic support often deviates from its explicit verbalized self-assessment of confidence. This misalignment suggests that relying on a single source of confidence information may be insufficient for robust reliability estimation in MLLMs.

The key motivations for this work are:
1. **Enhancing MLLM reliability**: Despite high accuracy on benchmarks, MLLMs can exhibit poor calibration, leading to overconfident hallucinations or unwarranted hesitations. Accurate confidence estimation is crucial for determining system trustworthiness in practical applications.
2. **Addressing the limitations of existing methods for MLLMs**: Current confidence estimation paradigms, largely developed for text-only LLMs, are often computationally expensive (e.g., self-consistency requiring multiple API calls) or rely on access to internal logits, which is not feasible for proprietary MLLMs. A generalizable, black-box compatible protocol is needed for multimodal settings.
3. **Leveraging dual-channel confidence signals**: The paper posits that by extracting and integrating confidence from two distinct cognitive stages—implicit token probabilities (termed "instinct") and explicit verbalized self-assessment (termed "reflection")—a more robust and calibrated understanding of MLLM uncertainty can be achieved. The observation of "instinct-reflection misalignment" motivates the development of a fusion mechanism to reconcile these potentially conflicting signals.
4. **Improving calibration and failure prediction**: The ultimate goal is to develop a framework that not only reduces calibration error (aligning predicted confidence with empirical accuracy) but also enhances the model's ability to predict its own failures, thereby supporting safer deployment strategies like selective prediction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a post-hoc and interpretable framework designed to calibrate the confidence of MLLMs by integrating dual-channel signals.

#### 3.1. Task Formulation

The research focuses on K-way multiple-choice Visual Question Answering (VQA) tasks. For each instance, an MLLM generates a single option label (e.g., "A", "B", "C", "D") and associated confidence scores. The objective is to post-hoc estimate the calibrated probability that the MLLM's top-1 prediction is correct.

#### 3.2. Dual-Channel Confidence Extraction

The method extracts two types of confidence signals from a single-pass inference, ensuring compatibility with black-box models:

- **Token Confidence (Implicit / Instinct)**: This captures the model's aleatoric uncertainty from its internal probability distribution over response tokens. For an input, the MLLM's log-probabilities for each option-label token at the answer position are converted into a probability vector, $p_i = \text{softmax}(z_i)$. The token confidence for the predicted option ($k^*_i = \arg\max_k p_{ik}$) is $r^{\text{token}}_i = p_{i,k^*_i}$.
- **Verbalized Confidence (Explicit / Reflection)**: This captures the model's epistemic uncertainty through self-deliberation. The MLLM is prompted to explicitly report its estimated probability for each option (e.g., as a percentage) in a structured format (e.g., JSON). These values form a verbalized confidence vector $s_i \in^K$, where individual components $s_{ik}$ do not necessarily sum to 1, allowing for expressions of ambiguity or ignorance. The verbalized confidence for the predicted option is $r^{\text{verbal}}_i = s_{i,k^*_i}$.

#### 3.3. Cross-Channel Confidence Consistency

To quantify the agreement between the instinct and reflection signals, a consistency score ($r^{\text{cons}}_i$) is calculated. For each option, the discrepancy between $p_{ik}$ and $s_{ik}$ is mapped into a similarity score using an RBF kernel function: $\kappa_{ik} = \exp\left(-\frac{|p_{ik} - s_{ik}|}{\gamma}\right)$. High agreement implies internal consensus, while divergence suggests hallucinated certainty or uncertainty. The consistency score for the predicted option is $r^{\text{cons}}_i = \kappa_{i,k^*_i}$.

#### 3.4. Reliability Features

A compact reliability descriptor $\phi_i \in \mathbb{R}^5$ is formulated by integrating evidence from both channels, comprising five features:
1. Log-odds transformation of token confidence: $\ell(r^{\text{token}}_i)$
2. Log-odds transformation of verbalized confidence: $\ell(r^{\text{verbal}}_i)$
3. Log-odds transformation of cross-channel consistency: $\ell(r^{\text{cons}}_i)$
4. Top-2 margin of token probabilities: $m_i = p_{i,(1)} - p_{i,(2)}$
5. Negative Shannon entropy of token probabilities: $-H(p_i)$ (where higher value indicates lower entropy/higher reliability).

These features are standardized to have zero mean and unit variance using calibration-split statistics.

#### 3.5. Monotone Confidence Fusion

The reliability features $\phi_i$ are then mapped to a probability of correctness ($\hat{q}_i$) using a coordinate-wise monotone logistic head: $\hat{q}_i = \sigma(b + \sum_{j=1}^d w_j \phi_{ij})$, where $w_j \geq 0$. The non-negativity constraint on weights ($w_j$) ensures that increasing any reliability cue does not decrease the predicted correctness, incorporating an inductive bias for interpretability. The weights $w_j$ are parameterized using a softplus function ($w_j = \text{softplus}(\tilde{w}_j)$) to ensure positivity during optimization.
The model parameters are optimized by minimizing the negative log-likelihood objective function using a calibration dataset. This fusion head generalizes standard post-hoc calibration methods like Platt scaling.

#### 3.6. Order-Preserving Mean Alignment

After fitting the fusion head, a post-hoc bias shift is applied to correct systematic base-rate misalignment. This involves adjusting the intercept $b$ by a constant $\delta$ such that the average predicted confidence matches the empirical accuracy on a validation split. This step is order-preserving, meaning it does not alter the relative ranking of samples, thus preserving utility for selective prediction. The optimal $\delta$ is found using a one-dimensional bisection search.

#### 3.7. Experiment Setup

- **Datasets**: Six diverse multiple-choice VQA benchmarks are used: MMBench, MMStar, ConBench, ScienceQA, AI2D, and MMMU-Pro. These cover general-purpose, high-difficulty reasoning, compositional reasoning, scientific, diagrammatic, and multi-discipline professional reasoning tasks.
- **Models**: Five open-source MLLMs (MiniCPM-V-2.6, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, InternVL2.5-4B, InternVL3.5-8B, Phi-3.5-Vision) and two closed-source MLLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini) are evaluated. Additional closed-source models (Claude-3.7-Sonnet, GPT-5, Gemini-2.0-Flash) are used in extended analyses.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Confidence is assessed using:
 - **Calibration**: Expected Calibration Error (ECE↓).
 - **Failure Prediction**: Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC↑), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC↑), and Normalized AUPRC (AUPRC-N↑).
 - **Predictive Quality**: Top-1 Accuracy (ACC↑).
 - **Risk-sensitive selective prediction**: Area Under the Risk-Coverage Curve (AURC↓).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Instinct-Reflection Misalignment

The empirical analysis consistently revealed a divergence between token-level confidence (instinct) and verbalized confidence (reflection). Models often exhibit high verbalized confidence while having low token confidence, or vice versa, highlighting the inherent unreliability of single-source metrics and motivating the proposed fusion approach. Case studies (e.g., Figures 14 and 15 in Appendix C) systematically illustrate instances of this Verbal-Internal Disconnect (VID).

#### 4.2. Token Confidence vs. Verbalized Confidence Baselines

- **Task Difficulty Impact**: The efficacy of both confidence channels is heavily influenced by task difficulty. Token confidence generally achieves superior calibration on foundational tasks (e.g., ScienceQA, InternVL2.5-4B ECE 0.88 for token vs. 7.64 for verbalized). However, for complex logical tasks (e.g., AI2D), token confidence calibration can significantly degrade (GPT-4o ECE 54.85).
- **Model-Specific Heterogeneity**: Closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o-mini) often demonstrate greater stability in verbalized calibration across tasks (ECE < 20.0), even when token ECE is volatile (e.g., 43.66 on MMStar). Open-source models exhibit more severe fluctuations in verbalized reliability, sometimes yielding negative AUPRC-N values on complex reasoning tasks (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B AUPRC-N -12.64 on ConBench), suggesting an inverse correlation between confidence and correctness.
- **Discriminative Power**: While models can maintain high discriminative power (AUROC > 90.0) on foundational tasks with token confidence, this ability often collapses with increasing task complexity for both channels.

#### 4.3. Performance of Monotone Confidence Fusion

The proposed monotone confidence fusion framework primarily functions as a post-hoc recalibration tool.
- **ECE Reduction**: The method consistently yielded substantial reductions in ECE across most benchmarks, particularly in high-complexity reasoning tasks like MMMU-Pro and AI2D. For InternVL3.5-8B on MMMU-Pro, ECE decreased by 39.50 (from 41.91 to 2.41 for verbalized confidence). This indicates effective mitigation of single-channel miscalibration.
- **Marginal Cost**: For foundational tasks where single-channel baselines already exhibit near-perfect calibration, the fusion method might introduce a marginal increase in ECE (e.g., +2.10 for GPT-4o on ScienceQA).
- **Discriminative Power (AUROC, AUPRC-N)**: Improvements in discriminative power were inconsistent and model-sensitive. GPT-4o showed consistent benefits (e.g., +8.99 AUPRC-N on MMMU-Pro), while other models might show degraded ranking performance in complex scenarios.

#### 4.4. Risk-Coverage Analysis

Risk-coverage (RC) curves for GPT-4o demonstrate task-dependent selective reliability. ScienceQA exhibited the lowest risk, maintaining high accuracy even at full coverage. In contrast, MMMU-Pro showed substantially higher risk and a less reliable trend, indicating limited effectiveness for confidence-based selection on expert-level tasks. Closed-source models generally achieved lower overall risk compared to open-source models, especially at high coverage.

#### 4.5. Effect of Temperature on Calibration

Experiments showed that increasing sampling temperature (T) can reduce ECE in challenging multimodal settings (e.g., MMMU-Pro for GPT-4o), helping to mitigate overconfidence. However, for many models and datasets (e.g., GLM-4.1V-9B, InternVL3.5-8B, MiniCPM-V-2.6, Phi-3.5-Vision in Appendix C), ECE and AUPRC-N remained relatively stable across temperature variations, indicating that simple temperature scaling is not a universal solution for multimodal calibration.

#### 4.6. Calibration and Reliability Analysis of Prompts

Comparative analysis of verbalized confidence across models using histograms and reliability diagrams (e.g., Figure 5, 8, 9, 10) revealed a persistent gap. Closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-5, Claude-3.7-Sonnet) demonstrated superior calibration, with reliability curves closely tracking the ideal diagonal and correct predictions concentrated in high-confidence regions. Open-source models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B) often exhibited severe overconfidence, assigning high confidence to incorrect answers and having reliability curves that deviated significantly from ideal. Prompting strategies alone, such as Chain-of-Thought or Roleplay, could shift confidence distributions but often failed to resolve intrinsic calibration errors or improve discriminative power, especially in high-difficulty tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work contributes to the field of MLLM reliability by systematically evaluating and proposing a method for calibrating confidence estimates.
The key contributions are:
1. **Identification of Instinct-Reflection Misalignment**: The paper introduces the concept of dual-channel confidence (implicit token probabilities as "instinct" and explicit verbalized self-assessment as "reflection") and empirically demonstrates their frequent divergence in MLLMs. This highlights a fundamental challenge in MLLM self-awareness.
2. **Novel Monotone Confidence Fusion Framework**: The proposed framework provides an interpretable and robust mechanism to integrate these dual-channel signals. By incorporating cross-channel consistency and other uncertainty features (margin, entropy) into a monotonically constrained logistic head, the method effectively unifies disparate confidence cues.
3. **Improved Calibration and Failure Prediction**: The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method consistently yields more reliable confidence estimates, reducing ECE and, in some cases, improving failure prediction metrics (AUROC, AUPRC-N). This indicates a step towards more trustworthy MLLM deployments.
4. **Systematic Evaluation Across Diverse MLLMs and Benchmarks**: The comprehensive evaluation covers a range of open-source and closed-source MLLMs on six diverse multimodal benchmarks, providing a broad empirical understanding of confidence behavior and the limitations of current models.

The potential impact of this work is primarily in enabling safer and more reliable deployment of MLLMs in high-stakes environments. By producing better-calibrated and more discriminative confidence estimates, the framework can support critical applications requiring:
- **Abstention**: Models can "know when they don't know" and abstain from answering, preventing potentially harmful outputs.
- **Human-in-the-loop triage**: Confidence scores can prioritize instances for human review, optimizing resource allocation.
- **Clearer uncertainty communication**: More accurate confidence can facilitate better communication of model reliability to users.

The authors acknowledge potential risks, including over-reliance on a single confidence score, increased persuasiveness of remaining wrong outputs, and sensitivity to prompt/API changes. They recommend using confidence as part of a broader safety stack, monitoring calibration under distribution shift, and periodic revalidation of the estimator. Future work aims to extend the framework to open-ended tasks and investigate internal design modifications to intrinsically enforce consistency between implicit and explicit uncertainty.
