# Learning to Reason for Hallucination Span Detection

> [!summary] Summary
> RL4HS is a reinforcement learning framework that trains Large Language Models to precisely identify hallucinated text segments within generated content. It achieves this by using a span-level reward function and Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO), significantly improving factual consistency detection over existing methods on the RAGTruth benchmark.

## Problem

- Prior hallucination detection focused on binary classification, which is insufficient for real-world applications requiring precise localization of unsupported content.
- The complex, multi-step nature of hallucination span detection requires robust reasoning, but existing LLMs often lack this specific capability, and simple prompting for reasoning shows limited gains.
- Existing general-domain reasoning models are not specialized for the unique task of cross-referencing generated text against a source for factual consistency.

## Method

- Proposes **RL4HS**, a generative modeling framework that uses ==Group Relative Policy== Optimization (GRPO) to train LLMs to output hallucinated text spans.
- Introduces a novel Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO) technique to address reward imbalance in GRPO, preventing a bias towards "no hallucination" predictions.
- Utilizes a verifiable span-F1 reward function that directly incentivizes accurate identification of hallucinated segments.

## Results

- RL4HS-**14B** achieved a top average span-F1 score of 58.3 on the RAGTruth benchmark, outperforming supervised fine-tuning and proprietary models like GPT-4o-mini and GPT-5-mini.
- Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO) effectively mitigated reward hacking in GRPO, leading to a balanced precision-recall trade-off and improved F1 scores.
- Qualitative analysis showed RL4HS learns structured, human-like reasoning for cross-referencing claims against source data, enhancing interpretability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learned, in-domain reasoning process, specifically trained for hallucination span detection, is essential and outperforms general-purpose reasoning models and simple prompting.
> - Reinforcement learning, particularly with span-level rewards, can effectively train LLMs to perform complex, multi-step reasoning for factual verification.
> - Reward imbalance, where predicting "no hallucination" is easier, can lead to suboptimal policies in RL, requiring specialized mitigation strategies like CAPO.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has brought forth unprecedented capabilities in natural language processing, but also significant challenges, most notably the phenomenon of "hallucination." Hallucinations, where LLMs generate content that is not supported by their input context or underlying knowledge, undermine their reliability and trustworthiness, posing critical risks in sensitive applications like summarization, question answering, and data-to-text generation.

Prior research in hallucination detection has largely focused on a **binary classification task**, determining only if an entire generated output contains any unsupported content. While a useful first step, this approach is insufficient for many real-world applications that require precise identification of *which specific parts* of the output are hallucinated. This paper directly addresses this limitation by focusing on **hallucination span detection**, aiming to pinpoint the exact text segments that constitute a hallucination. This shift represents a significant evolution in the field, moving from coarse-grained detection to fine-grained localization, which is crucial for enabling corrective actions or providing clearer feedback to users.

Concurrently, the broader NLP landscape has seen a growing emphasis on **reasoning capabilities** in LLMs, often facilitated by techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. CoT involves encouraging LLMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, which has shown promise in improving performance on complex tasks such as mathematics and coding. This paper explores the synergy between reasoning and hallucination detection, hypothesizing that hallucination span detection, being a complex multi-step decision-making process, could significantly benefit from a learned reasoning process. While some prior works explored CoT for binary hallucination detection, this paper is among the first to systematically investigate and train a reasoning model specifically for the more challenging task of fine-grained hallucination span detection using reinforcement learning.

Furthermore, the work contributes to the burgeoning area of **reinforcement learning (RL) for LLM alignment and enhancement**. Methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and more recently, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), are being employed to fine-tune LLMs for specific objectives beyond standard supervised learning. This paper leverages GRPO and introduces a novel variant, Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO), to tackle inherent reward asymmetry in span-level detection. By demonstrating the efficacy of RL with span-level rewards and an improved optimization strategy, this research advances the methodologies for building more robust and factual LLMs. It directly confronts the challenge of making LLMs not only powerful generators but also reliable purveyors of information, thereby contributing significantly to the development of trustworthy AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the critical need to enhance the reliability and trustworthiness of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly by addressing the pervasive issue of hallucination. While LLMs excel at generating fluent and coherent text, their propensity to produce factually unsupported content poses a significant barrier to their widespread adoption in sensitive applications.

The paper articulates two primary research questions, which serve as its key objectives:

1. **Is a learned reasoning process helpful for hallucination span detection, and how can an effective reasoning process be learned for this task?**
 - **Motivation:** Existing research largely treats hallucination detection as a binary classification problem, which is insufficient for real-world use cases where identifying *precisely which parts* of the text are hallucinated is necessary. Hallucination span detection is inherently a complex task, requiring careful analysis and multi-step decision-making—qualities that reasoning processes are designed to address. Preliminary experiments with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting showed that while single-sample CoT offered limited gains, repeated sampling with CoT significantly improved the chances of generating at least one correct answer. This indicated the *potential* of reasoning, but also the need for a more robust method to reliably harness it. Therefore, the authors were motivated to investigate if reinforcement learning (RL) could effectively *train* an LLM to perform such reasoning for span detection, thereby moving beyond simple prompting.

2. **Is it necessary to learn a reasoning process specifically for hallucination span detection, or do existing general-domain reasoning models suffice for this specific task?**
 - **Motivation:** With the proliferation of LLMs pre-trained on diverse reasoning tasks (e.g., mathematics, coding), it's a valid question whether these general-purpose reasoning capabilities can directly transfer to the specialized domain of hallucination detection. If general reasoning models were sufficient, it would simplify deployment and reduce the need for domain-specific fine-tuning. However, hallucination span detection requires a unique form of reasoning: cross-referencing generated text against a source context for factual consistency, which might differ substantially from mathematical or logical deduction. This question aims to determine if the specific nuances of fact-checking and span localization necessitate in-domain learning, or if a generic "reasoning" aptitude is transferable.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to develop a method that not only accurately identifies hallucinated spans but also elucidates the underlying reasoning process, making LLM outputs more interpretable and trustworthy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **RL4HS (Reinforcement Learning for Hallucination Span Detection)**, a framework designed to train Large Language Models (LLMs) to reason for fine-grained hallucination span detection. Their approach is characterized by leveraging reinforcement learning with a specific span-level reward function and introducing a novel optimization strategy to overcome reward imbalance.

#### 3.1. Generative Modeling for Span Detection

The paper adopts a **generative modeling approach**, where the LLM is trained to directly output a list of hallucinated text segments (spans). This contrasts with discriminative models that perform token-level binary classification. The generative approach is chosen because it naturally accommodates Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, where the model can first generate intermediate steps (its reasoning process) and then produce the final list of hallucinated spans. The model maps these predicted text segments back to their start and end indices in the generated response.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning with GRPO

RL4HS builds upon **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, an RL framework that optimizes policies without requiring an explicit value function (critic network), unlike more common methods like PPO. Instead, GRPO computes baselines from relative group scores. The learning objective is designed to maximize the expected advantage, where the advantage for a given trajectory (generated output and reasoning) is calculated relative to the average performance of other trajectories within its group, normalized by their standard deviation. This group-based normalization makes GRPO suitable for scenarios where relative performance is more informative.

#### 3.3. Verifiable Span-F1 Reward Function

A crucial component of RL4HS is its **span-level reward function**, which directly uses the target `span-F1` metric. Given a set of predicted hallucination spans (`$\hat{S}$`) and ground-truth spans (`$S$`), the reward (`$r_{span}$`) is defined as:
- `$r_{span} = 1$` if both predicted and ground-truth sets are empty (i.e., no hallucinations exist and none are predicted correctly).
- `$r_{span} = span-F1(\hat{S}, S)$` otherwise, reflecting the overlap quality.

This reward function directly incentivizes the model to make accurate span predictions, covering both cases where hallucinations are present and where they are absent.

#### 3.4. Addressing Reward Imbalance with Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO)

A significant challenge identified by the authors is a **reward imbalance issue** inherent in the `span-F1` reward function when used with GRPO. Due to the difficulty of precisely identifying hallucinated spans compared to simply predicting no hallucinations (which yields a high reward if no hallucinations are truly present), the GRPO framework tends to over-incentivize "non-hallucination" predictions. This leads to biased policies with high precision but suppressed recall, a form of "reward hacking."

To mitigate this, the paper introduces **Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO)**. CAPO modifies GRPO by introducing a scaling factor `$\alpha$` (set to 0.5 in experiments) to scale the advantage values computed specifically for samples belonging to the "non-hallucination" class. By making it relatively harder for non-hallucination predictions to achieve high advantages, CAPO aims to balance the contributions of both hallucination and non-hallucination classes, thereby preventing the dominance of the easier "no hallucination" prediction strategy.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Dataset:** RAGTruth benchmark, which provides human-annotated hallucination spans across three Conditional Natural Language Generation (CNLG) tasks: Summarization, Question Answering (QA), and Data-to-Text.
- **Models:** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct as base LLMs.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Baseline for span detection, trained with cross-entropy.
 - **RL4HS-GRPO:** The proposed RL4HS without the CAPO modification.
 - **Multi-View Attention:** A token-level detector from prior work (Ogasa and Arase, 2025).
 - **Pretrained LLMs (with/without CoT):** Qwen2.5/Qwen3 models, and proprietary models like GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5-mini, GPT-5, and o3, evaluated with and without CoT prompting.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Dataset-level span-F1, along with precision and recall.
- **Training Details:** Models trained on 8 H100 GPUs, with specific learning rates and batch sizes. Data class imbalance was addressed by upweighting the hallucination class.

This comprehensive methodology, combining generative modeling, advanced RL, a tailored reward function, and a novel imbalance-correction strategy, provides a robust framework for learning reasoning specifically for hallucination span detection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the RAGTruth benchmark consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of RL4HS, particularly with Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO), in detecting hallucination spans.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness of RL4HS

- **RL4HS Outperforms Baselines:** RL4HS consistently surpasses all baseline models, including proprietary LLMs (GPT-4o/5-mini, GPT-5, o3), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and other general-purpose reasoning models.
 - RL4HS-7B achieves an average F1 score of 55.9, significantly outperforming SFT-7B (50.1 F1) and even SFT-14B (55.4 F1), showcasing the efficiency of RL.
 - RL4HS-14B achieves the highest overall average F1 of 58.3, with strong performance across summarization (57.6), QA (54.8), and data-to-text (62.6).
- **Pretrained Models' Limitations:** Pretrained instruction-tuned models (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B/14B-Instruct) perform poorly (F1 below 30), indicating that simple prompting is insufficient for accurate span localization. Even with Chain-of-Thought (CoT), these models show limited gains, though CoT's potential is revealed when sampling multiple predictions (Span-F1@K increases significantly with K, as shown in Figure 1).
- **General Reasoning Models vs. Fine-tuned:** General-purpose reasoning models (QwQ-32B, Qwen3-8B/14B) transfer some reasoning ability, but their performance remains significantly below fine-tuned approaches (e.g., Qwen3-14B with 33.7 F1 vs. RL4HS-14B with 58.3 F1). This highlights the need for domain-specific training.

#### 4.2. CAPO Alleviates Reward Hacking and Improves Precision–Recall Balance

- **Reward Hacking in GRPO:** The study reveals that vanilla GRPO suffers from "reward hacking." As depicted in Figures 2 and 3, non-hallucination predictions consistently receive higher advantages, biasing the model towards predicting "no hallucination" to maximize reward. This leads to high precision but severely suppressed recall, as shown by the training dynamics in Figure 4. GRPO-trained models prioritize avoiding false positives over identifying actual hallucinations.
- **CAPO's Solution:** Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO) effectively addresses this imbalance. By scaling down the advantage for non-hallucination samples, CAPO stabilizes recall while maintaining strong precision. Figure 4 clearly illustrates that CAPO (blue line) achieves a better precision–recall trade-off throughout training, leading to consistently higher span F1 scores compared to GRPO (red line). This confirms CAPO's success in mitigating the identified reward imbalance.

#### 4.3. Necessity of In-Domain Reasoning

- **Superiority of In-Domain Fine-tuning:** Experiments with "leave-one-out" training (RL4HS-OOD-7B) demonstrate that in-domain reasoning, learned with span-level rewards, is essential for robust hallucination detection. RL4HS-OOD-7B consistently outperforms larger, general-purpose reasoning models (Qwen3, QwQ, and even GPT-4o-mini with CoT) on unseen tasks, despite being a much smaller model (Figure 5). This finding is crucial, suggesting that generalized reasoning abilities alone are not sufficient; specific adaptation to the hallucination detection task is required for optimal performance.

#### 4.4. Simple Reward Scaling is Insufficient

- **Limitations of Dr.GRPO:** An exploration of Dr.GRPO, a variant that scales the reward for successful non-hallucination predictions directly, showed that simply scaling rewards without GRPO's normalization step does not effectively solve the reward hacking issue. As shown in Table 2, various `$\gamma$` values for Dr.GRPO influence the precision–recall trade-off but result in overall inferior performance compared to standard GRPO and, critically, RL4HS (CAPO). This emphasizes that the normalization within GRPO is vital, and the nuanced scaling approach of CAPO is more effective.

#### 4.5. What RL4HS Learns: A Case Study

- **Structured Reasoning:** A qualitative case study (Table 3) illustrates that RL4HS learns a structured, human-like reasoning process. In an example involving restaurant data, the pretrained model fails to identify a hallucination about "catering services" because its reasoning doesn't effectively cross-reference the claim with structured data. In contrast, RL4HS-7B correctly flags the hallucination by performing systematic consistency checks that mirror a heuristic pipeline: identifying claims, cross-checking against structured data, and concluding inconsistency. This demonstrates that RL4HS goes beyond superficial reasoning traces, developing semantically grounded and faithful reasoning behaviors aligned with factual verification.

In summary, the results firmly establish RL4HS with CAPO as a leading method for hallucination span detection, driven by its ability to learn effective in-domain reasoning through carefully designed reinforcement learning with span-level rewards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Learning to Reason for Hallucination Span Detection" holds significant implications for the development and deployment of more reliable and trustworthy Large Language Models, with potential impacts across several dimensions:

1. **Enhanced LLM Trustworthiness and Reliability:** The primary significance of this work lies in its direct contribution to mitigating LLM hallucinations, a major barrier to their safe and widespread adoption. By enabling LLMs to not only detect the presence of hallucinations but also to precisely locate the unsupported spans, RL4HS provides a critical mechanism for improving the factual consistency and trustworthiness of generated content. This precision is invaluable for applications where accuracy is paramount, such as scientific summarization, legal document generation, and critical information retrieval.

2. **Advancement in Fine-Grained Factual Consistency:** Moving beyond binary hallucination detection to span-level detection marks a substantial step forward in factual consistency evaluation. This fine-grained capability allows for targeted correction, highlighting specific problematic phrases or sentences rather than flagging an entire output as unreliable. This enables more efficient human-in-the-loop review processes and lays the groundwork for automated hallucination correction systems.

3. **Novel Application of Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning:** The paper demonstrates a powerful application of reinforcement learning, specifically GRPO with a customized reward function, to imbue LLMs with a learned reasoning process tailored for a complex factual verification task. This work shows that RL can effectively distill the multi-sample reasoning potential observed in CoT prompting into a single, more reliable prediction, pushing the boundaries of what RL can achieve in complex NLP tasks.

4. **Addressing Reward Imbalance in RL for NLP:** The introduction of Class-Aware Policy Optimization (CAPO) is a key methodological contribution. It identifies and effectively solves a common problem in RL setups for NLP, where reward functions might implicitly favor easier-to-achieve outcomes (e.g., predicting "no hallucination"). CAPO's approach to balancing class-specific advantages can inspire similar solutions in other RL applications facing skewed reward landscapes or class imbalance.

5. **Importance of In-Domain Specificity for Reasoning:** The finding that in-domain reasoning, learned with span-level rewards, significantly outperforms general-purpose reasoning models underscores a crucial insight for LLM development. It suggests that while powerful, generic reasoning abilities are often insufficient for specialized, high-stakes tasks. This advocates for targeted fine-tuning and domain adaptation strategies, guiding future research toward more focused model development rather than solely relying on scaling up general models.

6. **Improved Interpretability and Explainability:** The qualitative case study illustrates that RL4HS learns reasoning traces that mimic human-like systematic consistency checks. This not only makes the model's decisions more transparent but also offers insights into *how* the model identifies hallucinations, fostering greater user confidence and potentially aiding in debugging and refining LLM behavior.

In conclusion, RL4HS represents a significant leap forward in making LLMs more reliable and responsible. Its methodology, particularly the integration of span-level rewards with an imbalance-aware RL framework, provides a robust blueprint for future research in hallucination detection and broader applications of reinforcement learning to enhance LLM capabilities for trustworthiness. The practical implications are vast, promising more accurate, verifiable, and ultimately, more useful LLM-powered applications across industries.
