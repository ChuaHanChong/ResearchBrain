# Rethinking Classifier-Free Guidance in On-Policy Diffusion Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Alibaba's Qwen Business Unit identified a limitation in On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for diffusion models using Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) when the teacher has privileged negative conditioning. They proposed Positive-Direction Matching (PDM), a branch-aware objective that mitigates "Negative Branch Asymmetry" and enhances control fidelity and robustness across guidance scales in tasks like dense-to-sparse video control.

## Problem

- Existing On-Policy Distillation (OPD) objectives that match the CFG-composed prediction are "under-identified at the branch level," allowing positive and negative branch errors to compensate for each other without individual error reduction.
- This branch ambiguity leads to "Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)" when the teacher's negative branch possesses privileged information, causing antagonistic error dynamics during optimization (reducing one error at the expense of increasing the other).
- NBA results in increased "guidance-scale sensitivity" in distilled student models, leading to performance degradation when the inference guidance scale differs from the training scale.

## Method

- Conducted theoretical analysis demonstrating the ==branch ambiguity== inherent in ==naive CFG-composed OPD objectives== and empirically validated ==Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)== in distillation settings with ==privileged negative conditioning==.
- Developed **Positive–Direction Matching (PDM)**, a ==branch-aware objective== that explicitly supervises the positive prediction and the ==CFG conditional direction==, thereby requiring individual branch errors to be zero and preventing NBA.
- Applied **PDM** to the complex task of dense-to-sparse video control, using an ==efficient supervision strategy== that focused on the first K student-visited states to manage computational cost while transferring knowledge.

## Results

- Empirical studies confirmed that in privileged negative conditioning settings, naive CFG-composed OPD exhibited antagonistic branch-error dynamics and increased guidance-scale sensitivity, confirming the emergence of NBA.
- PDM consistently delivered substantial improvements in control fidelity across pose, depth, and scribble modalities for dense-to-sparse video control, outperforming naive OPD, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), and off-policy distillation.
- PDM produced student models that were robust to changes in inference guidance scales, maintaining stable performance and visual quality where naive matching exhibited sharp degradation, blurring, and structural distortion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Naive CFG-composed OPD objectives are fundamentally under-identified at the branch level, enabling positive and negative branch errors to compensate without truly converging to zero for both.
> - This branch ambiguity becomes actively detrimental, causing "Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)," when the teacher model's negative branch has privileged information unavailable to the student.
> - Explicitly supervising individual branches or their components (like the positive branch and CFG conditional direction via PDM) resolves this ambiguity, preventing NBA and improving distillation robustness across guidance scales.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of generative artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on diffusion models and techniques for their adaptation and optimization. Diffusion models have become a foundational technology for various generative tasks, including image and video synthesis. A standard component of modern conditional diffusion models is Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG), which enhances the quality and adherence of generated outputs to a given condition by combining predictions from a conditioned model (positive branch) and an unconditioned or negatively conditioned model (negative branch) using an inference-time guidance scale (γ).

Parallel to the development of powerful diffusion models, significant effort has been directed towards distillation techniques. Diffusion model distillation generally aims to improve efficiency, typically by reducing the number of inference steps required for high-quality generation. Existing distillation methods include progressive distillation, consistency models, and distribution matching distillation. Many of these methods often distill the *CFG-composed* prediction of a teacher model into a *single* student prediction, thereby internalizing the guidance strength and potentially removing the CFG interface from the student for faster sampling.

More recently, a distinct paradigm known as On-Policy Distillation (OPD) has been applied to diffusion models. In OPD, a student model learns by generating its own denoising trajectories and then querying a stronger teacher model at these student-visited states, receiving dense local supervision. This approach is distinct from offline distillation or scalar-reward optimization. Current OPD formulations, such as D-OPSD, Flow-OPD, and DiffusionOPD, often involve matching teacher and student predictions.

This specific work distinguishes itself by focusing on OPD settings where *both* the teacher and student models *retain* the standard CFG interface, producing separate positive and negative branch predictions. While prior branch-retaining OPD studies have primarily considered scenarios where the teacher and student share the same negative conditioning (e.g., both use null text), this paper investigates a less understood regime: when the teacher's negative branch possesses privileged information that is unavailable to the student. The authors identify a fundamental limitation in naive CFG-composed OPD objectives under this specific condition and propose a method to address it, thereby advancing the understanding and practical application of OPD in complex knowledge-transfer scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from an identified limitation in how existing On-Policy Distillation (OPD) methods handle Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) when both teacher and student models maintain their separate positive and negative prediction branches.

The core problem is articulated as follows: when OPD objectives attempt to match the *CFG-composed* prediction of a teacher (v̂_T) with that of a student (v̂_S), this objective is "under-identified at the branch level." Mathematically, if e+ represents the error in the positive branch (v+_T - v+_S) and e- represents the error in the negative branch (v-_T - v-_S), the naive objective (L_naive ∝ ||v̂_T - v̂_S||^2) effectively constrains only a linear combination of these errors: γe+ + (1-γ)e- = 0. This condition allows for infinitely many pairs of non-zero e+ and e- that can still satisfy the objective, meaning that positive and negative branch errors can compensate for each other without alerting the loss function to individual branch inaccuracies.

The authors hypothesize that while this branch ambiguity might be benign under "shared negative conditioning" (where both teacher and student use the same null or negative text condition), it can become detrimental when the teacher’s negative branch receives "privileged information" that is inaccessible or only partially accessible to the student. In such cases, the joint reduction of both branch errors, which might occur under shared conditioning, could break down. This breakdown can lead to "antagonistic branch-error dynamics," where the optimization process might reduce the error in the positive branch at the cost of *increasing* the error in the negative branch.

This failure mode is termed "Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)." A direct consequence of NBA is increased "guidance-scale sensitivity" at inference time. If the student learns to compensate for errors at the training guidance scale (γ_train), this compensation will likely fail when the guidance scale changes, leading to performance degradation. This forms a crucial motivation for the study: to understand, diagnose, and mitigate this sensitivity.

Based on this, the key objectives of the research are:
1. **Characterize Branch Ambiguity**: To formally demonstrate that matching only the CFG-composed prediction in OPD leads to under-identification of individual positive and negative branch errors.
2. **Identify NBA**: To empirically investigate and define the specific conditions under which this branch ambiguity becomes problematic, leading to Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA), particularly in settings with privileged negative conditioning.
3. **Develop Branch-Aware Objectives**: To propose and evaluate new OPD objectives that explicitly supervise the individual branches or their components, thereby resolving the identified ambiguity and preventing NBA.
4. **Demonstrate Practical Effectiveness**: To apply the proposed branch-aware objective to a complex, real-world knowledge transfer task, such as dense-to-sparse video control, and quantitatively demonstrate its superiority in terms of control fidelity and robustness across inference guidance scales.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combination of theoretical analysis and empirical experimentation across diverse distillation settings to investigate and address the identified problem.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Analysis of Naive CFG-Based OPD

The paper formally defines the positive-branch error (e+ = v+_T - v+_S) and negative-branch error (e- = v-_T - v-_S). It then shows that the naive CFG-composed matching objective, L_naive ∝ ||γe+ + (1-γ)e-||^2, when minimized to zero, implies γe+ + (1-γ)e- = 0. This equation demonstrates that e+ and e- can have non-zero values while still satisfying the objective, allowing one error to grow if the other compensates proportionally, thus establishing the concept of "branch ambiguity."

#### 3.2. Empirical Investigation of NBA Emergence

To understand when this ambiguity becomes problematic, the authors conducted experiments in two image-domain distillation settings, contrasting different negative conditioning schemes:
- **Text-Rendering Distillation**: Utilized an SD3.5-Medium-based OCR teacher. Both teacher and student received the same null-text condition on their negative branches (shared negative conditioning). This served as a control scenario.
- **Reference-Conditioned Distillation**: Employed a FLUX-2-klein-base-4B model. The teacher received a reference image on both its positive and negative branches, while the text-only student did not (privileged negative conditioning). This setup was designed to expose the effects of asymmetric conditioning.

For both settings, the researchers tracked the L2 norms of the positive and negative branch errors (||e+|| and ||e-||) throughout training. They compared the naive matching objective with a "positive-only ablation" (ℓ+ = ||e+||^2) to isolate the effect of updating towards the positive target, and their proposed PDM. Additionally, "guidance-scale sensitivity" was assessed by evaluating model performance across a range of inference guidance scales (γ_inference) while keeping the training guidance scale (γ_train) fixed.

#### 3.3. Proposed Branch-Aware OPD Objectives

To counteract the branch ambiguity, two branch-aware objectives were introduced:
- **Positive–Direction Matching (PDM)**: This is the primary proposed objective. For a model M (Teacher or Student), the CFG conditional direction is defined as d_M = v+_M - v-_M. The PDM loss is given by ℓ_PDM = ||e+||^2 + λ ||d_T - d_S||^2, where λ is a weighting hyperparameter. This objective separately constrains the positive prediction and the CFG conditional direction. Achieving zero PDM loss requires both e+ and e- to be zero, eliminating the compensation freedom.
- **Independent Branch Matching (IBM)**: This served as a baseline foil. Its loss is ℓ_IBM = γ^2 ||e+||^2 + (γ-1)^2 ||e-||^2. IBM directly matches the positive and negative branches, using weights derived from the expanded naive objective, but explicitly removes the cross-branch interaction term, also enforcing e+=e-=0 for zero loss.

#### 3.4. Application to Dense-to-Sparse Video Control

The proposed PDM method was applied to a practical knowledge transfer scenario: dense-to-sparse video control using the Wan-VACE model.
- **Task Setup**: A teacher model was given dense, per-frame control signals (pose, depth, or scribble) throughout a video sequence, while the student model received only sparse keyframes of these control signals. The student's goal was to synthesize a video accurately following the sparse control, effectively learning from the teacher's dense control knowledge. This context naturally involved privileged negative conditioning for the teacher.
- **Efficient Supervision**: To manage the computational cost of full-trajectory OPD for video, the authors introduced an efficiency measure: supervising only the first K student-visited states of a denoising trajectory (L^(K)_OPD), where K is a hyperparameter.
- **Baselines**: The performance of PDM and IBM was compared against an unadapted sparse-control student, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on data-derived diffusion states, and off-policy teacher matching.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Performance was assessed using various control fidelity metrics (e.g., MPJPE, PCK for pose; CORR, RMSE, SI-RMSE for depth; Recall, Precision, F1 for scribble) and distributional quality metrics (FID, FVD) against ground truth videos.
- **Ablation Studies**: Further experiments investigated the impact of the direction-matching weight (λ) in PDM and the supervision horizon (K) on control fidelity and training efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding branch ambiguity, NBA, and the efficacy of branch-aware OPD objectives.

#### 4.1. Emergence of Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)

- **Shared Negative Conditioning (Text-Rendering Distillation)**: In this setting, where teacher and student shared the same null-text conditioning, the study observed that both positive- and negative-branch errors decreased jointly across all tested objectives (naive, PDM, and positive-only ablation). The student models generally tracked the teacher's intrinsic CFG response, indicating that the branch ambiguity was benign and did not lead to significant degradation or excess guidance-scale sensitivity.
- **Privileged Negative Conditioning (Reference-Conditioned Distillation)**: This setting revealed the problem.
 - **Antagonistic Error Dynamics**: Positive-only training significantly reduced the positive-branch error but concurrently *increased* the negative-branch error. Naive CFG-composed matching exhibited a similar antagonistic pattern, confirming that reducing one error came at the expense of increasing the other. This dynamic was identified as the optimization signature of NBA. In contrast, PDM managed to reduce the positive error while preventing sustained growth of the negative error, indicating its effectiveness in restoring balanced supervision.
 - **Distillation-Induced CFG Sensitivity**: While the teacher model maintained consistent style across different inference guidance scales, the naive matching student exhibited visual distortion and style drift, particularly at lower guidance scales (e.g., γ=1). PDM, however, closely tracked the teacher's stable behavior across guidance scales, demonstrating that it mitigated the excess sensitivity introduced by naive distillation.

#### 4.2. Application to Dense-to-Sparse Video Control

- **Improved Control Fidelity**: At the training guidance scale (γ=5), PDM consistently delivered the most substantial improvements in control fidelity across pose, depth, and scribble modalities compared to naive OPD, SFT, and off-policy distillation. IBM also generally outperformed naive matching across most control metrics. This indicates that branch-aware supervision enhances knowledge transfer even when no guidance scale shifts occur.
- **Robustness to Guidance-Scale Changes**: When models were evaluated at inference guidance scales different from the training scale (γ_train=5), naive matching exhibited sharp degradation, especially at γ=1. Conversely, both PDM and IBM maintained stable and robust performance across the evaluated range of inference guidance scales. This demonstrated that branch-aware supervision effectively prevented the excess guidance sensitivity that characterizes NBA. Qualitative results further illustrated that naive OPD suffered from severe blurring and structural degradation off-scale, while PDM preserved subject appearance and video structure.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Effect of Direction-Matching Weight (λ in PDM)**: An analysis of λ showed that λ=1 provided the optimal balance of control fidelity, achieving the lowest keyframe MPJPE and highest PCK@0.1. Larger λ values improved some distributional quality metrics but sometimes reduced control accuracy.
- **Effect of Supervision Horizon (K)**: For the dense-to-sparse video application, supervising the first K=8 student-visited states was found to offer the strongest control fidelity. This configuration also provided substantial computational efficiency compared to full-trajectory supervision (K=50).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and methodology of distilling diffusion models, particularly in the context of on-policy learning and classifier-free guidance.

The primary significance lies in identifying and characterizing **Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)**. By demonstrating that naive CFG-composed OPD objectives are under-identified at the branch level, and that this ambiguity becomes actively harmful under privileged negative conditioning, the paper uncovers a fundamental limitation in existing distillation approaches. This analysis provides a new diagnostic lens for evaluating and comparing diffusion model distillation techniques.

The introduction of **Positive–Direction Matching (PDM)** offers a principled solution to NBA. By separately supervising the positive prediction and the CFG conditional direction, PDM enforces more granular control over the learning process, ensuring that errors in both branches are reduced without detrimental compensation. This leads to several practical impacts:
- **Enhanced Robustness**: PDM produces distilled models that are significantly more robust to variations in inference guidance scales, which is a common requirement in practical applications where users may adjust guidance to achieve desired output characteristics. This reduces the need for re-training or complex adaptation strategies for different guidance settings.
- **Improved Knowledge Transfer**: The results in dense-to-sparse video control demonstrate that PDM facilitates more effective knowledge transfer from teachers with rich, dense control information to students with sparse inputs. This is crucial for developing efficient generative models that can operate with minimal user input while still achieving high fidelity.
- **Broader Applicability**: The findings suggest that the principles of branch-aware supervision could be beneficial in other scenarios involving conditioning asymmetries in diffusion models, potentially extending beyond the specific OPD context studied here.

Potential future impacts include guiding the design of more robust and generalizable distillation methods for various generative tasks, especially those where teachers have access to richer or different conditioning information than students. The work also establishes a foundation for further theoretical investigations into the optimization dynamics of branch-aware objectives and how different weighting schemes (e.g., in PDM versus IBM) influence convergence and performance.
