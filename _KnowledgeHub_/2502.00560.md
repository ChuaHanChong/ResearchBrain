# Solving Football by Exploiting Equilibrium Structure of 2p0s Differential Games with One-Sided Information

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Arizona State University and Purdue University formally proved that two-player zero-sum differential games with one-sided incomplete information exhibit an "atomic" equilibrium structure, enabling an exponential reduction in game-tree complexity. This theoretical finding, coupled with integrated algorithms, facilitated the solution of complex, continuous-action differential games, such as a 22-player American football scenario, on standard computational resources.

## Problem

- Existing solvers for imperfect-information extensive-form games (IIEFGs) face scalability challenges, with computational complexity growing exponentially with action set size and game depth (U^2K).
- Applying current IIEFG solvers to real-world differential games with continuous action spaces, continuous time, and continuous states is often intractable, requiring significant abstraction or overhead.
- Modeling and solving two-player zero-sum differential games with one-sided incomplete information (2p0s1) is challenging due to information asymmetry and strategic deception.

## Method

- The continuous ==differential game== is reformulated into discrete-time primal and dual dynamic programming (DP) problems, which explicitly define the value function and its convex conjugate.
- Novel algorithms (**CAMS** for value approximation, CAMS-DRL for model-free reinforcement learning, and CAMS-MPC for model predictive control) are developed to directly exploit the identified atomic equilibrium structure.
- A multigrid Full Approximation Scheme (FAS) is extended to these nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs, accelerating value approximation and enhancing scalability for longer time horizons.

## Results

- The CAMS approach for value approximation demonstrated computational costs invariant to the discretized action space size (U) and achieved superior accuracy on multi-stage Hexner's game compared to DeepCFR and JPSPG.
- The integration of a multigrid scheme reduced the runtime for a **10**-stage Hexner's game from **27.6** hours to **10.9** hours on an H100 GPU.
- The methodology enabled the solution of a **22**-player, **10**-time-step American football game with continuous actions in **30** minutes on an M1 Pro Macbook, a problem previously considered intractable with an estimated complexity of **10**^**440**, yielding realistic strategic deception behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Equilibrium behavioral strategies for the informed player (P1) are I-atomic (at most I discrete actions), and for the uninformed player (P2) are (I+1)-atomic, arising from the value function's convexification w.r.t. belief.
> - This atomicity drastically reduces game-tree complexity from U^2K to I^K (for P1) or (I+1)^K (for P2), where I is the number of payoff types.
> - The equilibrium strategies from discrete-time primal and dual formulations provably converge to the Nash equilibrium of the continuous differential game as the time step approaches zero.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of game theory and artificial intelligence has seen significant advancements in developing solvers for imperfect-information extensive-form games (IIEFGs), achieving superhuman performance in games such as Chess, Go, Poker, Diplomacy, and Stratego. However, existing IIEFG solvers, including those based on regret minimization (e.g., CFR+) or gradient descent-ascent (e.g., DeepCFR, JPSPG, PPO, MMD), face substantial scalability challenges. Their computational complexity generally increases with the size of the finite action set ($U$), and game-tree complexity grows exponentially with both the action space size ($U$) and the game's depth ($K$). This poses a barrier to applying these methods to real-world scenarios characterized by continuous action spaces, continuous time, and continuous state spaces, which are inherently differential games. Applying current solvers to differential games often necessitates game-specific abstractions or incurs considerable computational overhead for automated abstraction.

This research addresses a specific subset of these challenging games: two-player zero-sum differential games with one-sided incomplete information (termed "2p0s1" games). In this class, one player (P1, the informed player) possesses complete knowledge of the game's payoff type, while the other (P2, the uninformed player) operates under a probability distribution (belief) over a finite set ($I$) of possible payoff types. Such information asymmetry is characteristic of various strategic domains, including sports, defense, cybersecurity, and finance, where deception and belief manipulation by the informed player can be crucial.

Previous work on 2p0s games with incomplete information, notably by Harsanyi (1967), Aumann et al. (1995), and De Meyer (1996), established theoretical foundations for equilibrium strategies in repeated games, including the "Cav u" theorem and dual game reformulations. More recent contributions by Cardaliaguet (2007) and Ghimire et al. (2024) extended value existence proofs to 2p0s differential games. This paper builds upon these theoretical underpinnings by providing a detailed analysis of the convexification properties of behavioral strategies in differential games and, as a consequence, identifying their atomic structure.

The distinct contribution of this work is the demonstration that for this specific class of 2p0s1 differential games, the equilibrium strategies exhibit a sparse, "atomic" structure. This structural insight allows for a drastic reduction in game-tree complexity from $U^{2K}$ (for general IIEFGs) to $I^K$ (for P1) or $(I+1)^K$ (for P2), where $I$ is the number of payoff types. This exponential reduction in complexity aims to render previously intractable large-scale, continuous-action differential games solvable. Furthermore, the paper integrates this structural knowledge into existing multiagent reinforcement learning (MARL) and model predictive control (MPC) frameworks, and introduces a multigrid scheme to accelerate value approximation for the Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs underlying these games, extending techniques from literature such as Trottenberg et al. (2000) and Han & Wan (2013).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the scalability and efficiency of game-solving algorithms for a specific, yet broadly applicable, class of real-world extensive-form games. The core motivations driving this objective are:

1. **Addressing the Scalability Challenge of IIEFG Solvers:** Conventional IIEFG solvers struggle with games characterized by large or infinite action spaces ($U$) and extended time horizons ($K$). The fundamental issue lies in the game-tree complexity, which typically grows as $U^{2K}$. This exponential growth makes many real-world strategic interactions, particularly those modeled as differential games with continuous actions, computationally intractable. The research seeks to overcome this limitation for a specific game class.

2. **Focusing on Two-Player Zero-Sum Games with One-Sided Information (2p0s1):** The paper is motivated by the prevalence and strategic significance of 2p0s1 games across various domains. In these games, an "informed" player (P1) knows the true payoff type, while an "uninformed" player (P2) only holds a probabilistic belief over a finite set of $I$ possible types. This asymmetry introduces strategic elements of deception, information concealment, and belief manipulation by P1, which are critical in many competitive environments. Examples cited include tactical decisions in sports (e.g., football play calls), attack-defense scenarios in military or cybersecurity contexts (e.g., missile defense), and financial markets.

3. **Exploiting Undiscovered Equilibrium Structure:** A key motivation is the hypothesis that despite the apparent complexity, 2p0s1 differential games might possess a hidden, simpler equilibrium structure. The researchers aim to theoretically prove such a structure and demonstrate its practical utility. Specifically, they conjecture that equilibrium strategies for P1 and P2 might not require mixing over the entire continuous action space but rather concentrate on a limited number of "action prototypes" linked to the number of payoff types. This "atomic" nature, if proven, would inherently simplify the search for optimal strategies.

4. **Improving Solution Accuracy and Efficiency:** Beyond theoretical insights, the research is motivated by the desire to translate structural understanding into tangible improvements for practical game-solving. This involves:
 - Developing novel or adapting existing algorithms (e.g., multiagent reinforcement learning, model predictive control) to directly incorporate the identified atomic equilibrium structure.
 - Achieving qualitative improvements in solution accuracy and efficiency compared to state-of-the-art IIEFG solvers that do not leverage this specific structural property.
 - Enabling the solution of previously intractable, large-scale problems, such as a 22-player football game with continuous actions and multiple time steps, on readily available computational resources.

In essence, the research is driven by the need for scalable and effective solutions for complex, information-asymmetric differential games, pinpointing and leveraging a fundamental equilibrium property to achieve this.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on a theoretical characterization of equilibrium strategies in 2p0s1 differential games, followed by the development of practical algorithms that exploit these theoretical insights.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work considers a time-invariant dynamical system describing the evolution of a joint state $x \in X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$ under control inputs $u \in U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d_u}$ from Player 1 (P1) and $v \in V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$ from Player 2 (P2), given by $\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u, v)$. P1 is informed of the true game type $i \in [I]$, while P2 holds a public belief $p \in \Delta(I)$ over the possible types. P1 minimizes an expected cost $J_i$ (comprising running costs $l_i$ and terminal costs $g_i$), and P2 maximizes its overall expected cost $J = E_{i \sim p_0}[J_i]$. The game's value $V$ is proven to exist under standard conditions, including compact action spaces, Lipschitz continuous dynamics and costs, full observability of controls/states, perfect recall, and Isaacs' condition (A4), which ensures the existence of pure Nash equilibria in complete-information versions of the game. The objective is to compute a Nash equilibrium $(\{\eta^\dagger_i\}, \zeta^\dagger)$ that achieves this value.

#### 3.2. Primal-Dual Reformulation and Atomic Equilibrium Structure

The core of the methodology involves reformulating the continuous differential game into discrete-time primal and dual dynamic programming (DP) problems, denoted $G_\tau$ and $G^*_\tau$ respectively, with a time interval $\tau$.

- **The Primal Game ($G_\tau$):** In this discrete-time Stackelberg formulation, P2 plays a pure best response after P1 announces its action. The value $V_\tau(t, x, p)$ is defined by a backward DP equation (Eq. 3). The public belief $p$ is updated via Bayes' rule based on P1's announced action.
- **The Dual Game ($G^*_\tau$):** To derive P2's strategy, a separate DP formulation is used, leveraging the convex conjugate $V^*(t, x, \hat{p})$ of the value function (Eq. 4). $\hat{p}$ is a dual variable representing the type-dependent losses P1 would incur if it played best responses to P2's equilibrium strategy. P2's goal in the dual game is to minimize the worst-case risk across game types. The dual DP (Eq. 5) is solved backward in time.

**Theorem 4.1** is a central theoretical result, proving the "atomic" nature of equilibrium strategies:
- P1's equilibrium behavioral strategy $\eta^\dagger_{i, \tau}$ in $G_\tau$ is **I-atomic**, meaning it concentrates on at most $I$ discrete actions within its continuous action space $U$.
- P2's equilibrium behavioral strategy $\zeta^\dagger_\tau$ in $G^*_\tau$ is **(I+1)-atomic**, concentrating on at most $I+1$ discrete actions within its continuous action space $V$.
This atomicity arises from the convexification (or concavification in the dual) of the value function with respect to the belief variable, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This structure causes a collapse in the game-tree complexity from $U^{2K}$ to $I^K$ (for P1) or $(I+1)^K$ (for P2).

**Theorem 4.2** further establishes that the equilibrium strategies $(\{\eta^\dagger_{i, \tau}\}, \zeta^\dagger_\tau)$ computed from these discrete-time primal and dual formulations approach the true Nash equilibrium of the continuous differential game $G$ as the time interval $\tau \to 0^+$.

#### 3.3. Algorithmic Applications Incorporating Atomic Structure

The identified atomic structure is then leveraged across three practical game-solving paradigms:

1. **Value Approximation (CAMS - Continuous-Action Mixed-Strategy Solver):**
 - The time horizon is discretized into $K$ steps. At each time step $t$, the DP equations are solved at a set of collocation points $(x,p)$ using DS-GDA, an algorithm for nonconvex-nonconcave minimax problems.
 - A value network is trained on these minimax solutions to generalize the value and strategies across the state-belief space, serving as the terminal value for the preceding time step.
 - **Multigrid Scheme:** To counter the exponential computational complexity with $K$ (or $1/\tau$), a Full Approximation Scheme (FAS) is extended to these nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs. This multigrid method uses coarse-grid solutions to efficiently reduce low-frequency approximation errors, speeding up the convergence of fine-grid solutions.

2. **Model-Free Multiagent Reinforcement Learning (CAMS-DRL):**
 - Policy networks are designed to directly output I logit vectors and I action prototypes for P1 (and I+1 for P2), reflecting the atomic structure.
 - Standard policy gradient methods like PPO and MMD are then applied to these structured policy networks, aiming to learn the atomic Nash equilibrium.

3. **Model Predictive Control (CAMS-MPC):**
 - For known and differentiable dynamics, the primal/dual minimax objectives are formulated as a sum over the exponentially reduced game tree paths ($I^K$ or $(I+1)^K$).
 - A computational graph for the entire tree is constructed, where policy networks follow the CAMS-DRL architecture. DS-GDA is then applied to this differentiable loss, enabling an MPC approach that is feasible due to the collapsed tree complexity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical proofs and empirical validations across various game settings, demonstrating the utility of the identified atomic Nash equilibrium (NE) structure.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- The work formally proves that for 2p0s1 differential games, the equilibrium behavioral strategy of the informed player (P1) is *I-atomic*, concentrating on at most $I$ action prototypes, and that of the uninformed player (P2) is *(I+1)-atomic*. This atomicity arises from the value function's convexification (or concavification in the dual) with respect to the belief.
- This atomic structure leads to a significant reduction in game-tree complexity from $U^{2K}$ (for general IIEFGs) to $I^K$ for P1 and $(I+1)^K$ for P2, where $I$ is the number of payoff types.
- It is demonstrated that the equilibrium strategies derived from discrete-time primal and dual formulations ($G_\tau$, $G^*_\tau$) converge to the NE of the continuous differential game ($G$) as the time step $\tau$ approaches zero.

#### 4.2. Empirical Findings on Hexner's Game (Value Approximation)

Experiments on Hexner's game, a differential game with an analytical NE, validate the proposed CAMS approach (for value approximation) against state-of-the-art baselines:

- **Action-Size Invariance:** CAMS exhibits computational costs and wall-time performance that are invariant to the size of the discretized action space ($U$). In contrast, baseline solvers (CFR+, MMD, CFR-BR-Primal, JPSPG) show increasing costs as $U$ grows, confirming the scalability benefits of the atomic structure (Fig. 3a, 3b).
- **Superior Accuracy:** For multi-stage Hexner's games (e.g., 4-stage), CAMS achieves notably lower expected action errors (closer to ground-truth NE) compared to DeepCFR and JPSPG, often with comparable or less computational effort (Fig. 3c, 3d). For instance, CAMS achieved better results in 17 hours on an A100 GPU, compared to 24-34 hours for baselines.
- **Strategic Concealment:** CAMS successfully learns the P1's strategic behavior of concealing its type until a critical moment. The average predicted revelation time (0.60s ± 0.06s) closely matches the analytical ground truth (0.5s).
- **Multigrid Efficiency:** The integration of the multigrid scheme significantly accelerates CAMS runtime. For a 10-stage Hexner's game, multigrid reduced the runtime from 27.6 hours to 10.9 hours on an H100 GPU (Table 1), demonstrating improved scalability for longer time horizons.

#### 4.3. Empirical Findings on Hexner's Game (Model-Free MARL)

- **CAMS-DRL Performance:** In model-free settings, CAMS-DRL demonstrates superior accuracy in approximating NEs compared to standard policy gradient methods like MMD and PPO, particularly in multi-stage games (Fig. 6a, 6b).
- **PG Limitations:** The results suggest that standard policy gradient methods (PPO, MMD) may struggle to converge to non-interior solutions, which are characteristic of the atomic NEs in 2p0s1 games. R-NaD showed comparatively better performance among the baselines.

#### 4.4. Empirical Findings on Hexner's Game (MPC)

- For the 10-stage Hexner's game with known differentiable dynamics and I=2, CAMS-MPC (leveraging the differentiable game tree and DS-GDA) converges to the ground-truth NE in approximately 5 minutes on an M1 Pro Macbook.

#### 4.5. Empirical Findings on American Football Game (MPC)

- **Intractability Overcome:** The methodology enabled the solution of an 11-vs-11 (22-player), 10-time-step American football game with continuous action spaces. This problem, estimated to have a complexity of $10^{440}$ with a coarse mesh for traditional solvers, was solved in 30 minutes on an M1 Pro Macbook.
- **Qualitative Realism:** The learned strategies for offense (RB power push, QB throw) and defense (closing on ball carrier, guarding) exhibit realistic tactical behaviors observed in actual football.
- **Strategic Deception:** The offense demonstrates strategic concealment of its play selection for approximately 0.5 seconds, aligning with real-world coaching analyses of play development.
- **Minimal Exploitability:** Exploitability estimates indicate that the learned policies converge towards a low-exploitability state, suggesting proximity to a Nash equilibrium (Fig. 6c).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the tractability of complex multiagent sequential decision-making problems, particularly for a class of games with incomplete information. The core significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Solvability for Differential Games with Incomplete Information:** The discovery and formal proof of the atomic Nash equilibrium structure for 2p0s1 differential games fundamentally changes the computational landscape for these problems. By reducing the game-tree complexity from an intractable $U^{2K}$ to a more manageable $I^K$ (or $(I+1)^K$), the work renders a broad category of previously unsolvable or computationally expensive games accessible to existing algorithms. This theoretical insight directly addresses a major bottleneck in applying game theory to real-world scenarios.

2. **Bridging Theory and Practice in Game AI:** The research effectively translates a deep theoretical understanding of game equilibria into practical, implementable algorithms. By adapting value approximation, model-free reinforcement learning, and model predictive control frameworks to leverage the atomic structure, the paper demonstrates how theoretical breakthroughs can lead to tangible improvements in AI agent design and performance.

3. **Scalability to Continuous Action Spaces and Long Horizons:** The ability to solve complex, continuous-action differential games with multiple time steps (e.g., the 22-player football game) on consumer-grade hardware is a notable achievement. This overcomes a critical limitation of traditional IIEFG solvers that struggle with infinite action spaces and deep game trees, opening avenues for high-fidelity modeling and analysis of real-world physical systems.

4. **Modeling Strategic Deception and Counter-Deception:** The explicit focus on one-sided information and P1's ability to manipulate P2's belief makes this work highly relevant to understanding and generating deceptive and counter-deceptive strategies in autonomous agents. This capability is crucial for designing intelligent systems that operate in competitive environments where information asymmetry is inherent.

5. **Broad Applicability Across Diverse Domains:** The principles and methodologies developed have direct and indirect applications across various fields:
 - **Sports Analytics and AI Coaching:** As demonstrated with the American football game, the framework can model complex team dynamics and strategic play-calling, offering insights for strategy development and AI-driven coaching systems.
 - **Defense and Security:** The work is directly applicable to attack-defense scenarios, such as missile/drone defense, cybersecurity, and strategic planning, where one party has private information about intentions or capabilities.
 - **Finance and Economics:** Modeling market manipulation, insider trading scenarios, or strategic investment under asymmetric information could benefit from this framework.
 - **Risk-Sensitive Robotics and Autonomous Systems:** Designing robotic systems that operate in uncertain and adversarial environments requires understanding how to manage information and exploit or mitigate deception.

6. **Methodological Contributions to Multigrid and DRL:** The extension of the Full Approximation Scheme (FAS) to solve nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs in this game-theoretic context further advances numerical methods for optimal control and differential games. Additionally, the insights into why standard policy gradient methods may struggle with non-interior NE solutions contribute to the understanding of DRL convergence properties in complex game settings.

In summary, this research provides a foundational theoretical insight coupled with practical algorithmic solutions that significantly expand the frontier of solvable games, particularly those involving strategic deception in dynamic, continuous environments. This can lead to the development of more sophisticated and robust AI agents capable of operating in complex real-world competitive settings.
