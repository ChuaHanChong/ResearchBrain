# DynamicWAM: Dual-Path Motion Conditioning for World-Action Models in Dynamic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> DynamicWAM, developed by researchers from PKU-PI Lab, Peking University, and other institutions, enhances robot manipulation of moving objects by integrating dual-path motion conditioning and asynchronous execution into World–Action Models. The framework achieves state-of-the-art performance, with a 38.2% success rate on the DOMINO benchmark and a 46.67% average success rate across real-world dynamic tasks, by providing comprehensive motion awareness and responsive control.

## Problem

- Existing World–Action Models (WAMs) often lack comprehensive motion awareness, primarily conditioning on single observation frames, which can lead to ambiguity about target motion in dynamic environments.
- Many WAMs suffer from perception-execution mismatch due to synchronous execution, where target objects move significantly during the model's inference and action execution phases, leading to outdated predictions and degraded control.
- Prior work in dynamic manipulation struggled to effectively integrate both dense spatial motion cues and quantitative kinematic information for responsive robot control without altering the action space.

## Method

- Introduces **Dual-Path Motion Conditioning**, combining a ==History-Flow pathway== for dense spatial motion cues (using rendered optical flow history) and a ==Kinematic Token pathway== for quantitative motion magnitude and timing information.
- Employs a compact video expert distilled from a large pretrained model and integrates it with an action expert via ==joint world–action attention== for efficient processing and comprehensive information fusion.
- Utilizes ==Real-Time Chunking (RTC)== for asynchronous execution on real robots, overlapping inference with action execution to hide latency and improve responsiveness in dynamic scenarios.

## Results

- Achieved a **38.2%** success rate and **53.2** manipulation score on DOMINO Level 1, outperforming baselines like InternVLA-A1.5 (29.3% SR, 42.5 MS) and PUMA (17.2% SR, 35.0 MS).
- Demonstrated an average success rate of **46.67%** across 12 real-world dynamic tasks, surpassing the strongest external baseline (π_0.5_) by **22.92** percentage points (**23.75%** SR).
- Ablation studies confirmed that both history-flow and kinematic token conditioning independently improved performance, with their combination yielding an additional **7.7** SR points and **6.0** MS points in simulation, and an overall **4.59** percentage point gain from asynchronous execution in real-world tests.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Both dense spatial motion cues (history-flow) and quantitative kinematic information (kinematic tokens) provide distinct yet complementary benefits for dynamic manipulation, with their combination yielding superior performance.
> - Motion magnitude and timing information, often lost in normalized visual flow representations, are critical for tasks requiring precise contact with moving targets, and kinematic tokens effectively capture this.
> - Distilling a compact video expert and integrating it with asynchronous execution (RTC) enables practical, real-time robot operation in dynamic environments by effectively managing computational latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing from static environments to dynamic settings where target objects move during inference and action execution. This transition poses challenges for robot policies, which require both an understanding of target motion and responsive control mechanisms.

World–Action Models (WAMs) represent a class of robot control policies that integrate future visual prediction with action generation. Recent WAMs have aimed to reduce computational costs associated with future prediction through techniques such as compact video branches, low-resolution future latents, asymmetric denoising, and foresight distillation. However, many existing WAMs primarily condition their future predictions on a single current observation frame and often execute large model backbones synchronously at action-chunk boundaries. This approach can lead to limitations in motion awareness, as a single frame may not sufficiently convey the target's motion state, and can result in outdated predictions if the target moves significantly during the model's inference and action execution phases.

Prior work in dynamic manipulation has explored various motion representations for robot control, including dense optical flow, pixel displacement fields, and sparse point trajectories. Some approaches, such as PUMA, incorporate historical optical flow for short-horizon object-state prediction within a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework. FlowWAM uses generated optical flow as an action representation or a video-generation condition. Other generalist methods, like DynamicVLA, focus on improving responsiveness through compact architectures and continuous inference. Complementary execution methods, such as Real-Time Chunking (RTC), address latency by overlapping inference with action execution.

DynamicWAM contributes to this landscape by addressing the specific limitations of existing WAMs in dynamic environments. It introduces a dual-path motion conditioning strategy that aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of target motion than current WAMs. By combining both dense spatial motion cues (via history-flow conditioning) and quantitative motion magnitude and timing information (via kinematic token conditioning), DynamicWAM seeks to enhance both future prediction and responsive control within a WAM framework, without altering the robot's action space or requiring specialized task-specific controllers. Furthermore, its integration with RTC-based asynchronous execution aims to improve real-world responsiveness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a World–Action Model (WAM) capable of robust and responsive manipulation of dynamic objects. The authors identify two key limitations of existing WAMs in dynamic manipulation scenarios:

1. **Limited Motion Awareness from Single-Frame Conditioning:** Conventional WAMs typically condition their predictions and action generation predominantly on the current observation frame. As illustrated in the paper, visually similar current frames can correspond to entirely different target motions (e.g., an object moving left-to-right versus right-to-left passing through the same position). Relying solely on the current frame can lead to ambiguity regarding the target's motion state, which is critical for determining appropriate contact actions. This limitation motivates the need for mechanisms that can incorporate historical motion context.

2. **Lack of Responsive Control due to Synchronous Execution:** Many WAMs invoke their computational backbones synchronously at predetermined action-chunk boundaries. In dynamic environments, the target object continues to move during the time taken for the robot policy to infer and execute an action chunk. Predictions made at inference time can quickly become outdated, leading to a perception-execution mismatch and degraded control performance. This highlights the necessity for more responsive execution strategies that can mitigate the impact of inference latency.

To address these motivations, DynamicWAM sets forth the following key objectives:

- **Introduce Dual-Path Motion Conditioning:** The core objective is to design a system that captures comprehensive motion information through two complementary pathways.
 - **History-Flow Conditioning:** To encode dense spatial motion information (where motion occurs, its direction, and spatial organization), the model needs to process a history of optical flow frames. This path aims to integrate recent visual motion into the video prediction pathway without modifying the robot's action space. A specific challenge identified here is that standard optical flow renderings normalize magnitude, removing global displacement scale.
 - **Kinematic Token Conditioning:** To complement the history-flow by providing quantitative motion magnitude and timing (how far and how fast the target is moving, the duration of motion intervals), the model needs to extract and inject kinematic descriptors into the action expert. This aims to overcome the ambiguity of scale and timing lost in normalized flow renderings, which is crucial for precise contact with moving targets.

- **Integrate Motion Cues through Joint World–Action Attention:** The two complementary motion pathways (history-flow and kinematic tokens) should be effectively fused within the WAM's architecture. This fusion mechanism aims to allow both future visual prediction and action generation to leverage both forms of motion evidence collaboratively.

- **Enable Responsive Deployment with a Compact Architecture and Asynchronous Execution:** To facilitate practical, real-time robot operation, the model needs to be computationally efficient. This involves using a compact video expert (distilled from a larger model) and integrating it with asynchronous execution mechanisms like Real-Time Chunking (RTC) to hide inference latency and ensure smooth action generation.

By achieving these objectives, DynamicWAM aims to demonstrate improved performance in dynamic manipulation tasks compared to existing WAMs and VLAs, both in simulation and on real robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DynamicWAM extends a standard World–Action Model by integrating dual-path motion conditioning, a compact backbone, and an asynchronous execution strategy.

#### 3.1. World–Action Backbone

The backbone consists of a compact video expert and an action expert. The video expert is distilled from a large, pretrained video model (Wan2.2-TI2V-5B), while the pretrained video VAE used for encoding observations is kept frozen. The action expert predicts 16 joint-position actions. Both experts interact through layer-wise joint world–action attention, where video and action queries attend to concatenated keys and values from both streams. The model predicts conditional-flow-matching vector fields for both visual latents and actions, conditioned on the current multi-view observation, a language instruction, and the proprioceptive state.

#### 3.2. Dual-Path Motion Conditioning

DynamicWAM augments the conditioning of the WAM with two additional sources of information: rendered flow history and kinematic descriptors.

- **History-Flow Conditioning:**
 - **Construction:** For each policy query at time *t*, the observation history is partitioned into *K* = 4 intervals, each separated by ∆ = 4 policy steps. These intervals correspond to raw-frame offsets of relative to *t*.
 - **Optical Flow Calculation:** Dense Farnebäck optical flow is computed between adjacent frames in each interval from a fixed head camera. A forward–backward consistency check is applied to mask unreliable flow vectors, yielding a masked flow field. If the fraction of accepted pixels falls below 0.1, the interval is marked invalid.
 - **Rendering:** Each masked flow field is rendered as an RGB image, R_k. The direction of motion is mapped to hue, and the magnitude is mapped to intensity, normalized by the 99th percentile magnitude of flow within that specific frame. This per-frame normalization removes absolute displacement scale.
 - **Encoding:** The rendered history frames (R_1..., R_K) are concatenated with the current multi-view observation along the temporal axis. This combined input is then encoded by the frozen pretrained video VAE, providing spatial motion cues to the video prediction pathway. This approach conditions on observed flow without altering the robot's action space.

- **Kinematic Token Conditioning:**
 - **Descriptor Construction:** To address the scale and timing ambiguity of history-flow rendering, a 12-dimensional kinematic descriptor is computed for each valid interval. This descriptor includes:
 - Mean signed displacement (d_k)
 - Mean displacement magnitude (d_k-bar)
 - 99th-percentile magnitude (d_99_k)
 - Interval duration (∆τ_k)
 - Corresponding velocity statistics (v_k, v_k-bar, v_99_k), derived by normalizing displacement by ∆τ_k.
 - Acceleration (a_k) and its magnitude (∥a_k∥_2), estimated from changes in velocity between intervals.
 - **Standardization and Embedding:** These descriptors are standardized using dataset-level statistics. Invalid intervals or unavailable predecessor intervals for acceleration calculations are handled with learned invalid embeddings. Each 12-D descriptor is projected to the action-expert's width by a two-layer MLP and combined with interval-position and type embeddings to produce kinematic tokens (e_1..., e_K).
 - **Injection:** These kinematic tokens are inserted into the action stream alongside proprioceptive and action tokens. They participate in every joint-attention block, influencing both visual prediction and action generation, but the action decoder only reads the original state–action tokens, preserving the robot's action representation.

#### 3.3. Joint Training Objective

DynamicWAM is trained using a conditional flow matching objective. The joint objective optimizes both the action and video streams by minimizing the squared error between predicted and target conditional-flow-matching vector fields.

#### 3.4. Multi-Stage Training Procedure

The training process involves three stages:
1. **Video-expert distillation:** The compact WAN (video expert) is distilled from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B using ground-truth flow matching, hidden-state distillation, and motion distillation objectives, while the teacher model is frozen.
2. **Action-expert pretraining:** The action expert and kinematic-token module are trained with only the action flow-matching loss, while the compact WAN remains frozen.
3. **Joint refinement:** The action expert, kinematic-token module, and compact WAN core are jointly fine-tuned, optimizing both action and video flow-matching losses.

#### 3.5. RTC-Based Asynchronous Execution

For real-robot deployment, DynamicWAM employs Real-Time Chunking (RTC). RTC predicts the next action chunk while the current chunk is being executed, smoothly merging consecutive chunks. This overlaps inference with execution, thereby hiding inference latency and reducing perception–execution lag without reducing the latency of a single model query. Optical flow history is updated online from the head camera.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DynamicWAM was conducted across simulation experiments on the DOMINO benchmark and real-world robot experiments involving a custom 12-task suite.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments (DOMINO Level 1)

- **Overall Performance:** DynamicWAM achieved a 38.2% success rate (SR) and a 53.2 manipulation score (MS) on DOMINO Level 1 under the clean dynamic setting. This outperformed all evaluated baselines, including InternVLA-A1.5 (29.3% SR, 42.5 MS) and PUMA (17.2% SR, 35.0 MS).
- **Latency:** DynamicWAM's per-policy query latency was 173.7 ms, which was competitive with and faster than some strong baselines, demonstrating a favorable trade-off between efficiency and performance.
- **Ablation Study:**
 - **Impact of History-Flow Conditioning:** Adding only history-flow conditioning (DynamicWAM w/o motion) to a static WAM (DynamicWAM w/o flow & motion) improved SR from 22.7% to 27.2% and MS from 38.3 to 41.6. This indicated that preserving spatial structure and temporal ordering of recent motion is beneficial.
 - **Impact of Kinematic Token Conditioning:** The kinematic-token-only variant (DynamicWAM w/o flow) achieved 30.5% SR and 47.2 MS, surpassing the history-flow-only variant. This demonstrated that motion magnitude and timing information, not retained by normalized flow, provides distinct advantages.
 - **Complementary Gains:** Combining both paths (full DynamicWAM) resulted in 38.2% SR and 53.2 MS, outperforming the stronger single-path variant by 7.7 SR points and 6.0 MS points, confirming the complementary nature of the two motion-conditioning pathways.
- **Task-Level Effects:** The largest performance gains were observed in tasks requiring accurately timed contact, such as "move_pillbottle_pad" (42% SR to 91% SR with full model) and "beat_block_hammer" (36% SR to 84% SR). Kinematic tokens were particularly crucial for tasks like "place_phone_stand," where they significantly improved success over history-flow alone.
- **Motion Structure in Flow Latents:** t-SNE visualizations of history-flow latents showed locally coherent groups corresponding to differences in motion direction, spatial extent, and image location, indicating that the frozen video VAE effectively preserved motion-relevant spatial variations.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

- **Overall Performance:** DynamicWAM achieved an average success rate of 46.67% across 12 real-world tasks. This exceeded the strongest external baseline, π_0.5_, by 22.92 percentage points (23.75%).
- **Performance Across Motion Levels:**
 - **Level 1 (Linear Motion):** DynamicWAM achieved 70.00% success, compared to 32.50% for π_0.5_ and 30.00% for DynamicVLA.
 - **Level 2 (Circular Motion):** DynamicWAM achieved 51.25% success.
 - **Level 3 (Compound Motion):** DynamicWAM achieved 18.75% success, while external baselines recorded no successful trials in this more challenging category.
- **Ablation of Dual-Path Conditioning (Real-World):** Consistent with simulation results, both history-flow and kinematic token conditioning independently increased the average success rate from 25.00% (static WAM) to 36.25% and 40.00% respectively. Combining both paths further improved performance to 46.67%, reiterating their complementary benefits.
- **Effect of Asynchronous Execution (RTC):** RTC improved the average real-world success rate from 42.08% (synchronous execution) to 46.67% (+4.59 points). The gains were most pronounced on Level 3 tasks (+6.25 points), suggesting RTC's value in complex dynamic scenarios. The synchronous variant of DynamicWAM still outperformed all external baselines, indicating that motion conditioning and RTC provide distinct benefits.
- **Visual Generalization:** DynamicWAM demonstrated higher robustness under visual distribution shifts (background, lighting, object-color changes) on Level 1 tasks. It achieved an average out-of-distribution success rate of 36.3%, compared to 17.5% for π_0.5_ and 20.0% for a current-frame variant. The retention ratios (OOD-SR/ID-SR) were similar across methods, implying DynamicWAM's OOD advantage primarily stems from its stronger in-distribution performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in DynamicWAM offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for dynamic environments:

#### 5.1. Significance of Contributions

- **Enhanced Motion Awareness in WAMs:** DynamicWAM demonstrates that explicit conditioning on recent target motion is crucial for effective dynamic manipulation. By introducing dual-path motion conditioning, the model can disambiguate similar static observations that arise from different underlying object motions, a limitation of prior WAMs.
- **Complementary Motion Representation:** The research highlights the distinct yet complementary roles of dense spatial motion cues (history-flow) and quantitative kinematic information (kinematic tokens). History-flow preserves the spatial organization and temporal ordering of motion, while kinematic tokens provide the critical magnitude and timing information often lost in normalized visual flow representations. Their combined effect yields substantial performance improvements, suggesting a robust way to represent complex motion for robot control.
- **Responsive and Efficient Robot Control:** The integration of a distilled compact video expert with Real-Time Chunking (RTC)-based asynchronous execution addresses the critical issue of perception-execution latency in real-world dynamic tasks. This approach enables more responsive control by overlapping inference with action execution, thereby reducing pauses and improving performance in fast-changing environments.
- **Strong Empirical Performance:** DynamicWAM achieved state-of-the-art results on the DOMINO benchmark and demonstrated substantial improvements in a challenging real-world 12-task suite, especially on complex target motions where other generalist policies failed. This empirical validation supports the effectiveness of the proposed architecture and methodology.
- **Improved Visual Generalization:** The enhanced motion understanding contributes to better generalization under visual appearance shifts, which is important for deploying robots in diverse and unstructured environments.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Broader Adoption of WAMs in Dynamic Settings:** By addressing key limitations in dynamic environments, DynamicWAM could facilitate wider adoption and further development of WAMs for real-world applications beyond static tabletop tasks. This opens avenues for robots operating in warehouses, manufacturing, service industries, or even personal assistance, where objects are frequently in motion.
- **Inspiration for Future Motion-Aware Architectures:** The dual-path conditioning strategy could inspire new architectures that explicitly separate and fuse different aspects of motion information (e.g., spatial patterns vs. quantitative kinematics) in other robot learning paradigms.
- **Foundation for More Complex Dynamic Tasks:** The demonstrated performance on tasks with linear, circular, and compound target motions suggests that DynamicWAM provides a more robust foundation for tackling even more complex dynamic manipulation scenarios, such as human-robot collaboration with shared workspaces or handling unpredictable object behaviors.
- **Advancements in Real-Time Robot Systems:** The effective integration of a compact model with asynchronous execution strategies pushes the boundaries of real-time robot control, showing how inference latency can be managed without compromising performance.

#### 5.3. Identified Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge several limitations that indicate directions for future research:
- **2D Image-Space Motion:** The current framework relies on two-dimensional image-space motion from a fixed external camera. This approach can be sensitive to occlusions, changes in viewpoint, and distractors, and may not fully capture the three-dimensional nature of object motion.
- **Lack of Target Isolation:** The model does not explicitly isolate the instructed target from other moving regions in the scene. This can lead to increased sensitivity to distractors and complex background motion.

Future work aims to address these limitations by exploring target-aware motion extraction (to focus on the relevant object) and three-dimensional motion modeling. Such advancements would likely improve robustness in more cluttered and complex dynamic environments, expanding the applicability of DynamicWAM to a wider range of real-world scenarios.
