# Preference-Driven Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization with Conditional Computation

> [!summary] Summary
> POCCO introduces a framework for multi-objective combinatorial optimization problems that integrates conditional computation for adaptive model structures and preference-driven optimization for enhanced exploration. This approach consistently achieves superior performance and faster convergence compared to existing neural solvers across various benchmarks, while demonstrating strong generalization capabilities.

## Problem

- Multi-objective combinatorial optimization problems (MOCOPs) are NP-hard and computationally demanding, requiring the simultaneous optimization of multiple, often conflicting, objectives.
- Current 'one-to-many' neural MOCOP methods rely on a single neural network with fixed capacity, which struggles to handle the diverse characteristics of scalarized subproblems, leading to suboptimal performance.
- Traditional DRL algorithms like REINFORCE, when applied to MOCOPs, suffer from high gradient variance and inefficient exploration within vast combinatorial action spaces, impeding stable learning.

## Method

- **POCCO** integrates a ==Conditional Computation== (CCO) block into the decoder of neural MOCOP solvers, dynamically routing subproblems to specialized Feed-Forward or Identity experts for adaptive model structures.
- The framework employs a Preference-driven MOCO (PL) algorithm that replaces scalarized rewards with pairwise preference signals, utilizing a Bradley–Terry model to achieve lower gradient variance and more stable policy updates.
- Designed as a plug-and-play approach, POCCO augments existing state-of-the-art neural MOCOP solvers (e.g., CNH and WE-CA) without requiring substantial modifications to their core architectures.

## Results

- POCCO-W consistently achieved superior average hypervolume (HV) and established new performance benchmarks compared to WE-CA and other neural baselines across all tested MOCOP scenarios (e.g., MOTSP, MOCVRP, MOKP).
- Preference Learning (PL) demonstrated significantly faster convergence and 2 to 4 orders of magnitude lower gradient variance than REINFORCE, leading to more stable and efficient optimization dynamics.
- POCCO exhibited strong out-of-distribution generalization, maintaining robust performance on larger problem sizes (e.g., Bi-TSP150-1000) and unseen benchmark instances, outperforming other neural methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adapting model capacity through conditional computation allows for specialization on diverse subproblems, improving performance and generalization while maintaining computational efficiency.
> - Leveraging relative preference signals provides a more stable and informative learning gradient, significantly reducing variance compared to absolute reward signals and accelerating convergence in complex optimization tasks.
> - The strategic placement of the conditional computation block within the decoder and the use of an Identity expert contribute to architectural sparsity and effective generalization to out-of-distribution problem instances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-objective combinatorial optimization problems (MOCOPs) are characterized by the simultaneous optimization of multiple, often conflicting, objectives within a discrete decision space. These problems are prevalent in various domains, including manufacturing, logistics, and scheduling. Unlike single-objective combinatorial optimization problems (SOCOPs), MOCOPs aim to identify a set of Pareto optimal solutions that represent the inherent trade-offs among objectives, making them computationally more demanding. The NP-hard nature of MOCOPs often renders exact methods impractical for large-scale instances, as computational complexity can increase exponentially with problem size. Consequently, heuristic approaches, particularly multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs), have become primary methods for tackling MOCOPs. However, these traditional heuristics frequently require extensive manual design, parameter tuning, and problem-specific expertise, limiting their adaptability and scalability.

Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) have demonstrated success in solving SOCOPs by learning effective decision policies from data. This success has motivated the extension of neural methods to MOCOPs, offering advantages such as reduced reliance on manual heuristic design, acceleration through GPU parallelization, and enhanced adaptability. Neural approaches to MOCOPs typically decompose these problems into a set of scalarized subproblems, each representing a SOCOP defined by a specific weight vector. DRL is then employed to approximate the Pareto front by solving these subproblems.

Existing neural MOCOP methods generally fall into two paradigms: "one-to-one" and "one-to-many." The one-to-one paradigm trains or fine-tunes a separate neural model for each scalarized subproblem, sometimes leveraging transfer learning or meta-learning. While this approach can achieve good performance for specific subproblems, it incurs substantial computational resource requirements and struggles to generalize to subproblems with unseen weight vectors. The "one-to-many" paradigm, conversely, utilizes a single neural network to address all subproblems by incorporating weight-conditioned mechanisms, such as hypernetworks or direct encoding of weight vectors into problem representations. Methods like CNH and WE-CA represent the current state-of-the-art within this paradigm.

Despite their advancements, current state-of-the-art one-to-many methods exhibit limitations. They typically rely on a single neural network architecture with a fixed capacity to handle all subproblems, which can overcomplicate the learning task and lead to suboptimal performance due to the diverse nature of subproblems. Increasing model capacity directly is a potential solution, but determining optimal allocation remains an open challenge. Furthermore, these methods often employ REINFORCE as their training algorithm, which relies solely on scalarized objective values as reward signals. REINFORCE is known for high gradient variance and insufficient structured mechanisms for effective exploration, issues that are exacerbated in MOCOP settings due to the vast combinatorial action space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance and efficiency of neural methods for solving Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization Problems (MOCOPs) by addressing two identified limitations in existing state-of-the-art approaches.

The first key limitation targeted is the **constrained exploration within the vast combinatorial solution space**. Traditional DRL algorithms, particularly those based on REINFORCE, rely on scalarized reward signals to guide policy updates. This on-policy nature can lead to high gradient variance, which impedes stable learning and effective exploration. In the context of MOCOPs, the immense size and complexity of the discrete action space make efficient exploration particularly challenging. Suboptimal policies can result from insufficient exploration, as the model may fail to discover high-quality solutions or adequately approximate the diverse Pareto front. The motivation here is to develop a mechanism that can provide more stable and informative feedback to guide the policy search, thereby promoting more effective exploration and convergence to superior solutions.

The second core limitation addressed is the **reliance on a single, fixed-capacity model to handle all diverse subproblems** within a MOCOP framework. Existing one-to-many neural methods often employ a singular neural network architecture that processes all scalarized subproblems, regardless of their specific characteristics (e.g., different weight vectors representing distinct trade-offs). This uniform treatment can overcomplicate the learning task for the model, as it must learn a generalized policy capable of performing well across a spectrum of subproblems, some of which may benefit from specialized computation. A single model with limited capacity may struggle to capture the nuances required for optimal performance on each distinct subproblem. While increasing overall model capacity is an option, it is often inefficient without a mechanism to adaptively allocate this capacity. The motivation is to enable the model to dynamically adapt its computational structure to the specific demands of individual subproblems, allowing for specialization and more effective representation learning without uniformly increasing computational burden. This adaptive capacity is intended to lead to more efficient learning and ultimately, higher-quality solutions.

In response to these motivations, the authors propose POCCO (Preference-driven Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization with Conditional COmputation) as a plug-and-play framework designed to augment existing neural MOCOP methods with mechanisms for adaptive computation and preference-driven optimization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

POCCO is presented as a learning-based framework designed to augment neural Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization Problem (MOCOP) methods. It addresses the identified limitations by integrating two primary mechanisms: a conditional computation block for adaptive model structures and a preference-driven optimization algorithm.

#### 3.1. Overview of POCCO

The framework aims to train a portfolio of policies to solve scalarized subproblems, which are derived from an MOCOP instance through decomposition. Instead of a single policy handling all subproblems uniformly, POCCO promotes specialization by encouraging each policy to focus on a subset of subproblems. This is achieved through the activation of different subsets of model parameters via a Conditional COmputation (CCO) block. Additionally, POCCO replaces raw scalarized rewards with preference signals to encourage thorough exploration of the combinatorial solution space. This involves generating winning-losing solution pairs for each subproblem and maximizing a Bradley–Terry (BT) likelihood based on their implicit rewards. POCCO is designed as a generic, plug-and-play framework, demonstrated by its integration into two state-of-the-art neural MOCOP methods: CNH and WE-CA, resulting in POCCO-C and POCCO-W, respectively.

#### 3.2. Conditional Computation Block (CCO Block)

The CCO block is introduced into the decoder of the backbone Transformer-based architecture used in many neural MOCOP solvers. This strategic placement allows for the generation of multiple diverse solutions through a single encoder pass, balancing empirical performance and computational cost.

- **Structure and Placement:** The CCO block is inserted between the multi-head attention (MHA) layer and the compatibility layer within the decoder. It consists of multiple Feed-Forward (FF) experts and a single parameter-free Identity (ID) expert.
- **Dynamic Routing:** For a batch of MHA outputs, a sparse gating network (router) dynamically routes each context vector from its corresponding subproblem. This routing directs the context vector through either a selected subset of FF experts or the ID expert. This creates distinct computation paths, which function as specialized policies for different subproblems.
- **Gating Mechanism:** The gating network, parameterized by $W_G$, computes scores for each expert. A Top-k operator selects the k highest-scoring experts (with $k=2$ used as default), activating only a small subset of experts. The outputs of these selected experts are then weighted by a softmax-normalized version of their scores. The ID expert allows the model to bypass FF computation, promoting architectural sparsity and specialization.
- **Mathematical Formulation:** The output of the CCO block for a context vector $h^b_c$ is:
 $CCO(h^b_c) = IN\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m+1} G(h^b_c)_j E_j(h^b_c) + h^b_c\right)$
 where $G(h^b_c)_j$ is the output of the gating network for expert $j$, $E_j(h^b_c)$ is the output of expert $j$, and IN is instance normalization.
- **Benefits:** This design scales model capacity efficiently by combining principles of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) (multiple FF experts) and Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) (ID expert for skipping computation). It enables dedicated, weight-specific policies for individual subproblems while maintaining computational efficiency due to sparse activation.

#### 3.3. Preference-driven MOCO (PL)

To mitigate exploration inefficiencies of REINFORCE, POCCO adopts a preference-driven optimization approach, utilizing relative preferences instead of absolute objective values.

- **Generating Preference Pairs:** For each scalarized subproblem $(G, \lambda_i)$, the policy $p_\theta$ samples two candidate solutions, $\pi_w$ and $\pi_l$. An evaluator compares their scalarized objective values to determine the "winner" ($\pi_w$, the better solution) and "loser" ($\pi_l$, the worse solution). A binary preference label ($y=1$ if $\pi_w \prec \pi_l$, $y=0$ otherwise) is assigned.
- **Defining an Implicit Reward:** An implicit reward $f_\theta$ is defined as the average log-likelihood of a solution, normalized by its sequence length $|\pi|$:
 $f_\theta(\pi|G, \lambda_i) = \frac{1}{|\pi|} \log p_\theta(\pi|G, \lambda_i) = \frac{1}{|\pi|} \sum_{t=1}^{|\pi|} \log p_\theta(\pi_t|\pi_{<t}, G, \lambda_i)$
 This normalization helps mitigate length bias between solution pairs.
- **Learning from Pairwise Comparisons:** Preference learning is framed as a probabilistic binary classification using the Bradley–Terry (BT) model. The BT model maps reward differences to preference probabilities:
 $g_\theta(\pi_w \prec \pi_l | G, \lambda_i) = \sigma\left(\beta [f_\theta(\pi_w | G, \lambda_i) - f_\theta(\pi_l | G, \lambda_i)]\right)$
 where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function and $\beta > 0$ is a fixed temperature parameter.
- **Loss Function:** The loss function maximizes the likelihood of collected preferences:
 $L(\theta|p_\theta, G, \lambda_i, \pi_w, \pi_l) = -y \log \sigma\left(\beta\left[\frac{\log p_\theta(\pi_w|G, \lambda_i)}{|\pi_w|} - \frac{\log p_\theta(\pi_l|G, \lambda_i)}{|\pi_l|}\right]\right)$
 By maximizing this log-likelihood, the model is encouraged to assign higher probabilities to preferred solutions.
- **Gradient Analysis:** Compared to REINFORCE, which relies on absolute returns, PL utilizes relative returns within a sigmoid, resulting in the difference of two normalized log-likelihoods. This pairwise, centered signal significantly reduces gradient variance, leading to smoother and more stable policy updates and, consequently, faster and more reliable convergence.

#### 3.4. Integration with SOTA Solvers

The framework integrates with existing neural MOCOP solvers by modifying their decoder structures. For POCCO-W (based on WE-CA), the CCO block is applied to the context vector produced by the MHA layer. For POCCO-C (based on CNH), it additionally accounts for problem-size embeddings in the context vector formation before passing it to the CCO block.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of POCCO was conducted across four classic MOCOP benchmarks: multi-objective traveling salesman problem (MOTSP), multi-objective capacitated vehicle routing problem (MOCVRP), multi-objective knapsack problem (MOKP), and tri-objective TSP (Tri-TSP), for various problem sizes (e.g., n=20/50/100 for MOTSP). Performance was measured using average hypervolume (HV), average gap, and total runtime.

#### 4.1. Comparison Analysis (Tables 1 & 2)

- **Overall Superiority:** POCCO-W consistently achieved superior performance (higher HV) compared to its base model WE-CA across all tested benchmark scenarios and problem sizes, establishing new performance benchmarks among neural MOCOP solvers. Similarly, POCCO-C consistently outperformed CNH.
- **Augmentation Benefit:** Both POCCO variants also surpassed their instance augmentation-based counterparts (WE-CA-Aug and CNH-Aug) on specific instances (Bi-TSP20, Bi-CVRP100), suggesting enhanced solution space exploration. When combined with instance augmentation, POCCO demonstrated further performance gains.
- **Outperforming Traditional & Multi-Model Methods:** POCCO achieved better results on Bi-TSP50 than WS-LKH, a challenging setting for previous neural solvers. It also delivered superior results compared to multi-model approaches (DRL-MOA, MDRL, EMNH) while maintaining a single shared model, addressing the high training overhead of the one-to-one paradigm.
- **Efficiency:** POCCO significantly reduced computational time compared to classical MOEAs and powerful exact solvers. For instance, POCCO-W-Aug solved Bi-TSP100 in approximately 14 minutes, whereas WS-LKH required about 6 hours for comparable solution quality.

#### 4.2. Out-of-Distribution Generalization (Figure 3 & Table 6)

- POCCO-W-Aug consistently demonstrated strong generalization performance on out-of-distribution benchmark instances (KroAB100/150/200) compared to other neural baselines. POCCO-C-Aug also outperformed CNH-Aug across these scenarios.
- Experiments on larger-scale instances (Bi-TSP150/200/300/500/1000 and MOKP300/500/1000) further supported POCCO-W's ability to maintain strong performance against baselines in larger and out-of-distribution settings (Tables 7, 8, 9).

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Preference Learning (PL) (Figure 4a)

- Validation curves showed that PL achieved faster convergence and better performance with fewer training epochs compared to REINFORCE when applied to both WE-CA and POCCO-W models on Bi-TSP100.
- Theoretical analysis and empirical measurements (Table 3) confirmed that PL consistently exhibited significantly lower gradient variance (two to four orders of magnitude lower) than REINFORCE, contributing to faster and more stable optimization dynamics.
- A comparative experiment with DPO (Table 10) showed that while DPO was slightly better on the smallest instance, POCCO-W consistently outperformed DPO on larger instances (MOTSP50–200) with higher training efficiency due to DPO's requirement for a separate reference model.

#### 4.4. Effectiveness of the Conditional Computation (CCO) Block (Figures 4b, 4c & Tables 11, 12)

- Ablation studies comparing POCCO-W with variants (POCCO-E, POCCO-D, POCCO-ME, POCCO-MD, POCCO-MED, POCCO-Emp) demonstrated that all CCO variants outperformed the base model (WE-CA) on in-distribution data (Bi-TSP100).
- On out-of-distribution data (Bi-TSP200), only POCCO-W (with CCO in the decoder between MHA and compatibility layer) and POCCO-Emp (CCO with an empty expert) maintained strong performance, highlighting the importance of both the structure and placement of the CCO block for generalization. POCCO-ME (standard MoE) performed poorly on OOD data.
- **Hyperparameter Study:**
 - **Number of CCO Layers:** Two CCO layers yielded the highest HV but significantly increased inference time (e.g., 60% increase for Bi-TSP100). A single CCO layer was adopted as the default for a balance between performance and computational cost.
 - **Number of Experts:** POCCO-W with 5 experts (4 FF + 1 ID) generally achieved better overall performance than configurations with fewer (3E) or more experts (9E, 17E), confirming the efficiency of the 5-expert setup for balancing cost and quality.
 - **Top k:** Selecting k=2 experts per token converged fastest and attained the highest final HV. k=1 limited capacity, while k=3 increased computation without benefit.
 - **Beta ($\beta$) Parameter:** Moderate $\beta$ values consistently yielded best results (e.g., $\beta=3.5$ for bi-objective tasks, $\beta=4.5$ for tri-objective tasks).

#### 4.5. Impact of Neutralizing Non-Dominated Pairs (Table 5)

- An experiment where non-dominated pairs were treated as equivalent (preference signal set to 0) resulted in a significant degradation of model performance across all Bi-TSP instances. This indicates that using scalarized values for preference comparison, even if not reflecting global Pareto dominance in every instance, provides a necessary and consistent training signal within the decomposition-based learning paradigm.

#### 4.6. GPU Memory Usage (Table 15)

- POCCO-W incurred higher GPU memory usage compared to its backbone (WE-CA) and CNH, approximately doubling it for some problem sizes. This increased resource consumption is attributed to the expanded model capacity provided by the CCO block.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed POCCO framework addresses identified limitations in current neural methods for Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization Problems (MOCOPs) and demonstrates improvements in performance, efficiency, and generalization.

#### 5.1. Significance of Contributions

- **Enhanced Exploration and Solution Quality:** By implementing preference-driven optimization (PL), POCCO moves beyond the limitations of traditional REINFORCE algorithms that suffer from high gradient variance. The use of pairwise preference signals, based on a Bradley-Terry model, provides a more stable and informative learning signal. This approach guides the policy search more effectively towards promising regions of the solution space, leading to faster convergence and the approximation of higher-quality Pareto fronts.
- **Adaptive Model Capacity and Specialization:** The conditional computation (CCO) block enables the neural network to dynamically adapt its architecture to the specific characteristics of individual subproblems. By routing subproblems to specialized subsets of experts (Feed-Forward or Identity), POCCO efficiently scales model capacity. This mechanism promotes policy diversity and specialization, allowing the model to learn tailored solutions for different objective trade-offs without overcomplicating a single, monolithic network. This adaptive capacity is a significant improvement over fixed-architecture models that struggle to handle the inherent diversity of MOCOP subproblems.
- **Plug-and-Play Versatility:** The design of POCCO as a plug-and-play framework enhances its practical applicability. Its successful integration into two state-of-the-art neural MOCOP solvers (CNH and WE-CA) demonstrates its adaptability and potential to augment existing and future neural optimization methods.
- **Empirical Performance:** The experimental results across various MOCOPs (MOTSP, MOCVRP, MOKP, Tri-TSP) consistently show POCCO achieving superior performance compared to baseline neural methods, including their augmentation-based variants, and competitive or superior results against traditional exact and heuristic solvers. This establishes POCCO as a new state-of-the-art method in the domain of neural MOCOP solving.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** The framework exhibits strong generalization to out-of-distribution problem sizes and benchmark instances, indicating its robustness and potential for broader applicability in real-world scenarios where problem characteristics may vary.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Despite increasing model capacity, the sparse activation within the CCO block minimizes additional computational overhead per subproblem compared to a dense network. The preference-driven training also accelerates convergence, leading to reduced overall training time compared to REINFORCE-based methods.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Accelerated Decision-Making:** POCCO's improved performance and efficiency can accelerate the multi-objective decision-making process in critical real-world applications such as logistics (e.g., vehicle routing, supply chain optimization), manufacturing (e.g., job shop scheduling, resource allocation), and infrastructure planning. This can lead to more optimal and resource-efficient solutions for complex operational challenges.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By providing a framework that automates the discovery of effective policies and reduces the need for extensive manual tuning and domain-specific expertise characteristic of traditional heuristics, POCCO can lower the development costs and time associated with deploying optimization solutions.
- **Broader Applicability of AI:** The ability of POCCO to handle diverse subproblems and generalize effectively can broaden the applicability of neural solvers to larger and more complex real-world MOCOPs, which previously might have been intractable or required bespoke solutions.
- **Energy Efficiency:** The conditional computation block's design, which activates only the necessary network capacity for each subproblem, can lead to reduced computational operations (FLOPs) and potentially lower energy consumption compared to dense models with equivalent overall capacity. This aligns with broader efforts towards more sustainable AI.
- **Advancement in AI and Operations Research:** The methodological contributions—specifically the adaptive model structures and preference learning approach—advance the state-of-the-art in both deep reinforcement learning for optimization and the broader field of combinatorial optimization.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors acknowledge certain limitations, such as the current work's limited capability to address real-world MOCOPs with complex constraints or extremely large problem sizes. Future work may need to explore constraint-handling mechanisms or divide-and-conquer strategies to further scale the approach. Addressing these aspects could further unlock POCCO's potential for more pervasive real-world applications.
