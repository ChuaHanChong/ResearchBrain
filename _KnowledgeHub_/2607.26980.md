# Dense Soft Weighting for Radar Ego-Velocity Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Imperial College London developed Dense Soft Weighting (DSW), an analytic radar front-end that processes dense range-Doppler-angle spectra, continuously weighting weak Doppler evidence to estimate ego-velocity and its covariance. This method improved trajectory accuracy by 45% mean translational Absolute Pose Error on the ColoRadar dataset and demonstrated superior transferability across different radar configurations compared to sparse point-cloud methods.

## Problem

- Conventional radar pipelines discard valuable weak Doppler evidence by converting dense spectra into sparse point clouds, hindering accurate velocity estimation, especially for lateral motion.
- Existing ego-velocity estimation methods often lack reliable, measurement-derived velocity covariance, complicating robust integration into sensor fusion frameworks.
- Learning-based radar approaches suffer from data dependency, computational demands, and poor transferability across different radar hardware, limiting their utility on embedded platforms.

## Method

- Developed **Dense Soft Weighting (DSW)**, an analytic front-end that processes raw range-Doppler-angle (RDA) spectra by assigning continuous confidence weights to each cell based on normalized power and concentration.
- Implemented robust ==weighted least squares (WLS)== with a ==Cauchy loss== function to estimate ego-velocity from the densely weighted radar cells, including sub-bin angle refinement for accurate line-of-sight vectors.
- Provided a closed-form, three-component velocity covariance (WLS fit, angular uncertainty, Doppler quantization) directly derived from the weighted measurements, without requiring per-platform tuning.

## Results

- DSW reduced the mean translational Absolute Pose Error (APE) on the ColoRadar dataset by **45%** (from **2.12 m** to **1.17 m**) when fused with an Error-State Kalman Filter compared to the strongest point-cloud baseline.
- Achieved consistent per-frame ego-velocity RMSE of **0.08–0.12 m/s** on a self-collected dataset using different radar hardware without hyperparameter retuning, validating its cross-configuration transferability.
- Demonstrated real-time performance on embedded platforms, with latencies ranging from **1.8 ms** (GPU) to **17.8 ms** (CPU) for single-frame processing, meeting typical autonomous system requirements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuously weighting and aggregating all available Doppler evidence from dense radar spectra, rather than relying on sparse binary detections, significantly enhances ego-velocity accuracy, particularly for lateral motion.
> - An analytic, training-free radar front-end can achieve competitive or superior accuracy compared to data-dependent learning-based methods, while offering improved transferability across diverse radar configurations.
> - Deriving a principled, closed-form velocity covariance directly from weighted radar measurements provides a reliable uncertainty estimate crucial for robust integration into sensor fusion frameworks like ESKFs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Ego-velocity estimation is a fundamental component of state estimation for autonomous systems, particularly in environments where traditional visual or LiDAR-based sensors may be compromised. Millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar is identified as a suitable alternative for such conditions due to its robustness against poor illumination, textureless scenes, and airborne particulates, and its ability to provide direct Doppler velocity measurements.

The existing landscape for radar ego-velocity estimation primarily consists of two main approaches:
1. **Sparse Point-Cloud Methods:** Conventional pipelines typically employ a Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) detector to convert dense radar range-Doppler-angle spectra into sparse point clouds. Subsequent ego-velocity estimation often relies on robust least-squares methods, such as RANSAC. While efficient, this approach discards sub-threshold radar returns, which may contain useful Doppler motion cues, especially for off-boresight or weak reflectors. Methods like those by Kellner et al. and Doer et al., along with advancements like CREVE, VGC-RIO, MRIO, and RIV-SLAM, focus on improving the estimator or fusion around these detected points, but remain dependent on the initial sparse detection step.
2. **Learning-Based Dense Methods:** A more recent direction involves using machine learning models to directly process dense radar spectra. Approaches such as milliEgo, BatMobility, Radarize, 4DEgo, S3E, and UNRIO learn to predict motion, weights, or uncertainty. These methods can achieve high accuracy when trained on data specifically matched to the radar hardware and environment. However, they often face challenges related to transferability across different radar configurations, antenna layouts, and environments, as well as the computational demands of network inference on embedded platforms.
3. **Analytic Dense Methods for Spinning Radar:** Some analytic methods, like DRO, operate directly on dense radar intensity scans for scan-to-map registration. However, these are typically designed for mechanically scanning automotive radars and do not directly translate to compact single-chip mmWave phased arrays, which produce range-Doppler-angle spectra.

This work addresses a gap in the literature by proposing an analytic, dense radar front-end specifically for single-chip mmWave radar. It aims to leverage the full information content of the dense radar spectrum, avoiding the information loss inherent in sparse detection methods, while sidestepping the data-dependency and computational overhead of learning-based approaches. The research positions itself as providing a lightweight, transferable, and robust solution for ego-velocity estimation that can seamlessly integrate with standard inertial back-ends, maintaining real-time performance on embedded hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current ego-velocity estimation techniques in challenging environments and for embedded deployment using single-chip mmWave radar. The authors identify three practical challenges that their proposed method aims to address:

1. **Retention of Weak Doppler Evidence:** Conventional radar pipelines, which rely on CFAR thresholding, convert dense radar spectra into sparse point clouds. This process prematurely discards sub-threshold radar returns, including weak off-boresight Doppler evidence. Such weak signals, even if below a hard detection threshold, can contribute valuable geometric constraints that improve velocity estimation, particularly for lateral motion, and can reduce drift when fused with inertial measurements. The objective is to devise a front-end that can leverage these cues without discarding them.

2. **Provision of Usable Velocity Covariance:** For robust integration into established sensor fusion frameworks, such as Extended Kalman Filters (EKFs) or Error-State Kalman Filters (ESKFs), an ego-velocity measurement must be accompanied by a reliable and well-calibrated covariance matrix. This covariance quantifies the uncertainty of the velocity estimate and is crucial for the optimal weighting of measurements within the filter. Existing approaches often require complex calibration procedures or private pose regressors, limiting their general applicability. The objective is to provide a closed-form, measurement-derived velocity covariance directly from the radar data.

3. **Embedded Platform Feasibility and Transferability:** Deploying radar-inertial odometry on resource-constrained embedded platforms necessitates lightweight algorithms. Learning-based approaches, while accurate, often require extensive training data matched to a specific radar chip, antenna layout, and environment. This limits their transferability and increases development overhead for new platforms. The objective is to develop an analytic method that requires no platform-specific training data or learning-based uncertainty models, thereby supporting direct transfer across various single-chip radar configurations and operating efficiently on embedded hardware.

In summary, the core research problem is to develop a method that can assign per-cell confidence, compute a robust ego-velocity, and derive an associated covariance directly from dense range-Doppler-angle spectra, without reducing the spectrum to sparse detections, and while ensuring applicability across different radar configurations and embedded system compatibility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Dense Soft Weighting (DSW), is an analytic radar front-end designed to estimate ego-velocity and its covariance directly from dense range-Doppler-angle (RDA) spectra produced by single-chip mmWave radar. It integrates with a standard loose-coupled Error-State Kalman Filter (ESKF) for radar-inertial odometry (RIO).

#### 3.1. System Overview

The system begins with standard FMCW radar signal processing: raw ADC samples are decoded into IQ data, reshaped into MIMO channels, and processed with Range FFT and Doppler FFTs, combined with TDM demultiplexing. This yields a complex data cube, which is then beamformed onto a chip-aware azimuth-elevation grid, resulting in a dense power tensor P(d, b, r), where d, b, and r index Doppler bins, beam angles (azimuth-elevation), and range, respectively.

#### 3.2. Dense Soft Weighting (DSW) Front-End

Instead of applying CFAR to generate a sparse point cloud (the conventional path), DSW follows a dense branch:

1. **Range-Doppler Cell Confidence Weight (Section 3.4):**
 Each range-Doppler cell *i* = (d_i, r_i) provides a beam-domain power spectrum S_i(b). DSW calculates two key metrics:
 - **Peak Power (P_i):** The maximum power within the beam-domain spectrum for cell *i*.
 - **Cross-Beam Median (C_i):** The median power across all beams for cell *i*, serving as a local noise floor estimate.
 These metrics are combined to form a continuous confidence weight *w_i*:
 - **Normalized Power Score (u_i):** `sqrt(P_i / max_j P_j)` where `max_j P_j` is the maximum peak power across all cells in the frame. This factor makes the weighting scale-invariant and limits the influence of a single strong reflector.
 - **Concentration Gate (v_i):** A sigmoid function of the log peak-to-median ratio: `sigma(log(P_i/C_i) - log(tau) / kappa)`. This function smoothly maps the ratio to a value between 0 and 1, where `tau` is the ratio at which `v_i` is 0.5 (set to 200) and `kappa` controls the slope (set to 0.5).
 The final weight `w_i` is the product `u_i * v_i`. This approach allows cells with lower power or less concentrated energy (which would be discarded by a hard threshold) to still contribute to the estimation with a reduced confidence.

2. **Per-Cell Line of Sight and Robust Velocity Estimation (Section 3.5):**
 - **Angle Refinement:** For each weighted cell, the dominant beam direction `(hat_b_alpha, hat_b_beta)` is found by identifying the maximum in its beam-domain power spectrum. This coarse angle is then refined to sub-bin resolution using standard three-point parabolic interpolation along the azimuth and elevation axes. This refinement is critical for accurately resolving velocity components, especially along axes with coarse angular resolution. The refined angles are converted into a unit line-of-sight vector `hat_u_i` in the radar sensor frame.
 - **Measurement Model:** Each cell provides a Doppler radial velocity `d_i` and its LOS vector `hat_u_i`. The measurement model is `d_i = -hat_u_i^T v_R + epsilon_i`, where `v_R` is the 3D sensor-frame ego-velocity and `epsilon_i` is noise.
 - **Robust Weighted Least Squares (WLS):** The stacked system of `N` cells, `d = -U v_R + epsilon`, is solved using WLS. An initial velocity estimate `hat_v_R^(0)` is obtained using the weights `W = diag(w_i)`. To handle outliers from moving reflectors or multipath, a single Iteratively-Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) pass is performed with a Cauchy loss function. Residuals `r_i` are calculated, a robust scale `hat_sigma_r` is estimated from their median absolute deviation, and Cauchy weights `rho_i` are computed. The WLS is then re-evaluated with `W * rho` as the weighting matrix. A fixed cutoff `c=2` is used.

3. **Velocity Covariance Assembly (Section 3.6):**
 The system outputs a 3x3 sensor-frame covariance matrix `Sigma_R_v` by combining three terms in closed form, without per-platform tuning:
 - **WLS Covariance (Sigma_v_wls):** `hat_sigma_r^2 * (U^T W rho U)^-1`, derived from the robust WLS fit.
 - **Angular Uncertainty (Sigma_v_ang):** `||hat_v_R||^2 * J * diag(sigma_alpha^2, sigma_beta^2) * J^T`, accounts for per-axis pointing deviations mapped through the Jacobian `J` of the line-of-sight transformation.
 - **Doppler Bin Quantization (Sigma_v_dop):** `sigma_dop^2 * I`, representing a floor set by the radar's finite Doppler resolution.
 Finally, the estimated sensor-frame velocity and covariance are transformed to the body frame by removing the lever-arm effect (`omega x r` using gyroscope data) and applying the calibrated sensor-to-body rotation. The front-end delivers `(hat_v_body, Sigma_body)` to the downstream filter.

#### 3.3. IMU Fusion Back-End (Section 3.7)

A loose-coupled ESKF is used to fuse the radar velocity measurements with inertial propagation. The ESKF state includes position, velocity, attitude (quaternion), accelerometer and gyroscope biases, and an online-estimated radar-to-IMU temporal offset. All analytic methods evaluated (DSW and point-cloud baselines) share an identical ESKF configuration, including process noise parameters and a Mahalanobis `chi-squared` innovation gate, ensuring that performance differences are attributable solely to the radar front-end.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocol (Section 4)

The evaluation covers three datasets:
- **ColoRadar:** Handheld, AWR1843BOOST, indoor/outdoor, lidar-SLAM ground truth.
- **Radarize:** Handheld, cart, robot, AWR1843BOOST, indoor, VIO pseudo-ground truth (used for comparison with learning-based baselines).
- **Self-collected dataset:** Handheld, AWR6843AOP-EVM (different chip/aperture), 3D motion, motion-capture ground truth (used for cross-configuration transfer test).

Baselines include three point-cloud variants (ptcloud_onboard, ptcloud_dsp_capon, ptcloud_dsp_fft), all feeding the shared ESKF. Learning-based baselines (Radarize, milliEgo) are compared at the pose level. Metrics used are per-frame body-velocity RMSE (Δv), Absolute Pose Error (APE_t/r), and Relative Pose Error (RPE_t/r). Hyperparameters for DSW and all analytic baselines are fixed across all sequences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation focused on comparing DSW against sparse point-cloud methods and learning-based approaches, examining per-frame velocity accuracy, fused trajectory accuracy, cross-configuration transferability, and real-time embedded performance.

#### 4.1. Per-Frame Ego-Velocity Accuracy

DSW consistently achieved the lowest per-frame ego-velocity Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) on both the ColoRadar and self-collected datasets when compared to point-cloud baselines. For instance, on a representative ColoRadar sequence, DSW's velocity estimate more closely matched the ground truth than either the Capon or on-chip point-cloud baselines. This improvement is attributed to DSW's ability to utilize weak, off-boresight Doppler evidence with continuous weights, which is crucial for accurately constraining all velocity components, especially lateral motion, that sparse detectors might discard.

#### 4.2. Front-End Component Ablation

An ablation study on key components of DSW confirmed the necessity of both the power score (u_i) and the concentration gate (v_i) for the full weighting scheme (Eq. 4). Using only one of these factors resulted in a significant increase in mean translational APE (2.47 m for power score only, 2.97 m for concentration gate only, compared to 0.76 m for full DSW). Critically, replacing the soft gate with a hard threshold, similar to CFAR, caused the trajectory to diverge due to an insufficient number of retained cells. This result supports the design choice of continuous soft weighting over binary detection. The study also indicated that the single Cauchy pass for robustification, while not always yielding large gains on low-outlier sequences, contributes to overall robustness, and the three-component covariance model provides a modest APE reduction while preventing overconfidence in geometrically weak directions.

#### 4.3. Cross-Configuration Transfer

DSW demonstrated effective transferability across different radar hardware and motion regimes. When deployed on the self-collected dataset, which used a different radar chip (TI AWR6843AOP-EVM) with a more balanced azimuth-elevation field of view (60°/60° vs. 60°/15° for public datasets) and featured substantial 3D handheld motion, DSW achieved the lowest per-frame ego-velocity RMSE (0.08–0.12 m/s) on all sequences. This was accomplished without any retuning of the front-end hyperparameters, validating its analytic and configuration-agnostic design.

#### 4.4. Trajectory Accuracy from Ego-Velocity

The benefits of DSW's improved per-frame velocity estimation translated into enhanced global trajectory accuracy when fused with the shared ESKF back-end:
- **ColoRadar:** DSW achieved the lowest translational Absolute Pose Error (APE_t) and Relative Pose Error (RPE_t) on all three evaluated sequences. Mean APE_t was reduced by 45% (from 2.12 m for the strongest point-cloud baseline to 1.17 m). Mean RPE_t was also reduced to 0.92 m, compared to 1.38 m and 1.43 m for point-cloud baselines.
- **Radarize:** DSW performed comparably to, and in some cases exceeded, the accuracy of learning-based odometry methods. Mean translational APE for DSW was 1.11 m, compared to 0.81 m for the Radarize network and 4.22 m for milliEgo. On a median sequence, DSW’s APE was 0.77 m versus 0.71 m for Radarize. DSW also achieved lower per-frame forward-velocity RMSE on 69% of Radarize sequences.
- **Self-collected dataset:** DSW again achieved the lowest translational APE on every sequence, reducing mean APE_t from 0.39 m for point-cloud baselines to 0.27 m. RPE_t was competitive on short windows.

#### 4.5. Comparison Against Learning-Based Odometry

DSW, being an analytic and training-free method, showed accuracy levels on par with, and often superior to, learning-based radar odometry baselines (milliEgo, Radarize) on their native evaluation dataset (Radarize). This demonstrates that an analytically derived, dense front-end can achieve competitive performance without the data-dependency, training overhead, or platform-specific tuning typically associated with learned models.

#### 4.6. Runtime and Embedded Feasibility

The computational cost of DSW's front-end is linear with the number of range-Doppler cells. On a laptop CPU, single-core latency ranged from 3.8 ms (for 3072 cells) to 17.8 ms (for 16384 cells). With GPU acceleration (CUDA), the latency became largely independent of grid size, operating in 1.8 ms to 2.1 ms. Crucially, all analytic front-ends, including DSW, met the real-time requirements (10 Hz for ColoRadar/self-collected, 30 Hz for Radarize) on a single CPU core on both the development laptop and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX embedded target. Learning-based baselines also met the 30 Hz budget on the Orin NX's integrated GPU, but had higher CPU costs. This demonstrates DSW's practicality for embedded deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Dense Soft Weighting" (DSW) approach represents a significant advancement in radar ego-velocity estimation, with potential impacts across several domains:

1. **Enhanced Robustness in Challenging Environments:** DSW directly addresses the unreliability of visual and LiDAR sensors in visually degraded environments (e.g., fog, dust, darkness) by maximizing the utility of mmWave radar data. By retaining and continuously weighting weak Doppler evidence, DSW provides more comprehensive and accurate velocity estimates, thereby improving the robustness of state estimation for autonomous systems operating in critical conditions.

2. **Increased Transferability and Ease of Deployment:** Unlike learning-based methods that require extensive, platform-specific training and model tuning, DSW is an analytic front-end with fixed hyperparameters. Its demonstrated ability to transfer across different single-chip radar configurations (e.g., AWR1843BOOST vs. AWR6843AOP-EVM) and antenna layouts without recalibration significantly simplifies the development and deployment of radar-inertial odometry systems. This lowers the barrier for integrating radar sensing into diverse robotic platforms and applications.

3. **Efficiency for Embedded Systems:** The method's real-time performance on embedded hardware (such as the Jetson Orin NX) underscores its practical utility. This makes DSW suitable for resource-constrained applications where computational efficiency and low power consumption are paramount, expanding the applicability of high-performance radar ego-velocity estimation to a broader range of robots and autonomous vehicles.

4. **Improved Sensor Fusion:** DSW provides a robust ego-velocity estimate coupled with a closed-form, measurement-derived covariance. This output is directly compatible with standard inertial back-ends like ESKFs, enabling more principled and accurate sensor fusion. This eliminates the need for complex, often ad-hoc uncertainty models or private pose regressors, contributing to the overall reliability of radar-inertial odometry.

5. **New Paradigm for Radar Data Processing:** By moving away from binary detection thresholds, DSW proposes a paradigm shift in processing dense radar spectra. It demonstrates that continuous confidence metrics assigned to every range-Doppler cell can extract more information than traditional sparse point-cloud generation, leading to substantial accuracy improvements, particularly for lateral motion where off-boresight data is critical. This could inspire further research into dense, analytic processing for other radar sensing tasks.

In conclusion, DSW offers a practical, high-performance, and transferable solution for ego-velocity estimation using single-chip mmWave radar. Its contributions are likely to accelerate the adoption of radar-inertial odometry in applications requiring robust and autonomous navigation in complex and challenging environments.

#### 5.1. Discussion and Limitations

The results indicate that aggregating weighted evidence across the dense radar spectrum, rather than relying on sparse binary detections, significantly improves ego-velocity and trajectory accuracy. This is particularly beneficial for lateral motion and in low-reflectivity scenes where weak, off-boresight returns are important. The analytic, deterministic nature of DSW's outlier handling (single Cauchy reweighting) is shown to be effective for dense data, avoiding the stochastic cost and complexities of RANSAC. The closed-form covariance directly derived from the weighted normal equations is also a distinct advantage.

Limitations noted include potential biases in angle-FFT direction estimates due to sidelobe leakage, particularly underestimating lateral motion, which weighted aggregation alone cannot correct. The covariance model currently assumes white residuals, potentially underestimating uncertainty in multipath-rich environments. Furthermore, evaluation was limited to handheld and cart speeds, with higher-speed or aerial platforms remaining for future validation.
