# CARLA: An Open Urban Driving Simulator

> [!summary] Summary
> CARLA is an open-source urban driving simulator built as a layer over Unreal Engine 4, shipping free digital assets (two towns, buildings, 16 vehicles, 50 pedestrians), configurable sensor suites (RGB, ground-truth depth, semantic segmentation), 18 weather-illumination presets, and infraction telemetry. Using it, three driving approaches - modular pipeline, conditional imitation learning, and A3C reinforcement learning - are benchmarked on goal-directed navigation of increasing difficulty, exposing large generalization gaps.

## Problem

- Physical-world training and validation of urban driving needs prohibitive funds, manpower, and fleets, putting corner-case data collection beyond most research groups' reach.
- Open racing simulators lack pedestrians, intersections, cross traffic, and traffic rules, so they omit the complications that define urban rather than track driving.
- Commercial games rendering cities at high fidelity are closed, offering little environment control, scripting, sensor specification, or per-infraction feedback needed for policy benchmarking.

## Method

- ==Server-client architecture== over **Unreal Engine 4**: a Python client sends steering/throttle/brake commands plus meta-commands resetting the world, weather, traffic density, and camera rig; server returns sensors and measurements.
- Purpose-built free asset library and two hand-laid towns (2.9 km training, 1.4 km testing) with randomized pedestrian outfits, vehicle paint, nine weather types, and two illumination regimes.
- A ==benchmarking protocol== with four graded navigation tasks, held-out town and weather sets, plus five logged infraction types, used to compare a **modular pipeline**, ==conditional imitation learning==, and **A3C**.

## Results

- No method is perfect even on straight empty roads; on navigation with dynamic obstacles, the best methods reach **77-83%** in training conditions and only **38-44%** in the new town.
- Weather generalization is nearly free while town transfer halves performance: modular pipeline navigation drops from **80%** to **24%**, imitation learning from **86%** to **40%**.
- A3C underperforms badly (**14%** navigation versus **86%** for imitation) despite **10 million** steps, roughly 12 days of driving against imitation learning's **14 hours** of demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modular pipelines fail bimodally: when the perception stack breaks under new textures and weather, the rule-based planner and controller collapse entirely.
> - Training across many weathers buys weather robustness but not new-environment robustness; visual variety must span the axis you intend to generalize along.
> - Reward-driven agents optimize what is penalized: the RL agent avoided pedestrians best yet still hit cars and static objects frequently, revealing rare-event brittleness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous urban driving systems represents one of the grand challenges in machine learning and robotics, demanding sophisticated sensorimotor control in complex, dynamic, and unpredictable 3D environments. This task is inherently difficult due to multi-agent interactions, strict traffic rules, the need to recognize and respond to a vast array of objects and events, and the "long tail" of rare but critical scenarios.

Historically, research in this field has been severely hampered by several factors:
- **High Infrastructure Costs:** Instrumenting and operating even a single robotic car is prohibitively expensive for most research groups, limiting data collection and experimentation.
- **Logistical Difficulties and Safety Concerns:** Testing in the physical world is complex, time-consuming, and often too dangerous for critical scenarios (e.g., simulating a child running into the road).
- **Data Scarcity:** Real-world data collection, while valuable, often fails to capture the sheer multitude of corner cases required for robust training and validation, especially for data-hungry deep learning models.

Existing simulation solutions prior to CARLA had significant limitations:
- **Early Simulators:** While simulation has been used since the early days of autonomous driving, many were ad-hoc or lacked the fidelity required for modern machine learning.
- **Racing Simulators (e.g., TORCS):** These are open-source but fail to capture the complexity of urban driving, lacking pedestrians, intersections, diverse traffic, and intricate traffic rules.
- **Commercial Games (e.g., Grand Theft Auto V):** While offering high visual fidelity, their closed-source nature restricts customization, scenario scripting, sensor suite specification, and detailed feedback on traffic rule violations, making them unsuitable for rigorous research benchmarking.

CARLA directly addresses these critical gaps. By providing an **open-source, high-fidelity urban driving simulator with free, purpose-built digital assets**, it fills a crucial void in the research ecosystem. It allows researchers to:
- **Democratize Access:** Lower the barrier to entry for autonomous driving research, enabling more academic and smaller industry groups to participate.
- **Generate Synthetic Data at Scale:** Produce vast amounts of labeled data for training perception and control systems, including scenarios that are too dangerous or rare in the real world. This is particularly vital for deep learning approaches, which require massive datasets.
- **Enable Controlled Experimentation:** Test and validate different autonomous driving algorithms (modular vs. end-to-end, imitation learning vs. reinforcement learning) under precisely controlled and repeatable environmental conditions and scenarios.
- **Facilitate System Verification:** Safely stage and evaluate system performance in critical "corner cases" to ensure robustness and safety before real-world deployment.
- **Provide a Standardized Benchmark:** Offer a common platform for comparing different approaches, fostering competition and accelerating progress in the field.

In essence, CARLA positions itself as a foundational infrastructure project within the autonomous driving research landscape. It aims to become the "ImageNet" or "MuJoCo" of autonomous driving, providing the necessary tools and environment for researchers to push the boundaries of perception, planning, and control algorithms without the prohibitive costs and risks of real-world testing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this paper is to **introduce CARLA (Car Learning to Act), an open-source simulator specifically designed for autonomous urban driving research.**

The core motivations behind CARLA's development stem directly from the limitations and challenges prevalent in autonomous driving research at the time:

- **To Support Comprehensive System Development:** The simulator is developed "from the ground up to support development, training, and validation of autonomous urban driving systems." This holistic approach differentiates it from more specialized simulation tools.
- **To Overcome Real-World Research Hurdles:** The prohibitive infrastructure costs and logistical complexities associated with training and testing autonomous systems in the physical world are a major barrier. CARLA seeks to circumvent these by providing a virtual environment.
- **To Democratize Autonomous Driving Research:** By making the simulator and its assets open-source and free, the authors aim to make autonomous driving research accessible to a broader community, including academic institutions and smaller research groups that lack the resources for physical vehicle fleets.
- **To Enable System Verification for Dangerous Scenarios:** Some critical scenarios are too dangerous to stage in the real world (e.g., a child running onto the road). CARLA allows for the safe and controlled simulation of such events for comprehensive system verification.
- **To Address Shortcomings of Existing Simulators:** The paper explicitly criticizes existing open-source racing simulators for lacking urban complexity and commercial games for their closed nature and limited research utility. CARLA is designed to fill this gap by offering:
 - **Open Digital Assets:** Unique in providing freely usable urban layouts, buildings, vehicles, and pedestrians, created specifically for research purposes.
 - **Flexible Sensor Suite Specification:** Allowing researchers to configure various types and placements of sensors (RGB, depth, semantic segmentation) to simulate different vehicle setups.
 - **Configurable Environmental Conditions:** Supporting a wide range of weather and time-of-day settings to test system robustness under diverse conditions.
 - **Detailed Feedback and Metrics:** Providing fine-grained data on vehicle state, collisions, and traffic rule infractions, crucial for evaluating and debugging driving policies.
- **To Serve as a Benchmark Platform:** A secondary, but crucial, objective demonstrated in the paper is to use CARLA to rigorously evaluate and compare different autonomous driving approaches. By testing a classic modular pipeline, an end-to-end imitation learning model, and an end-to-end reinforcement learning model, the authors showcase CARLA's utility as a standardized benchmarking tool for controlled scenarios of increasing difficulty.

In essence, CARLA's motivation is to be a foundational infrastructure project that democratizes, accelerates, and standardizes autonomous driving research by providing a rich, flexible, and open virtual environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of the paper involves the design and implementation of the CARLA simulator itself, followed by its application to benchmark three distinct autonomous driving paradigms.

#### 3.1. CARLA Simulator Design

- **Engine Foundation:** CARLA is built as an open-source layer on top of **Unreal Engine 4 (UE4)**. This choice leverages UE4’s state-of-the-art rendering quality, realistic physics simulation (PhysX), basic Non-Player Character (NPC) logic, and an extensive ecosystem of plugins.
- **Client-Server Architecture:** The simulator operates as a server-client system. The server runs the simulation and renders the scene, while the client (with a Python API) interacts with the server, sending control commands (steering, throttle, brake) and meta-commands (resetting simulation, changing environment properties like weather/NPC density, modifying sensor suites) and receiving sensor readings and environment measurements.
- **Environment and Assets:**
 - **3D Models:** A dedicated team of digital artists created proprietary 3D models for static objects (buildings, vegetation, traffic signs) and dynamic objects (vehicles, pedestrians). These models balance visual quality with rendering speed, using low-weight geometry and textures with carefully crafted materials and level-of-detail optimization.
 - **Urban Environments:** Two distinct towns were manually designed using these assets: Town 1 (2.9 km drivable roads) for training, and Town 2 (1.4 km drivable roads) for testing, ensuring separation for generalization studies.
 - **NPC Behavior:** Non-player vehicles use UE4's PhysXVehicles with adjusted kinematic parameters and a basic controller for lane following, respecting traffic lights, speed limits, and intersection decision-making. Pedestrians navigate via a cost map encouraging sidewalk use and marked crossings, with collision avoidance logic. NPC appearance (outfits, accessories, vehicle paint) is randomized for visual diversity.
 - **Environmental Conditions:** The simulator supports 18 illumination-weather combinations, including midday/sunset lighting and nine weather conditions (cloudiness, precipitation, puddles).
- **Sensors and Measurements:**
 - **Sensor Suite Flexibility:** Allows configuration of multiple RGB cameras at various positions, orientations, and parameters (FOV, depth of field).
 - **Pseudo-Sensors:** Provides ground-truth depth maps and semantic segmentation (12 classes: road, lane-marking, traffic sign, sidewalk, vehicle, pedestrian, etc.), valuable for perception research.
 - **Vehicle State and Compliance Metrics:** Outputs crucial data like GPS coordinates, orientation, speed, acceleration, accumulated collision impact, percentage of overlap with wrong-way lanes or sidewalks, traffic light states, speed limits, and exact locations/bounding boxes of all dynamic objects. These are vital for training rewards and detailed performance analysis.

#### 3.2. Autonomous Driving Approaches for Benchmarking

The authors use CARLA to evaluate three representative autonomous driving paradigms:
1. **Modular Pipeline (MP):** This classic approach decomposes the driving task into sequential modules:
 - **Perception:** Uses a semantic segmentation network (RefineNet) to estimate lanes, road limits, and dynamic objects, trained on 2,500 CARLA-generated images. An AlexNet-based classifier determines proximity to intersections.
 - **Local Planner:** A rule-based state machine (road-following, turn, intersection-forward, hazard-stop) generates waypoints based on perception output and high-level topological commands (turn left/right, straight).
 - **Continuous Controller:** A PID controller actuates steering, throttle, and brake to follow waypoints and maintain a target cruise speed (20 km/h).
2. **Imitation Learning (IL):** An end-to-end deep neural network that maps sensory input (forward-facing camera images) and high-level commands (follow lane, turn left/right, straight) directly to driving actions (steering, throttle, brake).
 - **Training Data:** Collected from a mix of automated agents (80%) and human drivers (20%) within CARLA (approx. 14 hours of data). Noise (triangular impulse) was injected into expert steering during data collection to improve robustness.
 - **Architecture:** Composed of perception (CNN), measurement (FC), joint input, and command-conditional control (4 branches of FC) modules.
 - **Training:** Adam optimizer, mini-batches balanced per command, extensive data augmentation (blur, noise, brightness, contrast, saturation variations), and dropout to improve generalization.
3. **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** An end-to-end deep neural network trained using the **Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C)** algorithm.
 - **Input:** Two most recent 84x84 images plus a measurement vector (speed, distance to goal, collision damage, one-hot command).
 - **Reward Signal:** A weighted sum designed to encourage speed and progress towards the goal, while penalizing collisions, sidewalk impingement, and driving on the opposite lane.
 - **Training:** 10 parallel actor threads, 10 million simulation steps (approx. 12 days of non-stop driving in simulation). Limited hyperparameter search due to computational costs.

#### 3.3. Experimental Protocol

- **Tasks:** Four goal-directed navigation tasks of increasing difficulty: Straight, One turn, Navigation, and Navigation with dynamic obstacles.
- **Environments:** Evaluated in both training (Town 1) and testing (Town 2) environments.
- **Weather Conditions:** Tested under a Training Weather Set (used during training) and an unseen Test Weather Set.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Primary metric is the percentage of successfully completed episodes (reaching goal within a time budget). Secondary metrics include average distance traveled between specific infractions (opposite lane, sidewalk, collision with static object/car/pedestrian). Each condition involved 25 episodes.

This comprehensive methodology, from simulator design to rigorous experimental evaluation, allows for a thorough analysis of both CARLA’s capabilities and the performance characteristics of different autonomous driving paradigms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using CARLA yielded several key insights into the performance of the three autonomous driving approaches and the challenges of generalization:

- **Overall Performance Limitations:** None of the methods achieved perfect performance, even on the simplest "Straight" task. Success rates consistently declined as task difficulty increased, particularly for navigation in dynamic urban environments. This indicates that autonomous driving remains an unsolved problem, even in simulation, due to the high variability in sensory inputs and the need for robust generalization.

- **Generalization Challenges:**
 - **New Weather vs. New Town:** Generalization to previously unseen weather conditions was significantly easier for the modular pipeline (MP) and imitation learning (IL) agents, often showing performance comparable to, or even better than, training conditions. This suggests that training with diverse weather conditions within a single environment is effective for weather generalization.
 - **New Town Difficulty:** In stark contrast, generalization to a previously unseen town (Town 2) proved to be a major hurdle for all three approaches. On challenging navigation tasks, performance dropped by at least a factor of two. This highlights that models trained in one environment struggle when faced with different 3D models, textures, and urban layouts, underscoring the critical need for training in geographically diverse virtual environments.

- **Modular Pipeline vs. End-to-End Learning (Imitation Learning):**
 - **Comparable Performance:** Surprisingly, the performance of the classic modular pipeline and the end-to-end imitation learning approach was remarkably close across most testing conditions, typically differing by less than 10% in success rate.
 - **MP's Fragility:** The MP approach showed a bimodal failure mode: if its perception stack (e.g., semantic segmentation) failed systematically in complex or new environments/weather, the entire system would fail completely. This indicates a potential fragility due to cascading errors in its sequential processing.
 - **IL's Robustness:** While overall success rates were similar, IL sometimes exhibited slightly more robust behavior in very challenging unseen conditions, suggesting a more graceful degradation or resilience compared to the brittle "all-or-nothing" failures of the MP's perception stack.

- **Imitation Learning vs. Reinforcement Learning:**
 - **RL Underperformance:** The most striking finding was the significant underperformance of the reinforcement learning (RL) agent compared to the imitation learning (IL) agent across all tasks. This occurred despite the RL agent being trained with substantially more data (12 days of simulated driving vs. 14 hours for IL).
 - **Possible Reasons for RL's Weakness:** The authors attribute this to several factors:
 - **RL Brittleness:** Deep RL methods are known to be brittle and often require extensive, task-specific hyperparameter search, which was infeasible given the realistic simulation's computational cost.
 - **Task Complexity:** Urban driving is a more complex task than many typical RL benchmarks (e.g., Atari games, simple maze navigation), involving continuous control, complex dynamics, and cluttered, dynamic visual environments.
 - **Lack of Regularization:** Unlike IL, the RL agent was not trained with data augmentation or regularization techniques like dropout, which are crucial for generalization in deep learning.

- **Infraction Analysis:**
 - **General Trend:** All methods showed more infractions in the test town compared to the training town.
 - **IL's Strength:** Imitation learning demonstrated the best performance in avoiding common traffic infractions like driving on the opposite lane or veering onto the sidewalk.
 - **RL's Peculiarity:** Reinforcement learning surprisingly collided least often with pedestrians, possibly due to the large negative reward associated with such events during training. However, it performed poorly in avoiding collisions with cars and static objects.
 - **MP's Collision Avoidance:** The modular pipeline generally performed best in avoiding collisions with cars and static objects.
 - **Rare Events Challenge:** The results highlight the susceptibility of end-to-end approaches to rare events (e.g., swerving for a pedestrian), suggesting that while CARLA can increase the frequency of such events, deeper algorithmic advancements may be necessary for robust handling of "long tail" scenarios.

In summary, the results underscore the immense challenges still facing autonomous driving, particularly concerning generalization to novel environments. While IL and MP perform comparably, IL shows some resilience. RL, despite its promise, struggled significantly in this complex, realistic environment, pointing to the need for more robust and data-efficient RL algorithms for real-world robotic tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CARLA represents a significant contribution to the autonomous driving research community, with substantial potential impact across academia and industry:

- **Democratization of Research:** By providing an open-source simulator with freely available, high-quality urban assets, CARLA dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for autonomous driving research. This allows a much wider range of researchers, from university labs to startups, to experiment with and develop new algorithms without the prohibitive costs and logistical complexities of real-world vehicle fleets. This fosters broader participation and accelerates innovation.

- **Standardized Benchmarking and Reproducibility:** CARLA offers a controlled, reproducible environment for evaluating and comparing diverse autonomous driving approaches. This is crucial for scientific progress, as it enables fair comparisons between different algorithms (e.g., modular vs. end-to-end, imitation vs. reinforcement learning) and allows researchers to build upon each other's work effectively. It establishes a common ground for assessing performance.

- **Scalable Data Generation:** The ability to generate vast amounts of labeled, high-fidelity synthetic data is one of CARLA's most profound impacts. This is indispensable for training data-hungry deep learning models, which are central to modern autonomous driving systems. Furthermore, CARLA can simulate "long tail" and dangerous scenarios (e.g., unexpected pedestrian behavior, complex multi-car interactions, adverse weather) that are difficult, expensive, or unsafe to collect in the real world. This capability is vital for building robust and safe autonomous systems.

- **Enhanced System Verification and Safety:** CARLA enables thorough testing and verification of autonomous driving systems in scenarios that are too dangerous to stage physically. This is critical for improving the safety, reliability, and ultimately, the public acceptance of autonomous vehicles. It allows developers to stress-test their systems against edge cases and potentially catastrophic events in a safe, virtual environment.

- **Fine-Grained Analysis and Debugging:** The detailed metrics provided by CARLA, including collision impacts, traffic rule infractions, and vehicle state information, allow for exceptionally granular analysis of driving policies. This detailed feedback is invaluable for diagnosing specific failure modes, understanding the strengths and weaknesses of different approaches, and guiding targeted improvements and algorithm development. The infraction analysis presented in the paper exemplifies this utility.

- **Driving Future Research Directions:** The paper's experimental results themselves highlight key challenges in the field, such as the difficulty of generalization to new environments and the current limitations of reinforcement learning in complex, realistic scenarios. This directly points to critical areas for future research in areas like domain adaptation, robust generalization, and more sample-efficient and stable deep reinforcement learning algorithms. CARLA serves as the ideal platform for exploring these research questions.

- **Community Building and Collaboration:** By being open-source, CARLA encourages community contributions, extensions, and the development of new functionalities and assets. This collaborative ecosystem can further accelerate advancements and foster a shared understanding of common challenges and solutions in autonomous driving.

In conclusion, CARLA is more than just a simulator; it is a foundational research tool that addresses critical bottlenecks in autonomous driving development. Its open, flexible, and high-fidelity nature positions it to significantly advance the field by democratizing access, enabling scalable data generation, facilitating rigorous evaluation, and guiding future research into the fundamental challenges of real-world autonomous navigation.
