# What Matters When Cotraining Robot Manipulation Policies on Everyday Human Videos?

> [!summary] Summary
> The research from MIT, UIUC, and Harvard identifies critical factors for effectively cotraining robot manipulation policies using everyday human videos, demonstrating that high-quality 3D hand pose labels and embodiment-specific architectural specialization significantly improve robot task performance, particularly in low-robot-data settings.

## Problem

- Generalist robot foundation models face data scarcity challenges, as traditional robot data collection is costly and unscalable.
- Prior work on learning from human videos either relies on modular approaches or uses highly curated "lab data" that lacks the diversity and unconstrained nature of real-world human actions.
- Everyday Internet-style human videos, while abundant, introduce difficulties like unconstrained motion, heterogeneous cameras, task mismatches, and noisy 3D hand pose estimates, hindering direct robot transfer.

## Method

- Created the **TriHands** dataset, consisting of 28 hours of multi-view triangulated 3D hand poses from everyday cooking videos, to provide high-quality ground truth for natural human motions.
- Developed a cotraining framework based on conditional flow matching, incorporating image-space scale alignment and action space alignment to bridge human and robot kinematic differences.
- Implemented a cross-embodiment architecture with token-level fusion and embodiment-specific visual and action experts to allow specialization while encouraging positive transfer.

## Results

- Cotraining with human data (HC) consistently improved task success rates, achieving an average absolute gain of **29.7%** over Robot Only (RO) training in low-robot-data settings.
- Analysis of failure modes showed HC resolved **100%** of "local errors" (failures by a few centimeters) for 5 out of 6 tasks where HC significantly outperformed RO, indicating transfer of fine-grained motion knowledge.
- The proposed token-level fusion architecture with embodiment-specific action encoders and decoders consistently outperformed baseline architectures, yielding substantial improvements across diverse manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The quality of 3D hand pose labels is paramount for effective human-to-robot transfer, with performance degrading monotonically as label noise increases.
> - Architectural designs allowing embodiment-specific specialization (e.g., token-level fusion, distinct action encoders/decoders) are crucial for successfully cotraining with natural human videos, which have a large "action gap" from robot kinematics.
> - Diverse, natural human motions from everyday videos can provide more beneficial generalization for robot policies than larger volumes of less diverse, highly curated lab data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robotic foundation models is challenged by data scarcity. Traditional methods, such as teleoperated demonstrations and reinforcement learning, face limitations in scalability, cost, reward design, exploration, and sim-to-real transfer. Internet videos of human activities represent a potentially vast and scalable data source for achieving physical intelligence in robots.

Prior research on learning from human video for robot manipulation generally falls into two categories:
- **Modular Pipelines:** These approaches decompose the policy into multiple stages or components, often relying on intermediate representations such as 3D poses, affordances, or 2D tracks derived from Internet videos. Examples include works by Shi et al. (2025), Bharadhwaj et al. (2024), and Track2Act (2024). This modularity has been attributed to the historical lack of robust 3D hand pose estimators.
- **End-to-End Cotraining on Aligned Data:** This category involves directly cotraining end-to-end policies on both human and robot data. However, existing work in this area typically utilizes highly curated "lab data" where human demonstrations are carefully orchestrated to mimic robot movements, cameras are standardized, and specialized hardware captures accurate 3D hand poses. Examples include Egomimic (Kareer et al., 2025) and Egobridge (Punamiya et al., 2025). Datasets like ARCTIC (Fan et al., 2023) and EgoDex (Hoque et al., 2025) provide accurate hand/head poses but with limited motion and scene diversity. Other cotraining efforts, such as those by Yang et al. (2025) and HoloAssist (Wang et al., 2023), sometimes rely on data with limited scene diversity or noisy hand labels, which can result in minimal transfer to robots.

The current work distinguishes itself by investigating cotraining policies using *everyday Internet-style human videos* characterized by natural, unconstrained motions, heterogeneous camera setups, and task mismatches. This setting presents challenges regarding the availability of ground-truth 3D hand labels and the significant "action gap" between human and robot kinematics. The paper aims to rigorously study the factors enabling transfer from such videos, focusing on hand pose quality and the action gap, thereby charting a path toward robust cotraining with more challenging, real-world human video data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate the factors that enable successful cotraining of robot manipulation policies using abundant, everyday Internet-style human videos. This differs from prior work that often relies on highly curated human demonstrations or modular approaches. The motivation stems from the recognition that while Internet videos offer a scalable data source for robotic intelligence, their inherent characteristics—unconstrained motion, heterogeneous cameras, task mismatch, and noisy hand pose estimates—pose significant challenges for direct transfer to robots.

Specifically, the authors aim to address two primary sources of difficulty:
1. **3D Hand Pose Quality:** Real-world human videos typically lack accurate 3D hand pose labels. The quality of these labels is hypothesized to be critical for successful cotraining, but a rigorous study has been difficult due to the absence of datasets with everyday human motions and high-quality ground truth hand labels. The research seeks to quantify the impact of hand pose quality on transfer.
2. **Action Gap (Natural Human Motion and Task Mismatch):** Human motions are naturally different from robot kinematics, and tasks performed by humans in everyday videos may not perfectly align with target robot tasks. This "action gap" can hinder direct transfer. The study aims to understand how to bridge this gap, particularly through architectural and algorithmic choices that allow for embodiment-specific specialization while encouraging positive transfer.

To achieve these objectives, the researchers did not aim to produce a method directly applicable to existing Internet video data today, given the immediate unavailability of ground-truth 3D hand labels for such data. Instead, their strategy was to study cotraining in a "proxy setting" that retains key challenges of Internet video (egocentric views, natural motions, camera and task mismatch) while allowing for the generation of high-quality ground truth 3D hand labels through extrinsic camera triangulation. This controlled experimental setup enables the isolation and empirical investigation of the specific bottlenecks, providing insights to guide future work in scaling up hand pose estimators and developing more robust cotraining recipes for truly in-the-wild data.

The paper also outlines four specific hypotheses:
- **H1:** Cotraining with human data increases zero-shot generalization to new objects and backgrounds.
- **H2:** Human data transfers fine-grained motion knowledge, not just coarse visual features.
- **H3:** Higher quality 3D hand labels lead to greater transfer.
- **H4:** Allowing the network to specialize to each embodiment leads to improved robot task performance.

By investigating these aspects, the research endeavors to identify crucial design principles for cotraining robot policies on diverse human video data, laying groundwork for future advancements in generalist robot foundation models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic approach involving dataset creation, framework development, and extensive experimentation to evaluate the effectiveness of cotraining robot manipulation policies with everyday human videos.

#### 3.1. TriHands Dataset Construction

A primary challenge is the lack of everyday human videos with clean 3D hand labels. To address this, the authors constructed the **TriHands dataset** by multi-view triangulating 3D hand poses on 532 cooking videos from the EgoExo4D dataset.
- **Source Data:** EgoExo4D provides egocentric and multiple (4-5) exocentric views. The EgoExo4D 2D keypoints were found to be a bottleneck.
- **Improved 2D Keypoints:** The authors utilized 3D model-based pose estimators (specifically, a version of HaWoR initialized with WiLoR) to generate more accurate 2D keypoints, especially in challenging conditions like poor lighting or partial occlusion. A fine-tuning process with a reprojection loss (L_proj) was applied on a small subset of EgoExo4D's partial 2D keypoint labels.
- **3D Triangulation:** DLT (Direct Linear Transformation) with nonlinear refinement was used to triangulate 3D hands from the egocentric and exocentric views. Safeguards were implemented to maximize the number of cameras used while enforcing reprojection error thresholds and requiring valid hand detections in the egocentric view to avoid hands of secondary individuals.
- **Post-processing:** After filtering and 0.4s linear interpolation, the dataset comprises 3,042,406 right-hand frames at 30fps, totaling over 28 hours of training data.
- **MANO Parameters:** Triangulated 3D joints were converted into MANO parameters using GPU-batched inverse kinematics, initialized with HaWoR's predictions for better convergence.

#### 3.2. Framework for Cotraining Policies

The framework balances encouraging positive transfer from human data while preventing harmful representation alignment due to embodiment differences. It is based on a conditional flow matching architecture.

- **Data Streams:**
 - **Human Data:** Images from an egocentric head-camera; raw actions derived from triangulated 3D hand poses (TriHands) or monocular estimation (for ablation studies).
 - **Robot Data:** Images from a similarly positioned egocentric camera; actions are 6-DOF TCP pose and discrete grasp commands for an AgileX Piper arm.
- **Image-space Scale Alignment:** To address significant differences in camera parameters (e.g., FOV, focal length) between human and robot datasets:
 - Human fisheye images are undistorted to a pinhole projection.
 - The robot camera extrinsics are adjusted (rotated 90 degrees and translated to a specific depth `z_robot`) to approximately match the image-space object scale of human videos while retaining 3D scene extent. This ensures objects appear at a similar scale in both image streams.
- **Action Space Alignment:**
 - The human 3D hand pose is mapped to the robot action space by using the "middle finger proximal" MANO joint frame (chosen for better contact alignment) as the human TCP. A rotation aligns it with the robot TCP frame.
 - To stabilize actions against head camera motion, per-timestep camera-frame poses are transformed to the current head frame.
 - Action distributions are mean-centered independently for each dataset and scaled to ±1 to resolve remaining biases (e.g., human hand chirality bias, depth differences).
- **Cross-embodiment Architecture:**
 - The architecture is Transfusion-inspired, using separate FFN experts for visual and action tokens, but with embodiment-specific weights to account for the large motion gap between human and robot data.
 - It retains the full grid of ViT image tokens and concatenates them with noisy action tokens via shared attention (token-level fusion).
 - Embodiment-specific 3-layer MLPs are used for action and proprioception encoders and decoders.
- **Cotraining Algorithm and Weighted Loss:**
 - To implicitly upweight robot data influence, minibatches are sampled with an equal number of samples independently from each embodiment. This effectively weights the robot loss higher due to its smaller dataset size relative to the human dataset.
 - The loss function used is a conditional flow-matching imitation learning loss.

#### 3.3. Experimental Design

The experiments aim to validate the four hypotheses (H1-H4) across six real-world manipulation tasks.
- **Tasks and Environments:** Six diverse manipulation tasks (Pick, Stack, Pull, Reorient, Book, Pour) are used, ranging from simple to intricate. To test generalization (H1), each task is evaluated across 10 training and 4 test environments, each with unique objects and backgrounds within a category.
- **Data Sources:**
 - **Robot Data:** 50 demonstrations per environment (500 per task, 3000 total across 6 tasks).
 - **Human Data:** RGB images from all 532 TriHands videos (28 hours). Hand actions are from triangulation (TriHands) and monocular estimation for H3. Gaussian noise (0.5× and 1.0× TriHands–HaWoR error) is added to triangulated hands to study quality impact.
- **Architecture Ablations (H4):** Token-level fusion and embodiment-specific encoders/decoders are ablated against:
 - Image-bottleneck (CLS-token): Pools ViT tokens to a single vector, common in prior work.
 - Shared action encoder/decoder weights across embodiments.
- **Experiments:**
 - **Human Cotraining (HC) vs. Robot Only (RO):** Comparison at increasing robot data levels ({3, 5, 10} environments) to test H1.
 - **Motion Transfer Analysis (H2):** Qualitative analysis of failure modes (global vs. local errors) for HC and RO in tasks where HC significantly outperforms RO.
- **Baselines:**
 - **CLS-token:** Image-bottleneck architecture.
 - **EgoDex:** Cotraining with a large-scale lab-collected dataset (145 hours).
 - **HaWoR:** Human videos labeled with a monocular hand estimator.
 - **EgoBridge:** Uses a representational alignment loss based on action similarity, requiring CLS-token.
 - **PiZero:** Employs shared action encoders and decoders.

A total of 3,480 real-world rollouts are conducted for evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the factors that influence cotraining robot manipulation policies on everyday human videos.

#### 4.1. Impact of Human Cotraining

- **Improved Task Performance:** Human Cotraining (HC) consistently improved task performance compared to Robot Only (RO) training across all robot data regimes. The most substantial gains were observed in the low-robot-data setting (3 environments), with an absolute success rate gain of 29.7% on average (41.7% for HC vs. 12.0% for RO).
- **Scaling Behavior:** While the benefits of human data diminished as the amount of task-specific robot data increased, HC continued to offer improvements. This trend is consistent with prior work on cross-task cotraining.
- **Scene Diversity:** The TriHands dataset, despite having 5 times fewer hours of data than EgoDex (28 hours vs. 145 hours), demonstrated better robot transfer. This suggests that the scene diversity and natural motions in TriHands contribute more to generalization than sheer data volume in lab-curated datasets.

#### 4.2. Motion Transfer vs. Coarse Visual Features

- **Local Error Resolution:** For 5 out of 6 tasks where HC significantly outperformed RO, 100% of the RO failures were categorized as "local errors" (robot failing by a few centimeters). HC resolved these local errors, achieving 82.8% success rate compared to RO's 29.2%. This indicates that human data transfers fine-grained motion knowledge rather than only coarse visual features.
- **Complex Task Limitations:** For the most complex task (Pour), RO often failed to servo near the object, making it difficult to definitively assess fine-grained motion transfer. The authors suggest that the overall recipe may be less effective on highly complex tasks due to reliance on kinematic retargeting and a larger inherent motion gap.

#### 4.3. Hand Pose Quality

- **Quality Matters:** Higher quality 3D hand labels led to greater transfer. Monocular-estimated hands (HaWoR) showed worse average performance compared to triangulated hands (TriHands).
- **Transfer with Noise:** Even noisy monocular-estimated hand labels provided some benefit, with HaWoR showing a mean success rate of 24.7% compared to RO's 11.9%. This suggests that current state-of-the-art monocular estimators can enable some transfer, but significant room for improvement remains.
- **Degradation with Noise:** Experiments demonstrated that transfer degraded monotonically as controlled Gaussian noise was added to the triangulated hands, further confirming the importance of hand pose quality. The noise in depth values in monocular estimation was noted to dominate hand joint translations over a 1-second prediction horizon.

#### 4.4. Embodiment-Specific Specialization

- **Architecture Impact:** Enabling embodiment-specific specialization through architectural choices was crucial for significant transfer from natural motion human data.
 - **Token-level Fusion:** The proposed token-level fusion architecture consistently outperformed the CLS-token (image-bottleneck) architecture on 5 out of 6 tasks. This suggests that for data with a large motion gap, allowing the model to specialize visual understanding per embodiment through token-level fusion is more effective than pooling visual tokens into a single vector.
 - **Untied Action Encoders/Decoders:** Decoupling action encoders and decoders for different embodiments resulted in substantial improvements across 5 out of 6 tasks, indicating the necessity of allowing the network to specialize to distinct action distributions.
- **Scale Alignment:** Image-space scale alignment for human fisheye images, using an extent-matched pinhole camera model, doubled performance over Robot Only training. Heavily misaligned camera models yielded no transfer benefit, highlighting its criticality.
- **Baseline Comparisons:** EgoBridge showed improvements over the CLS-token baseline on 4/6 tasks but performed less effectively than the proposed BC-only recipe, possibly due to the larger action gap in the data making its optimal transport loss less reliable. PiZero, which uses shared action encoders/decoders, also performed worse than the proposed method, supporting the need for embodiment-specific specialization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by systematically investigating the challenges and solutions for cotraining robot policies using diverse, everyday human videos. The findings have several implications and potential impacts:

1. **Advancing Generalist Robot Models:** The work addresses a core challenge in building generalist robotic foundation models: data scarcity. By demonstrating that robust transfer from abundant everyday human videos is possible with specific considerations, it opens a pathway for leveraging a vast, readily available data source that is significantly cheaper and more scalable than traditional robot data collection. This could accelerate the development of more capable and general-purpose robots.

2. **Highlighting Critical Factors for Transfer:** The study rigorously identifies and quantifies the importance of key factors for successful human-to-robot skill transfer:
 - **3D Hand Pose Quality:** The creation of the TriHands dataset provides a valuable resource for future research, offering high-quality ground-truth 3D hand labels for natural human motions. The finding that higher hand pose quality leads to better transfer underscores the need for continued advancements in monocular hand pose estimation, particularly for improving depth and joint translation accuracy.
 - **Image-space Scale Alignment:** Identifying scale inconsistency as an overlooked issue and demonstrating the benefits of image-space scale alignment (even with vastly different cameras) offers a practical solution that can be integrated into existing and future Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.
 - **Embodiment-Specific Specialization:** The finding that architectural choices enabling specialization (token-level fusion, separate action encoders/decoders) are crucial for handling the inherent "motion gap" in natural human videos provides fundamental design principles for cotraining architectures. This challenges assumptions made by approaches designed for more aligned lab data.

3. **Guiding Future Research:** The paper not only identifies bottlenecks but also suggests directions for future work. For example, the limitations observed on complex tasks due to kinematic retargeting and the larger motion gap point towards the need for novel representational alignment and action retargeting techniques specifically designed for the complexity of everyday videos. The improved performance of TriHands over large-scale lab datasets like EgoDex emphasizes the value of scene diversity and natural motion over mere data volume.

4. **Practical Utility in Low-Data Regimes:** The significant performance gains (absolute success rate gain of 29.7%) in the low-robot-data regime are particularly impactful. Many real-world robot deployment scenarios operate with limited task-specific demonstrations. Cotraining with readily available human video data can provide a substantial initial boost, potentially reducing the need for extensive robot data collection or enabling faster deployment and adaptation of robots to new tasks with minimal specific training.

In summary, this research advances the empirical understanding of cross-embodiment learning, provides a new high-quality dataset, and offers concrete architectural and data preprocessing strategies that facilitate more effective robot learning from diverse, unconstrained human video data. This work is foundational for developing more robust and generalizable robot manipulation policies.
