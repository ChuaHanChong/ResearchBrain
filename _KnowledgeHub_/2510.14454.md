# Towards Adaptable Humanoid Control via Adaptive Motion Tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework from Shanghai AI Lab and Shanghai Jiao Tong University enables humanoid robots to achieve adaptable, high-fidelity control from just a single reference motion by combining sparse keyframe editing with a two-stage reinforcement learning approach. This method produced robust and agile performance on a Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating improved success rates and smoother motions across diverse tasks compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid motion control methods struggle to balance precise imitation with broad adaptability across varying conditions.
- Current approaches often demand extensive, diverse datasets or continuous teleoperation for adaptation, which is resource-intensive and impractical.
- Traditional single-motion editing techniques frequently result in physically implausible or dynamically inconsistent adaptations when applied to robots.

## Method

- AdaMimic introduces a two-stage reinforcement learning framework that generates an augmented dataset from a *single* human reference motion.
- The augmented dataset is created by sparsely editing keyframes based on task-dependent variables, while strictly preserving the original local joint trajectories.
- The framework incorporates jointly learned phase and tracking adapters to enable adaptive time warping and action compensation, significantly enhancing motion flexibility and precision.

## Results

- AdaMimic consistently outperformed state-of-the-art baselines in simulation, achieving higher success rates and lower tracking errors across seven agile tasks with varied adaptation difficulties.
- The trained policies successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, exhibiting robust and smooth real-world execution, particularly in challenging adaptation scenarios where baselines failed.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the two-stage training, sparse keyframe editing, and the adaptive phase and tracking adapters are crucial components contributing to AdaMimic's superior adaptability and imitation accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining sparse keyframe editing with reinforcement learning can effectively generate dynamically plausible and adaptable motions from minimal reference data.
> - A two-stage RL training strategy, beginning with fixed phase tracking and then integrating adaptive temporal and action compensation via learned adapters, is critical for stable optimization and robust performance.
> - Adaptive time warping, governed by a learned phase adapter, enables the preservation of motion style while flexibly adjusting global execution parameters, a key factor for adaptability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in humanoid robot control: enabling robots to perform complex, agile motions adaptably across diverse real-world conditions while faithfully preserving the nuances of demonstrated human motion. This lies at the intersection of **motion imitation learning** and **adaptive control** for complex, high-dimensional robotic systems.

Traditionally, approaches to learning humanoid skills from reference motions fall into two main paradigms:

- **Motion Prior-based Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Methods like Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) use human motion data as a "style regularizer" or prior within an RL framework. These methods often achieve good adaptability and can generate motions beyond the exact training data. However, they typically sacrifice precise imitation accuracy, may lead to jerky motions, and often require extensive reward engineering and tuning to achieve desired behaviors.
- **Motion Tracking (Mimicry) Methods:** Approaches like DeepMimic directly track reference motions, often achieving high fidelity and accurate reproduction of specific trajectories. They generally require less reward engineering. However, their primary limitation is a lack of adaptability. They tend to perform well only on the exact motions they were trained on or on very similar ones. Adapting to diverse conditions usually necessitates large-scale training datasets covering every possible variation or requires a new, specifically tailored reference motion for each test-time scenario, which is often impractical or unavailable.

The core tension in the existing landscape is thus a trade-off between **imitation accuracy** and **adaptability**, coupled with a reliance on extensive data. Recent efforts have explored large-scale datasets to improve generalization in motion tracking, but these still often depend on substantial data curation, teleoperation, or pre-collected reference motions during deployment and may struggle with highly agile behaviors.

AdaMimic positions itself as a novel solution designed to **combine the strengths of both paradigms**: achieving accurate imitation *and* broad adaptability, crucially, from **only a single reference motion**. This significantly reduces data dependence and the burden of data collection, a major bottleneck in robot learning. By introducing a new motion tracking algorithm that leverages sparse keyframe editing and adaptive time warping, AdaMimic attempts to overcome the limitations of prior motion editing techniques (which often yield physically implausible motions) by integrating them with robust, physics-based RL. It builds upon ideas from motion editing, data-driven generation, and physics-based RL for plausibility, but innovates in their specific combination for single-motion adaptability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable humanoid robots to **acquire adaptable whole-body control capabilities from a single reference motion, while meticulously preserving the underlying motion patterns**. This goal is motivated by several key considerations:

1. **Emulating Human Motor Learning:** Humans possess an innate ability to observe a skill, mimic it, and then adapt it flexibly to novel situations (e.g., a tennis player adjusting a stroke based on ball position). Humanoid robots are envisioned to possess a similar capacity for agile, versatile movement in complex, dynamic environments.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Methods:**
 - **Data Dependence:** Current motion tracking methods often necessitate vast datasets of varied motions or real-time teleoperation for adaptation, which is costly, time-consuming, and limits generalizability.
 - **Imitation-Adaptation Trade-off:** Motion prior methods offer adaptability but often sacrifice the high-fidelity imitation desired for complex, expressive movements. Pure motion tracking methods achieve high fidelity but lack the inherent adaptability to new environmental conditions or task parameters.
 - **Physical Plausibility of Motion Editing:** Traditional motion editing techniques, while allowing single-motion input, often generate physically implausible or dynamically inconsistent motions when adapted, making them unsuitable for real-world robotic deployment.
3. **Enhancing Real-World Deployability:** For humanoid robots to operate effectively outside of controlled laboratory settings, they must be robust and adaptable to variations in terrain, task parameters, and environmental interactions. A method that can generate physically consistent adaptations from minimal data is crucial for practical application.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the gap between accurate, high-fidelity motion reproduction and the broad, flexible adaptability necessary for robust humanoid performance, all while minimizing the reliance on extensive, pre-collected training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AdaMimic (Adaptive Motion Tracking) introduces a novel, two-stage reinforcement learning framework to achieve adaptable humanoid control from a single reference motion.

**A. Motion Processing and Augmented Dataset Creation (D_init_ref → D_edit_ref):**
The process begins by converting raw human motion (e.g., from videos) into an SMPL (Skinned Multi-Person Linear) model, which is then retargeted to the target humanoid robot (Unitree G1). From this single reference motion ($D_{init}^{ref}$), an *augmented dataset* ($D_{edit}^{ref}$) is generated.
1. **Keyframe Selection:** Instead of editing every frame, the method sparsely selects `N` keyframes. Some keyframes are chosen based on semantic contexts (e.g., take-off, landing in a jump), while others are uniformly sampled for overall smoothness.
2. **Light Keyframe Editing:** A small subset of these keyframes ($\Phi_{edit}$) is then physically edited. This editing primarily transforms the *global pose* (e.g., position, orientation) of the robot at these keyframes based on a task-dependent variable ($\psi_i$, such as jumping distance). Crucially, the *local joint trajectories* of the original motion are strictly preserved across all edited motions. This design ensures that the core "style" or pattern of the original movement remains, while its global execution can vary.
3. **RL for Intermediate Frames:** Unlike classical motion editing that interpolates intermediate frames with fixed rules (which can lead to physically implausible motions), AdaMimic leverages reinforcement learning in the subsequent stages to generate dynamically plausible intermediate frames that connect these edited keyframes.

**B. Two-Stage Policy Training:**

**Stage 1: Motion Tracking with Fixed Phase Interval**
In this initial stage, a tracking policy ($\pi_{track}$) is trained using a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (RL) framework (Markov Decision Process) to accurately reproduce motions from the augmented dataset ($D_{edit}^{ref}$) using a *fixed phase interval* ($\Delta\phi$).
1. **Sparse Global Reward ($r_{global}$):** This reward encourages accurate alignment of the robot's global pose with the reference motion, but *only at the selected keyframes* ($\Phi_{key}$). This sparsity prevents over-constraining the robot's global trajectory between keyframes, allowing RL to find dynamically plausible paths. To stabilize training with sparse signals, a separate value function ($V_{track}^{sparse}$) is used to estimate returns.
2. **Dense Local Reward ($r_{local}$):** This reward is applied at *every timestep* and encourages the robot to match the local joint-space positions of the reference motion. This ensures that the fine-grained movement patterns and style of the original motion are preserved throughout the trajectory. A separate value function ($V_{track}^{dense}$) is used for this dense signal.
This stage provides an initial, robust policy capable of tracking the "keyframed" motions under a stable training setup.

**Stage 2: Adapters Learning with Adaptive Phase Interval**
This stage addresses the rigidity of the fixed phase interval from Stage 1, which limits adaptability, especially for larger variations. The tracking policy ($\pi_{track}$) from Stage 1 is *frozen*, and two "adapters" are jointly learned:
1. **Phase Adapter ($\pi_{\Delta}^{phase}$):** This adapter takes the current observation and outputs a *delta phase interval* ($\Delta\phi_{\Delta_k}$). This delta is added to the fixed base interval ($\Delta\phi_k$) to produce an *adaptive phase interval* ($\Delta\phi_{ada_k}$). This mechanism enables flexible time warping, allowing the robot to accelerate or decelerate its motion execution to better match the adapted global trajectory without altering the fundamental local motion pattern.
2. **Tracking Adapter ($\pi_{\Delta}^{track}$):** Given the newly computed adaptive phase interval, this adapter generates a compensation term for the low-level actions ($a_{\Delta_k}$). This term is scaled by the delta phase interval and added to the original tracking action, ensuring that the robot's movements are precisely adjusted to account for the temporal warping.
This two-stage design with adapters allows the policy to adapt motion speed and fine-tune actions, significantly improving both imitation accuracy and adaptability. Separate critics ($V_{\Delta}^{phase}$, $V_{\Delta}^{track}$) are used to optimize these adapters.

**C. Real-World Deployment:**
The trained policies are directly deployed on a **Unitree G1 humanoid robot**. Robustness is enhanced through:
- A 5-step observation history to stabilize control.
- Lidar odometry (via FastLIO) for global localization, crucial given the adaptive nature of the tasks.
- A hierarchical control architecture with different operating frequencies: policy (50Hz), low-level PD control (500Hz), and odometry (10Hz).
- Specific hardware considerations like locking waist joints for stability.

**D. Implementation Details:**
The system uses PPO for RL, Isaac Gym for high-throughput simulation, 3-layer MLPs for policies and value functions, L2C2 regularization for smoothness, and extensive domain randomization to bridge the sim-to-real gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive evaluations in both simulation and on a real humanoid robot, demonstrating the superior performance of AdaMimic.

**A. Simulation Results:**
AdaMimic consistently outperformed multiple baselines (AMP-Style, AMP-Mimic, DeepMimic variants, UniTracker, and ablated versions of AdaMimic) across seven agile tasks (various jumps, tennis/badminton hits) and a wide range of adaptation conditions (easy and hard).

- **Superior Performance:** AdaMimic achieved significantly higher success rates, lower dense local tracking errors (E_dense_l-bpe), and lower sparse global tracking errors (E_sparse_g-bpe) compared to all baselines. Its smoothness (E_dense_smth) was also highly competitive.
- **Comparison with Motion Priors (AMP):** While AMP-Mimic improved upon AMP-Style by incorporating phase information, both exhibited moderate adaptability but struggled with imitation accuracy and generated jerky motions due to weak motion constraints. AdaMimic surpassed them in all metrics.
- **Comparison with Motion Tracking (DeepMimic):** DeepMimic-NoAdapt, as expected, lacked adaptability. DeepMimic-Adapt, using rule-based augmented motions, showed adaptability but significantly degraded in performance for "hard" adaptation cases. This was attributed to the physical implausibility of the rule-based edited motions. AdaMimic, by contrast, leveraged RL to generate dynamically plausible intermediate frames between sparsely edited keyframes, leading to robust adaptation.
- **Comparison with Large-Scale Data Trackers (UniTracker):** Even UniTracker, pre-trained on massive datasets and fine-tuned, underperformed AdaMimic. This crucial finding suggests that simply having more data isn't enough; a principled approach to generating physically plausible adaptations (as in AdaMimic) is more effective for agile humanoid skills.
- **Effectiveness of Adapters:** The phase and tracking adapters were crucial. They enabled flexible time warping (e.g., extending airtime for longer jumps) and compensatory actions, leading to substantial reductions in tracking errors and improved smoothness, especially in challenging adaptation scenarios.
- **Keyframe Editing vs. Per-Frame Editing:** The sparse keyframe editing strategy of AdaMimic proved superior to dense, per-frame editing (AdaMimic-Dense). The combination of sparse global rewards at keyframes and dense local rewards with adapters allowed for robust learning.
- **Necessity of Two-Stage Training:** Ablation studies showed that training with adaptive phase intervals from scratch (AdaMimic-Stage1-∆ϕ_ada) degraded performance compared to Stage 1. This highlighted the importance of the two-stage approach for stable optimization.
- **Importance of Freezing Tracking Policy:** Freezing the Stage 1 tracking policy during Stage 2 (when training adapters) was critical. Finetuning the policy alongside adapters (AdaMimic-NoFreeze) led to noticeable degradation in smoothness and tracking accuracy, indicating increased optimization difficulty.

**B. Hardware Results:**
The trained AdaMimic policies were successfully deployed on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating strong real-world performance, especially in hard adaptation conditions.

- **Robust Real-World Execution:** AdaMimic achieved high success rates, reduced local joint errors, and smoother motions on the real robot across various tasks and adaptation difficulties. This validates its ability to generate physically plausible and robust behaviors.
- **Baselines' Hardware Limitations:**
 - DeepMimic-Adapt failed in extreme cases (e.g., high jumps) due to the physical inconsistencies of its rule-based augmented motions, which could not be reliably executed on hardware.
 - AMP-Style exhibited jerky, unstable motions, particularly in dynamic tasks like tennis hitting, leading to poor balance.
 - AdaMimic-Stage1 (without the adapters) showed significant sim-to-real gaps, with issues like ankle joint instability during forceful motions, demonstrating the critical role of temporal and action adaptation provided by the adapters.

In summary, the results unequivocally demonstrate that AdaMimic's novel combination of sparse keyframe editing, a two-stage training strategy, and adaptive phase and tracking adapters allows humanoids to achieve both accurate motion imitation and broad adaptability from minimal reference data, leading to robust and agile performance in both simulation and the real world.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AdaMimic represents a significant advancement in humanoid robot control, particularly in the domain of motion imitation and adaptive skill acquisition. Its key contributions and potential impacts are multi-faceted:

1. **Bridging the Imitation-Adaptation Gap from Minimal Data:** The most notable significance is AdaMimic's ability to achieve both high-fidelity motion imitation and broad adaptability from *only a single reference motion*. This addresses a long-standing challenge in humanoid control, where researchers often had to choose between accurate reproduction and flexible adaptation, or rely on prohibitively large and diverse datasets. This breakthrough drastically reduces the data requirements and complexity of training adaptable humanoid skills.
2. **Enhanced Physical Plausibility and Robustness:** By leveraging reinforcement learning to generate intermediate frames between sparsely edited keyframes, AdaMimic overcomes the limitations of traditional motion editing techniques that often produce dynamically inconsistent or physically implausible trajectories. This results in more robust and stable motions, which is paramount for real-world deployment on complex hardware like humanoid robots.
3. **Flexible Time Warping and Action Compensation:** The introduction of jointly learned phase and tracking adapters allows the robot to dynamically adjust the speed and timing of its motion (time warping) and compensate its low-level actions accordingly. This temporal flexibility is crucial for adapting a single motion pattern to varied task parameters (e.g., different jump distances or hitting points) while preserving the underlying motion style.
4. **Practical Real-World Deployability:** The successful deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, showcasing robust performance across diverse, agile tasks under various adaptation conditions, validates the practical utility and real-world applicability of AdaMimic. It demonstrates that controllers trained with this framework can handle complex dynamics and environmental variations.
5. **Simplified Policy Learning and Deployment:** By reducing data dependence and the need for extensive reward engineering or test-time reference motions, AdaMimic simplifies the overall pipeline for acquiring new humanoid skills. This can accelerate research and development in humanoid robotics, making it easier to imbue robots with a wider repertoire of complex, adaptable behaviors.
6. **Foundation for Advanced Humanoid Skills:** This work lays a strong foundation for developing more advanced and interactive whole-body skills for humanoids. Its ability to adapt core motion patterns opens doors for learning complex athletic tasks, intricate manipulation skills, or even nuanced social interactions that require precise, yet flexible, execution.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Democratization of Robot Skill Learning:** Lowering the data barrier for learning complex, adaptable skills could make advanced humanoid control more accessible to researchers and developers without access to massive motion capture datasets or extensive teleoperation setups.
- **Enabling Versatile Humanoid Applications:** Robots equipped with AdaMimic-like capabilities could perform a wider range of tasks in unstructured environments, from assisting in logistics and manufacturing to participating in sports or emergency response, where adaptability to varied conditions is critical.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The novel combination of sparse keyframe editing, two-stage RL, and adaptive temporal control will likely inspire new lines of research in motion generation, control, and sim-to-real transfer for highly dynamic robotic systems. The identified limitations, such as extending to wider task scopes, developing general motion editing mechanisms, and incorporating perception for interactive tasks, also provide clear avenues for future impactful work.

In conclusion, AdaMimic marks a significant step towards truly agile and adaptable humanoid control, offering a powerful framework that addresses key limitations of prior art and paves the way for more sophisticated and generalized robotic capabilities.
