# TACO: Benchmarking Generalizable Bimanual Tool-ACtion-Object Understanding

> [!summary] Summary
> The TACO dataset, featuring 2.5K bimanual interaction sequences and 5.2M frames, enables benchmarking generalizable hand-object understanding across novel tool geometries, interaction triplets, and tool categories. Its automatic acquisition pipeline provides detailed 4D annotations for 131 unique tool-action-object compositions, covering 20 object categories and 15 daily actions.

## Problem

- Existing datasets for hand-object interaction (HOI) primarily focus on unimanual actions or bimanual interactions with a single object.
- The scarcity of comprehensive datasets with diverse tool-action-object compositions limits the study of generalizable bimanual manipulation skills.
- Current computer vision systems struggle to adapt manipulation skills to novel objects or interaction contexts due to insufficient data for complex, multi-object interactions.

## Method

- Introduced TACO, a large-scale real-world 4D dataset capturing diverse bimanual tool-action-object interactions across 20 object categories and 15 daily actions.
- Developed an automatic data acquisition pipeline leveraging multi-view RGB, egocentric RGBD, and optical motion capture for precise 3D hand-object mesh, 2D segmentation, and marker-removed visual data.
- Established three generalization benchmarks (compositional action recognition, motion forecasting, grasp synthesis) with four distinct data splits to systematically evaluate model performance in novel interaction scenarios.

## Results

- TACO includes 2.5K motion sequences and 5.2M video frames, annotated with precise 3D hand/object meshes and 2D segmentation masks, covering 131 unique tool-action-object triplets.
- Compositional action recognition accuracy decreased from 86.15% (no generalization) to 44.00% (compound generalization) for the best baseline, highlighting generalization challenges.
- Benchmarked grasp synthesis methods showed higher collision ratios and poorer contact for novel object geometries and categories (e.g., S2, S4 splits), indicating struggles with physical plausibility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing methods for action recognition, motion forecasting, and grasp synthesis exhibit a significant performance drop when generalizing to novel interaction triplets or tool categories compared to novel object geometries.
> - Modeling the interaction context (e.g., the second hand and target object) is crucial for cooperative grasp synthesis, significantly improving contact and reducing collisions.
> - Generalization of motions for dominant interaction entities (like the tool-wielding hand and the tool itself) proves more challenging than for non-dominant entities (left hand, target object).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision and robotics seeks to enable artificial systems to understand and synthesize human-like hand-object interactions (HOI). A fundamental human capability is the ability to transfer manipulation skills to novel objects by inferring their functional regularities. However, existing technical approaches for analyzing and synthesizing HOI are largely limited to unimanual actions or bimanual interactions with a single object. This limitation is primarily attributed to the scarcity of comprehensive datasets.

Previous dataset efforts, such as FHPA, ObMan, ContactPose, GRAB, HO3D, DexYCB, OakInk, HOI4D, HMDO, and ARCTIC, have primarily focused on single-hand grasping or general manipulation of a single object. While some datasets, including KIT Bimanual Manipulation, H2O, and H2O-3D, address bimanual interactions, they often exhibit restricted object diversity or cover a limited number of distinct tool-action-object triplets. For instance, the KIT Bimanual Manipulation dataset, a relevant prior work, includes only 12 objects and fewer than 20 distinct triplets, which limits its utility for studying generalizable bimanual interactions where the goal is to interpret actions involving novel object geometries or previously unseen combinations of tools, actions, and objects.

The research presented here addresses this identified data gap by introducing TACO, a large-scale dataset specifically designed to facilitate and benchmark the understanding of *generalizable bimanual tool-action-object interactions*. By focusing on diverse tool-action-object compositions in real-world scenarios, TACO aims to advance research in areas such as compositional action recognition, generalizable motion forecasting, and cooperative grasp synthesis, which require models to adapt to novel interaction contexts and object variations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the TACO dataset is to overcome the limitations of existing datasets in supporting the development of technical approaches for generalizable bimanual hand-object manipulation. Humans routinely perform complex tasks involving both hands, often using one object as a tool to interact with another. They can adapt these skills to novel objects and situations, a capability that current computer vision systems largely lack due to insufficient data.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Construct a Large-Scale Bimanual HOI Dataset:** To create an extensive dataset named TACO that captures rich bimanual hand-object interactions, specifically focusing on scenarios where one hand uses a "tool" to interact with a "target object." This dataset is intended to provide a comprehensive knowledge base for multi-object cooperation.
2. **Ensure Diversity in Tool-Action-Object Compositions:** To include a wide array of tool-action-object triplets. The dataset aims to cover 131 distinct triplet types across 20 object categories, 196 specific object instances, and 15 daily actions. This diversity is crucial for studying generalization across different interaction contexts and object geometries.
3. **Facilitate Generalizable Understanding:** To structure the dataset and its associated benchmarks to explicitly support the study of generalization. This includes testing models' abilities to generalize to novel object geometries (unseen instances of known categories), novel interaction triplets (unseen combinations of tool, action, and object categories), and entirely novel tool categories.
4. **Develop an Automatic Data Acquisition Pipeline:** To address the challenges of capturing high-quality bimanual interaction data. The goal is to create a fully automatic pipeline that combines multi-view sensing with an optical motion capture system, ensuring the precise recovery of hand-object 3D meshes, 2D segmentation masks, and realistic visual data (including marker removal) at each time step.
5. **Establish Benchmarks for Generalizable Tasks:** To define and benchmark three specific, challenging tasks related to generalizable bimanual HOI:
 - **Compositional Action Recognition:** Recognizing actions in video frames, especially when tool-action-object triplets are novel at test time.
 - **Generalizable Hand-Object Motion Forecasting:** Predicting future 3D hand and object motions given short observed motion clips, with generalization to unseen geometries and triplets.
 - **Cooperative Grasp Synthesis:** Generating realistic and physically plausible hand grasps on tools, taking into account the interaction context provided by a second hand and a target object.
6. **Uncover Insights and Challenges:** Through benchmarking, to reveal the current limitations of existing methodologies when confronted with generalization challenges and to highlight new research opportunities in the analysis and synthesis of hand-object interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors constructed TACO, a large-scale real-world 4D bimanual hand-object interaction dataset. The methodology encompasses data capturing, a fully automatic data annotation pipeline, and a systematic approach to data splitting for generalization benchmarks.

#### 3.1. Data Capturing

The data capturing system was designed to simultaneously capture high-quality visual and motion data from multiple perspectives:
- **Multi-View RGB Cameras:** 12 synchronized industrial FLIR cameras provided allocentric (third-person) views with a resolution of 4096x3000 pixels.
- **Egocentric RGBD Camera:** A helmet-mounted Realsense L515 camera captured first-person RGBD videos at 1920x1080 resolution.
- **Optical Motion Capture (Mocap) System:** A NOKOV optical motion capture suite with 6 infrared Mars4H cameras tracked the motion of the Realsense L515 camera and objects.
- **Synchronization:** All cameras and the mocap system operated at 30 Hz, with time synchronization achieved through a shared signal generator and nearest-neighbor timestamp matching.
- **Object Model Acquisition:** Each object's accurate 3D mesh was obtained using an industrial EinScan 3D scanner, represented by up to 100K triangular faces. Four markers were attached to the surface of each rigid object for mocap tracking.

#### 3.2. Data Annotating Pipeline

A fully automatic data acquisition pipeline was developed to process the raw sensor data and generate detailed annotations:
1. **Object Pose Optimization:** Object motions (6-dimensional) were tracked using the mocap system's marker positions. An optimization process, inspired by Mosh++, associated marker positions with the 3D object mesh. This involved minimizing a combined loss function that encouraged marker-to-surface contact and prevented interpenetration, yielding accurate object poses.
2. **3D Hand Keypoint Localization:**
 - **2D Keypoint Estimation:** For the initial frame, YOLOv3 detected hand bounding boxes. For subsequent frames, Track-Anything was used to track hands based on previous frames' poses, generating masks and bounding boxes. MMPose was then applied to these single-hand sub-images to estimate 2D keypoints for each hand in each camera view.
 - **3D Keypoint Fusion:** Multi-view 2D keypoints were fused into 3D using RANSAC to filter out imprecise detections and triangulation to localize 3D keypoints in the world coordinate system.
3. **Contact-Aware Hand Pose Optimization:**
 - **MANO Model:** The MANO (body model for hands) model was used to formulate 3D hand meshes. Shape parameters (β) were precomputed for each participant.
 - **Optimization Process:** An initial optimization fitted the MANO model by minimizing losses for 2D and 3D joint alignment, angle constraints (natural hand poses), and temporal smoothness. A refinement step then introduced attraction loss (L_a) to encourage hand-object contact and penetration loss (L_p) to prevent hand-object interpenetration, balancing these competing objectives.
4. **Hand-Object Segmentation:** 2D hand-object masks were generated by rendering silhouettes from the optimized 3D hand and object meshes. These masks were then refined using the Segment Anything Model (SAM), with sampled hand-object keypoints serving as prompts. Track-Anything was employed to propagate and track these masks across subsequent frames.
5. **Marker Removal:** To improve the realism of the captured objects, markers were automatically removed from the RGB images. Marker positions from the mocap system were used to render 2D masks of spheres (larger than the actual markers) in each camera view. These regions were then inpainted using a pre-trained LAMA network, replacing the markers with natural-looking object surfaces.

#### 3.3. Dataset Statistics

- **Scale:** TACO comprises 2.5K motion sequences and 5.2M video frames.
- **Diversity:** The dataset covers 20 object categories, 196 distinct object 3D models, involves 14 participants, and includes 15 daily actions. It is organized into 131 unique <tool category, action label, target object category> triplets.
- **Functional Diversity:** Tools and target objects are utilized in up to 7 different action types, ensuring varied functional manipulations.
- **Annotations:** The dataset provides high-resolution color images from 12 allocentric views and RGBD images from one egocentric view, alongside precise hand-object 3D meshes, 2D segmentation, and realistic appearances (marker-removed).

#### 3.4. Data Split for Generalization Benchmarks

The dataset was divided into a training set and four test sets to systematically evaluate generalization capabilities:
- **Test set 1 (S1 - No Generalization):** Both tool geometries and interaction triplets are present in the training set.
- **Test set 2 (S2 - Geometry-Level Generalization):** Tool geometry is novel (unseen instance), but the interaction triplet (tool category, action, target object category) is included in the training set.
- **Test set 3 (S3 - Interaction-Level Generalization):** The interaction triplet is novel, but the tool categories and geometries are seen in the training set.
- **Test set 4 (S4 - Compound Generalization):** The tool category itself is novel, leading to unseen geometries and triplets.
The training set and the four test sets were split with a ratio of 4:1:1:1.5:2.5, respectively.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the quality of the TACO dataset annotations and benchmarked existing methods on three generalizable hand-object interaction tasks.

#### 4.1. Data Quality Evaluation

- **Qualitative Contact Optimization:** Visual inspection demonstrated that the combined attraction and penetration losses used during hand pose optimization resulted in realistic hand-object contacts while largely preventing interpenetration, outperforming optimization with only one of these losses.
- **Quantitative Hand Pose Evaluation:** A cross-dataset evaluation with DexYCB was performed using two hand mesh recovery methods, CMR and MobRecon. When training on one dataset and testing on the other, performance significantly decreased, highlighting the domain gap. However, combining TACO and DexYCB data for training led to performance gains on both datasets (e.g., CMR on TACO+DexYCB improved PA-MPJPE from 10.73 to 10.25 on TACO and from 6.51 to 6.44 on DexYCB for their respective original training/testing scenarios), indicating that TACO's hand pose annotations are accurate and complementary.
- **Marker Removal Quality Evaluation:** A U-Net network trained to segment marker regions on raw images achieved a mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 63.8%. When the same network was trained and tested on marker-removed images, its performance dropped sharply to 11.1% mIoU. This reduction indicates that the inpainted regions are visually realistic and ambiguous to a segmentation network, suggesting the effectiveness of the marker removal process in enhancing realism.

#### 4.2. Benchmarking Generalizable Tasks: Compositional Action Recognition

- **Objective:** Identify action labels from egocentric RGB video frames, with optional hand/object bounding boxes, specifically when test triplets are unseen during training.
- **Baselines:** AIM (traditional action recognition) and CACNF (compositional and few-shot action recognition).
- **Results (Table 3):**
 - Accuracy significantly decreased from S1 (no generalization) to S4 (compound generalization). For CACNF, Top-1 accuracy dropped from 86.15% (S1) to 44.00% (S4), underscoring the challenge of compositional generalization.
 - Performance on S2 (novel tool geometries) was similar to S1, suggesting baseline models possess some inherent generalizability to geometric variations within known categories.
 - S3 (novel interaction triplets) showed a substantial accuracy drop compared to S1, indicating models struggle more with unfamiliar interaction contexts.
 - CACNF generally outperformed AIM on challenging sets (S3, S4), attributed to its bounding box-based approach that aims to disentangle actions from specific object/tool geometries.

#### 4.2.2. Generalizable Hand-Object Motion Forecasting

- **Objective:** Predict future 3D poses of two hands, a tool, and a target object from a short observed motion clip.
- **Baselines:** Two generative models (InterVAE, MDM) and two predictive models (InterRNN, CAHMP).
- **Results (Table 4):**
 - Generative models generally yielded larger errors compared to predictive models, particularly for hands and tools, potentially due to the rapid and complex nature of manipulation motions.
 - Methods consistently exhibited significant performance declines for the right hand (often controlling the tool) and the tool itself when tested on generalized sets (S2-S4) compared to S1.
 - The left hand and target object showed more stable performance across generalization settings.
 - This trend suggests particular difficulty in generalizing motions of dominant interaction entities. Qualitative results (Figure 17 in Appendix) showed failures in forecasting fast movements and understanding interaction intentions.

#### 4.2.3. Cooperative Grasp Synthesis

- **Objective:** Generate realistic and physically plausible right-hand grasps on a tool, considering the presence and interaction of a left hand and a target object.
- **Baselines:** ContactGen and HALO-VAE (CVAE-based approaches, modified to incorporate environment), and HALO-VAE− (HALO-VAE without environment input).
- **Results (Table 5):**
 - HALO-VAE− showed significantly worse contact and collision ratios compared to HALO-VAE, demonstrating the importance of modeling the interaction environment (left hand and target object).
 - All three approaches exhibited higher collision ratios and FID scores on novel object geometries and categories (S2, S4) compared to seen ones (S1, S3).
 - There was a decline in the ability to achieve precise tool contact and prevent penetrations in generalization scenarios.
 - Failure cases (Figure 8) highlighted challenges in generating physically feasible and natural grasps for objects with complex or slender geometries.

### 5. Significance

This research introduces TACO, a dataset that addresses a recognized gap in the study of generalizable bimanual hand-object interactions. Its contributions are multi-faceted:
- **First Large-Scale Bimanual 4D Dataset:** TACO is presented as the first large-scale, real-world 4D bimanual hand-object manipulation dataset. It provides diverse tool-action-object compositions and object geometries, which is crucial for moving beyond unimanual or single-object bimanual interaction studies.
- **Automatic Data Acquisition:** The developed automatic pipeline streamlines the complex process of capturing and annotating bimanual interactions. It ensures high-quality data, including precise hand-object meshes, 2D segmentation, and realistic visual appearances (through marker removal), which are valuable for a wide range of computer vision and robotics tasks.
- **Benchmarking Generalization:** By defining and benchmarking three distinct tasks (compositional action recognition, generalizable hand-object motion forecasting, and cooperative grasp synthesis) across four carefully designed generalization splits, TACO provides a standardized framework for evaluating model performance in novel interaction scenarios. This systematically identifies the limitations of current methodologies.
- **New Insights and Challenges:** The extensive experiments reveal that existing methods struggle significantly with generalization, especially when dealing with novel interaction triplets or dominant interaction entities (like the tool and the hand holding it). These findings highlight current research gaps and point towards new directions for developing more robust and generalizable HOI analysis and synthesis techniques.

**Potential Impact:**
The TACO dataset and its benchmarks are anticipated to have a notable impact on several research areas and applications:
- **Advancing Generalizable HOI:** It provides a rich resource for researchers to develop and test new algorithms that can understand and synthesize bimanual interactions in a generalizable manner, extending beyond specific object instances or action types.
- **Robotics and Dexterous Manipulation:** Improved understanding and synthesis of bimanual interactions can directly benefit the design and control of dexterous robotic hands and human-robot cooperative systems, enabling robots to perform more complex, adaptable tasks in human environments.
- **Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR):** Realistic hand-object interaction models are fundamental for creating immersive and intuitive VR/AR experiences. TACO can contribute to developing more accurate and natural virtual hand interactions.
- **Human Activity Recognition:** The diverse and fine-grained annotations can enhance human activity recognition systems, particularly for complex, multi-object tasks, which are common in daily life.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge three main limitations of TACO:
1. **Lack of Articulated Objects:** The dataset currently does not include articulated objects (e.g., scissors, pliers), which could introduce additional complexities in interaction modeling.
2. **Limited Scene Diversity:** While rich in object geometries and HOI behaviors, the dataset does not extensively cover diverse scene backgrounds or environmental contexts, which are also relevant for understanding human manipulations in varied real-world settings.
3. **Imperfection of Marker Removal:** The marker removal solution, relying on generative models, may not perfectly recover the original object appearances, potentially introducing subtle artifacts.
