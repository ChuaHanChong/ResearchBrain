# Towards Long-Lived Robots: Continual Learning VLA Models via Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from CASIA, UCAS, and BAAI developed LifeLong-RFT, a post-training paradigm for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that uses interaction-free reinforcement fine-tuning with a Multi-Dimensional Process Reward. This approach improves multi-task success rates by up to 8.7% in real-world tasks and significantly reduces catastrophic forgetting in continual learning while requiring 80% less data than supervised methods.

## Problem

- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), the primary adaptation method for VLA models, requires substantial task-specific data, limiting rapid adaptation in low-data scenarios.
- SFT causes catastrophic forgetting, where VLA models lose previously acquired knowledge when learning new skills, preventing continual skill accumulation.
- Existing reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) strategies for VLAs typically rely on costly online environment interaction or potentially unreliable pre-trained reward models.

## Method

- Introduced LifeLong-RFT, a post-training paradigm for VLA models that performs chunking-level on-policy reinforcement fine-tuning without requiring direct environment interaction.
- Developed Group Relative Policy Optimization (==GRPO==) to efficiently estimate advantages and optimize VLA policies by evaluating groups of sampled action chunks.
- Proposed the Multi-Dimensional Process Reward (MDPR), a verifiable and scalable reward mechanism composed of Quantized Action Consistency Reward (QACR), Continuous Trajectory Alignment Reward (CTAR), and Format Compliance Reward (FCR), to guide policy optimization.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate improvement of **8.7%** in real-world tasks (Franka robot) and **4.4%** on Google Robot in multi-task scenarios compared to SFT baselines.
- Demonstrated superior continual learning capabilities on the LIBERO benchmark, with up to **19.6%** forward transfer improvement and significantly reduced negative backward transfer (e.g., NBT of 1.5 vs SFT's 6.8).
- Adapted to new tasks using only **20%** of the training data typically required by SFT, showcasing enhanced data efficiency and faster skill acquisition.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement fine-tuning of VLA models can be effectively performed without direct environment interaction by constructing internal, verifiable reward signals from reference demonstrations.
> - A multi-dimensional reward mechanism, which assesses discrete action consistency, continuous trajectory alignment, and output format compliance, is crucial for comprehensive guidance of VLA policy optimization.
> - On-policy methods combined with chunking-level reward signals can mitigate catastrophic forgetting and enhance data efficiency in continual learning for VLA models, addressing the plasticity-stability dilemma.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards developing generalist robot policies, often achieved through Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models are typically pretrained on extensive and diverse datasets, demonstrating initial capabilities in generalization and adaptability. However, a significant challenge remains in adapting these VLA models to new, specific tasks in downstream domains. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is the prevailing method for this adaptation.

This work addresses two primary limitations of SFT in the context of VLA models:
1. **High Data Dependence**: SFT typically requires a substantial quantity of task-specific demonstration data. This requirement can hinder rapid adaptation in scenarios with limited data (e.g., few-shot learning).
2. **Catastrophic Forgetting**: When VLA models are continually fine-tuned on new tasks using SFT, they tend to degrade or entirely lose knowledge and skills acquired from previously learned tasks. This phenomenon, known as catastrophic forgetting, impedes the development of "long-lived" robotic agents capable of accumulating and retaining a broad range of skills over time.

The plasticity-stability dilemma, which describes the trade-off between acquiring new knowledge (plasticity) and retaining old knowledge (stability), is central to continual learning. While foundation models and large representations have reduced data requirements for new tasks, SFT still struggles with catastrophic forgetting. Existing continual learning techniques, often developed for specialized models, face scalability issues when applied to VLA settings with numerous tasks and high-capacity parameterization.

Reinforcement Learning (RL), particularly on-policy RL, has shown promise in Large Language Models (LLMs) for exhibiting stronger robustness to forgetting compared to SFT. This research explores applying this concept to VLA models. Previous reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) approaches for VLAs typically rely on environment-provided ground-truth rewards (often simulation-dependent) or model-based reward estimation (prone to inaccuracies and reward hacking). Both categories necessitate extensive environmental interaction, leading to high training costs and scalability limitations. Furthermore, most existing RFT methods primarily focus on optimizing performance on the fine-tuning tasks themselves, often neglecting the crucial continual learning properties necessary for long-lived agents.

This paper positions itself by proposing a novel RFT strategy that aims to mitigate SFT's data dependence and catastrophic forgetting, specifically by enabling continual learning for VLA models without requiring online environmental feedback or pre-trained reward models. It seeks to bridge the gap by designing a scalable and reliable reward signal for on-policy RFT that supports both multi-task learning and continual adaptation in a data-efficient manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a post-training paradigm for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that addresses the critical limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): substantial data dependence and susceptibility to catastrophic forgetting. The overarching motivation is to enable the evolution of VLA models into "long-lived" robotic agents capable of continually acquiring new skills with limited demonstrations while effectively preserving previously learned knowledge.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Overcoming Data Dependence for Rapid Adaptation:** SFT necessitates a large volume of task-specific demonstration data. This requirement hinders the ability of VLA models to adapt quickly to new tasks, especially in low-data or few-shot settings. The motivation is to design a fine-tuning strategy that can achieve effective adaptation with significantly fewer demonstrations, thereby increasing the efficiency and practicality of deploying VLA models in dynamic, real-world scenarios where extensive data collection is costly or infeasible.
- **Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting for Knowledge Preservation:** When VLA models are sequentially trained on new tasks using SFT, they often overwrite or degrade previously acquired skills. This catastrophic forgetting is a fundamental obstacle to creating generalist robots that can continuously expand their skill repertoire. The motivation is to develop a method that promotes stability, allowing VLA models to integrate new knowledge without compromising their existing capabilities, thereby building a cumulative and robust skill base over a robot's operational lifespan.
- **Enabling Continual Learning without Environmental Interaction:** Existing reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) methods for VLA models often rely on environment-provided rewards or complex reward models, which demand extensive and costly interactions with simulators or physical robots. This limits their scalability and applicability in real-world settings due to factors like the sim-to-real gap and the difficulty of obtaining dense, reliable rewards. The motivation is to devise an RFT strategy that is independent of online environmental feedback and pre-trained reward models. This would significantly reduce training costs, enhance scalability, and simplify deployment, making continual learning more accessible for VLA development.
- **Designing an Efficient and Verifiable Reward Signal for On-Policy RL:** The transition to on-policy reinforcement learning for VLA fine-tuning requires a robust and informative reward mechanism. Current reward signals are often too sparse, difficult to engineer, or prone to "reward hacking." The motivation is to create a multi-dimensional process reward that provides comprehensive feedback at the action chunk level, guiding policy optimization effectively. This reward mechanism should decompose the learning problem into distinct, quantifiable aspects, such as action token consistency, continuous trajectory alignment, and structural output validity, to provide detailed and actionable feedback without needing external environmental interaction.
- **Promoting Both Multi-Task Learning and Continual Learning Performance:** The ultimate goal for long-lived robots is to master multiple tasks concurrently and sequentially. SFT often performs well in multi-task settings if all data is available simultaneously, but struggles with sequential learning. The motivation is to develop a single post-training paradigm that not only demonstrates strong performance when learning multiple tasks jointly but also exhibits superior capabilities in continually learning new tasks while retaining old ones, which is crucial for long-term robot autonomy.

By addressing these motivations, the proposed LifeLong-RFT aims to advance VLA models closer to the vision of truly long-lived, adaptable, and generalist robotic agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, LifeLong-RFT, is a reinforcement fine-tuning strategy designed for VLA models to facilitate continual learning with limited data and without reliance on online environmental feedback. It integrates a chunking-level on-policy reinforcement learning algorithm with a novel multi-dimensional process reward mechanism.

**A. Chunking-Level On-Policy Reinforcement Learning**

To eliminate the need for environment interaction, LifeLong-RFT adopts an approach where individual "action chunks" sampled by the VLA model are evaluated independently, rather than relying on full trajectories and environment-provided rewards. The core reinforcement learning algorithm employed is **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.

GRPO differs from traditional algorithms like PPO by estimating advantages through group-wise comparisons of sampled outputs, thereby reducing computational overhead. The process is as follows:
1. **Sampling Action Outputs**: For a given observation (o) and language instruction (l), a group of *G* action outputs {a_i}*G*i=1 is sampled from the current policy π_θ_old(a|o,l).
2. **Reward Computation**: Corresponding rewards {r_i}*G*i=1 are computed for each sampled action output using a task-specific reward function (detailed below).
3. **Advantage Estimation**: The relative advantage A_i for each output is calculated based on the mean and standard deviation of intra-group rewards:
 A_i = (r_i - mean({r1..., r_G})) / std({r1..., r_G})
4. **Policy Optimization**: The policy parameters θ are optimized by maximizing a GRPO objective function. This objective includes a clipped policy probability ratio to stabilize training and a KL divergence regularization term (γD_KL[π_θ||π_ref]) to prevent the new policy from deviating excessively from a reference policy π_ref (often the original SFT policy).

This chunking-level, on-policy approach allows for efficient policy updates without requiring the VLA model to physically interact with an environment or a world model for reward generation.

**B. Multi-Dimensional Process Reward**

The cornerstone of LifeLong-RFT is the **multi-dimensional process reward mechanism**, which quantifies the contributions of intermediate action chunks across three dimensions to guide policy optimization without environmental interaction. The overall reward *r* is a weighted combination of these three components:
r = ω ⋅ QACR + (1 − ω) ⋅ CTAR + λ ⋅ FCR

Here, ω controls the trade-off between discrete action consistency and continuous trajectory alignment, and λ scales the importance of structural format compliance.

The three dimensions of the reward are:

1. **Quantized Action Consistency Reward (QACR)**:
 - **Purpose**: To ensure accurate action prediction within the discrete action space generated by VLM backbones.
 - **Mechanism**:
 - **Format Check**: First, it verifies if the generated action token sequence adheres to the predefined specifications (e.g., chunk size, action dimensions) of the action tokenizer (Fast+). Invalid formats receive a zero reward.
 - **Consistency Assessment**: For valid sequences, it calculates a consistency score by performing a position-wise match between the predicted action token sequence and its ground-truth counterpart. The QACR is the sum of matching tokens divided by the maximum length of the predicted or ground-truth sequence, normalized to be between 0 and 1.
 - **Formula**: QACR = (∑_ℓ=1^min(U,V) I(a_ℓ = ˜a_ℓ)) / max(U, V), if valid; 0, otherwise.

2. **Continuous Trajectory Alignment Reward (CTAR)**:
 - **Purpose**: To ensure precise control and physical execution by aligning decoded continuous action chunks with reference trajectories.
 - **Mechanism**:
 - **Format Check**: Similar to QACR, it first validates the predicted action token sequence. Invalid sequences receive a zero reward.
 - **Decoding**: Valid action tokens are decoded into a continuous action chunk *y* (sequence of *H* actions), comprising pose (y_pose) and gripper (y_grip) components at each time step.
 - **Pose Reward (r_pose_t)**: Encourages precise end-effector pose alignment. It's an exponentially decaying function of the normalized L1 distance between the predicted pose vector and the ground-truth pose (exp(−α ⋅ d_t)). Hyperparameter α regulates sensitivity to pose deviation.
 - **Gripper Reward (r_grip_t)**: Incentivizes precise gripper actuation. It's a binary reward (1 if predicted gripper state matches ground truth, 0 otherwise).
 - **Combination and Averaging**: The CTAR for an action chunk is the average of a weighted combination of pose and grip rewards over the chunk size *H*. Hyperparameter β ∈ balances the importance of pose and gripper rewards.
 - **Formula**: CTAR = (1/H) ∑_t=1^H (β ⋅ r_pose_t + (1 − β) ⋅ r_grip_t), if valid; 0, otherwise.

3. **Format Compliance Reward (FCR)**:
 - **Purpose**: To guide the model in generating structurally valid token sequences, crucial for successful decoding and execution. Generative VLA backbones can produce ill-formed outputs.
 - **Mechanism**: It is a binary reward (1 if the generated token sequence complies with the required output shape for the Fast+ tokenizer, 0 otherwise).
 - **Formula**: FCR = 1, if valid; 0, otherwise.

**C. Implementation Details**

- **Base VLA Model**: NORA-Long is adopted as the base VLA model, using the Fast+ tokenizer for action representation.
- **Optimization**: Full-parameter optimization is performed during reinforcement fine-tuning.
- **Hyperparameters**: GRPO rollout group size is 8. AdamW optimizer is used with a learning rate of 1 × 10−6. For CTAR, α = 5 and β = 0.8. For the multi-dimensional process reward, weighting coefficients are ω = 0.7 and λ = 0.1.
- **Hardware**: Experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs.

This methodological design enables LifeLong-RFT to refine VLA policies effectively by providing dense, self-generated reward signals at the action chunk level, promoting both accuracy and structural integrity of robot actions, without requiring external environmental interaction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated LifeLong-RFT across multi-task learning and continual learning scenarios in simulated (SimplerEnv, LIBERO) and real-world environments.

**A. Multi-Task Learning Experiments**

1. **Performance on SimplerEnv (Table I):**
 - LifeLong-RFT demonstrated consistent improvements over the SFT baseline (NORA-Long).
 - Average success rate increased by **3.5%** on WidowX tasks and **4.4%** on Google Robot tasks.
 - For instance, on "Put Carrot on Plate" (WidowX), success rate increased from 46.0% (SFT) to 50.2% (RFT). On "Pick Coke Can" (Google Robot), it improved from 86.0% (SFT) to 94.0% (RFT).

2. **Performance on LIBERO (Table II):**
 - LifeLong-RFT surpassed all compared continuous and discrete action models, achieving an average success rate of **95.6%**.
 - This represents a **3.8%** improvement over the NORA-Long SFT baseline.
 - Notable gains were observed on LIBERO-Goal (+4.8%) and LIBERO-Long (+6.6%), indicating effectiveness in complex and long-horizon tasks.

3. **Performance on Real-World Tasks (Table III):**
 - Evaluated on a Franka robot across four tasks (Pick Banana, Pick Bread, Pull Drawer, Hang Chinese Knot).
 - LifeLong-RFT achieved an average success rate improvement of **8.7%** over the SFT baseline (NORA-Long).
 - Specifically, for "Hang Chinese Knot," a dexterous manipulation task, it outperformed SFT by **15%**. "Pull Drawer" reached 100% success rate with LifeLong-RFT.

**B. Continual Learning Experiments**

1. **Performance on LIBERO (Table IV):**
 - **Training Protocol:** Base task stage (first six tasks of each suite, 50 demonstrations/task), followed by lifelong learning stage (remaining four tasks, 10 demonstrations/new task, 5 demonstrations/previous task for Experience Replay).
 - LifeLong-RFT consistently outperformed other methods across all task suites.
 - **LIBERO-Object:** Achieved a **11.2%** gain in Forward Transfer (FWT), a **5.3%** reduction in Negative Backward Transfer (NBT), and a **15.1%** increase in Area Under the Success Rate Curve (AUC) over the SFT baseline.
 - **LIBERO-Goal:** Demonstrated substantial superiority, achieving a **19.6%** gain in FWT, a **22.1%** reduction in NBT, and a **35.9%** gain in AUC over the SFT baseline.
 - **LIBERO-Long:** Showed gains of **13.2%** in FWT, **4.5%** in NBT reduction, and **17.2%** in AUC.
 - The results indicate effective adaptation to new tasks (higher FWT) and better preservation of prior knowledge (lower NBT), leading to improved overall performance (higher AUC).

2. **Performance on Real-World (Table V):**
 - **Training Protocol:** Sequential learning of four tasks (20 demonstrations/new task, 5 demonstrations/previous task).
 - LifeLong-RFT showed a substantial improvement of **23.7%** in FWT over the SFT baseline.
 - It yielded an NBT of **6.1**, indicating strong preservation of learned tasks, and an AUC increase of **31.7%**.
 - The model achieved an average success rate of **75.9%** across the learning cycle, demonstrating robust continual learning.

**C. Ablation Studies**

1. **Effectiveness of Multi-Dimensional Process Reward (Table VI):**
 - Experiments on LIBERO-Goal investigated the contribution of each reward component.
 - **w/o QACR**: Led to a **2.8%** average performance drop, confirming its role in accurate quantized action prediction.
 - **w/o CTAR**: Resulted in a dramatic **90.9%** average performance drop, demonstrating its critical role in enabling task completion through continuous trajectory alignment.
 - **w/o FCR**: Showed a **2.6%** average degradation, notably a **4.4%** drop in LIBERO-Long, highlighting its importance for structural output validity.
 - These results support the consistent effectiveness of each reward component.

2. **Efficiency of New Task Adaptation (Fig. 4):**
 - LifeLong-RFT consistently exhibited superior adaptation efficiency compared to SFT across representative new tasks from LIBERO suites.
 - On "Pick Orange Juice" (Object task), LifeLong-RFT achieved 100% success with 5 demonstrations, while SFT reached 98% with 50.
 - On "Pick Bowl" (Spatial task), LifeLong-RFT matched SFT's 50-demonstration performance with only 5 demonstrations and achieved 100% success with 10.
 - It also surpassed SFT by 30% on "Put Wine Bottle" (Goal task) and showed superior performance on "Put Alphabet Soup and Cream Cheese" (Long task) with the full demonstration set.

**D. Generalization across Discrete Action Models (Table XIV):**
- Tested on RoboBrain-X0 (another discrete VLA model), LifeLong-RFT showed significant superiority.
- On LIBERO-Spatial, it achieved a **12.2%** improvement in FWT and a **13.3%** reduction in NBT, indicating its generalizability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents LifeLong-RFT, a post-training strategy for VLA models that addresses two significant limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): high data requirements and catastrophic forgetting. By integrating chunking-level on-policy reinforcement learning with a multi-dimensional process reward mechanism, the work contributes to the development of more capable and robust robotic agents.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to enable VLA models to adapt efficiently to new tasks with limited demonstrations while effectively preserving previously acquired knowledge. This is a crucial step towards the realization of "long-lived" robots that can continually learn and expand their skill sets throughout their operational lifespan without needing to be retrained from scratch or losing past expertise. The proposed method's independence from online environmental feedback and pre-trained reward models is a key advantage, as it bypasses the prohibitive training costs, sim-to-real gaps, and reward engineering complexities associated with traditional reinforcement learning approaches. This contributes to making advanced robotic learning more scalable and practical for real-world deployment.

The multi-dimensional process reward mechanism, comprising the Quantized Action Consistency Reward (QACR), Continuous Trajectory Alignment Reward (CTAR), and Format Compliance Reward (FCR), offers a novel and effective way to provide dense, verifiable feedback. By quantifying heterogeneous contributions of action chunks across discrete token generation, continuous trajectory alignment, and structural output validity, it provides fine-grained guidance for policy optimization. This detailed reward structure, designed without relying on external environment interaction, represents a significant methodological advancement for reinforcement fine-tuning of VLA models.

The empirical results consistently demonstrate that LifeLong-RFT outperforms SFT-based methods in both multi-task and continual learning benchmarks across simulated and real-world environments. The notable improvements in average success rates, Forward Transfer (FWT), Negative Backward Transfer (NBT), and Area Under the Success Rate Curve (AUC) indicate enhanced adaptability to new tasks and superior knowledge retention. The demonstrated efficiency in new task adaptation, often requiring significantly fewer demonstrations than SFT, has substantial implications for reducing the data burden in robot skill acquisition.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Long-Lived Robotic Agents:** This work directly contributes to the goal of creating robots that can learn continuously over extended periods, making them more autonomous and versatile in dynamic environments.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** The ability to adapt to new tasks with limited demonstrations can significantly decrease the cost and effort associated with deploying robots in novel settings, accelerating the development and adoption of robotics in various applications.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** By mitigating catastrophic forgetting and improving adaptation, LifeLong-RFT can lead to more robust VLA models that retain a broader range of skills and generalize better across different tasks and environments.
- **Scalable Reinforcement Learning for Robotics:** The environment-independent reward mechanism offers a path towards more scalable reinforcement fine-tuning for large VLA models, sidestepping the need for extensive environmental interaction which is often a bottleneck.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The paper acknowledges that its primary focus is on discrete action models, and their performance still falls short of continuous action models in certain aspects. A key area for future research identified by the authors is extending the LifeLong-RFT training strategy to continuous action models. Such an extension could further enhance the dexterity and precision of VLA models, potentially accelerating their transition from laboratory research to industrial applications where continuous and precise control is often critical. Addressing the suboptimal performance observed on certain long-horizon tasks (e.g., LIBERO-Long Task 7 at 36% and Task 9 at 34%) with limited demonstrations also presents a direction for further exploration.
