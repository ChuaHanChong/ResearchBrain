# Deep Reinforcement Learning for Robotics: A Survey of Real-World Successes

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of real-world Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) applications in robotics evaluates existing successes, identifies enabling factors, and outlines open challenges across various robotic competencies. The research introduces a novel taxonomy and a 'Level of Real-World Success' metric, providing a structured assessment of DRL's current maturity and guiding future research directions in the field.

## Problem

- Despite DRL's theoretical promise, its transition from simulation to real-world robotic systems is hindered by the 'sim-to-real gap', high costs, and safety concerns of physical interaction.
- Previous surveys on RL in robotics lacked an explicit focus on real-world deployments and a comprehensive, modern taxonomy to assess the true maturity of DRL solutions in physical robots.
- A need existed for a realistic, up-to-date assessment of DRL's current capabilities and limitations in real-world robotics, moving beyond achievements confined to simulation or game environments.

## Method

- The authors conducted a structured survey focused exclusively on DRL papers demonstrating at least some degree of real-world success, primarily from the last five years.
- A novel, four-axis taxonomy was introduced to categorize and analyze DRL solutions: ==Robot Competencies==, Problem Formulation, Solution Approach, and a 'Level of Real-World Success' metric (0-5).
- The survey systematically reviewed DRL applications across various robotic competencies (locomotion, manipulation, navigation, HRI, multi-robot interaction), identifying common enabling factors and persistent challenges.

## Results

- DRL has enabled Level 5 (commercial deployment) solutions for quadruped locomotion, notably through robust on-policy RL and extensive domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer.
- Impressive real-world successes are demonstrated in constrained manipulation tasks (e.g., grasping, in-hand manipulation), often leveraging zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and structured task definitions.
- Significant open challenges remain in areas such as general-purpose open-world manipulation, robust and sample-efficient real-world learning without high-fidelity simulators, and scaling DRL solutions for complex human-robot and multi-robot interactions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is a dominant and effective strategy for many mature real-world DRL applications where simulation fidelity is high.
> - The use of dense, carefully shaped reward functions combined with robust on-policy reinforcement learning algorithms, particularly PPO, correlates strongly with real-world DRL successes.
> - DRL has achieved higher maturity and real-world deployment in constrained robotic domains like quadruped locomotion and specific manipulation tasks, while areas such as human-robot interaction and multi-robot systems show sparser successes due to modeling and safety complexities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL), and more recently Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with its integration of deep neural networks, has emerged as a powerful paradigm for solving complex decision-making problems. Its successes in domains like board games (e.g., AlphaGo), video games (e.g., Atari, Gran Turismo), healthcare, and recommendation systems have showcased its immense potential, particularly in handling high-dimensional observation spaces and nonlinear dynamics. The application of DRL to robotics is a natural extension, promising to enable robots to acquire sophisticated behaviors that traditional control and planning methods struggle with.

However, the transition of DRL from simulated environments to the physical world of robotics presents fundamental difficulties. Real-world robot interactions are inherently complex, costly, time-consuming, and often unsafe. This "sim-to-real gap" has historically limited DRL's widespread deployment in physical robots, with most notable DRL milestones confined to simulation or game environments where extensive trial-and-error learning is feasible. Despite these challenges, recent advancements have facilitated DRL's success in specific real-world robotic tasks, such as champion-level drone racing and versatile quadruped locomotion control integrated into commercial products.

This survey positions itself as a critical and timely contribution to this evolving landscape by addressing several key gaps in existing literature:

- **Focus on Real-World Successes:** While previous surveys on RL for robotics exist (e.g., Kober et al. pre-deep learning, Sunderhauf et al. when DRL was mostly simulation-based), they often do not explicitly prioritize or extensively evaluate *real-world* deployments. This survey specifically aims to assess the current state and open challenges of DRL for *real-world robotic applications*, identifying the underlying factors that contribute to genuine successes.
- **Novel and Comprehensive Taxonomy:** Unlike prior surveys that might focus on specific tasks or techniques, this work introduces a comprehensive taxonomy. This multi-axis categorization (robot competencies, problem formulation, solution approach, and level of real-world success) allows for a structured and holistic analysis of the DRL landscape in robotics, enabling comparisons across diverse domains and identification of broadly applicable techniques and common challenges.
- **Modern Perspective:** Given the rapid and impressive pace of DRL progress, particularly in the last five years, a refreshed analysis is essential. This survey provides an up-to-date overview, emphasizing the most recent achievements and the techniques that have enabled them, making it highly relevant to contemporary research and development.

By focusing on "real-world successes," the paper aims to shift the narrative from what DRL *can do in simulation* to *what it has demonstrably achieved in physical robots*, offering a more practical and grounded understanding of its current maturity and future potential.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this survey stems from the dual reality of Deep Reinforcement Learning in robotics: its tremendous theoretical promise contrasted with the inherent practical difficulties of deploying it in the physical world. While DRL has shown remarkable capabilities in various complex decision-making domains, its transition to real-world robotic systems is hindered by factors like the cost and safety of physical interaction, the complexity of robot dynamics, and the challenge of accurately replicating the real world in simulation. Despite these hurdles, recent breakthroughs have demonstrated DRL's ability to achieve sophisticated behaviors in actual robots, such as high-performance drone racing and adaptable quadruped locomotion. However, the maturity and applicability of DRL solutions vary significantly across different robotic tasks.

Against this backdrop, the authors establish several key objectives for their comprehensive survey:

- **To comprehensively evaluate the current progress of DRL in real-world robotic applications:** This involves a detailed examination of existing literature to understand the extent to which DRL has successfully been applied to physical robots.
- **To identify the key factors underlying those exciting successes:** By analyzing successful deployments, the survey aims to uncover common techniques, methodologies, and environmental conditions that have facilitated DRL's transition from simulation to reality.
- **To reveal underexplored areas and common open challenges:** Beyond successes, the survey seeks to highlight domains where DRL solutions are less mature or where significant fundamental problems persist, pointing to critical directions for future research.
- **To provide an overall characterization of the status of DRL in robotics:** This involves synthesizing findings across different robotic competencies to offer a holistic understanding of DRL's current capabilities and limitations in the field.
- **To offer insights for both RL practitioners and roboticists:** The survey is designed to be a valuable resource, guiding researchers in DRL towards practical robotics problems and informing roboticists about the potential and practicalities of integrating DRL into their systems to create generally capable real-world robotic systems.
- **To introduce a novel and comprehensive taxonomy:** A crucial objective is to establish a structured framework for categorizing and analyzing DRL solutions, allowing for systematic comparison and assessment of their real-world maturity.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need for a realistic assessment of DRL's impact on robotics, moving beyond theoretical achievements and simulation benchmarks to focus on verifiable successes in the physical world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a structured and comprehensive survey methodology to achieve their objectives, which is notably underpinned by a novel taxonomy designed for evaluating real-world DRL successes in robotics.

**A. Literature Selection and Focus:**
The survey primarily focuses on DRL papers that have demonstrated *at least some degree of real-world success*. This selective criterion ensures the analysis is grounded in practical applications rather than purely theoretical or simulation-based achievements. The authors emphasize reviewing papers published predominantly within the last five years, reflecting the rapid advancements in DRL and its increasing application to physical robots during this period.

**B. Novel Taxonomy for Categorization and Analysis:**
A cornerstone of this survey's methodology is its unique, four-axis taxonomy. This framework allows for a multi-faceted analysis of the literature, enabling a nuanced understanding of DRL solutions across different robotic contexts:

1. **Robot Competencies Learned with DRL:** This axis classifies papers based on the specific robotic capabilities or tasks learned and realized using DRL.
 - **Single-Robot Competencies:**
 - **Mobility:** Divided into *Locomotion* (motor skills for traversal, e.g., quadruped walking, drone flight) and *Navigation* (strategies for reaching destinations, e.g., wheeled navigation, legged navigation).
 - **Manipulation:** Divided into *Stationary Manipulation* (e.g., robot arm on fixed base) and *Mobile Manipulation* (integrating manipulation with mobility).
 - **Multi-Agent Competencies:**
 - **Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** Focuses on robot abilities to operate alongside humans.
 - **Multi-Robot Interaction:** Deals with robot interactions with groups of other robots (cooperative, adversarial, general-sum).

2. **Problem Formulation:** This axis characterizes how the robotic task is abstracted into an RL problem.
 - **Action Space:** Classified as *low-level* (joint/motor commands), *mid-level* (task-space commands), or *high-level* (temporally extended task-space commands or subroutines).
 - **Observation Space:** Categorized as *high-dimensional* (e.g., images, LiDAR) or *low-dimensional* (e.g., state vectors).
 - **Reward Function:** Distinguished as *sparse* (rewards only for terminal states) or *dense* (providing continuous feedback).

3. **Solution Approach:** This axis details the DRL techniques employed to solve the problem.
 - **Simulator Usage:** *Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer* (trained purely in sim, deployed directly), *few-shot sim-to-real transfer* (pre-trained in sim, fine-tuned in real), or *no simulator* (direct real-world learning).
 - **Model Learning:** Whether a transition dynamics model (or a part of it, like residuals) is learned from robot data.
 - **Expert Usage:** Whether human demonstrations or oracle policies are used to facilitate learning.
 - **Policy Optimization:** Type of RL algorithm: *Planning* (using a learned model), *Offline*, *Off-policy*, or *On-policy* RL.
 - **Policy/Model Representation:** Neural network architectures used: MLP, CNN, RNN, or Transformer.

4. **Level of Real-World Success (Novel Metric):** This crucial axis, inspired by autonomous driving levels and Technology Readiness Levels (TRL), quantifies the maturity of DRL solutions in real-world settings:
 - **Level 0:** Validated only in simulation.
 - **Level 1:** Validated under limited lab conditions.
 - **Level 2:** Validated under diverse lab conditions.
 - **Level 3:** Validated under confined real-world conditions.
 - **Level 4:** Validated under diverse, representative real-world operational conditions.
 - **Level 5:** Deployed on commercialized products.
 The survey considers Levels 1-5 as achieving "at least some degree of real-world success," acknowledging the subjectivity inherent in assessing success based solely on reported experimental results.

**C. Structure of Review:**
The main body of the report is organized by "Competency-Specific Review." Each competency (Locomotion, Navigation, Manipulation, Mobile Manipulation, Human-Robot Interaction, Multi-Robot Interaction) is discussed in detail. Within each section, the authors further subcategorize based on specific problems (e.g., Quadruped Locomotion, Biped Locomotion, Quadrotor Flight Control). Each subsection concludes with a summary of observed trends and persistent open challenges, complemented by tables that provide a comprehensive categorization of reviewed papers according to the defined taxonomy, highlighting their achieved level of real-world success. This systematic approach allows for both in-depth analysis of specific domains and the identification of overarching themes and challenges across the field.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's comprehensive analysis across various robotic competencies reveals several overarching trends and specific insights into the real-world successes and limitations of Deep Reinforcement Learning in robotics.

**A. General Trends in Real-World DRL Successes:**

- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer Dominance:** For the most mature (Levels 3-4) DRL solutions in robotics, zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is a prevalent strategy. This approach is particularly effective for problems where the robot's dynamics are relatively stable and easy to simulate accurately, such as terrestrial locomotion and certain navigation tasks.
- **Importance of Dense Rewards and On-Policy RL:** Success often correlates with the use of dense and carefully shaped reward functions, which simplify exploration and enhance sample efficiency. This, in turn, frequently leads to the predominant use of stable and robust model-free, on-policy algorithms like PPO, despite their higher sample complexity.
- **Constrained Domains See More Success in Manipulation:** In manipulation, DRL has been more successful in tasks with constrained action and object spaces (e.g., grasping known objects, assembly, in-hand manipulation), where dense reward functions can be designed a priori. Open-world, general-purpose manipulation (e.g., arbitrary pick-and-place) remains more challenging due to the need for generalization to diverse objects and tasks.
- **Role of Human Expertise and Offline Data:** When high-fidelity simulation is lacking or direct real-world interaction is prohibitive, human demonstrations and offline data have proven effective in bootstrapping or accelerating learning, especially in contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- **Challenges in Human-Robot and Multi-Robot Interaction:** DRL has seen sparser real-world successes in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) and Multi-Robot Interaction. This is often attributed to the difficulties in accurately modeling complex human behavior, the high cost and safety concerns of real-world multi-agent interactions, and the lack of stable and scalable multi-agent RL (MARL) algorithms.

**B. Competency-Specific Findings:**

- **Locomotion:**
 - **Quadrupeds:** DRL has achieved high maturity (Level 5, commercial deployment) for quadruped locomotion. Key enabling factors include robust on-policy RL, extensive domain randomization in simulation to bridge the sim-to-real gap, and the use of privileged learning/asymmetric actor-critic for handling unobservable terrain properties.
 - **Bipeds and Quadrotors:** DRL solutions are less mature compared to quadrupeds. Bipeds are inherently less stable and harder to control, while quadrotors are fragile and demand high precision/robustness for safety-critical flight. Hardware accessibility and cost also play a role.
- **Navigation:**
 - DRL shows promise for sub-modules like local planning and exploration. End-to-end policies can work in structured indoor environments (simulated), but integrating with explicit mapping/planning often yields better real-world performance.
 - **Challenges:** Generalization, interpretability, and safety guarantees remain issues, especially for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving, limiting widespread commercial deployment. Jointly learning navigation and low-level locomotion is a promising direction for agile robots but complicates training stability.
- **Manipulation:**
 - **Grasping, In-Hand, Assembly:** Demonstrated impressive real-world successes, often leveraging zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and structured task definitions.
 - **Open-World Manipulation:** Still challenging due to object diversity, long horizons, and the difficulty of accurate contact simulation. Innovations in multi-task learning, sample efficiency, and reward learning are ongoing.
- **Mobile Manipulation (MoMa):**
 - Initial successes primarily on short-horizon, interactive tasks. Training heavily relies on simulation due to large workspaces and safety concerns in real-world exploration.
 - Challenges include selecting appropriate action spaces for diverse morphologies and effectively handling long-horizon tasks and partial observability.
- **Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):**
 - Limited real-world success, particularly in physical collaboration, due to the complexity of human modeling and the cost of interaction data.
 - Approaches include training in simplified real-world scenarios, learning human models for simulation, or hardcoding human behaviors.
- **Multi-Robot Interaction:**
 - Mostly successful in cooperative collision avoidance and specific instances like robot soccer.
 - Key challenges are scalability with increasing robot numbers, communication, convergence stability, and generalization beyond cooperative settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey, "Deep Reinforcement Learning for Robotics: A Survey of Real-World Successes," holds significant importance for the robotics and artificial intelligence communities. It serves as a vital compass, charting the current landscape of DRL applications in physical robots and illuminating the pathways toward more robust and generally capable robotic systems.

**A. Comprehensive Roadmap and Assessment:**
The paper provides an invaluable, up-to-date roadmap for both DRL practitioners and roboticists. By systematically categorizing and analyzing DRL successes across various robotic competencies (locomotion, navigation, manipulation, mobile manipulation, HRI, multi-robot interaction) and technical dimensions (problem formulation, solution approach, and real-world success levels), it offers a holistic and practical overview. This structured assessment helps researchers understand where DRL has genuinely excelled in the real world and, critically, why. The introduction of the "Level of Real-World Success" metric is a particularly significant contribution, providing a much-needed framework for evaluating and comparing the maturity of DRL solutions beyond purely simulated benchmarks.

**B. Identification of Key Success Factors:**
The survey clearly identifies recurring factors that underpin real-world DRL successes. These include the judicious application of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer when simulation fidelity is adequate, the design of dense and well-shaped reward functions to facilitate learning, and the preference for stable, robust on-policy RL algorithms in many domains. This insight allows future research to focus on refining these proven strategies or addressing the conditions where they fall short.

**C. Articulation of Critical Open Challenges:**
Beyond celebrating successes, the survey meticulously outlines the major unresolved challenges that impede DRL's broader adoption in real-world robotics:

1. **Algorithmic Stability and Sample Efficiency:** The need for more robust and sample-efficient RL algorithms, particularly off-policy and offline methods, is paramount for costly real-world interactions and long-horizon tasks. Scalability and stability for multi-robot systems also remain a hurdle.
2. **Robust Real-World Learning:** Developing mechanisms for safe and efficient real-world data collection (e.g., automated resets, safe exploration strategies) and methods for targeted real-world fine-tuning (e.g., adapting perception modules or learning residual dynamics) are crucial where simulation is insufficient.
3. **Long-Horizon Task Solving:** Composing and sequencing learned skills, along with principled methods for skill discovery (e.g., goal-conditioned, unsupervised RL) and combination (e.g., hierarchical RL, planning), are essential for tackling complex, multi-stage robotic tasks.
4. **Principled System Design:** Moving beyond heuristic choices in reward design, action space selection, and the integration of learned components with classical control and planning methods is necessary for scalable and reliable DRL system development.
5. **Standardized Benchmarking:** The call for standardized evaluation protocols and benchmarks for real-world performance is critical to enable accurate comparisons and track progress, especially for complex, open-world problems.
6. **Leveraging Foundation Models:** The survey anticipates the transformative potential of integrating large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) with DRL, foreseeing opportunities for automated reward design, domain randomization, and language-conditioned policies, which could dramatically enhance generalization and task specification.

**D. Guiding Future Research and Development:**
By clearly delineating successes and challenges, the survey provides a clear direction for future research. It encourages investigations into fundamental algorithmic improvements, procedural innovations for real-world learning, and holistic approaches for integrating diverse robotic competencies. The emphasis on practical deployment ensures that future DRL research in robotics remains grounded in solving real-world problems.

In conclusion, this survey is a landmark publication that moves beyond the hype of DRL to provide a sober yet optimistic assessment of its real-world impact on robotics. Its detailed analysis and forward-looking perspective will undoubtedly shape research agendas, foster collaboration between RL and robotics communities, and accelerate the development of truly intelligent, adaptive, and deployable robotic systems in the years to come.
