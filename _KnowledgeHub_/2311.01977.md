# RT-Trajectory: Robotic Task Generalization via Hindsight Trajectory Sketches

> [!summary] Summary
> RT-Trajectory conditions a robot manipulation policy on coarse 2D trajectory sketches drawn over the camera image, rather than language or goal images. Hindsight sketches of end-effector paths, with color-graded time and height plus gripper interaction markers, are auto-extracted from existing demonstrations and fed to an RT-1 transformer trained by behavior cloning. It reaches 67% success on seven unseen skills versus 26% for the best baseline.

## Problem

- One-hot task conditioning cannot express similarity between tasks, so policies gain no leverage from motions shared across different task labels.
- Language conditioning lacks motion specificity: a policy trained on pick-and-place fails at folding even when the required arm motion closely resembles training motions.
- Goal-image and video conditioning over-specify end states or whole trajectories, are hard to learn from, and are impractical to provide at test time.

## Method

- ==Hindsight trajectory labeling== projects recorded end-effector 3D positions into camera pixels via known extrinsics/intrinsics, auto-generating sketch conditioning from existing demonstrations without manual annotation. This forms **RT-Trajectory**.
- Sketch encoding uses the red channel for normalized timestep, the green channel for normalized gripper height (the 2.5D variant), and green/blue circles as grasp/release interaction markers.
- Policy is ==RT-1== with ==FiLM== language layers removed; the sketch is concatenated channel-wise to each of six history RGB frames, with new first-conv weights zero-initialized.

## Results

- Across 64 trials on seven unseen skills, RT-Trajectory scores **50%** (2D) and **67%** (2.5D) versus RT-1 **17%**, RT-2 **11%**, and goal-conditioned RT-1-Goal **26%**.
- Height cues matter most where depth is ambiguous: Pick from Chair rises from **0%** (2D) to **38%** (2.5D) and Swivel Chair from **0%** to **70%**.
- Sketches from out-of-distribution sources transfer: from human videos, **94-100%** on Pick and **75%** on Fold Towel versus an IK planner's **42%** and **25%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mid-level motion-centric conditioning generalizes better than either under-specified language or over-specified goal images, since motion similarity is what the policy can actually interpolate over.
> - Rendering conditioning as an image lets extra scalar channels (time, height) ride along free, and admits sketches from humans, videos, code-writing LLMs, or generative models.
> - Visual prompt engineering can substitute for retraining when a policy fails, but requires a calibrated static camera and a stationary base, limiting mobile whole-body use.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Generalization remains one of the most critical and challenging aspects of developing robust robot learning systems. The ideal robot policy should not only perform well on tasks it has been explicitly trained on but also adapt seamlessly to novel objects, scenes, and, crucially, novel motions or tasks not directly represented in its training dataset. The current reliance on vast, exhaustive datasets for every conceivable scenario is impractical and prohibitive, highlighting the need for policies that can generalize broadly.

Historically, various approaches to task specification and policy conditioning have been explored, each with its own strengths and limitations:
- **One-hot task conditioning:** This early method assigns a unique identifier to each task. While simple, it fundamentally lacks the ability to capture similarities between tasks, severely limiting generalization to unseen tasks.
- **Language conditioning:** Represented by recent successes like RT-1 and RT-2, language conditioning has significantly improved generalization to novel language commands and semantic concepts. However, it suffers from a lack of specificity for complex or precise motions. A language-conditioned policy trained on "pick-and-place" might struggle with "folding" even if the underlying arm trajectory is similar, because the new task falls outside its semantic language space. Describing intricate motions purely through language can be difficult and ambiguous.
- **Goal image or video conditioning:** These methods offer the promise of capturing nuanced visual details that are hard to verbalize. A goal image defines the desired end-state, while a video shows the entire desired trajectory of states. However, learning from high-dimensional visual inputs is notoriously challenging and data-intensive. Moreover, providing precise goal images or videos at test time for novel situations can be cumbersome and sensitive to task-irrelevant visual factors like background changes, making them less practical for broad deployment. Goal images can also be "over-specified," defining irrelevant visual aspects alongside the crucial ones.

This paper addresses a critical gap: the need for a conditioning modality that is both **expressive enough** to convey detailed motion guidance and **practical enough** for easy specification, while fundamentally enabling **better generalization to novel tasks and motions**. The authors observe that while existing methods focus on semantic or visual generalization, "task generalization" – particularly when new motions are involved – remains a significant hurdle. RT-Trajectory seeks to bridge this gap by proposing *coarse trajectory sketches* as a middle-ground solution. By explicitly representing the robot's end-effector motion, the policy can leverage similarities between different movements, even if the semantic task description is new. This approach directly tackles the challenge of generalizing to new motions by providing a modality that is intrinsically motion-centric, unlike language, and less cumbersome than full video demonstrations, unlike goal-image/video conditioning. It offers a "lightweight coarse level of state-specification" that aims to strike a balance between sufficient detail and learnability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enhance the generalization capabilities of robotic policies to novel tasks and motions** that extend significantly beyond their training data. The authors are specifically motivated by the limitations of existing policy conditioning mechanisms in addressing this particular challenge.

The core motivation and key objectives can be summarized as follows:
- **Overcome limitations of existing conditioning methods:** The paper highlights that while language conditioning (e.g., RT-1, RT-2) excels at semantic generalization, it struggles with novel motions that are difficult to describe verbally. Goal-image or video conditioning, while visually rich, is often difficult to learn from and impractical to specify for new tasks. The authors seek a modality that provides a better balance.
- **Propose a novel, effective conditioning signal:** The central objective is to introduce and validate the use of "rough trajectory sketches" as a novel conditioning signal for robotic policies. This modality, termed "RT-Trajectory," is envisioned to be practical, easy to specify, and powerful enough to guide policies through new tasks.
- **Enable generalization to novel motions:** A key goal is to demonstrate that conditioning on trajectory sketches can enable the policy to perform tasks requiring motions it has not explicitly seen during training, even if those motions are conceptually similar to components of seen tasks. This is a crucial step towards true generalist robot policies.
- **Strike a balance between detail and flexibility:** The authors aim for trajectory sketches to be "detailed enough to express low-level motion-centric guidance" while simultaneously being "coarse enough to allow the learned policy to interpret the trajectory sketch in the context of situational visual observations." This balance is critical for both effectiveness and robustness.
- **Develop a practical and versatile interface:** Another objective is to show that trajectory sketches can serve as a useful, human-interpretable interface for communicating with robots. This includes demonstrating methods for specifying these sketches through simple human inputs (drawings, videos) and automated generation methods (LLMs, image-generating models).
- **Demonstrate real-world applicability at scale:** The research explicitly aims to evaluate RT-Trajectory on a variety of real-world robotic manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against state-of-the-art baselines using the same training data to validate its superior generalization capabilities.

In essence, the authors are motivated to design a policy conditioning method that is inherently motion-aware, practical for humans, compatible with modern AI tools, and capable of unlocking significantly broader task generalization for robots in complex, unseen environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RT-Trajectory framework proposes a novel policy conditioning method using coarse trajectory sketches. The methodology involves a two-stage process: training and inference, supported by specific architectural modifications and diverse sketch generation techniques.

#### 3.1. Overview

The core idea is to train a robotic control policy to interpret and execute tasks specified by 2D trajectory sketch images. During training, the policy learns from existing robot demonstration datasets, where 2D trajectory labels are automatically extracted. During inference, a user or high-level planner provides a rough 2D trajectory sketch, which the trained policy then uses to perform the desired manipulation task.

#### 3.2. Hindsight Trajectory Labels (Training Data Acquisition)

To scale the approach and reuse existing datasets, the authors develop a method for automatically extracting "hindsight trajectory labels" from demonstration episodes. This involves three basic elements:
- **2D Trajectory:** For each episode, the 3D position of the robot end-effector is extracted and projected onto the camera's 2D field of view, assuming a stationary robot base and calibrated camera. These projected 2D points are connected to form a curve on a blank image.
- **Color Grading:** To encode temporal information (relative motion) and, optionally, height:
 - The red channel of the trajectory image specifies the normalized time step, indicating temporal progression.
 - For **RT-Trajectory (2.5D)**, the green channel encodes the normalized height of the end-effector relative to the robot base, adding depth information to the 2D sketch.
- **Interaction Markers:** Crucial moments of gripper interaction (grasping and releasing) are highlighted. These are identified by monitoring changes in sensed vs. target gripper joint positions. Green circles are drawn at the 2D end-effector points for grasping, and blue circles for releasing.

Based on these elements, two main trajectory representations are proposed:
- **RT-Trajectory (2D):** An RGB image containing the 2D trajectory with temporal information (red channel) and interaction markers.
- **RT-Trajectory (2.5D):** Extends RT-Trajectory (2D) by incorporating height information into the green channel, providing a more detailed, depth-aware sketch.

#### 3.3. Policy Training

The policy is trained using **Imitation Learning** and **Behavior Cloning**, a widely successful approach for multitask robotic manipulation.
- **Dataset:** A collection of successful robot demonstration episodes, each comprising observations (RGB images from the head camera, hindsight trajectory sketch) and actions.
- **Architecture:** The authors leverage a **Transformer-based control policy**, specifically modifying the existing RT-1 framework.
 - **Conditioning Integration:** The trajectory sketch is concatenated with each RGB image along the feature dimension in the input sequence (a history of 6 images). This combined input is then processed by an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B3 image tokenizer.
 - **Modifications to RT-1:** Since language instructions are not used, the FiLM layers originally designed for language conditioning in RT-1 are removed. New weights in the first convolution layer for the additional input channels (from trajectory sketch) are initialized with zeros.
- **Learning Objective:** The policy learns to minimize the log-likelihood of predicting the demonstrated actions given the input image and trajectory sketch.

#### 3.4. Trajectory Conditioning During Inference

During inference, various methods can be used to generate the required trajectory sketch:
- **Human-drawn Sketches:** Users can draw sketches directly onto an initial robot camera image using a simple graphical user interface (GUI). This allows for intuitive and direct specification of desired motions, including interaction markers and simple height annotations.
- **Human Demonstration Videos with Hand-object Interaction:** First-person human demonstration videos can be converted into robot trajectory sketches. Human hand poses are estimated from video frames using tools like Mediapipe, lifted to 3D, converted to robot end-effector poses, and then used to generate a sketch.
- **Prompting LLMs with Code as Policies (CaP):** Large Language Models (LLMs) can be prompted to write code that generates a series of 3D robot waypoints, given task instructions and object labels. These waypoints can then be processed to draw a trajectory sketch.
- **Image Generation Models:** Text-guided image generation models (e.g., PaLM-E style VLMs) can generate trajectory sketches. Given an initial image and a language instruction, the VLM outputs vector-quantized tokens representing the trajectory image, which are then de-tokenized into 2D pixel coordinates for the sketch.

#### 3.5. Measuring Motion Generalization

To quantitatively understand generalization to novel motions, the authors utilize the **discrete Fréchet distance** to measure the similarity between two end-effector motion trajectories. This metric is suitable because it is order-preserving and parameterization-independent, reflecting how similar the overall shapes and temporal sequence of two curves are. By comparing evaluation trajectories to the most similar ones in the training dataset, they can gauge the "novelty" of the required motions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on a real mobile manipulator robot from Everyday Robots, utilizing a 7-degree-of-freedom arm and a two-fingered gripper.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Training Data:** The RT-1 demonstration dataset was used, comprising approximately 73,000 real robot demonstrations across 542 distinct tasks, covering 8 manipulation skills (e.g., "Pick Object," "Open Drawer") operating on 17 household kitchen items.
- **Unseen Skills:** To evaluate generalization, 7 novel skills were introduced (e.g., "Place Fruit," "Fold Towel," "Pick from Chair," "Swivel Chair"). These tasks included unseen objects, manipulation workspaces, deformable objects, and scenarios requiring combinations of seen skills or entirely new motions (e.g., moving objects within a drawer at a different height than tabletop motions). A total of 64 trials were conducted across these 7 scenarios.
- **Baselines:** RT-Trajectory was compared against:
 - **RT-1:** A language-conditioned policy trained on the same data.
 - **RT-2:** A language-conditioned policy trained on a mixture of the training data and internet-scale Vision-Question Answering (VQA) data.
 - **RT-1-Goal:** A goal-image conditioned policy trained on the same data.

#### 4.2. Unseen Task Generalization

The results demonstrated a significant improvement in generalization for RT-Trajectory methods:
- **RT-Trajectory (2.5D)** achieved an overall success rate of **67%**.
- **RT-Trajectory (2D)** achieved an overall success rate of **50%**.
- In contrast, the baselines performed considerably worse: **RT-1 (16.7%)**, **RT-2 (11.1%)**, and **RT-1-Goal (26%)**.
The superior performance of RT-Trajectory (2.5D) over RT-Trajectory (2D) was particularly evident in tasks where height information was crucial, such as "Pick from Chair," indicating the value of the added depth cue. Language-conditioned policies struggled because new tasks often involved semantically unseen instructions, even if the underlying motions had similarities to training data. Goal-conditioned policies showed some improvement over language but were noted as harder to acquire and sensitive to irrelevant visual factors.

#### 4.3. Diverse Trajectory Generation Methods

RT-Trajectory's ability to generalize to different input modalities for generating sketches was also evaluated:
- **Human Demonstration Videos:** When trajectory sketches were derived from human demonstration videos, RT-Trajectory (2.5D) achieved 100% success for "Pick" and 75% for "Fold Towel," significantly outperforming a non-learning Inverse Kinematics (IK) Planner (42% and 25% respectively). This showed robustness to "squigglier", less precise human-generated trajectories.
- **Prompting LLMs with Code as Policies (CaP):** For LLM-generated trajectories (which tend to be precise and linear), RT-Trajectory (2.5D) achieved 89% success for "Pick" and 60% for "Open Drawer." While the IK Planner performed competitively on "Open Drawer" (71%), RT-Trajectory's ability to adapt motion to scene nuances (like object orientation) in diverse pick tasks was a notable advantage.
- **Image Generation Models:** Qualitative results demonstrated that even with noisy trajectory sketches generated by text-guided image models, RT-Trajectory could still perform reasonably, suggesting promising future capabilities as these generation models improve.

#### 4.4. Emergent Capabilities

Several emergent behaviors and capabilities were observed:
- **Visual Prompt Engineering:** RT-Trajectory enabled a novel form of "visual prompt engineering." By modifying the trajectory sketch (e.g., drawing a higher peak for a placement task), robot behavior could be predictably altered, often turning failure into success without retraining. This mirrors the concept of prompt engineering in LLMs and offers a new paradigm for debugging and fine-tuning robot policies.
- **Generalization to Realistic Settings:** Qualitative evaluations in new buildings and rooms (with novel backgrounds, lighting, objects, layouts, and furniture geometries) demonstrated RT-Trajectory's robustness to simultaneous physical and visual distribution shifts. With minimal prompt engineering, it successfully performed tasks requiring novel motions in highly out-of-distribution real-world scenarios.
- **Retry Behavior:** The policy exhibited intelligent retry behavior. For example, if an initial attempt to open a drawer by grasping the handle failed, the robot might autonomously adjust its grasp to the drawer's edge and succeed.
- **Height-Aware Disambiguation (RT-Trajectory 2.5D):** The added height information in RT-Trajectory (2.5D) effectively resolved ambiguities inherent in 2D sketches, allowing the robot to correctly infer whether to move to a deeper or higher position, crucial for precision tasks.

#### 4.5. Measuring Motion Generalization

Analysis using the discrete Fréchet distance showed that while some unseen tasks had trajectories surprisingly similar to training examples (e.g., "place a fruit into a tall bowl" resembling "move X near Y"), many others required motions significantly different from any single training trajectory. Even visually similar trajectories could differ in crucial factors like z-height or semantic relevance, confirming that the unseen skills indeed necessitated a blend of interpolating seen motions and generalizing to entirely novel ones.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of RT-Trajectory marks a significant advancement in the pursuit of generalist robot policies, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of task generalization to novel motions.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Paradigm Shift in Task Specification:** RT-Trajectory proposes a novel and highly effective modality for task specification: coarse trajectory sketches. This method strikes a crucial balance between the ambiguity of language commands and the impracticality/over-specification of goal images/videos. By providing explicit, motion-centric guidance, it directly addresses the problem of generalizing to new physical motions, a gap largely left by prior work focusing on semantic or visual generalization.
- **Superior Generalization Performance:** The empirical results are compelling, demonstrating that RT-Trajectory, especially its 2.5D variant, significantly outperforms state-of-the-art language- and goal-conditioned policies on a diverse set of unseen, real-world manipulation tasks. This suggests a fundamental improvement in how robots can adapt to novel task requirements.
- **Practicality and Accessibility:** The framework emphasizes practicality through hindsight trajectory labeling (enabling the reuse of existing datasets) and versatile inference methods. The ability for humans to intuitively draw sketches, coupled with compatibility with automated generation tools like LLMs and image models, makes RT-Trajectory a highly accessible and flexible interface for robot control.
- **Emergent Capabilities and New Research Avenues:** The observed emergent behaviors, such as "visual prompt engineering" and robust generalization to complex, simultaneous distribution shifts, open new avenues for research and development. Visual prompt engineering offers a novel, intuitive way for users to interact with and refine robot behaviors without requiring policy retraining, akin to the impact of prompt engineering in large language models. The ability to perform in "in the wild" realistic settings underlines its potential for real-world deployment.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Accelerated Robot Deployment:** By enabling robots to generalize more broadly to new tasks with simpler, intuitive conditioning, RT-Trajectory could significantly lower the barrier to deploying robots in diverse, less structured environments, from household assistance to logistics and manufacturing.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** The ability to generalize from existing datasets and abstract new motions from rough sketches could reduce the immense data collection burden typically associated with training robust robot policies for every conceivable task.
- **More Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:** The human-interpretable nature of trajectory sketches could foster more natural and effective collaboration between humans and robots, where users can quickly convey desired actions without complex programming or precise demonstrations.
- **Foundation for Future Generalist Robots:** This work contributes a crucial piece to the puzzle of building truly generalist robots. By providing a robust mechanism for task generalization beyond semantic understanding, it complements existing advancements in visual perception and language comprehension, paving the way for more versatile and autonomous robotic systems.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors openly acknowledge several limitations:
- **Stationary Robot and End-Effector Control:** Current work assumes a stationary robot and focuses solely on end-effector manipulation. Extending RT-Trajectory to mobile manipulation scenarios with whole-body control is identified as a promising future direction, which would significantly expand its applicability.
- **Lack of Explicit Constraint Specification:** The current trajectory sketches provide guidance but do not allow for explicit spatial constraints (e.g., "avoid this fragile object"). Integrating such constraint specification into the trajectory sketch interface is an interesting area for future research, enhancing safety and precision.
- **Dependency on Image Generation Quality:** While promising, automated sketch generation via image models is currently noisy. Continuous improvements in these foundation models will naturally enhance the performance and reliability of RT-Trajectory in such scenarios.

Despite these limitations, RT-Trajectory represents a substantial leap forward in robotic task generalization, offering a practical, expressive, and highly effective framework that promises to accelerate the development of more adaptable and intelligent robotic systems.
