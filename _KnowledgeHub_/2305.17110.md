# IndustReal: Transferring Contact-Rich Assembly Tasks from Simulation to Reality

> [!summary] Summary
> The IndustReal framework, a collaboration between NVIDIA, USC, and Stanford, enables robust sim-to-real transfer for complex, contact-rich robotic assembly tasks. It successfully performs end-to-end assembly of industrial parts, demonstrating up to 100% success for unseen connectors without real-world policy adaptation or fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic assembly systems are expensive, brittle, and lack adaptability to variations, relying on high-precision hardware and meticulously pre-programmed trajectories.
- Achieving sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich robotic assembly is challenging due to the "reality gap," complex contact dynamics, and the need for long-horizon sequential decision-making.
- Prior sim-to-real methods for assembly often focus on simplified sub-tasks, require large clearances, or necessitate real-world policy fine-tuning, force/torque sensors, or human demonstrations.

## Method

- A comprehensive framework leveraging ==GPU-accelerated simulation== (NVIDIA Factory within Isaac Gym) and ==Reinforcement Learning== to train policies for ==end-to-end assembly== tasks.
- Novel simulation-based algorithms, including ==Simulation-Aware Policy Update (SAPU)==, ==SDF-Based Dense Rewards==, and ==Sampling-Based Curriculum (SBC)==, are introduced to address specific learning challenges in contact-rich environments.
- The ==Policy-Level Action Integrator (PLAI)== is developed to minimize steady-state errors caused by unmodeled dynamics during real-world deployment on a standard Franka Panda robot and Intel RealSense camera.

## Results

- The combination of SAPU, SDF-Based Dense Reward, and SBC algorithms significantly boosted simulation insertion success rates to **82.0-88.6%** for various industrial parts, outperforming baseline methods.
- The Policy-Level Action Integrator (PLAI) reduced real-world steady-state position errors to approximately **2 mm**, demonstrating robust mitigation of unmodeled dynamics at deployment.
- The system achieved high end-to-end real-world assembly success rates of **80%** for pegs, **97.5%** for gears, and **100%** for NEMA connectors, including generalization to unseen objects without any real-world policy fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively preventing agents from exploiting simulator inaccuracies like spurious interpenetrations during training, via Simulation-Aware Policy Update, is crucial for learning policies that transfer robustly to reality.
> - Utilizing Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) to provide dense reward signals offers a superior and robust metric for complex geometric alignment in contact-rich assembly tasks.
> - Integrating policy actions relative to the last desired state at deployment time (PLAI) effectively mitigates unmodeled real-world dynamics, reducing steady-state errors without requiring real-world adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

**Challenges in Robotic Assembly:**
Robotic assembly stands as a formidable challenge in robotics, demanding high precision, accuracy, and robust contact-rich interactions. Traditional industrial robotic assembly systems typically rely on expensive, high-precision hardware, meticulously engineered fixtures, and pre-programmed trajectories, often lacking adaptivity to variations in parts, poses, or environments. Such solutions are time-consuming to develop, costly to deploy, and highly sensitive to perturbations, limiting their applicability in high-mix, low-volume manufacturing settings. The longstanding goal in the field is to imbue robots with greater intelligence, enabling them to adapt to unmodeled conditions and perform complex assembly tasks without extensive human intervention.

**Role of Simulation and the Reality Gap:**
Simulation has proven to be an indispensable tool in many engineering disciplines, from finite element analysis to integrated circuit design. In robotics, simulators are crucial for developing algorithms, generating vast datasets for machine learning, and training intelligent agents in safe and parallelizable environments. However, simulation has had a comparatively limited impact on robotic assembly due to several factors:
1. **Complexity of Contact Dynamics:** Accurate simulation of contact-rich interactions involving geometrically complex parts requires sophisticated physics engines capable of handling thousands of contacts per rigid body pair and solving non-linear complementarity problems (NCPs) efficiently. This has only recently become feasible with advancements in GPU-accelerated simulators like NVIDIA Isaac Gym and its Factory framework.
2. **Long-Horizon Sequential Decision-Making:** Assembly tasks often involve long sequences of precise actions, making them challenging for data-driven methods like reinforcement learning (RL), which have high sample complexity. This necessitates faster-than-real-time simulation capabilities.
3. **The "Reality Gap":** Despite advances, simulation models inevitably differ from reality due to inherent modeling limitations, inaccurate parameters, and numerical artifacts. This "reality gap" is particularly pronounced for contact-rich tasks, where even minor discrepancies in friction or stiffness can lead to vastly different real-world behaviors.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer Methods:**
To bridge the reality gap, researchers have developed sim-to-real transfer methods, including system identification and domain randomization. These techniques have shown remarkable success in areas like robot locomotion and general manipulation. However, their application to contact-rich *assembly* tasks has been scarce and often comes with significant caveats. Prior sim-to-real efforts for assembly typically involved:
- Simulators like MuJoCo or PyBullet, often requiring simplified geometries.
- Focus primarily on simpler tasks like peg-in-hole insertion, often with large clearances (≥1mm) or large parts.
- Reliance on force/torque (F/T) sensors, human demonstrations, baseline motion plans, or a policy fine-tuning phase in the real world.
- Assumption of pre-grasped objects, rather than tackling the full assembly sequence from detection to insertion.

**Positioning IndustReal:**
IndustReal directly addresses these limitations and fits into the broader landscape by pushing the boundaries of sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich assembly. It distinguishes itself by:
- **End-to-End Assembly:** It is one of the first systems to demonstrate RL-based sim-to-real transfer for the *entire* assembly sequence—detection, grasping, part alignment, and insertion—without requiring any real-world policy adaptation or fine-tuning.
- **Realistic Challenges:** It uses a high-performance, GPU-accelerated simulator (Factory within Isaac Gym) capable of accurately modeling complex contact dynamics. It employs commonly used, less precise hardware (Franka Panda robot, Intel RealSense D435 camera) and realistic part clearances (≤0.5-0.6mm), reflecting actual industrial tolerances. It explicitly avoids F/T sensors, human demonstrations, baseline plans, and dynamics randomization.
- **Novel Algorithmic Contributions:** The paper introduces new algorithms specifically designed to overcome the challenges of learning contact-rich tasks in simulation and transferring them to reality effectively.
- **Reproducibility:** A significant contribution is the provision of open-source hardware (IndustRealKit) and software (IndustRealLib) to encourage reproducibility and further research, a crucial aspect often missing in complex robotics projects.

By tackling the full assembly pipeline with realistic constraints and demonstrating robust transfer without real-world adaptation, IndustReal represents a significant step towards enabling more intelligent, adaptive, and cost-effective robotic assembly solutions in manufacturing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of IndustReal is to enable robotic systems to successfully perform complex, contact-rich assembly tasks by leveraging simulation-based reinforcement learning and achieving robust policy transfer to the real world.

The key motivations driving this research are rooted in the limitations of current robotic assembly methods and the untapped potential of advanced simulation:

1. **Overcoming Traditional Assembly Challenges:** Current industrial assembly solutions are typically expensive, labor-intensive (due to meticulous engineering), and brittle (sensitive to perturbations). There is a critical need for adaptive, intelligent robotic systems that can handle diverse parts and environments without extensive re-engineering.
2. **Unlocking Simulation's Potential for Assembly:** While simulation is powerful in other domains, its impact on robotic assembly has been constrained by the difficulty of accurately simulating contact-rich interactions and the high computational demands of training data-driven policies for long-horizon tasks. The recent advent of high-performance, GPU-accelerated physics simulators (like Isaac Gym with Factory) provides an opportunity to overcome these technical barriers.
3. **Bridging the Reality Gap for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The "reality gap" is particularly challenging for contact-rich tasks, as small modeling inaccuracies can lead to large discrepancies in real-world performance. A core motivation is to develop systematic approaches that facilitate reliable sim-to-real transfer specifically for these types of tasks, minimizing the need for real-world fine-tuning or extensive domain randomization.
4. **Enabling End-to-End Assembly:** Most prior sim-to-real efforts for assembly have focused on isolated sub-problems, such as insertion, assuming objects are pre-grasped and positioned. IndustReal aims to solve the complete end-to-end assembly problem, from part detection and grasping to alignment and insertion, reflecting a more realistic and practical application scenario.
5. **Promoting Reproducibility and Community Engagement:** A significant objective is to provide a complete, reproducible system (hardware designs, software libraries, trained policies) to lower the barrier for other researchers to build upon this work, benchmark new algorithms, and accelerate progress in robotic assembly.

In essence, IndustReal seeks to develop a robust, intelligent, and deployable robotic assembly system that overcomes the precision and adaptivity limitations of current methods by harnessing the power of advanced simulation and novel algorithms for effective sim-to-real transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

IndustReal proposes a comprehensive set of algorithms, systems, and tools designed to solve contact-rich assembly tasks in simulation using Reinforcement Learning (RL) and achieve successful transfer to the real world. The approach is systematically broken down into policy learning in simulation and policy deployment in the real world.

**Problem Setup and Decomposition:**
The system uses a Franka Emika Panda robot with a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera. The tasks involve assembling industrial-style parts, inspired by the NIST Task Board 1, specifically pegs-and-holes, gears-and-gearshafts, and connectors-and-receptacles, all with realistic clearances (≤0.5-0.6mm). The parts have randomized 3-DOF poses, with sockets bolted to a breadboard to mimic industrial fixturing.

The overall assembly problem is decomposed into three sequential phases for each part type:
1. **Pick:** Robot grasps a randomly positioned plug.
2. **Place:** Robot transports the grasped plug close to its corresponding socket.
3. **Insert:** Robot brings the plug into contact and inserts it, aligning parts as needed.

Policies are trained for Reach (for initial movement), Pick, Place, and Insert using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) within the NVIDIA Factory simulation framework, which runs on Isaac Gym for high-performance GPU-based physics simulation.

**Simulation-Based Policy Learning (Algorithms for Contact-Rich RL):**
The authors identified that general RL strategies were insufficient for the complex "Insert" phase (achieving only ~12% success rates), leading to the development of three key algorithms:

1. **Simulation-Aware Policy Update (SAPU):**
 - **Motivation:** In contact-rich simulators, spurious interpenetrations between assets are common, especially in real-time. RL agents can exploit these inaccuracies to maximize reward, leading to policies that fail in the real world.
 - **Approach:** SAPU encourages policies to avoid interpenetrations. A GPU-based module (using Warp) checks for maximum interpenetration depth between plug and socket meshes at each episode. This depth is then used to filter and/or weight the cumulative reward during policy updates. The "Filter and Weight" strategy (discarding episodes with interpenetration above a threshold, and weighting others inversely to their depth) proved most effective.
 - **Impact:** Significantly improved success rates and learned policies that avoided unrealistic simulator behaviors, boosting performance from <40% to ~85% for realistic interpenetration thresholds.

2. **SDF-Based Dense Reward:**
 - **Motivation:** Traditional keypoint-based rewards can be inadequate for complex assembly tasks. Collinear keypoints underspecify alignment for non-axisymmetric parts (e.g., rectangular pegs), while non-collinear keypoints overspecify symmetric parts. Chamfer distance, another common metric, also proved suboptimal.
 - **Approach:** This method uses Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) to provide a dense reward signal. For each plug mesh, N points are sampled on its surface. During training, these points are transformed to the current plug pose, and their SDF values (precomputed from the target socket mesh) are queried. The root-mean-square SDF distance is used to generate a continuous reward, effectively measuring alignment between complex geometries.
 - **Impact:** Dramatically improved alignment accuracy and success rates for insertion tasks (e.g., from 1.8-54.2% to 88.6% for Pegs and Holes assembly), demonstrating superior performance over keypoint or Chamfer distance methods.

3. **Sampling-Based Curriculum (SBC):**
 - **Motivation:** Naive curriculum learning (gradually increasing initial distance from goal) was ineffective for insertion tasks, as agents would often overfit to partially-inserted states and fail to progress when initialized from higher distances.
 - **Approach:** SBC exposes the agent to the *entire range* of initial state distributions from the start of the curriculum, but gradually increases the *lower bound* of this range. For instance, the initial height of the plug is uniformly sampled between a `z_low` (which increases over time) and a constant `z_high`. `z_low` is increased when success rate is high and decreased if low, allowing the agent to continuously learn from diverse initial conditions while progressively increasing task difficulty.
 - **Impact:** Substantially improved success rates (e.g., from 66.8% to 88.6%) and reduced position error compared to baseline or standard curriculum approaches, preventing overfitting and facilitating learning for discontinuous contact tasks.

**Policy Deployment in Real World (Algorithm for Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
After training, policies are transferred to the real Franka robot without any real-world adaptation phase.

1. **Policy-Level Action Integrator (PLAI):**
 - **Motivation:** Real-world dynamics (e.g., non-linear friction, imperfect gravity compensation) often differ from simulation, leading to steady-state errors in policy deployment. Classical PID control can minimize these errors but requires tuning.
 - **Approach:** Instead of applying policy actions (incremental state targets `a_t`) to the *current* robot state (`s_t` to get `s_{d,t+1} = s_t \oplus a_t`), PLAI applies them to the *last desired state* (`s_{d,t+1} = s_{d,t} \oplus a_t`). This effectively integrates policy actions over time, similar to the integral term in a PID controller. A "Leaky PLAI" version clamps accumulated control effort to mitigate windup when in contact.
 - **Impact:** Reduced steady-state position error in both simulation (under friction/gravity perturbations) and real-world tests (from ~4-5mm for Nominal/PID to ~2mm for PLAI), demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating unmodeled dynamics at deployment time with minimal implementation effort.

**System and Tools:**
- **Communications:** A custom Python library, IndustRealLib, interfaces trained Isaac Gym policies with the Franka robot via frankapy, streaming commands to the robot's low-level controller (libfranka).
- **Perception Pipeline:** Consists of one-time camera calibration (intrinsic & extrinsic), per-experiment workspace mapping (using AprilTags), and per-experiment object detection from a single RGB image (fine-tuned Mask R-CNN model on custom industrial assets). This estimates 2D poses (x, y, θ) of parts, which are combined with human-specified nominal heights to form 6-DOF end-effector targets.
- **Dynamics Strategy:** Careful elimination of arbitrary energy dissipation (e.g., heuristic friction/damping) in the simulator and asset descriptions to minimize discrepancies with real-world dynamics.
- **Reproducibility Kits:** IndustRealKit (3D-printable CAD models and parts list) and IndustRealLib (Python library for deployment and training code, including trained policy weights) are provided to the community.

This comprehensive methodology, combining novel algorithms for learning and deployment with a robust system architecture, allows IndustReal to achieve high performance and reliable sim-to-real transfer for challenging contact-rich assembly tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a thorough evaluation of IndustReal's algorithms and system, both in simulation and the real world, demonstrating its effectiveness across various contact-rich assembly tasks.

**Simulation-Based Algorithm Evaluation:**

The three proposed simulation-based algorithms (SAPU, SDF-Based Dense Reward, and SBC) were rigorously tested on the "Insert" policies for Pegs and Holes and Gears and Gearshafts assemblies.

- **Simulation-Aware Policy Update (SAPU):**
 - **Finding:** The "Filter and Weight" strategy for SAPU significantly outperformed other strategies (Baseline, Filter Only, Weight Only).
 - **Results:** For the Peg and Hole Insert policy, Filter and Weight achieved success rates of 84.9-87.6% (depending on the interpenetration threshold), whereas other strategies remained below 40%. This indicates that SAPU not only encouraged policies to avoid spurious interpenetrations but also generally improved the learning process for contact-rich tasks.

- **SDF-Based Dense Reward:**
 - **Finding:** The SDF Query Distance reward formulation was vastly superior to keypoint-based or Chamfer Distance rewards for alignment tasks.
 - **Results:** For the Peg and Hole Insert policy, SDF Query Distance achieved an 88.6% success rate, a 96.6% engagement rate (partial insertion), and remarkably low terminal position and rotation errors (3.80 mm and 0.086 rad, respectively). In contrast, keypoint-based rewards yielded success rates of 15.4-54.2% and Chamfer Distance only 1.8%, with significantly higher errors. This highlights the effectiveness of SDFs in providing a geometrically accurate and robust alignment metric for complex objects.

- **Sampling-Based Curriculum (SBC):**
 - **Finding:** SBC significantly improved learning performance compared to no curriculum or a standard curriculum for insertion tasks.
 - **Results:** For the Peg and Hole Insert policy, SBC achieved an 88.6% success rate and 96.6% engagement rate with low position error (3.80 mm). The "Standard" curriculum yielded only 32.4% success, and the "Baseline" (no curriculum) 66.8%. This demonstrates that gradually exposing agents to the full range of initial states from the start, while progressively increasing the lower bound, effectively prevents overfitting and facilitates robust learning for tasks with discontinuous contact.

- **Joint Evaluation (All Three Algorithms in Tandem):**
 - **Results:** When all three algorithms were used together, the Pegs and Holes assembly Insert policy achieved 88.6% success and 96.6% engagement. The Gears and Gearshafts assembly Insert policy achieved 82.0% success and 85.2% engagement. The policies demonstrated robustness to moderate state randomization and observation noise, with worst-case performance being 67.88% under high out-of-distribution randomization and best-case 92.4% with no randomization.

**Real-World Experimental Results:**

The trained policies, combined with the Policy-Level Action Integrator (PLAI), were deployed on a physical Franka Panda robot. The evaluation covered individual phases (Pick, Place, Insert) and end-to-end assembly (Pick-Place-Insert), also demonstrating generalization to unseen objects.

- **Policy-Level Action Integrator (PLAI) Evaluation:**
 - **Finding:** PLAI effectively minimized steady-state error caused by unmodeled real-world dynamics.
 - **Results:** In simulation, PLAI maintained ≈2 mm error even under significant friction and gravity perturbations, outperforming "Nominal" (standard action application) and "PID" methods, which had higher errors and variance. In the real world, PLAI also achieved ≈2 mm error, substantially lower than "Nominal" (≈4.45 mm error) and even outperforming Franka's built-in TSI controller. This confirms PLAI's ability to robustly handle sim-to-real discrepancies at deployment time.

- **Pick Experiment:**
 - **Task:** Picking up various pegs from randomized positions.
 - **Results:** Achieved extremely high success rates of 98.8% over 160 trials across 6 different pegs. Failures were rare (e.g., missed detection, grasping peg and tray simultaneously).

- **Place Experiment:**
 - **Task:** Accurately placing a laser-mounted gripper to target locations.
 - **Results:** Demonstrated low steady-state errors, with a mean distance-to-goal of 4.23 ± 1.96 mm.

- **Sort Demonstration:**
 - **Task:** Qualitatively sorting different pegs and gears into separate bins using Pick and Place policies.
 - **Results:** Showed highly repeatable performance, highlighting the robustness and generalization of the Pick and Place policies.

- **Insert Experiment:**
 - **Task:** Inserting various plugs (pegs, gears, NEMA connectors) into their corresponding sockets, with initial precise placement in the gripper fingers but randomized target observations.
 - **Results:** Achieved high engagement rates (partial insertions): 86.7% for pegs, 95.0% for gears, and 100% for NEMA connectors. Full insertion success rates were moderately high: 76.7% for pegs, 92.5% for gears, and 85% for connectors. The Pegs and Holes Insert policy successfully generalized to unseen NEMA connectors. Failures were primarily attributed to object slip in the gripper or the wedging phenomenon. The robot also demonstrated recovery behavior when perturbed and search behavior on the socket surface.

- **Pick, Place, and Insert (PPI) Demonstration (End-to-End Assembly):**
 - **Task:** Full end-to-end assembly of pegs, gears, and NEMA connectors from randomized initial positions.
 - **Results:** Achieved success rates of 80% (pegs), 97.5% (gears), and 100% (connectors), with corresponding engagement rates of 88.3%, 100%, and 100%. These higher success rates compared to the isolated Insert experiment suggest that the randomization and noise ranges in the latter might have been particularly challenging.

Overall, the findings underscore IndustReal's capability to learn robust, contact-rich assembly skills in simulation and transfer them effectively to the real world without requiring real-world policy adaptation, a significant advancement for intelligent robotic assembly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

IndustReal represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic assembly and sim-to-real transfer, offering both novel algorithmic advancements and a concrete, reproducible system. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows.

1. **First End-to-End Sim-to-Real Transfer for Assembly Without Real-World Adaptation:** This is a major breakthrough. Previous works often focused on isolated sub-tasks, required human demonstrations, baseline plans, or a crucial real-world fine-tuning phase. IndustReal demonstrates success across the entire assembly pipeline—detection, grasping, transport, and insertion—making it a truly deployable system. This reduces the cost and complexity of integrating robotic assembly solutions.
2. **Addressing Key Simulation-to-Reality Challenges:** The paper introduces four highly effective algorithms that tackle specific pain points in learning contact-rich tasks and transferring them:
 - **SAPU** mitigates the "sim-reality gap" introduced by simulator inaccuracies (interpenetrations), enabling the agent to learn physically plausible behaviors.
 - **SDF-Based Dense Rewards** provide a robust and accurate metric for alignment, crucial for precision tasks with complex geometries, outperforming traditional reward formulations.
 - **SBC** offers a robust curriculum learning strategy that prevents overfitting and facilitates learning for long-horizon, discontinuous contact tasks.
 - **PLAI** effectively compensates for unmodeled dynamics in the real world (e.g., friction, gravity compensation errors) at deployment time, requiring minimal effort and improving robustness without needing real-world data collection for system identification or fine-tuning.
3. **Validation with Realistic Constraints:** By using a standard collaborative robot (Franka Panda) and camera (Intel RealSense), realistic part clearances, and avoiding specialized grippers or F/T sensors, the work demonstrates practical applicability to real-world industrial scenarios where such hardware is common and high-precision sensors are often cost-prohibitive or fragile.
4. **Benchmarking and Reproducibility:** The paper provides the first simulation results for benchmark tasks proposed in the Factory framework and, critically, commits to open-sourcing IndustRealKit (hardware CAD models and parts list) and IndustRealLib (software library, trained policy weights). This commitment to reproducibility is invaluable for the research community, lowering the barrier to entry, facilitating comparison of new methods, and accelerating progress in robotic assembly.
5. **Enabling More Intelligent and Adaptive Assembly:** By allowing robots to learn complex contact-rich skills in simulation and transfer them reliably, IndustReal paves the way for a future where assembly robots are more intelligent, adaptive to part variations, and less reliant on rigid, pre-programmed trajectories and expensive fixturing. This can unlock new possibilities in flexible manufacturing and high-mix, low-volume production.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors candidly acknowledge several limitations that naturally lead to future research directions:
1. **Task Scope:** The current work primarily investigates NIST-inspired benchmark tasks. Future work could explore a wider variety of assets and more complex assembly sequences using larger datasets of simulation-compatible assets.
2. **Failure Cases:** Primary real-world failures were due to object slip in the gripper and the wedging phenomenon. Future research could integrate simulated visuotactile readings during training with corresponding real-world sensors (e.g., GelSight) and improve friction modeling in simulators.
3. **Passive Compliance:** The paper did not explore passive mechanical compliance, which is a known facilitator for assembly tasks. Optimizing policy, controller, and passive dynamics simultaneously could further enhance performance.
4. **Simulator Accuracy and Efficiency:** For even higher complexity tasks (e.g., assembly of elastic cables), current simulators might still be insufficient. The authors propose a "sim-to-real-to-sim" feedback loop as a compelling training strategy, where real-world deployment data iteratively refines the simulator or policies.

In conclusion, IndustReal represents a robust, well-validated framework for tackling the formidable challenge of contact-rich robotic assembly through advanced simulation and policy transfer. Its contributions—novel algorithms, strong empirical results, and a commitment to reproducibility—are poised to significantly influence the future direction of intelligent robotic assembly systems.
