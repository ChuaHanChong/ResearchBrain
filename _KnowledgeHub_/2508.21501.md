# Few-Shot Neuro-Symbolic Imitation Learning for Long-Horizon Planning and Acting

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tufts University and AIT introduce a neuro-symbolic imitation learning framework that jointly learns high-level symbolic planning models and low-level continuous control policies from few raw demonstrations. This approach achieves robust generalization and solves long-horizon tasks with significantly less data than purely neural baselines.

## Problem

- Traditional imitation learning methods require large datasets, struggle with long-horizon tasks, and exhibit poor generalization to novel scenarios.
- Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) relies on manually crafted symbolic models that are laborious to create and difficult to adapt to new environments or tasks.
- Existing neuro-symbolic approaches often require predefined symbolic state representations, known predicates, or a relatively large number of demonstrations to extract symbolic abstractions.

## Method

- A ==bi-level neuro-symbolic framework== jointly learns abstract symbolic planning domains and continuous control policies from raw demonstration trajectories.
- The method constructs a domain structure graph from visual snapshots and skill labels, minimizes it using ==bisimulation==, and then infers a first-order ==PDDL== symbolic domain theory via an ==Answer Set Programming (ASP) solver==.
- An **'Oracle'** function derives skill-relevant, end-effector relative observations for low-level policies, which are learned using ==diffusion models== after automatically decomposing skills into simpler action steps.

## Results

- Achieved high success rates on diverse long-horizon tasks (e.g., Towers of Hanoi, Nut Assembly, Kitchen) with as few as **five** skill demonstrations, where end-to-end neural baselines completely failed even with hundreds of demonstrations.
- Demonstrated robust zero-shot generalization to harder task configurations (e.g., **7 cubes** in Towers of Hanoi from **3-cube** demos) and significant spatial shifts without further training.
- Enabled few-shot fine-tuning to further improve performance on complex tasks with minimal additional data, leading to interpretable high-level PDDL plans.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is possible to jointly learn both a high-level symbolic planning model and low-level continuous control policies from minimal, unannotated expert demonstrations.
> - Graph minimization via bisimulation combined with Answer Set Programming can effectively extract a PDDL-based symbolic domain theory and predicates from sparse, raw robotic demonstrations.
> - Filtering observations to focus on task-relevant objects and transforming coordinates to be relative to the robot's end-effector (the 'Oracle') significantly improves the data efficiency and generalization of learned low-level policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in artificial intelligence and robotics: enabling intelligent systems to perform complex, long-horizon tasks in novel environments with minimal supervision. This area is at the intersection of imitation learning (IL), task and motion planning (TAMP), and neuro-symbolic AI.

Traditional imitation learning methods are effective for acquiring short-horizon skills but suffer from several limitations:
- **Data Hunger:** They typically require large datasets of expert demonstrations to learn robust policies.
- **Poor Generalization:** They struggle to generalize to novel task variations, out-of-distribution states, or distribution shifts over time.
- **Long-Horizon Challenges:** They are ill-suited for long-horizon tasks that require hierarchical planning and reasoning over abstract states.

Conversely, **Task and Motion Planning (TAMP)** approaches are designed for long-horizon tasks by separating high-level symbolic planning from low-level continuous motion execution. However, TAMP traditionally relies on **manually crafted symbolic models** (e.g., PDDL domains, predicates, object types), which are laborious, brittle, and difficult to adapt to new environments or tasks.

Recent research has attempted to bridge this gap by learning either symbolic models or low-level controllers from demonstrations, or combining these elements in neuro-symbolic architectures. However, the authors point out a crucial gap:
- Many approaches that learn symbolic models still require predefined symbolic state representations, known predicates, object types, or mapping functions from continuous states to symbolic facts.
- Methods that combine neural control with symbolic planning often assume access to a pre-existing symbolic domain.
- Approaches extracting symbolic abstractions from raw data, such as clustering techniques, often still require a relatively large number of demonstrations (e.g., fifty or more).
- Even sophisticated SAT/ASP-based model learning methods, while reducing data for symbolic domain acquisition, typically assume a predefined symbolic domain and already solved plans, not raw demonstrations.

This paper positions itself as the **first neuro-symbolic imitation learning framework that jointly learns low-level control policies and high-level symbolic planning models from *few raw demonstrations*, without predefined states, predicates, lexicons, or domain knowledge.** By tackling this joint learning problem from minimal, unannotated data, the work significantly pushes the boundaries of data-efficient, generalizable, and interpretable robotics, directly addressing the core limitations of existing approaches and contributing to the growing field of neuro-symbolic AI. It builds upon ideas from bisimulation for graph compaction and Answer Set Programming (ASP) for symbolic model extraction, integrating these with modern imitation learning techniques like diffusion policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind this research stems from the recognized difficulties in teaching robots complex, long-horizon tasks. Humans excel at such tasks by employing hierarchical reasoning and abstraction, abilities that current robotic systems often lack. The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Joint Learning of High-Level Symbolic Models and Low-Level Control Policies:** To overcome the limitations of manually specified symbolic models in TAMP and the purely data-driven nature of end-to-end IL, the authors aim to automatically learn both abstract symbolic planning domains and continuous control policies from demonstrations.
2. **Data Efficiency:** A primary goal is to achieve this learning with *minimal supervision*, specifically from "a few skill demonstrations," contrasting with data-hungry deep learning methods. This is crucial for practical applications where collecting vast amounts of expert data is costly or impractical.
3. **Robust Generalization:** The framework aims to enable robots to generalize effectively to out-of-distribution tasks, novel configurations, spatial shifts, and previously unseen problems, moving beyond the specific scenarios seen during training. This includes both zero-shot and few-shot generalization capabilities.
4. **Interpretability and Explainability:** By extracting a symbolic representation (PDDL) for planning, the framework seeks to provide interpretable decision-making, allowing humans to understand the robot's high-level reasoning.
5. **Scalability to Long-Horizon Tasks:** The hierarchical neuro-symbolic approach is designed to inherently handle complex, multi-step tasks that would overwhelm traditional flat imitation learning methods.
6. **Minimal Human Input:** The human input is explicitly limited to demonstrating individual skills and visually matching high-level states, without requiring expertise in symbolic logic or manual symbolic annotations.

In essence, the authors are motivated to develop a more human-friendly and robust method for robot learning that combines the strengths of symbolic reasoning (interpretability, generalization) with the adaptability of neural networks (continuous control), all while being highly data-efficient.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework is a novel bi-level neuro-symbolic learning approach that integrates graph construction, symbolic abstraction, planning, action decomposition, and imitation learning.

#### 3.1. Learning Symbolic Structures from Sparse Demonstrations

1. **Demonstration Processing:** Raw demonstration trajectories (state-action pairs) are augmented with initial and final visual snapshots of the high-level environment state. A human observer assigns a label to each skill demonstration and matches the start and end visual snapshots to existing "black-box nodes" in a graph, or creates new ones if necessary. This process constructs a **domain structure graph G = ⟨V, E, L⟩**, where nodes (V) are abstract high-level states (represented by visual snapshots) and edges (E) are skill transitions (labeled by L). This input relies solely on visual comparison, not symbolic expertise.
2. **Graph Minimization (Bisimulation):** To reduce redundancy and the search space for symbolic learning, a minimal bisimulation $\bar{G}$ of the constructed graph G is computed. This ensures that only structurally unique states and transitions are retained, compacting the graph while preserving behavioral equivalence.
3. **Symbolic Abstraction via ASP:** The minimized graph $\bar{G}$ is then fed into an **Answer Set Programming (ASP) solver** (specifically, building on previous work by Blai & Hector, and Rodriguez et al.). The ASP solver's task is to infer a **first-order symbolic domain theory $\sigma$ (in PDDL form)** and corresponding instances that are consistent with the observed graph structure. This process automatically discovers predicates (F) and operators (O) that describe the high-level state transitions and their preconditions/effects, without any prior symbolic knowledge or hand-coding. The resulting PDDL domain enables classical planners to operate over the abstracted symbolic space.

#### 3.2. The Oracle - Filtering Skill-Relevant Data

To enhance learning efficiency and generalization of low-level policies, an "Oracle" function, $\phi$, is introduced. This Oracle automatically derives a structured perception for each skill, ensuring that neural controllers focus only on task-relevant information:
1. **Object Filtering ($\gamma$):** For a given grounded symbolic operator $o_i$ (e.g., `pick(cube1)`), the Oracle identifies the *minimal subset of objects* $E_{o_i}$ whose symbolic state changes under that operator. The observation function $\gamma$ then filters the full continuous state $\tilde{s}$ to include only these relevant objects: $\gamma(\tilde{s}, o_i) = \tilde{s}(E_{o_i})$. This acts as a symbolic attention mechanism.
2. **Coordinate Transformation ($\alpha$):** The Oracle further transforms the absolute coordinates of these relevant objects into coordinates relative to the agent's end-effector. This makes the learned policies invariant to global positioning, significantly improving generalization across different spatial configurations.
The combined function $\phi(\tilde{s}_t) = \alpha \circ \gamma(\tilde{s}_t, o_i)$ provides a reduced, task-centric observation space for each low-level skill.

#### 3.3. Learning Continuous Control Policies

1. **Skill Decomposition:** Each high-level skill demonstration is automatically partitioned into a sequence of simpler "action steps" (e.g., a "MOVE" operator might decompose into `reach-pick`, `pick`, `reach-drop`, `drop`). This decomposition is achieved by identifying consistent sequential action space patterns, which reduces the complexity of individual policies and restricts their action spaces.
2. **Diffusion Policies for Control:** For each action step within an operator, a low-level continuous control policy is learned using **diffusion policies**. Diffusion models are chosen for their robustness and sample efficiency, particularly their ability to capture multi-modal action distributions and avoid mode collapse common in direct regression methods. The policies minimize the action prediction loss, learning to generate actions by denoising perturbed expert actions. The input to these policies is the filtered and transformed observation $\phi(\tilde{s}_t)$.
3. **Execution Phase:** During execution, a classical PDDL planner (e.g., MetricFF) generates an abstract plan of symbolic operators. Each operator is then mapped to its corresponding neural skill (which itself consists of a sequence of action-step sub-policies). The Oracle dynamically provides the relevant, transformed observations for each sub-policy, and execution proceeds until a learned termination condition is met.

This hierarchical, modular framework enables efficient learning, robust generalization, and interpretable planning by tightly integrating symbolic reasoning with neural control from sparse, raw demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate their neuro-symbolic (N-S) framework across six diverse simulation domains: Robosuite environments (Stacking, Kitchen, Nut Assembly, Towers of Hanoi) and ROS 2 & Gazebo environments (Forklift Loading/Unloading, Multiple Pallets Storage). These domains involve different robotic arms, diverse object interactions, and varying levels of kinematic complexity.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Exceptional Data Efficiency:** The N-S framework demonstrates high data efficiency, achieving strong performance with as few as **five skill demonstrations**. This is a significant improvement over existing end-to-end neural baselines, which often require hundreds or thousands of demonstrations and still fail on complex tasks.
2. **Superior Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks:**
 - **Baselines Failure:** End-to-end neural baselines (IL, H-IL, H-IL Dense) *completely fail* to solve long-horizon tasks like Towers of Hanoi, even with 500 full demonstrations. They struggle to ground sub-goals and diverge into random outputs.
 - **N-S Success:** In stark contrast, the N-S method consistently abstracts problem constraints, builds correct symbolic domains, and reliably solves complex tasks such as Towers of Hanoi, Multiple Pallets Storage, Nut Assembly, and Kitchen. Failures for N-S only occur when low-level skills (e.g., precise fork insertion) occasionally fail, not due to reasoning errors.
3. **Effectiveness of the Oracle and Skill Decomposition:**
 - In short-horizon tasks like Stacking, the N-S agent starts to outperform baselines by effectively leveraging the Oracle's observation filtering (relative observations via $\alpha$ and symbolic abstraction via $\gamma$).
 - Action space clustering (skill decomposition) further optimizes skill execution, enabling near 100% success in Stacking with minimal data.
 - Even in challenging domains like Forklift Loading/Unloading (due to articulated kinematics), 30 demonstrations are sufficient for the N-S agent to achieve high success rates.
4. **Robust Generalization Capabilities:**
 - **Zero-Shot Generalization:** The framework shows encouraging zero-shot generalization to harder Towers of Hanoi configurations (e.g., 7 cubes x 5 pegs from 3x3 demos) and significant spatial shifts (peg displacements up to 10cm). It also generalizes to the Multiple Pallets Storage task from basic forklift demos.
 - **Few-Shot Fine-Tuning:** With just a few additional expert demonstrations (e.g., 5+Expert corrections) for harder Hanoi configurations, the agent significantly improves its performance, outperforming agents trained from scratch with 30 demonstrations using less data overall. This demonstrates the benefit of curriculum learning.
5. **Interpretable Decision Making:** The learned PDDL symbolic domains (examples provided in the appendix) allow for clear, interpretable high-level plans, addressing a key limitation of opaque neural networks.
6. **Domain Agnosticism:** The approach is demonstrated to be domain-agnostic, working effectively across very different robotic setups and task types (manipulation, assembly, logic puzzles, vehicle control).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of artificial intelligence and robotics, offering a neuro-symbolic imitation learning framework with far-reaching implications:

1. **Addressing Core Limitations of AI:** The framework directly tackles the grand challenges of data efficiency, generalization, and interpretability in complex, long-horizon tasks, which are critical for advancing intelligent systems beyond narrow applications.
2. **Enabling Human-Taught Robotics:** By requiring only a few raw demonstrations and simple visual matching from humans (without demanding expertise in symbolic logic or extensive data annotation), the approach makes it significantly easier for non-experts to teach robots new and complex behaviors. This greatly enhances the practicality of imitation learning for real-world deployment.
3. **Robustness and Adaptability:** The demonstrated zero-shot and few-shot generalization capabilities, coupled with efficient fine-tuning, mean that robots can adapt to novel environments, configurations, and even entirely new tasks with minimal additional effort. This adaptability is crucial for open-world scenarios where unforeseen situations are common.
4. **Foundation for More Capable Robots:** The ability to jointly learn both abstract reasoning and continuous control from limited data paves the way for robots that are not only skilled but also "smart"—capable of understanding, planning, and executing tasks in a human-like hierarchical manner. This supports flexible execution and replanning under abstraction-induced constraints, fulfilling desiderata for robust planning identified in prior work.
5. **Reduced Development Costs:** Eliminating the need for large datasets, extensive manual symbolic engineering, or direct environment interaction during learning drastically reduces the development time and cost associated with deploying robots for new tasks. This is particularly valuable for industrial and commercial applications.
6. **Safety and Explainability:** The interpretable symbolic plans provide a clear understanding of the robot's high-level decision-making process, which is essential for debugging, verification, and building trust in autonomous systems, especially in safety-critical applications.
7. **Future Integration with Foundation Models:** The proposed future work to integrate Foundation Models (e.g., CLIP, GPT) to automate state matching and skill labeling suggests a path toward even greater autonomy and reduced human supervision in the symbolic abstraction phase, making the framework even more powerful.

While the approach has limitations (e.g., reliance on demonstration quality, accurate grasping point estimation), its contributions in data efficiency, generalization, and interpretability make it a powerful paradigm for the next generation of intelligent, human-friendly robotic systems. This work lays a strong foundation for scalable, cognitive robotics in diverse real-world applications.
