# Safe Execution of RL Policies Via Acceleration-Based CBF-QP Constraint Enforcement for Real-World Robotic Deployments

> [!summary] Summary
> Acc-CBF-QP, an acceleration-based Quadratic Program, acts as a runtime safety filter for reinforcement learning policies on robots, enforcing critical safety constraints without modifying the original policy. It reduced constraint violations by 92% on a Unitree H1 humanoid robot, enabling safer deployment of learned behaviors on physical hardware.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning policies lack formal safety guarantees, leading to unsafe commands that can damage hardware or endanger human operators, especially in out-of-distribution states.
- Training-time safety methods provide statistical guarantees that often degrade in real-world environments, require retraining for constraint changes, and can be overly conservative.
- Current execution-time safety filters often lack direct integration with learned RL policies or sufficient validation on physical robots for a comprehensive set of constraints.

## Method

- An acceleration-based ==Quadratic Program== (**Acc-CBF-QP**) acts as a real-time ==safety filter==, adjusting robot accelerations to enforce operational limits.
- ==Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)== define a unified set of safety constraints, including joint position, velocity, torque, and self-collision avoidance, within the QP framework.
- The system integrates with pre-trained RL policies by formulating QP tasks (e.g., **TorqueTask**) that minimize deviation from the policy's desired torques while ensuring constraint satisfaction.

## Results

- The Acc-CBF-QP preserved nominal RL task performance on a Kinova Gen3 manipulator and successfully prevented hardware safety shutdowns in impending joint limit violation scenarios.
- On a Unitree H1 humanoid robot, the TorqueTask formulation reduced real-world constraint violations by **92%** (from **10.04 violations/s** to **0.80 violations/s**) compared to a Safe-RL baseline.
- The system operated with low computational overhead (**0.348 ms** per cycle on H1) and improved median survival time by roughly **20%** in aggressive simulation, transforming unsafe violations into physically plausible falls.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Runtime safety filtering with Acc-CBF-QP effectively enforces hard constraints on RL policies without requiring policy retraining, addressing real-world deployment challenges.
> - Integrating disturbance observers is crucial for robust CBF enforcement on physical hardware, mitigating the impact of modeling errors and unmodeled external forces.
> - The TorqueTask formulation, which minimizes deviation from RL-desired torques, exhibited superior robustness on physical robots compared to acceleration-tracking tasks due to dynamic modeling inaccuracies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in complex robotic control tasks, including agile locomotion and dexterous manipulation. However, a significant barrier to its widespread adoption and deployment on physical hardware is the absence of formal safety guarantees. RL policies, especially when operating near the physical limits of a robot or in unforeseen "out-of-distribution" (OoD) states, can generate unsafe commands that lead to hardware damage, system shutdowns, or pose risks to human and environmental safety.

The existing research landscape addresses robot safety in RL through two primary categories:
- **Training-time safety methods (Safe RL):** These approaches aim to embed safety directly into the policy learning process. Examples include constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO), and methods that incorporate Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) during training or use probabilistic safety filters. While effective in simulation, these methods often rely on statistical guarantees that can degrade under distributional shifts in real-world environments. They also typically require retraining if constraints change and may not provide consistent per-state constraint satisfaction during deployment, particularly for OoD states. Additionally, enforcing strict safety during training can be overly conservative, potentially limiting exploration and slowing convergence.
- **Execution-time safety filters:** This category involves post-processing layers that modify or filter unsafe commands generated by a pre-trained policy during deployment. Traditional robotics has long used optimization-based controllers, such as Quadratic Programs (QPs), to enforce feasibility and safety constraints in real-time. These model-based approaches, common in whole-body control, offer formal guarantees but historically do not directly integrate with learned policies. More recent developments include predictive safety filters and CBF-CLF-QP-based safety layers, which provide varying levels of safety assurances. However, many of these methods have been primarily validated in simulation or lack extensive testing with physical constraints like joint limits or collision avoidance on the outputs of learned policies.

This work positions itself within the execution-time safety filter paradigm, specifically by bridging model-based optimization control with learned RL policies. It extends an acceleration-based QP-CBF framework to filter RL policy outputs in real-time, offering a runtime safety layer that complements, rather than replaces, training-time safety methods. This approach aims to provide robust, real-time safety enforcement that addresses the limitations of purely training-time methods, particularly their vulnerability to distributional shifts and OoD states on hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the safety and reliability of Reinforcement Learning policies for real-world robotic deployments by introducing a runtime safety filtering mechanism. This mechanism, named Acc-CBF-QP, is designed to ensure that RL-generated commands adhere to critical safety constraints without necessitating modifications to the original RL policy training process.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in deploying RL on hardware:
- **Lack of Safety Guarantees in RL:** Standard RL policies, optimized for performance metrics, typically do not inherently guarantee adherence to safety-critical operational limits. This deficiency poses a significant risk for hardware deployment, where even minor violations can lead to damage, system failure, or endanger human operators.
- **Limitations of Training-Time Safety:** While "Safe RL" methods attempt to embed safety during training, they often provide only statistical or distributional guarantees. These guarantees can degrade when a robot encounters out-of-distribution states or unmodeled dynamics in the real world. Moreover, training-time methods require retraining if safety constraints change, and overly strict safety during training can impede exploration and learning efficiency.
- **Dynamic Nature of Safety Requirements:** Real-world deployments may introduce new or evolving safety constraints (e.g., dynamic obstacle avoidance based on online perception) that were not present or fully specified during initial policy training. A runtime filter can adapt to these changes without costly retraining.
- **Bridging Paradigms:** Traditional robotics heavily relies on model-based optimization for real-time constraint enforcement, offering strong guarantees. However, these methods are not inherently designed to integrate with learned, often model-free, RL policies. A key motivation is to bridge the adaptability and performance benefits of RL with the formal safety guarantees of optimization-based control.

To achieve its primary objective, the research outlines several specific goals:
- **Develop a Unified Constraint Enforcement Framework:** Implement an acceleration-based Quadratic Program (QP) using Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) to enforce diverse constraints, including joint position, velocity, torque, and self-collision avoidance, within a single optimization framework.
- **Integrate with Arbitrary RL Policies:** Design the safety filter to operate as a post-processing layer, accepting nominal commands from any pre-trained RL policy without requiring its retraining or modification.
- **Regulate Deviation from RL Commands:** Formulate specific tasks within the QP (TorqueTask and Forward Dynamics Task) that minimize deviation from the RL policy's intended behavior when constraints become active, providing mechanisms to control the trade-off between safety enforcement and task performance.
- **Enhance Robustness to Real-World Dynamics:** Incorporate state estimation and disturbance observers to mitigate the effects of modeling errors and unmodeled external forces, which are critical for consistent CBF enforcement on physical robots, especially floating-base systems.
- **Empirical Validation on Diverse Platforms:** Systematically validate the proposed framework on both a fixed-base manipulator (Kinova Gen3) and a floating-base humanoid robot (Unitree H1), conducting experiments in both simulation and on real hardware to demonstrate its effectiveness in reducing constraint violations and improving system stability.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** Provide an open-source implementation of the full pipeline to facilitate broader adoption and further research in safe RL deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around Acc-CBF-QP, an acceleration-based Quadratic Program (QP) that functions as a real-time safety filter for Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies. This approach is designed to impose safety constraints on the robot's motion by adjusting the commanded accelerations, thereby ensuring that the resulting torques and positions remain within safe operating limits.

**A. Acceleration-Based QP Formulation:**
The control problem is formulated as a QP where the decision variables are the generalized accelerations, $\ddot{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The QP minimizes a quadratic cost function, which represents the deviation from a desired task objective, subject to a set of linear and convex constraints. The general form is:
$\ddot{q}^* = \arg \min_{\ddot{q}} \sum_k \|W_k(A_k \ddot{q} - b_k)\|^2$
s.t. $A_c \ddot{q} \leq b_c$
Here, $(A_k, b_k)$ define the linear tasks, $W_k$ are weight matrices for task prioritization, and $(A_c, b_c)$ encode the linearized safety constraints.

**B. Constraint Set using Control Barrier Functions (CBFs):**
Safety is defined as the forward invariance of a safe set $C$, where all safety functions $h_i(q, \dot{q}) \geq 0$. These barrier functions are continuously differentiable, and their forward invariance is enforced by CBF conditions expressed as affine inequalities in $\ddot{q}$.
1. **Torque Limits:** Actuator torque limits ($\tau_a^- \leq \tau_{QP} \leq \tau_a^+$) are enforced, where $\tau_{QP}$ is the torque required to achieve the QP-computed acceleration $\ddot{q}^*$, derived from the robot's inverse dynamics equation: $\tau_{QP} = M(q)\ddot{q}^* + C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) - \hat{\tau}_{ext}$. This formulation incorporates an estimate of external disturbances.
2. **Joint Velocity Limits:** For an upper velocity bound $\dot{q}^+$, the barrier function $h_{vel}(\dot{q}) = \dot{q}^+ - \dot{q}$ is used. As it has relative degree one with respect to $\ddot{q}$, the standard CBF condition $\dot{h}_{vel} + \alpha(h_{vel}) \geq 0$ is applied, yielding $\ddot{q} \leq -\lambda(\dot{q} - \dot{q}^+)$.
3. **Joint Position Limits:** For an upper position limit $q^+$, the barrier function $h_{pos}(q) = q^+ - q$ is used. Since it has relative degree two, an Exponential CBF (ECBF) condition $\ddot{h}_{pos} + \alpha_1 \dot{h}_{pos} + \alpha_2 h_{pos} \geq 0$ is applied. This translates to $\ddot{q} \leq -\lambda \dot{q} + \frac{\lambda^2}{4\zeta^2}(q^+ - q)$, where $\lambda$ and $\zeta$ are tuning gains.
4. **Self-Collision Avoidance:** For each monitored body pair, the minimum distance $e(q)$ is used to define $h_{col}(e) = e - d_s$, where $d_s$ is a safety margin. This also has relative degree two, so an ECBF condition similar to joint position limits is applied: $J_e(q)\ddot{q} \geq -\dot{J}_e(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} - \lambda \dot{e} - \frac{\lambda^2}{4\zeta^2}(e - d_s)$, where $J_e$ is the distance Jacobian.

**C. External Disturbance Estimation:**
To enhance the accuracy of the robot's dynamic model (Eq. 1 in paper) and thus the reliability of CBF enforcement, a Generalized-Momentum Observer (GMO) is employed. The GMO estimates external generalized forces/torques ($\hat{\tau}_{ext}$), accounting for external contacts, torque tracking errors, and other modeling inaccuracies. For floating-base robots (like the Unitree H1), a Tilt Observer is used to estimate base velocity, as not all generalized velocities are directly measured. This observer uses IMU data and estimates of ground reaction forces (from knee torques on H1 due to lack of force sensors).

**D. Robustness Analysis:**
The paper acknowledges that model errors and disturbances can impact safety guarantees. A robustness analysis demonstrates an input-to-state safety property: under a known bounded disturbance $\bar{\delta}$, the constrained trajectory remains within an $O(\bar{\delta}/\lambda)$ neighborhood of the safe set. This clarifies why some minor violations might still occur on hardware despite theoretical guarantees.

**E. Integration of RL with QP Tasks:**
The RL policy typically outputs a joint position command $q_{RL}$, which is then converted into motor torques $\tau_{RL}$ by a low-level PD controller. The QP framework integrates with this $\tau_{RL}$ by formulating acceleration tasks:
1. **TorqueTask:** This task aims to make the QP-generated torque $\tau_{QP}$ match the RL-desired torque $\tau_{RL}$. It translates to the objective: $M(q)\ddot{q} = \tau_{RL} - (C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) - \hat{\tau}_{ext})$.
2. **Forward Dynamics Task (FDTask):** This alternative converts $\tau_{RL}$ into a reference acceleration $\ddot{q}_{ref} = M(q)^{-1}(\tau_{RL} - (C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) - \hat{\tau}_{ext}))$ and minimizes the deviation of $\ddot{q}^*$ from this reference. This task is more sensitive to dynamic modeling errors due to the inertia matrix inversion.

**F. Experimental Setup:**
- **Robots:** Kinova Gen3 (7-DoF manipulator) and Unitree H1 (19-DoF humanoid).
- **RL Policies:** For Kinova, an off-the-shelf PPO policy without initial safety constraints. For H1, a PPO policy trained with the Constraints as Terminations (CaT) Safe-RL method.
- **Control Frequencies:** RL policies run at lower frequencies (100 Hz for Kinova, 40 Hz for H1), while the Acc-CBF-QP controller runs at higher frequencies (1 kHz for Kinova, 400 Hz for H1).
- **Hardware Interface:** Kinova allows direct torque commands. H1 uses a PD interface, so $\tau_{QP}$ is converted back to an equivalent position command $q_{cmd} = q_a + K_p^{-1}(\tau_{QP} + K_d \dot{q}_a)$ to be sent to the robot's PD controller.
- **Model Challenges:** The H1 robot had a significant mass mismatch (over 8 kg) in its URDF, which was empirically corrected, but inherent inaccuracies remained, making it a challenging testbed for model-based control.
- **QP Infeasibility Handling:** In cases of QP infeasibility (e.g., conflicting constraints under severe perturbations), two recovery strategies were tested: switching to a damping mode ( $\tau_{cmd} = -K_d \dot{q}_a$ ) or temporarily reverting to a torque-saturated RL baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on a 7-DoF Kinova Gen3 manipulator and a 19-DoF Unitree H1 humanoid, in both simulation and on real hardware, to evaluate the performance of Acc-CBF-QP.

**A. Manipulator Experiments (Kinova Gen3):**
1. **Nominal Conditions:** When the robot operated within safe limits, the Acc-CBF-QP (with both TorqueTask and FDTask formulations) preserved the nominal task performance of the RL policy. The Cartesian deviation from the mean RL trajectory remained comparable to the intrinsic repeatability of the RL policy itself. Statistical analysis indicated no significant degradation in task performance in violation-free regimes.
2. **Out-of-Distribution Scenario:** When the RL policy was initialized in a configuration that led to an impending joint limit violation, the baseline RL policy caused a hardware safety shutdown. In contrast, both Acc-CBF-QP formulations (TorqueTask and FDTask) successfully enforced joint limits and prevented the violation, demonstrating effective runtime safety intervention.

**B. Humanoid Experiments (Unitree H1):**
This robot represented a more challenging floating-base system, and the RL policy was trained using a Safe-RL method (CaT), providing a comparative baseline for runtime filtering versus training-time safety.
1. **Torque and Acceleration Error Analysis:**
 - **Torque Tracking:** TorqueTask significantly reduced the mean torque tracking error ($\|\tau_{QP} - \tau_{RL}\|$) by 79.0% on hardware and 71.6% in simulation compared to FDTask. This indicates TorqueTask's effectiveness in maintaining the desired torque profile from the RL policy.
 - **Acceleration Tracking:** FDTask achieved lower mean acceleration error in simulation (14.3% reduction) as expected, but this advantage was negligible on hardware (0.9% reduction) due to modeling inaccuracies.
 - **Robustness:** TorqueTask exhibited greater robustness to the inherent modeling errors present on the physical H1 robot compared to FDTask.
2. **Computational Cost:** The Acc-CBF-QP pipeline demonstrated low computational overhead, with average per-cycle times of 0.348 ms (TorqueTask) and 0.360 ms (FDTask) on the H1 robot, which is well within the 2.5 ms control period (400 Hz).
3. **Constraint Violation Rates:**
 - **Hardware:** The baseline RL policy (trained with CaT) yielded an average of 10.04 violations/s, primarily due to torque limits. Acc-CBF-QP with TorqueTask reduced this by 92% to 0.80 violations/s. Acc-CBF-QP with FDTask achieved 1.32 violations/s. The remaining violations on hardware were predominantly in joint velocities, which the authors attribute to phase lag in the Generalized-Momentum Observer during foot-ground impacts.
 - **Simulation:** While the baseline RL alone produced 3.14 torque limit violations/s, both Acc-CBF-QP tasks achieved zero constraint violations in simulation, where modeling errors are minimal.
4. **Performance and Stability Interference (Simulation):**
 - **Aggressive Velocity Command (Flat Terrain):** Under aggressive commands, the RL policy (even with CaT) frequently violated joint limits (84%) and collision constraints (16%), leading to a median survival time of 2.59 s. Adding torque saturation alone did not improve this. Both Acc-CBF-QP variants eliminated all constraint violations and extended the median survival time to 2.96 s (FDTask) and 3.10 s (TorqueTask), representing approximately a 20% improvement. This indicates that the QP layer successfully converted unsafe violations into physically admissible falls, preventing hardware-damaging failures without sacrificing task longevity.
 - **Uneven Terrain Robustness:** When tested on procedurally generated uneven terrain, QP infeasibility was observed (approx. 5% for TorqueTask, 11% for FDTask). While recovery strategies (damping or rollback) were implemented, median survival times remained comparable across all controllers (10-11 s). The raw RL policy predominantly terminated due to joint violations (79%), whereas QP-based controllers significantly reduced these violations, shifting failure modes towards physically plausible falls (42-53%). Statistical tests revealed no significant differences in survival duration between the QP formulations or infeasibility handling strategies in this scenario.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Acc-CBF-QP framework represents a development in bridging the operational gap between advanced Reinforcement Learning policies and the strict safety requirements of real-world robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Enabling Real-World Deployment of RL:** A primary barrier to deploying powerful but unverified RL policies on hardware is the lack of guaranteed safety. Acc-CBF-QP offers a runtime, post-training safety layer that allows researchers and engineers to deploy learned behaviors with greater confidence, knowing that fundamental physical and operational constraints will be respected. This directly addresses a core challenge in the field of robot learning.
- **Complementary Safety Strategy:** The proposed method functions as a robust runtime filter, complementing training-time Safe RL approaches like CaT. While training-time methods aim to learn safe behaviors in expectation, Acc-CBF-QP provides per-control-cycle guarantees, mitigating issues arising from distributional shifts, modeling errors, and out-of-distribution states that often cause failures on hardware. This dual approach could lead to more comprehensively safe robotic systems.
- **Versatility and Modularity:** The framework is designed to be policy-agnostic and robot-platform-independent, demonstrating its utility on both a fixed-base manipulator and a floating-base humanoid. Its modular structure allows for the integration of various constraint types (position, velocity, torque, collision) within a unified QP framework, making it adaptable to diverse robotic applications and evolving safety requirements.
- **Preservation of Nominal Performance:** A key finding is that Acc-CBF-QP maintains the nominal task performance of the RL policy when constraints are not active. This indicates that safety is enforced without unduly penalizing the learned behavior in safe operating regimes, which is crucial for retaining the performance benefits of RL.
- **Increased System Longevity and Reduced Failures:** By actively preventing constraint violations, the framework demonstrably reduces hardware-damaging failures and system shutdowns. This translates to increased robot uptime, reduced maintenance costs, and safer experimental protocols, particularly under aggressive or challenging operating conditions. The significant reduction in violations on real hardware (92% on H1) underscores its practical effectiveness.
- **Principled Control over Safety-Performance Trade-offs:** The introduction of TorqueTask and Forward Dynamics Task formulations provides principled methods for controlling how the QP modifies the RL command when constraints are active. This allows engineers to tune the system's response based on the specific application's priorities, whether minimizing torque deviation or acceleration deviation.
- **Robustness to Modeling Errors:** The integration of disturbance observers (GMO, Tilt Observer) to estimate external forces and mitigate modeling inaccuracies is a critical component for achieving consistent safety enforcement on physical hardware, where perfect models are often unavailable.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The decision to make the full pipeline open-source accelerates research and development in this area, enabling wider adoption, comparative studies, and further extensions by the robotics community.

In conclusion, Acc-CBF-QP offers a practical, real-time mechanism to enhance the safety and robustness of RL-controlled robots. Its ability to enforce hard constraints without retraining policies positions it as a significant step towards enabling the reliable and responsible deployment of complex learned behaviors in real-world robotic applications.
