# Wrench-Aware Admittance Control for Unknown-Payload Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A wrench-aware admittance control framework for pick-and-place of unknown payloads on a UR5e. Wrist force-torque data serves two roles: a three-axis translational excitation term cancels payload-induced forces during transport without raising virtual stiffness, and a short post-grasp translational segment estimates the payload center-of-mass offset from the tool center point, which then corrects the horizontal release waypoint. Trials show 3.5 mm offset RMSE and balanced three-layer stacking.

## Problem

- Unmodeled payload wrench is misread by compliant controllers as external interaction force, causing unintended compliant motion, larger tracking error, and degraded transport accuracy.
- Raising virtual stiffness suppresses payload-induced sag but destroys the compliance that motivates admittance control in the first place, so it is not a usable remedy.
- Prior payload estimation assumed near-symmetric mass distribution or used separate static identification stages, ignoring offset center of mass and its effect on object-level placement.

## Method

- ==Translational excitation term== inside the ==admittance loop==: online vertical mass estimate from payload force over TCP acceleration builds a three-axis payload-force estimate that cancels the residual disturbance without stiffness increase.
- Task-embedded ==center-of-mass offset estimation==: stacked ==skew-symmetric linear system== from wrist wrench samples collected during the post-grasp translational segment, identifiable when the coefficient matrix reaches ==rank 3==.
- ==Placement correction law== shifts the commanded TCP release position by the negative filtered horizontal offset, extended across stacking layers by adding layer index times object height.

## Results

- Representative trial reached **3.5 mm** x-offset RMSE over the post-measurement interval while TCP x-position RMSE stayed at **1.2 mm**, so compliant tracking was preserved.
- Corrected placement gave a **2.73 mm** correction-command error, **1.03 mm** execution error, and **3.38 mm** final release error against the ideal corrected point.
- Three-layer stacking matched manual reference offsets closely, for example **-85.00 mm** actual versus **-81.91 mm** estimated and **-81.60 mm** implemented, with all blocks remaining balanced.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate tool-center-point placement does not imply correct object placement; when the center of mass is offset, only object-level correction satisfies the equilibrium condition.
> - A single wrench sample leaves the offset component parallel to the measured force unobservable, so identification requires diverse samples spanning rank 3.
> - Off-centered grasps induce torque-driven micro-slip that corrupts wrench readings, so friction-enhancing fingertip material is a prerequisite, not an optional refinement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Compliant control mechanisms, such as admittance control, are widely employed in robotic manipulation tasks where robots operate in environments with inherent uncertainties or require physical interaction with objects. This approach modifies robot motion based on measured external forces, making it suitable for compliant manipulation using position-controlled industrial robots in applications like material handling, packaging, and warehouse automation.

A persistent challenge in this field arises when robots manipulate objects with unknown payloads. If the mass and mass distribution of a payload are not incorporated into the robot's nominal dynamic model, the force-torque (F-T) sensor at the wrist measures a combined wrench, encompassing both the true interaction wrench and the payload-induced wrench. Without proper separation and compensation, this unmodeled wrench can lead to unintended compliant motion, increased tracking errors, and reduced accuracy during transport. This issue is particularly pronounced when the payload's center of mass (CoM) is not aligned with the tool center point (TCP). In such scenarios, the payload generates an offset wrench at the wrist, which, during motion, includes inertial force and moment components beyond static gravitational effects. Unaccounted for, these components further disrupt compliant motion and can compromise the accuracy of final task outcomes, such as object placement.

Previous strategies to mitigate payload-induced motion, such as increasing virtual stiffness, often compromise the inherent compliance that admittance control aims to provide, thereby limiting its practical benefits. More recent research has explored adaptive and variable admittance strategies to handle uncertain interactions, focusing on online parameter adaptation and enhanced disturbance rejection. Prior work by the authors addressed aspects of this problem by estimating unknown payload mass online and using an excitation force to reduce vertical sag during waypoint tracking. However, this approach primarily focused on payload-force effects and was largely validated with objects having approximately symmetric mass distributions.

The problem of CoM offset extends beyond transport to critical tasks like object placement and stacking. An off-centered object, even when the robot reaches a nominal placement pose, may not achieve a balanced rest position upon release. Recent developments in force-torque sensing have shown potential for improving placement robustness under geometric or sensing uncertainty, and wrist wrench information has been utilized for estimating object mass and CoM for general manipulation. This current work builds upon these advancements by integrating the use of wrist wrench measurements not only for mitigating transport disturbances but also for correcting object placement based on estimated CoM offset. Unlike methods that involve separate static or dedicated identification phases for payload properties, this research integrates CoM-offset estimation directly into the manipulation task itself, seeking to offer a more task-embedded and efficient solution within the existing landscape of compliant robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing compliant control strategies when handling unknown payloads with significant center of mass (CoM) offsets. In such scenarios, the unmodeled payload wrench, which includes both static and dynamic components due to mass and CoM eccentricity, can be misinterpreted by compliant controllers as an external interaction force. This misinterpretation leads to several undesirable outcomes, including unintended compliant motion, deviations from desired trajectories, increased tracking errors during transport, and compromised accuracy during final object placement and stacking. Relying solely on increasing virtual stiffness to counteract these effects is often counterproductive, as it reduces the very compliance that admittance control aims to provide. Furthermore, prior work, including the authors' own, addressed payload mass estimation and vertical force compensation but did not comprehensively account for CoM offsets, particularly those affecting moments and horizontal placement. The need for a unified framework that can address both payload-induced forces and moments while preserving compliance is therefore evident.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this research are:

- **To develop a wrench-aware admittance control framework** specifically designed for pick-and-place tasks involving unknown payloads. This framework aims to robustly handle objects regardless of their mass distribution.
- **To utilize force-torque (F-T) measurements from the robot's wrist in a dual capacity:**
 - **During transport:** Implement a three-axis translational excitation term within the admittance control loop. The purpose of this term is to actively reduce payload-induced force effects throughout the transport phase, critically, without requiring an increase in the robot's virtual stiffness, thereby maintaining compliant behavior.
 - **After grasping:** Implement a sequential estimation process. First, the controller is designed to estimate the payload mass. This estimated mass is then used for initial transport compensation. Subsequently, during a translational motion segment, the controller estimates the payload's CoM offset relative to the TCP using wrist F-T measurements.
- **To leverage the estimated CoM offset for improved object placement:** The filtered CoM offset estimate is intended to be used to correct the horizontal placement waypoint. This correction aims to position the object's CoM closer to its intended equilibrium location on the support surface, rather than merely placing the TCP at a nominal point. This is particularly relevant for balanced placement and stable stacking.
- **To integrate these capabilities into a single, task-embedded framework:** A central objective is that the payload mass and CoM offset estimation processes occur online and dynamically during the manipulation task itself, eliminating the need for separate, dedicated identification stages.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The presented methodology develops a wrench-aware admittance control framework for compliant pick-and-place operations involving unknown payloads. This approach is implemented on a UR5e manipulator utilizing Cartesian admittance control and waypoint-based motion planning.

#### 3.1. Admittance Control Model

The core of the control system is a standard translational admittance model, expressed by the equation:
$F = M_a (\ddot{p}_a - \ddot{p}_{ref}) + B_a (\dot{p}_a - \dot{p}_{ref}) + K_a (p_a - p_{ref}) - F_{exc}$
Here, $p_a$ represents the position generated by the admittance model, and $p_{ref}$ is the reference position trajectory. $M_a$, $B_a$, and $K_a$ are the virtual inertia, damping, and stiffness matrices, respectively. $F_{exc}$ is an excitation or compensation term. For waypoint-level motion, $\dot{p}_{ref} = 0$ and $\ddot{p}_{ref} = 0$, simplifying the model for calculating admittance acceleration and subsequently commanded Cartesian velocity via integration.

#### 3.2. Assumptions for Payload Modeling

Two key assumptions underpin the payload modeling:
- **Assumption 1:** During transport and CoM-estimation, the payload remains rigidly attached to the TCP, and the end effector undergoes translational motion with negligible angular velocity and acceleration ($ \omega \approx 0, \alpha \approx 0 $).
- **Assumption 2:** During the CoM-estimation segment, wrist wrench measurements are sufficiently accurate, and the collected translational samples provide enough diversity for the stacked offset-estimation system to achieve a rank of 3.

#### 3.3. Mass Estimation and Transport Compensation

The methodology for mass estimation and transport compensation builds upon previous work. The measured wrist force ($F$) during translational motion is modeled as the sum of the true interaction force ($F_{int}$) and the payload-induced translational force ($F_p$). The objective of transport compensation is to reduce the effect of $F_p$ without increasing virtual stiffness. This is achieved by defining a residual payload force ($ \tilde{F}_p := F_p - F_{exc} $). If $F_{exc}$ accurately approximates $F_p$, the admittance response is primarily driven by the true interaction force.

The payload mass ($ \hat{m}_u $) is estimated online during translational motion using the formula:
$ \hat{m}_u(k) = \frac{f_{p,z}(k)}{a_{TCP,z}(k) - g} $
where $f_{p,z}(k)$ is the payload-induced vertical force and $a_{TCP,z}(k)$ is the measured TCP linear acceleration in the vertical direction. This estimated mass is then used to form the translational payload-force estimate, $ \hat{F}_p(k) = \hat{m}_u(k)(a_{TCP}(k) - g) $, which serves as the excitation term $F_{exc}$ in the admittance model.

#### 3.4. Payload Offset Estimation from Wrist Wrench

Under Assumption 1 (negligible rotational dynamics), the measured wrist moment ($ \tau $) is approximated as $ r \times f $, where $r$ is the CoM offset relative to the TCP and $f$ is the measured force.
A single wrench sample does not uniquely determine $r$, as any component of $r$ parallel to $f$ is unobservable (Lemma 1). To overcome this, the methodology proposes collecting multiple wrench samples $ \{(f_k, \tau_k)\}_{k=1}^N $ over a translational segment. These samples are used to form an approximate linear system:
$ \tau_k \approx r \times f_k = -[f_k]_{\times} r $
where $ [f_k]_{\times} $ is the skew-symmetric matrix associated with $f_k$. Stacking these equations yields a linear system $ Ar = b $, where $A$ is formed by stacking the skew-symmetric matrices. The offset vector $r$ is identifiable if the coefficient matrix $A$ has a rank of 3 (Proposition 1). This allows for online CoM offset estimation during the manipulation task's translational motion segment.

#### 3.5. Placement Correction Strategy

The estimated payload offset ($ \hat{r} = [\hat{r}_x, \hat{r}_y, \hat{r}_z]^T $) is used to correct the robot's TCP placement command. If $p_{place,nom}$ is the nominal TCP placement position, the corrected command $p_{place,cmd}$ is defined as:
$ p_{place,cmd} = p_{place,nom} - [\hat{r}_x, \hat{r}_y, 0]^T $
In this work, the correction is applied only in the horizontal plane, as the vertical placement coordinate is determined by the task geometry. This aims to align the object's CoM with the support location after release, rather than just aligning the TCP. For stacking tasks, this command is extended across successive layers: $ p^{(n)}_{place,cmd} = p_{place,nom} - [\hat{r}_x, \hat{r}_y, 0]^T + [0, 0, nh]^T $, where $n$ is the layer index and $h$ is the effective layer spacing.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup and Validation

The method is implemented on a UR5e manipulator equipped with a wrist-mounted F-T sensor and a Robotiq 2F-140 gripper. A Linux PC is used for control communication. The validation task involves a waypoint-based pick-and-place experiment using objects with off-centered CoMs and a narrow support bar to define the desired equilibrium line. To ensure robust grasping and reduce wrench corruption from micro-slip, friction-enhancing material is added to the gripper fingertips.
Validation tasks include:
- Analyzing the convergence and accuracy of the CoM offset estimation during a short translational measurement segment.
- Evaluating single object placement performance, comparing corrected placement (using the estimated CoM offset) against nominal placement (without correction).
- Conducting repeated placement and stacking trials to assess the consistency and effectiveness of the estimated offset and corrected placement command across multiple layers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the wrench-aware admittance control framework yielded several findings regarding CoM offset estimation, transport performance, and object placement accuracy.

#### 4.1. Offset Estimation and TCP Response

During the designated translational segment for payload-offset estimation, the controller demonstrated the ability to estimate the CoM offset. In a representative trial, the estimated CoM offset in the x-direction converged towards the reference value following an initial transient spike that occurred at the gripping moment. This initial spike was attributed to pickup dynamics, object settling, and initial payload sag before transport compensation became effective. After this transient, the estimated x-offset remained close to the reference. For this trial, the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for the x-offset, computed from the start of the measurement window, was 3.5 mm. Concurrently, the TCP motion in the x-direction remained close to the ideal admittance trajectory during the estimation stage, with a corresponding TCP x-position RMSE of 1.2 mm over the same interval. These results indicate that the CoM offset can be estimated during translational motion without requiring separate rotational or static measurement steps, while maintaining the desired TCP motion.

#### 4.2. Placement Validation

The impact of CoM offset on final object placement was assessed by comparing corrected and uncorrected placement strategies.
- **Corrected Placement:** When the placement command was corrected using the estimated horizontal offset, the robot adjusted its TCP release position. In a representative trial, the estimated correction vector was (82.84 mm, -1.66 mm) for the x and y directions, respectively. This resulted in a commanded TCP release position of (-217.16 mm, 298.34 mm). The actual TCP release position showed an error of 3.38 mm relative to the ideal corrected position, and an execution error of 1.03 mm relative to the commanded position. This demonstrated that the controller could translate the estimated offset into a placement correction that aligned the object's CoM closer to the desired equilibrium point on the support. Figure 4 illustrates that the final implemented object pose, after correction, closely matched the desired object pose.
- **Nominal Placement (Without CoM Correction):** A comparative trial without CoM correction highlighted the issue. In this scenario, while the robot placed the object body near the nominal target TCP position, the object's CoM remained displaced from the equilibrium line by its inherent offset. This resulted in an alignment error, demonstrating that accurate TCP placement alone is insufficient to guarantee balanced object placement when the payload's CoM is offset from the TCP. The visual representation in Figure 6 showed the object's CoM significantly separated from the equilibrium line in the absence of correction.

#### 4.3. Repeated Placement and Stacking Validation

The consistency and effectiveness of the CoM correction were further validated through repeated placement and stacking trials. The corrected placement rule was extended to successive layers by incorporating the object height. A representative three-layer stacking result showed that for all layers, the projected CoM locations remained close to the equilibrium line after release, and the stacked blocks maintained balance (Figure 7). A summary table (Table 2) comparing manually measured CoM x-locations, estimated CoM x-locations from the measurement stage, and implemented CoM x-locations after placement demonstrated agreement across multiple objects. For Object 1, the actual CoM was -57.00 mm, estimated was -56.44 mm, and implemented was -56.20 mm. For Object 2, these values were 0.00 mm, -0.57 mm, and -0.61 mm. For Object 3, they were -85.00 mm, -81.91 mm, and -81.60 mm. This consistency supported the reliability of the estimated offset and the corrected placement command across successive placements and stacking steps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a critical challenge in compliant robotic manipulation: the handling of unknown payloads with arbitrary mass distributions. The significance of this work stems from its integrated approach and its potential to enhance the capabilities and robustness of robotic systems in various real-world applications.

#### 5.1. Integrated Wrench-Aware Control

The primary significance lies in the integration of online payload mass estimation, force-based transport compensation, and wrench-based CoM offset estimation for placement correction into a unified admittance control framework. This comprehensive approach allows the robot to adapt to varying payload properties within a single control loop, mitigating both payload-induced forces during transport and moments that affect final placement. This contrasts with previous methods that often addressed these issues separately or relied on less dynamic compensation strategies.

#### 5.2. Improved Manipulation Performance

The framework directly leads to improved performance in pick-and-place tasks. By compensating for payload-induced forces, the method reduces unintended compliant motion and tracking errors during transport, ensuring that the robot's trajectory remains closer to the desired path without compromising the system's inherent compliance. Crucially, the CoM offset estimation and subsequent placement correction enable more accurate and stable object placement, particularly for off-centered objects or tasks requiring precise stacking. This capability directly enhances the success rate of complex manipulation tasks where object stability after release is paramount.

#### 5.3. Practical Application and Efficiency

A key practical advantage is the "task-embedded" nature of the payload property estimation. By performing mass and CoM offset estimation online during a translational segment of the manipulation task itself, the method eliminates the need for separate, dedicated identification stages. This streamlines operations, reduces task cycle times, and allows for greater adaptability when dealing with a stream of unknown objects. The ability to maintain compliant motion throughout these operations is also vital for interacting safely and robustly with uncertain environments or deformable objects.

#### 5.4. Broad Applicability

The advancements presented in this paper have broad implications for various industrial and service robotics applications. These include:
- **Material Handling and Logistics:** Robots in warehouses and logistics centers often handle objects of varying weights and shapes. This framework can improve the efficiency and reliability of bin-to-shelf transfers, packaging, and sorting.
- **Assembly and Manufacturing:** Tasks requiring precise placement and alignment of components, especially those with non-uniform weight distribution, can benefit from CoM-aware manipulation.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** Maintaining compliant behavior while accurately handling objects is essential for safe and effective human-robot interaction.

#### 5.5. Foundation for Future Work

The research also lays a foundation for future developments. The authors identify extending the framework to rotational admittance as a natural next step. This would allow the controller to account for both translational and rotational payload effects during more general manipulation tasks, further enhancing the adaptability and robustness of robotic systems in highly dynamic and complex environments. Relaxing the pure-translation assumption would broaden the applicability to tasks involving more complex end-effector movements.
