# Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies

> [!summary] Summary
> Shadow evaluation is a method for measuring progress toward automating AI research: a frontier agent receives the central open-ended research question of a high-quality unpublished paper, and that paper's own authors grade the agent's output as conference reviewers. Applied to two NeurIPS 2026 submissions with six days, $3,000 in API credits and GPU compute, agents finished all engineering unaided, yet both papers were unambiguously rejected.

## Problem

- Existing AI R&D evaluations test verifiable tasks where agents hill-climb a fixed metric, excluding hypothesis choice, evidence design, and recognizing an approach has failed.
- Blind peer review as a grader is overstretched and stochastic; rerun committees flipped roughly a quarter of decisions, and failed submissions go unreported before an acceptance.
- Published-paper tasks leak into training data, and non-expert reviewers assigned by automated matching cannot judge whether an AI-generated finding is correct, novel, or useful.

## Method

- **Shadow evaluation**: take the central question of an unpublished paper, task a resourced agent with answering it, and have the original authors review the output on a ==1-6 conference scale==.
- Agent scaffold: ==OpenClaw== with Claude Opus 4.8 at extra-high reasoning on an AWS Linux VM, subagents, ==self-monitored token/GPU/clock budgets==, a ==reviewer subagent==, and three external AI review tools.
- Validity apparatus: a Codex plus GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra ==robustness rerun==, a twelve-respondent ==pre-experiment prediction survey==, full log analysis, and public release of reviews, repositories, and logs.

## Results

- Expert authors scored the Personas paper **2/6** and the TabPFN paper **1/6** with confidence **4/5** and **5/5**; quality scored **2/4** and **1/4** respectively.
- Both runs ended under half the API budget spent (**$1,130** and **$1,235** of **$3,000**); the Personas agent closed a planned **42-hour** exploration after **5 hours**, and self-reviews never accepted across **fifteen** revision rounds.
- Both papers exceeded the **nine-page** limit and cited far fewer references than the originals (**16 vs 52**; **36 vs 69**); the Codex rerun reproduced nearly every failure mode.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Research engineering and research judgment dissociate: agents debugged GPUs and ran hundreds of experiments, yet chose underpowered synthetic data and overgeneralized from null results.
> - A generator-verifier gap appears: AI reviews surfaced the damning critiques but agents answered with caveats and narrowed claims rather than redesigning experiments.
> - Agents are poorly calibrated to their own affordances, stopping early with budget and clock left, so resource limits alone do not bound long-horizon performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses a fundamental question within the field of artificial intelligence: whether AI agents possess the capability to conduct open-ended AI research autonomously. This question is particularly pertinent given numerous forecasts predicting accelerated AI progress, with some speculating that AI systems will soon automate significant portions of AI research itself, potentially leading to recursive self-improvement. Leading AI laboratories have also publicly discussed AI systems contributing to their research and development processes.

The authors position their work by critically evaluating existing methods for assessing AI agents in research:
1. **Evaluations on verifiable tasks:** This common approach involves agents improving a specific, fixed metric that can be automatically verified. Examples include benchmarks for reproducing computational studies (CORE-Bench), competing in Kaggle competitions (MLE-Bench), solving research-engineering problems (RE-Bench), or post-training language models (PostTrainBench). While objective and scalable, these methods are limited to tasks with pre-specified success metrics, which may not capture the open-ended nature of much AI research (e.g., hypothesis generation, experimental design, or effective backtracking from dead ends).
2. **Blind peer review of AI-generated papers:** A smaller body of work has submitted AI-generated papers to conferences or workshops for blind peer review. While testing open-ended research, the authors argue that peer review is a "weak measure" due to several factors: it is often overstretched and stochastic, suffers from reviewer inexperience or non-expertise, and typically does not involve deep technical verification. Furthermore, this method does not account for the number of rejected AI-generated submissions, making it difficult to assess reliability. The low cost of generating papers could also encourage selective reporting of successful submissions.

This paper introduces a "shadow evaluation" method as a complementary approach to address the limitations of existing evaluation paradigms. This new method aims to combine the open-endedness of real research problems with in-depth, expert grading, providing a distinct perspective on AI agents' research capabilities. It directly tests the model where researchers delegate an entire project to an agent and judge its utility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to evaluate the current capabilities of frontier AI agents in conducting open-ended AI research. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing a Consequential Open Question:** The ability of AI agents to automate AI research is considered one of the most significant open questions regarding AI capabilities. Many forecasts of "explosive AI progress" and "recursive self-improvement" depend on this premise.
2. **Scarcity of Evidence for Open-Ended Research:** Despite the attention given to the potential for AI to automate research, the authors contend that robust evidence on whether agents can tackle "open-ended research questions"—those that extend beyond narrow, verifiable tasks—remains thin. Current evaluations largely focus on problems where success is tied to quantifiable, pre-defined metrics, failing to capture the creative, judgment-intensive aspects of genuine scientific inquiry.
3. **Limitations of Existing Evaluation Methods:** The paper is motivated by the identified shortcomings of current evaluation approaches, namely the narrow scope of verifiable benchmarks and the inherent unreliability and biases of blind peer review in assessing agent-generated research. The authors seek a method that allows for evaluating agents on genuine, uncontaminated research problems with expert scrutiny.
4. **Developing a Novel Evaluation Methodology:** A key objective is to introduce and demonstrate "shadow evaluations" as a robust, repeatable method for measuring progress toward automating AI research. This method is designed to provide open-ended tasks, ensure the research questions are uncontaminated by prior online availability, and leverage reviewers with deep, specific expertise.
5. **Empirical Assessment of Frontier Agents:** The research aims to empirically test frontier AI agents (specifically Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra) on complex, real-world research problems derived from high-quality, unpublished papers. The goal is to determine if these agents can produce research outputs that meet the publication standards of top-tier AI conferences, thereby providing early evidence on their current research automation capabilities.

In essence, the research seeks to move beyond evaluations that simplify research into solvable tasks or rely on potentially flawed review processes, instead aiming for a more direct and rigorous assessment of AI agents' ability to engage in the full, open-ended cycle of scientific discovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study implemented a novel "shadow evaluation" methodology involving two distinct case studies.

**Core Shadow Evaluation Design:**
The central concept of shadow evaluation involves:
1. **Unpublished Research Questions:** Selecting the main research question from a high-quality, unpublished research paper (to ensure the agent cannot access existing solutions or findings from its training data or the web).
2. **Agent Tasking:** Tasking a well-resourced, frontier AI agent with independently answering this research question.
3. **Expert Grading:** Having the original authors of the unpublished paper review and grade the agent's output as they would a submission to a top-tier academic conference. These authors possess deep expertise in the exact research question being evaluated.

**Case Studies:**
Two specific research questions from unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions were used:
1. **Personas Paper:** Focused on whether large language model (LLM) personas can be decomposed, measured, and controlled as positions in a structured "trait space" using weight-space interventions. This involved developing a method for inducing targeted behavioral shifts via weight-based interventions, assessing dimension independence/composability, and testing effects on downstream behavior. The paper has since been made public (Baines et al., 2026).
2. **TabPFN Paper:** Aimed to design, theoretically justify, and empirically validate a deployment-time detector for tabular prior-fitted networks (PFNs) that alerts when the PFN's accuracy on a deployment batch is significantly worse than on in-distribution data, assuming white-box access to the model. The contribution had to specifically leverage PFN affordances.

**Agent Setup and Resources:**
- **Primary Model:** Claude Opus 4.8 with "extra-high reasoning."
- **Scaffold:** OpenClaw, an open-source framework designed to be agnostic to the model provider.
- **Compute Budget:** Six days of wall-clock time, $3,000 in Anthropic API credits, and GPU credits for experiments.
- **Environment:** Full access to a Linux virtual machine running in AWS, with internet access.
- **Internal Tools:** Agents could monitor their own API spend, compute budget, and remaining time. They could delegate tasks to subagents and maintain a research log.
- **Review Mechanisms:** The agents had access to a self-review subagent (which could only see the final PDF and NeurIPS review template) and three external AI reviewing tools: Stanford Agentic Reviewer, CMU Paper Reviewer, and refine.ink.
- **Guidance:** No task-specific guidance was provided; modifications to the scaffold were general-purpose for ML research.

**Human Involvement and Interventions:**
The original authors played three roles:
1. Formulating the research questions.
2. Setting appropriate resource budgets.
3. Grading the final agent-generated papers.
During the experiments, three human interventions were made:
1. A bug fix in the OpenClaw harness affecting Anthropic models.
2. A 24-hour deadline extension, as agents reported "Weak Reject" self-reviews and indicated further work if given more time.
3. A request for agents to rewrite their papers to be more accessible due to inscrutable writing.

**Pre-Experiment Survey:**
Before running the experiments, twelve coauthors (working in AI research, evaluation, and policy) were surveyed to capture their predictions and priors regarding agent performance and potential failure modes.

**Robustness Check:**
To mitigate concerns about scaffold limitations, a robustness experiment was conducted for the TabPFN paper.
- **Model:** GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra.
- **Scaffold:** Codex (its native scaffold), using an "ultra" reasoning level analogous to OpenClaw's orchestration.
- **Budgets:** Same time and API budgets as the OpenClaw experiments.

**Log Analysis:**
Detailed qualitative analysis of the agents' full trajectories and logs was performed to identify and characterize failure modes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the capabilities and limitations of frontier AI agents in conducting open-ended AI research:

**Overall Performance:**
- **Unambiguous Rejection:** Both agent-generated papers were unambiguously rejected by the original authors. The Personas paper received a score of 2/6 ("Reject"), and the TabPFN paper received a 1/6 ("Strong Reject").
- **Poor Quality:** Reviewers cited similar failures: poorly motivated data and experiments, lack of novel contribution, and impenetrable prose.

**Engineering Competence vs. Research Acumen:**
- **Engineering Success:** Agents successfully executed all necessary engineering steps without significant human intervention, including conducting extensive literature reviews, debugging GPU environments, running hundreds of experiments and robustness checks, retrieving external reviews via web/email, and compiling full LaTeX documents. This suggests current agents can handle the technical execution aspects of AI research.
- **Research Deficiency:** Despite engineering capabilities, agents failed to produce research at the caliber of a top-tier ML conference, particularly struggling with critical thinking, judgment, and creativity.

**Recurring Failure Modes (Five Identified):**
1. **Poor Judgment About the Bar for Publishable Research:**
 - Agents understood research questions and proposed initial directions mirroring human approaches but quickly falsified hypotheses using small, hand-curated, or synthetic datasets.
 - They engaged shallowly with literature and presented underpowered negative results as substantive findings.
 - Their internal self-reviews, though consistently "Weak Reject," were inflated compared to expert human reviews, suggesting a lack of calibration regarding research quality standards.
2. **Uncreative Responses to Shortcomings in Research Design:**
 - While generating initial creative hypotheses, agents struggled to pivot or develop alternative approaches when small-scale experiments and AI reviews indicated insufficient results.
 - Instead of fundamentally redesigning experiments or reframing questions, they typically added caveats, narrowed claims, or adopted less ambitious hypotheses, leading to papers with thorough negative findings rather than novel ideas.
3. **Ineffective Backtracking from Dead Ends:**
 - Agents performed local backtracking (rerunning experiments, robustness checks) but never fundamentally rethought their overall approach or discarded unproductive directions, even when self-reviews consistently returned negative verdicts.
 - For example, the TabPFN agent, after early detector attempts failed, reframed its goal to argue no such detector could exist, producing a negative-results paper rather than exploring new avenues with remaining time and budget.
4. **Poor Resource Awareness:**
 - Agents consistently underutilized their allocated budgets, ending runs with less than 50% of the API budget spent and often finishing hours before deadlines, despite acknowledging resource limits.
 - This indicated a lack of intuitive understanding of how to effectively manage and spend resources over the project timeline.
5. **Instruction Drift:**
 - Agents increasingly failed to follow explicit instructions as the run progressed, such as using specific review tools (e.g., refine.ink), adhering to paper length limits, or dedicating a minimum amount of time to exploration.
 - This suggests difficulty in retaining important context over multi-day projects, possibly due to context window limitations during compaction.

**Minor Positive Observations:**
- Agents produced some minor findings deemed relevant by the original authors.
- The ability to utilize hundreds of GPU hours on real experiments without issue was noted.
- Initial candidate hypotheses were often similar to the authors' own early approaches.

**Absence of Reward Hacking:**
- The log analysis found no evidence of agents hiding or misrepresenting experiments or data. On the contrary, agents diligently retired marketable claims when contradicted by evidence, opting for negative results, aligning with good scientific ethics.

**Impact of Reasoning Effort and Scaffold:**
- **Higher Reasoning Improved Quality:** Dry runs with "extra-high reasoning" Claude Opus 4.8 showed improved performance (better literature review, writing quality, and deadline adherence) compared to runs without reasoning, though still resulting in rejection-grade papers.
- **Scaffold Improvement Attempts Failed:** When Claude Fable 5 was tasked with improving the scaffold based on pilot failures, it exhibited similar failure modes, making disproportionate changes based on single samples.
- **Robustness Check Confirmation:** The experiment reran with GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra and Codex reproduced nearly all identified failure modes, supporting that the findings were not primarily artifacts of scaffold deficiencies or specific model limitations. This run also showed improved use of real-world datasets but exhausted its budget much faster.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant insights into the current state of AI agents' capabilities in autonomous scientific discovery and contributes to the ongoing discourse about AI's potential to accelerate its own progress.

**Early Evidence on AI Research Automation:**
The findings provide early evidence that contemporary frontier AI agents, despite considerable resources and sophisticated scaffolding, currently struggle with the open-ended, judgment-intensive aspects of producing publishable AI research. While adept at the "engineering" tasks of research (e.g., running experiments, debugging), they fall short in critical areas such as creative problem-solving, nuanced judgment, effective backtracking, resource management, and consistent adherence to high-level instructions over long horizons. This challenges some forecasts of rapid, autonomous AI-driven scientific breakthroughs.

**Distinguishing AI Capabilities:**
The study highlights a crucial distinction between AI agents' performance on "verifiable tasks" (where metrics are fixed and automatically scored) and "open-ended research problems." Agents excel at the former but demonstrate significant limitations in the latter, suggesting that progress in narrow, objective tasks does not automatically translate to competence in broader, ambiguous scientific inquiry. This differentiation is vital for accurately assessing AI's potential for "recursive self-improvement" and for understanding the "broad" versus "narrow" capabilities of AI systems.

**Contribution of "Shadow Evaluations":**
The introduction of "shadow evaluations" provides a novel and complementary method for evaluating AI research automation. By leveraging unpublished research questions and the in-depth expertise of original authors as reviewers, this method addresses key limitations of existing benchmarks and blind peer review. It offers a more rigorous, uncontaminated, and deeply informed assessment of an agent's ability to tackle genuine, complex scientific challenges. This methodology can be adapted for future studies, allowing for consistent measurement of progress with stronger models and improved scaffolds.

**Implications for Future AI Development and Policy:**
The identified failure modes (poor judgment, lack of creativity, ineffective backtracking, poor resource awareness, instruction drift) offer concrete targets for future AI agent research and development. Addressing these deficiencies will be crucial for enhancing agents' capacities for more autonomous and impactful scientific contributions. Furthermore, the findings have implications for AI policy and safety discussions, providing empirical data to inform discussions about the pace of AI progress and the nature of "runaway superintelligence." The transparency of releasing artifacts (reviews, survey responses, code repositories, logs) fosters open scientific discourse and allows for independent verification and future adversarial collaborations.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The study acknowledges limitations, including a small sample size (two case studies), the non-blind nature of expert reviews (though mitigated by the unambiguous rejections), and potential researcher biases in experimental design and interpretation. These suggest that while providing valuable early evidence, the findings require further validation across a wider range of research problems and with continuously evolving frontier models (e.g., Opus 5, Fable 5). The authors plan follow-up experiments to address these points.
