# H-VLA: Hierarchical Vision-Language-Action Model with Key-Action Reasoning and Motion Planning in a Unified Action Space

> [!summary] Summary
> H-VLA is a hierarchical vision-language-action framework that decouples subgoal reasoning from motion generation. A diffusion Key-Action Model predicts the next subgoal end-effector pose and gripper state, a Motion Planning Model generates dense action chunks conditioned on it, and states and actions live in a unified camera-centric space. It reaches 91% on Google Robot visual matching and beats the best real-robot baseline by 47 points under position shift.

## Problem

- Most VLA models map language and RGB observations directly to dense actions, weakening the semantic reasoning that pre-trained vision-language models were optimized to provide.
- Direct dense-action prediction is fragile under spatial variation: changed object positions, scene layouts, robot embodiments and camera viewpoints all shift the base-frame action distribution.
- Prior intermediate-reasoning work uses language traces, affordances or image-space subgoals, and camera-grounded policies exist, but none jointly close reasoning, dense control and cross-embodiment consistency.

## Method

- **Key-Action Model**: ==diffusion transformer== conditioned on VLM cognition token and encoded end-effector state, predicts next subgoal ==6-DoF pose== plus gripper; labels auto-derived from gripper-state transitions and trajectory ends.
- **Motion Planning Model**: second ==diffusion transformer== generating 16-step frame-to-frame delta action chunks, conditioned on the key-action, cognition and CLS visual features, wrist-view features and a wrist-camera availability mask.
- **Unified Camera-Centric Action Space** plus two-stage training: base-to-camera extrinsics map all states, key-actions and actions into the ==third-person camera frame==; pre-training weights key-action loss 10:1, fine-tuning weights equally.

## Results

- On SimplerEnv, H-VLA averages **91%** Google Robot visual matching, **84%** variant aggregation and **81%** WidowX visual matching, topping DAM-VLA's **83/81/71%**; two-step Drawer+Apple hits **98%** versus **78%**.
- On Agilex dual-arm tasks with **150** demonstrations, H-VLA scores **93%** in-distribution versus **83%** for the best baseline, **83%** under OOD position versus **36%**, and **66%** under OOD scene/object versus **50%**.
- Ablations: removing mixed pre-training and two-stage training drops Google Robot visual matching from **91%** to **85%**, removing camera-centric actions to **68%**, and removing key-actions to **56%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting a structured 6-DoF subgoal pose, rather than language or image-space hints, gives a dense controller a concrete target and preserves the VLM's reasoning role.
> - Expressing states and actions in the third-person camera frame is the single largest robustness lever here, letting heterogeneous embodiments and viewpoints share supervision.
> - Caution: gripper-transition labels miss non-prehensile contact events, and camera-centric actions assume reliable extrinsics whose calibration sensitivity was never measured.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models extend vision-language models to robotic control by mapping camera observations and natural-language instructions to robot actions. Early systems such as RT-2 demonstrated that semantic knowledge from large-scale vision-language pre-training could transfer to embodied tasks. OpenVLA and related models further developed open or general-purpose robot policies, while systems such as CogACT and the π series introduced diffusion- or flow-based continuous action generation to improve temporal consistency and control quality.

A central design choice in many VLA models is direct prediction of dense low-level actions from images and language. This approach provides a simple end-to-end interface, but it places semantic reasoning, spatial grounding, trajectory generation, and embodiment-specific control in the same prediction problem. The paper argues that this formulation is difficult for a VLM whose original training objective is primarily visual and linguistic understanding. It can also be sensitive to changes in object location, camera viewpoint, scene arrangement, and robot morphology.

H-VLA belongs to a second research direction that introduces intermediate representations between perception and control. Related methods use language reasoning traces, affordances, visual subgoals, image-space waypoints, or relational keypoints. H-VLA differs by representing an intermediate subgoal as a structured three-dimensional end-effector target: a six-degree-of-freedom pose plus gripper state. It also combines this hierarchy with a camera-centric action representation intended to make data from different robots and datasets more compatible.

The paper therefore addresses three connected problems: how to preserve semantic reasoning while generating precise motions, how to represent intermediate manipulation goals, and how to train one model across single-arm and dual-arm datasets with different robot coordinate systems.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Main Objectives

The work aims to develop a VLA policy that separates the decision about what the robot should accomplish next from the generation of the motion used to accomplish it. H-VLA decomposes manipulation into two stages:

1. A Key-Action Model predicts the next salient manipulation subgoal.
2. A Motion Planning Model generates a sequence of low-level actions conditioned on that subgoal.

The key-action is expressed as an absolute end-effector position, orientation, and gripper state. In this formulation, a task such as opening a drawer and placing an apple can be represented through intermediate targets associated with grasping, opening, releasing, and final placement.

A second objective is cross-embodiment learning. Demonstrations from Franka, WidowX, Google Robot, and Agilex platforms normally express poses relative to different robot bases. H-VLA transforms those quantities into the coordinate frame of the third-person camera. The authors intend this representation to align action supervision with the visual frame used by the VLM and to reduce differences between datasets.

A third objective is to establish a training procedure that first emphasizes transferable subgoal reasoning and later emphasizes detailed motion generation. The underlying motivation is that key-action prediction may provide a more general learning target across heterogeneous datasets than direct dense action prediction.

#### 2.2. Hypothesized Benefits

The hierarchy is intended to reduce the burden on the VLM by allowing it to produce a semantically meaningful target rather than an entire sequence of low-level commands. The motion model can then use local wrist observations, proprioceptive information, and the predicted target to generate context-dependent trajectories. The authors also hypothesize that a camera-centric representation may improve robustness to object-position and viewpoint changes because the action coordinates are directly related to the image frame.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Model Architecture

H-VLA uses a Prismatic-7B vision-language model. Third-person RGB images are processed by DINOv2 and SigLIP visual encoders, while language is handled through a LLaMA-2 language backbone. A learnable action token is appended to the multimodal sequence, and its final hidden state serves as a cognition feature for action-related prediction. The DINOv2 class token provides an additional global visual feature.

Optional left and right wrist images are processed by the same visual encoder. A wrist-camera availability mask indicates which views are present, allowing the model to retain a fixed input interface when one or both wrist cameras are unavailable.

Both the Key-Action Model and Motion Planning Model are diffusion-based transformer modules. The Key-Action Model predicts one structured target. The Motion Planning Model predicts a chunk of future actions.

#### 3.2. Action Representation

For a single arm, the state and key-action are represented by a seven-dimensional vector:

\[
[x,y,z,\phi,\theta,\psi,g],
\]

where the first three values specify position, the next three specify Euler orientation, and \(g\) specifies the gripper state. Dual-arm representations concatenate the left and right vectors into a 14-dimensional representation.

The current state and key-action are absolute poses. By contrast, position and orientation in future action commands are frame-to-frame deltas, while the gripper remains an absolute binary state. The final model uses a shared 14-dimensional interface. For single-arm demonstrations, the seven-dimensional data are replicated into both arm branches rather than zero-padded or masked. The authors report that this replication strategy reduced distribution differences between single-arm and dual-arm data during joint training.

#### 3.3. Key-Action Construction

Key-actions are generated automatically from demonstration trajectories. A frame immediately following a gripper opening or closing transition is designated as a key-action. The final frame of every trajectory is also included. At each time step, the target is the nearest future key-action.

For dual-arm data, key-actions are extracted independently for each arm. Consequently, the left and right arms can have different future key-action times, which allows the representation to capture sequential activities such as handover.

This labeling procedure is practical because it does not require manual subgoal annotation. However, it assumes that important manipulation events correlate with gripper-state changes. The appendix documents failure cases in pushing, pouring, drawer opening, and object movement, where critical events occur without a gripper transition.

#### 3.4. Motion Planning

The Motion Planning Model receives the VLM cognition feature, global visual feature, wrist-view features, current end-effector state, and key-action. During training, it is conditioned on the ground-truth key-action; during inference, it uses the key-action predicted by the Key-Action Model.

The model generates a future chunk of 16 action commands. Longer behaviors are produced through repeated closed-loop queries. The use of a fixed action horizon means that the key-action distance does not directly determine the number of commands in a single query.

#### 3.5. Camera-Centric Transformation

For each dataset, the end-effector pose is transformed from the robot-base frame into the third-person camera frame using calibrated camera-to-base extrinsics. The same transformation is applied to current states, key-actions, and future action labels.

Datasets with available calibration, such as DROID, use their provided parameters. RoboCOIN and the newly collected Agilex data use Perspective-n-Point self-calibration based on annotated image points and forward-kinematics-derived gripper positions. BridgeDataV2 and FractalData use SimplerEnv calibration, with additional corrections for selected BridgeDataV2 tasks.

#### 3.6. Training Strategy and Data

Stage one, termed Key-Action-Centric Pre-training, uses approximately 183,000 processed trajectories from RoboCOIN Cobot Magic, DROID, BridgeDataV2, and FractalData. The key-action loss is weighted ten times more heavily than the dense-action loss. Stage two fine-tunes the model on downstream data with equal weights for key-action and action losses. All model parameters, including the VLM, are updated in both stages.

For simulation transfer, the model is fine-tuned on FractalData or BridgeDataV2 and evaluated in SimplerEnv. For real-robot adaptation, 150 demonstrations are collected on an Agilex dual-arm platform across PickBottles, HandOver, and LiftPot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

On SimplerEnv, H-VLA reports average success rates of 91% on Google Robot Visual Matching, 84% on Google Robot Variant Aggregation, and 81% on WidowX Visual Matching.

Its task-level results include 98% on the Google Robot Drawer+Apple task under Visual Matching, which requires opening a drawer and then placing an apple. This result is relevant to the paper’s central claim because the task involves sequential manipulation stages rather than a single isolated action. On WidowX, H-VLA obtains 96% on Spoon Towel, 88% on Carrot Plate, 58% on Stack, and 83% on Eggplant Basket.

The comparison includes several end-to-end and structured-action baselines. H-VLA exceeds the reported average of DAM-VLA by 8 percentage points on Google Robot Visual Matching, 3 points on Google Robot Variant Aggregation, and 10 points on WidowX Visual Matching. The results support the usefulness of combining intermediate targets with camera-centric action supervision, although the baselines differ in pre-training data and evaluation provenance. Some results were reproduced by the authors, while others were taken from prior publications.

#### 4.2. Real-Robot Results

On the Agilex dual-arm benchmark, H-VLA achieves 93% average success under in-distribution conditions, with task-level rates of 100% for PickBottles, 90% for HandOver, and 90% for LiftPot.

Under out-of-distribution position changes, it achieves 83% on average, compared with 36% for MolmoAct2 and 31% for both π0 and π0.5. Under broader scene and object changes, H-VLA achieves 66%, compared with 50% for π0.5 and 44% for π0. The advantage is not uniform across tasks: π0 and π0.5 perform better than H-VLA on LiftPot in the scene/object condition, while H-VLA performs better on PickBottles and HandOver.

The real-robot data are limited to 150 demonstrations and three tasks, so these results indicate transfer within a constrained dual-arm tabletop setting rather than broad validation across many robot platforms.

#### 4.3. Ablation Results

Removing mixed single-/dual-arm pre-training and staged training lowers the SimplerEnv averages from 91% to 85% on Google Robot Visual Matching, from 84% to 81% on Variant Aggregation, and from 81% to 74% on WidowX.

Removing the camera-centric representation produces larger reductions, to 68%, 59%, and 68%, respectively. Removing the Key-Action Model as well reduces the corresponding averages to 56%, 60%, and 54%. These results suggest that the camera-centric representation and mixed pre-training are important contributors, while key-action conditioning provides additional benefits, particularly in Google Robot Visual Matching and WidowX.

The stage-one checkpoint, before downstream fine-tuning, still achieves 73%, 49%, and 53% on the three simulation splits. This indicates that key-action-focused pre-training also produces usable dense-action predictions, although stage-two adaptation substantially improves performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

H-VLA contributes a concrete intermediate representation for hierarchical robot control. Unlike language-only reasoning traces or two-dimensional visual waypoints, its key-action is an executable three-dimensional end-effector target that can directly condition a motion generator. This may offer a practical mechanism for connecting VLM semantic representations with continuous control.

The unified camera-centric action space is also relevant to generalist robot learning. If camera calibration is available, demonstrations from different embodiments can be converted into a common visual coordinate system. This creates the possibility of sharing subgoal and motion supervision across robots without requiring all demonstrations to use the same robot-base convention.

The reported results indicate improved robustness to object-position and scene changes in the evaluated settings, especially for sequential and bimanual tasks. The approach may therefore be useful for systems that must combine general semantic interpretation with relatively local motion execution.

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. Gripper-based labels do not reliably identify important events in non-prehensile, deformable, dynamic, or contact-rich manipulation. Camera-centric control depends on accurate camera–robot extrinsics, and sensitivity to calibration error was not systematically measured. The VLM primarily performs two-dimensional visual reasoning while the key-action is a three-dimensional target, leaving a remaining grounding problem. Finally, the model has a 7.7-billion-parameter scale and approximately 103 ms end-to-end inference latency on an RTX 4090, which may affect deployment on lower-power hardware. These issues define important directions for future work, including learned key-action discovery, explicit contact modeling, calibration-aware policies, and evaluation across broader embodiments and manipulation regimes.
