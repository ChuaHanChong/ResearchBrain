# INSIGHT: INference-time Sequence Introspection for Generating Help Triggers in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> INSIGHT is an introspection framework that lets an autoregressive vision-language-action policy decide when to request human help. Running alongside pi-0-FAST, it extracts per-token entropy, negative log-probability, and Dirichlet-based aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, then feeds these sequences to a sub-million-parameter transformer classifier. Strong step-level supervision reaches 0.887 accuracy and 0.782 F1 in-distribution, far above conformal-prediction thresholds.

## Problem

- VLA models autoregressively emit low-level action tokens with no introspective mechanism to anticipate failure, signal uncertainty, or query a human supervisor during deployment.
- Prior uncertainty-alignment work targets LLM planners choosing among a few discrete symbolic actions, so it does not transfer to long variable-length continuous control token sequences.
- Conformal prediction and perplexity baselines collapse uncertainty into one sequence-level score, missing the gradual drift and compounding control error that characterizes VLA failure.

## Method

- ==Uncertainty feature extraction== builds a 4-dimensional per-token vector (==entropy==, ==negative log-probability==, ==aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty==) from ==LogTokU== Dirichlet evidence over top-K logits.
- A compact ==transformer encoder== (64 hidden, 4 heads, sinusoidal positions, ==masked attention pooling==, roughly 300k parameters) maps the padded 4xN per-step feature matrix to a help score.
- ==Weak supervision== recasts help prediction as ==multi-instance learning==: step logits pool via ==log-sum-exp== with temperature 6.0 into an episode success/failure logit trained by cross-entropy.

## Results

- In-distribution against step labels, strong supervision reaches **0.887** accuracy and **0.782** F1 versus **0.502** accuracy and **0.442** F1 for entropy-based conformal prediction near chance.
- Weak supervision is conservative: only **0.185** recall and **0.288** F1 against strong labels, yet competitive under weak-label evaluation (**0.812** accuracy, **0.751** F1 under distribution shift).
- Real-world-trained strong models transfer to simulated LIBERO out-of-distribution rollouts, the jumbo variant hitting **0.820** accuracy and **0.748** F1 versus **0.936** for the sim-only oracle.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Uncertainty in autoregressive control is temporal, not scalar: modeling how token-level signals evolve beats thresholding any aggregated sequence score.
> - Label quality dominates dataset size; adding distribution-shift data slightly hurt strongly-supervised accuracy while denser step labels consistently improved fidelity.
> - Supervision choice sets the safety-versus-unobtrusiveness knob: step labels trigger early and often, episode labels rarely interrupt successes but intervene late.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an advancement in robotic policies, offering capabilities to map observations and language instructions to action sequences through autoregressive token prediction. These models, exemplified by architectures like π0-FAST, OpenVLA, RT-1, and RT-2, are pre-trained on large datasets and demonstrate generalization across diverse sensory inputs and action spaces. However, a limitation of current VLA models is their lack of introspective mechanisms. They typically do not provide feedback on data needs, signal uncertainty in predictions, or possess the ability to request human intervention when a failure is imminent. This absence poses challenges for safe and reliable robot operation, particularly in unstructured or out-of-distribution (OOD) environments where model performance can degrade, leading to task failures or unsafe behaviors.

The broader research landscape has explored introspection, particularly uncertainty quantification, in large language models (LLMs). Approaches like entropy, perplexity, and Dirichlet-based methods for distinguishing aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty have been applied to detect unreliable outputs or determine when LLM-based planners should abstain from action. For example, KnowNo demonstrated conformal prediction for LLM-based planners to identify uncertainty in high-level action selection. However, directly applying these LLM-centric introspection methods to VLAs is challenging. LLM-based planners operate on discrete, symbolic actions, whereas VLAs generate continuous, low-level control sequences from long, variable-length token sequences. Errors in VLA policies can manifest gradually within an action sequence, requiring a fine-grained, temporal understanding of uncertainty rather than a single, high-level confidence score.

This work addresses the research gap concerning introspection in VLAs. It aims to develop a framework for VLA models to recognize and signal when their predictions are unreliable, thereby enabling them to request human help. This aligns with a human-in-the-loop, lifelong learning paradigm, where models can improve iteratively by querying human supervisors during uncertain situations and incorporating that feedback. The paper specifically investigates the utility of token-level uncertainty signals for help prediction in embodied VLA settings, a domain where such methods have not been systematically evaluated.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current VLA models regarding introspection. While these models can execute complex tasks, they lack the ability to anticipate failures or communicate their uncertainty, which is critical for safe and robust deployment in real-world, dynamic environments. The authors envision a future where robots equipped with VLA models can participate in iterative learning cycles: training, deployment, selective human querying based on introspection, and subsequent model improvement. This paper focuses on the "introspection step" within this cycle.

To address this, the research sets out to answer two main questions:

**Research Question #1: Can uncertainty signals extracted from token-level probability distributions at inference time reliably predict when a VLA should request human help?**
This objective focuses on whether fine-grained, token-level probabilistic cues, which have been explored in LLMs, can be effectively translated and leveraged within the VLA context. The paper introduces INSIGHT to process these signals and make step-by-step predictions for help requests. The motivation here is to move beyond simple success/failure detection to proactive intervention based on internal model state.

**Research Question #2: How does the source of training labels affect this capability for within-/out-of-distribution tasks?**
This objective investigates the practical aspects of training introspection models. Obtaining dense, step-level annotations for "needs help" is resource-intensive and potentially subjective. In contrast, episode-level success/failure outcomes are easier to obtain but provide a coarser, noisier signal. The research aims to compare two supervision paradigms:
- **Strong supervision:** An expert provides binary "needs help" or "no help" labels for each action timestep. This is expected to yield higher-fidelity models but comes at a higher annotation cost.
- **Weak supervision:** The model learns from only episode-level outcomes (success/failure), framed as a multi-instance learning problem where the model must infer which steps within a failed episode might have required help. This offers a more scalable labeling approach.

The overarching goal is to systematically evaluate the trade-offs between labeling effort, predictive accuracy, and generalization capabilities across different task distributions, ultimately informing how introspective VLA systems can be practically developed and deployed. The paper also aims to demonstrate that temporal modeling of uncertainty signals is crucial for VLA introspection, in contrast to static, sequence-level metrics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The INSIGHT framework is designed to integrate with existing autoregressive VLA models, specifically π0-FAST, to provide real-time help triggers. The methodology comprises several key stages: uncertainty feature extraction, temporal encoding via a transformer classifier, and training under different supervision regimes.

**Underlying VLA Model and Tokenization:**
The base policy used is π0-FAST, an autoregressive VLA model that translates observations (language instruction, RGB image, robot state) into sequences of action tokens. At each step `t`, the model predicts a sequence of tokens `T_1:n_t`, which are then de-tokenized into a continuous action "chunk" `a_1:H_t`. A key characteristic is that π0-FAST produces a variable number of tokens (`n`) for a fixed action chunk size (`H`), meaning individual tokens do not directly correspond to single actions.

**Uncertainty Feature Extraction:**
For each predicted token `i` within a step `t`, INSIGHT extracts a 4-dimensional feature vector `u_i_t` from its predictive probability distribution `P_i_t` and logits. These features are:
1. **Entropy:** Measures the spread of the model's output distribution, indicating low-confidence predictions.
2. **Negative Log-Probability:** Related to perplexity, quantifying model "surprise" and serving as a proxy for confidence.
3. **Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU):** Derived from a Dirichlet distribution over logits (LogTokU framework), capturing inherent data ambiguity.
4. **Epistemic Uncertainty (EU):** Also derived from the Dirichlet distribution, reflecting the model's knowledge gaps.
These token-level features are aggregated into a `4xN` matrix `u_t` for each step `t`, where `N` is a fixed maximum token length, with padding for shorter sequences.

**Classifier Model Architecture:**
INSIGHT employs a compact transformer-based classifier to process these sequential uncertainty features:
- **Input Processing:** Token embeddings are projected to a 64-dimensional hidden space and enriched with sinusoidal positional embeddings.
- **Transformer Encoder:** A Transformer encoder with one self-attention layer and four heads processes the embedded token sequences. This enables the model to capture non-local and irregular temporal patterns in the uncertainty signals.
- **Prediction Head:** The encoded tokens are aggregated using masked attention pooling and passed through a two-layer feed-forward network (32 hidden units) to produce a step-level help score `r_t` (a probability between 0 and 1).
- **Parameter Count:** The strong supervision model has approximately 300k parameters. The weak supervision model, with an additional pooling operation, has approximately 500k parameters.

**Training Paradigms (Supervision Regimes):**
1. **Strong Supervision:** Each step `t` within an episode is annotated with a binary label `y_t ∈ {0, 1}` indicating whether help was needed (i.e., if the action did not contribute to task progress). The classifier is trained with binary cross-entropy loss based on these step-level labels.
2. **Weak Supervision:** Each episode `E^(e)` receives a binary label `Y^(e) ∈ {0, 1}` for success or failure, respectively. This assumes that successful episodes required no help, while failed episodes required help at at least one step. Step logits `l_t` are pooled into an episode-level logit `~l^(e)` using log-sum-exp pooling with a temperature parameter `λ = 6.0`. The episode prediction `Y^(e)` is then optimized with episode-level binary cross-entropy. This setup addresses the multi-instance learning problem, where the model learns to localize predictive patterns preceding failure without explicit step-level labels.

**Data Collection and Pre-training:**
- **Fine-tuning π0-FAST:** The base π0-FAST model was fine-tuned on a custom dataset collected using an xArm7 manipulator in a toy kitchen environment. This dataset, comprising 80,419 action steps across 5 task types (Lift, Put, Knock, Wipe, Stir), adapted π0-FAST to the specific robot and task settings.
- **Rollout Data for Introspection:** Rollouts were collected by deploying the fine-tuned π0-FAST policy across various settings:
 - **In-distribution:** 160 policy rollouts (episodes) on four tasks with standard configurations.
 - **Distribution-shift:** 469 rollouts for the same tasks but with novel environmental configurations (varied object locations, orientations, unseen objects).
 - **Simulated Out-of-Distribution (OOD):** 500 rollouts from a π0-FAST model fine-tuned on the LIBERO dataset, representing a significant shift in task families and policy behavior.
- **Labeling Process:**
 - **Strong Labels:** Each timestep was manually annotated based on whether the robot's action contributed to task progress. This process acknowledged potential subjectivity but aimed for consistent application.
 - **Weak Labels:** Episodes were labeled as success or failure based on task completion within maximum steps, providing objective but coarser supervision.

**Evaluation Metrics and Baselines:**
The framework was evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score for classification performance. For real-time analysis, Time-to-First-Help (TTFH), Trigger Count (for successful and failed episodes), and Trigger Rate were used.
Baselines included Conformal Prediction (CP) with nonconformity scores based on entropy (CP-E) and sequence-level perplexity (CP-P). CP was calibrated using both strong and weak labels to provide distribution-free guarantees on missed help events under ideal conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of INSIGHT, alongside Conformal Prediction (CP) baselines, was conducted across various distributional settings and supervision regimes. The main findings are summarized as follows:

**1. Predictive Power of Uncertainty Metrics:**
Token-level uncertainty signals (entropy, log-probability, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty) consistently demonstrated predictive power for help requests, with accuracy and F1 scores generally above random guessing (F1 > 0.5). This indicates that these internal model signals are actionable for introspection in VLA models. The exception was the weak-training/strong-testing case under distribution shift, where F1 dropped below 0.5 due to supervision/evaluation mismatch.

**2. Superiority of Temporal Modeling (INSIGHT) over Aggregate Scores (CP):**
INSIGHT, which leverages the sequential structure of token-level uncertainty signals with transformers, consistently outperformed Conformal Prediction (CP) baselines that rely on aggregated, sequence-level scores.
- In-distribution tests (Fig. 3a): Strongly-supervised INSIGHT achieved an F1 score of 0.782 ± 0.075 when tested with strong labels, significantly higher than CP-S (entropy) at 0.450 ± 0.096 and CP-W (entropy) at 0.442 ± 0.094. When tested with weak labels, weakly-supervised INSIGHT reached an F1 of 0.751 ± 0.126, which was comparable to CP-W (entropy) at 0.710 ± 0.111, but still demonstrating better balance.
- CP often exhibited near-chance performance in accuracy and F1, especially when evaluated against strong labels (e.g., Fig. 3a, strong training/strong testing). This indicates that sequence-level aggregation of uncertainty is insufficient to capture the fine-grained, temporal dynamics of VLA errors.

**3. Trade-offs between Strong and Weak Supervision:**
- **Strong Supervision:** Models trained with strong (step-level) labels generally provided the most reliable and precise performance, achieving the highest F1 scores across most conditions, particularly when evaluated against strong labels. For instance, in the in-distribution test with strong labels, strong supervision yielded an F1 of 0.782. Qualitatively, these models reacted earlier to subtle deviations and increased uncertainty (Appendix A). However, strong labeling is resource-intensive and potentially subjective.
- **Weak Supervision:** Models trained with weak (episode-level) labels were more conservative in triggering help, leading to lower recall and F1 scores when evaluated against strong labels. For example, in the in-distribution test with strong labels, weak supervision had an F1 of 0.288. However, when evaluated under weak labels, they remained competitive with strong supervision (e.g., F1 of 0.751 for weak supervision vs. 0.821 for strong supervision in large in-distribution test, Fig. 3c), suggesting it is a viable, scalable alternative when dense annotation is impractical. Qualitatively, these models tended to detect failures later in the episode (Appendix A).

**4. Timing and Frequency of Help Triggers (Table I):**
- **Strongly-supervised INSIGHT:** Triggered help earliest (TTFH (fail) = 5.597 ± 0.809) and most frequently in failed episodes (Trigger Count = 7.062 ± 1.225), maximizing failure coverage but also had a higher trigger rate in successful episodes (0.047 ± 0.029), indicating potential over-intervention.
- **Weakly-supervised INSIGHT:** Was more conservative, triggering help later (TTFH (fail) = 7.929 ± 1.867) and less frequently (Trigger Count = 1.566 ± 1.025), with a very low trigger rate in successful episodes (0.008 ± 0.011), but risked late or missed interventions.
- **CP-W (Entropy):** Struck a middle ground with moderate timing (TTFH (fail) = 6.891 ± 2.257) and frequency.

**5. Transferability under Distribution Shift:**
- **Limited Distribution Shift (Fig. 3b):** All models experienced performance degradation, but strongly-supervised INSIGHT models exhibited greater resilience. For instance, when tested with strong labels, strongly-supervised INSIGHT maintained an F1 of 0.532 ± 0.101, while weakly-supervised INSIGHT dropped to 0.167 ± 0.049. When tested with weak labels, both strong and weak INSIGHT variants showed competitive F1 scores (0.821 ± 0.038 and 0.686 ± 0.081 respectively).
- **Significant OOD Simulation Shift (Fig. 4, Table VIII):** Surprisingly, INSIGHT models trained on real-world data demonstrated effective transfer to a simulated OOD setting (LIBERO dataset) despite substantial differences in environments and task families. The strongly-supervised "jumbo" model (trained on the largest real-world dataset) achieved an F1 of 0.748 ± 0.142 and accuracy of 0.820 ± 0.057, approaching the performance of a model specifically trained on the simulation data (sim-only, F1=0.918). This suggests that token-level uncertainty features possess robustness across environments and policy checkpoints.

**6. Effect of Data Scale:**
Expanding the in-distribution training dataset with distribution-shift data (creating a "large in-distribution" dataset) did not consistently improve robustness. Strongly-supervised transformers saw slight decreases in accuracy and F1 in this setting, potentially due to added variability (Fig. 3c). Weakly-supervised transformers benefited more from increased data when evaluated on weak labels, but overall, label quality was a more dominant factor than dataset size for performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study represents an initial systematic evaluation of uncertainty-based introspection in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, a previously underexplored area. The introduction of INSIGHT provides a foundational framework for enabling VLA models to recognize and signal when they require human assistance.

The key significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enabling Human-in-the-Loop Robotics:** By demonstrating the feasibility of using token-level uncertainty to generate help triggers, INSIGHT lays groundwork for more robust and reliable robotic systems. Robots equipped with such introspection capabilities could operate more safely in unstructured or unfamiliar environments, reducing the risk of failures or unsafe behaviors by proactively requesting intervention. This facilitates a continuous learning paradigm where human feedback can address immediate task failures and contribute to long-term model improvement.
2. **Highlighting the Importance of Temporal Modeling:** The research empirically demonstrates that modeling the sequential structure of token-level uncertainty signals with transformers provides significantly greater predictive power for help detection than static, sequence-level scores (as used in Conformal Prediction). This finding is crucial for VLA models, where errors often manifest gradually over an action sequence, distinguishing it from introspection challenges in high-level LLM planning.
3. **Characterizing Supervision Regimes:** The systematic comparison of strong (step-level) and weak (episode-level) supervision offers practical guidance for developing introspective VLA systems. Strong supervision, while more labor-intensive, leads to precise and reliable help detection, which is critical for safety-sensitive applications. Weak supervision, being more scalable and objective to collect, provides a competitive alternative when dense annotation is impractical, albeit with a trade-off in fidelity. This informs resource allocation and development strategies for real-world deployment.
4. **Robustness to Distribution Shifts:** The finding that strongly-supervised INSIGHT models exhibit effective transfer to significantly out-of-distribution (OOD) settings, even with changes in the underlying VLA policy, is notable. It suggests that the token-level uncertainty features captured by INSIGHT are relatively stable across different environments and model checkpoints. This property could reduce the need for extensive re-annotation or re-training of introspection modules when deploying VLA models in novel contexts.
5. **Opening New Research Avenues:** This work establishes a benchmark and opens several avenues for future research:
 - **Active Learning:** Uncertainty-guided help triggers can be used to selectively query humans for labels, facilitating more efficient data acquisition for model improvement.
 - **Lifelong Learning:** Introspection can drive continuous model improvement by integrating human feedback iteratively throughout a robot's operational lifespan.
 - **Hybrid Architectures:** The framework can be extended to emerging VLA architectures that combine autoregressive and diffusion-based action experts.
 - **Model Agnosticism:** Since the uncertainty metrics are derived from token-level probability distributions, the framework is presented as largely model-agnostic, implying potential applicability across a range of VLA policies.

In summary, INSIGHT contributes to the development of more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems by endowing VLA models with a crucial self-awareness capability, bridging the gap between advanced robotic control and human supervision.
