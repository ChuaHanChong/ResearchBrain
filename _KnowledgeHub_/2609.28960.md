# Echo in the Steps: Learning Perceptive Humanoid Parkour with Gated Memory

> [!summary] Summary
> Echo in the Steps is an end-to-end perceptive humanoid parkour framework that lets a 29-DoF Unitree G1 cross sparse footholds and narrow supports using only onboard depth. A saliency prior from vertical depth differences weights historical depth latents fused by a gated residual memory, and symmetry regularization with an alternation loss encourages alternating steps. Success rate reaches 92.7% across five terrains, beating a strong baseline by 16.7%.

## Problem

- Depth-conditioned locomotion policies give insufficient geometric evidence for foothold discrimination when support regions are sparse and only partially visible in egocentric views.
- Elevation-map and voxel-grid pipelines depend on accurate localization and consistent map updates, so they become unreliable during fast, highly dynamic parkour.
- Dense temporal history adds redundancy across adjacent frames, while uniform mirror-symmetry constraints collapse into synchronized two-legged hopping that degrades exploration and performance.

## Method

- ==Saliency prior==: a per-frame scalar from vertical depth differences weighted by row position, highlighting near-field depth discontinuities as foothold-critical without handcrafted obstacle rules or terrain-specific tuning.
- **Saliency-guided temporal perception**: relative saliency plus an MLP scorer and learnable frame bias softmax-weight the historical depth latents; a ==sigmoid-gated residual== injects them into the current latent.
- **Symmetry regularization**: mirror-consistency loss plus an ==alternation loss== penalizing positive cosine similarity between left and right leg action subvectors above a 0.25 base-speed threshold.

## Results

- Averaged over boxes, stakes, beams, wedges and trapezoids, success reaches **92.7%** and foothold accuracy **83.6%**, improving on the Hiking baseline by **16.7%** and **7.8%**.
- Ablation: adding the saliency prior raises success rate by **47.49 points** and foothold accuracy by **7.98**; gated memory adds a further **10.13 points**, reaching **98.27%**.
- Symmetry regularization lifts box-terrain success from **85.0%** to **96.7%**; the alternation loss cuts double-support ratio from **0.806** to **0.308** and improves training efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A cheap hand-derived saliency scalar steers learned temporal attention better than letting an MLP learn frame weights from scratch, which alone barely helped.
> - Residual gating keeps fused features anchored to the current observation, so history compensates for missing cues instead of overwriting present geometry.
> - Symmetry priors need an anti-degeneracy term; mirror consistency alone can collapse gaits into synchronized hopping that is unsafe on narrow supports.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A humanoid crossing separated, narrow supports has little room for a misplaced step. A single depth view may also miss some of the useful geometry. The robot must choose where to land and coordinate which foot lands next, often while seeing only part of the next foothold.

The approach, *Echo in the Steps*, has the controller remember the depth cues that matter for footholds instead of treating every visual frame alike. It also regularizes the legs toward alternating steps instead of synchronized hops. It does not add a separate explicit footstep planner. The paper shows perceptive parkour over sparse footholds and narrow supports.

The paper's Figure 1 shows six scenes: (a) trapezoids with large height variations, (b) discontinuous stepping boxes, (c) sparse footholds captioned as 20 cm in diameter, (d) irregularly oriented wedges, (e) a curved support captioned 20 cm wide, traversed at approximately 1 m/s, and (f) a narrow beam captioned 2 m long.

### 2. Keeping the useful glimpse

The mechanism works in two steps. First, a saliency prior marks local vertical depth discontinuities and weights regions closer to the robot more strongly. It then compresses this map into a scalar summary for each frame.

Second, a shared depth encoder turns current and historical frames into features. The system scores the historical features using their relative saliency and learned weights. It then injects the weighted feature differences through a learnable gate into the current feature. The current view remains the anchor, while selected past views can fill in missing or weak foothold cues. The fused feature joins proprioception to produce actions.

The paper's Figure 3. Left, "Saliency Weighting": historical latent features and relative saliency values are concatenated, scored by an MLP with learnable bias, and passed through softmax to produce weights. Right, "Gated Memory Update": weighted historical feature differences from the current latent are combined with the current latent through a sigmoid gate to form the fused latent.

The authors' ablation tests each part of this mechanism. It compares four conditions: uniform averaging, learned weighting, saliency-guided weighting, and saliency-guided weighting plus gated residual fusion. Averaged over five terrain categories, the success rates are 52.12%, 40.65%, 88.14%, and 98.27%, and foothold accuracy is 70.80%, 82.65%, 90.63%, and 91.10%.

In this aggregate, learned weighting alone did not improve success over uniform averaging. Adding the saliency prior is associated with a large improvement, and gated fusion further raises success. This is evidence from the tested settings; it does not show that saliency is the only cause.

A separate loss addresses the legs. Mirror consistency encourages bilateral coordination, and the authors add a loss that discourages similar left- and right-leg actions during movement, aiming to avoid synchronized hopping.

### 3. Against a strong baseline

The closest comparison is [[2601.07718|Hiking in the Wild: A Scalable Perceptive Parkour Framework for Humanoids]]. That prior system already maps depth and proprioception to humanoid locomotion actions and demonstrates parkour. The paper describes it as trained under the same setting.

Two metrics measure performance. Success rate is the share of trials reaching the end platform without ground contact or a fall. Foothold accuracy is the fraction of foot-contact samples on intended footholds during inter-platform steps, excluding stationary steps at the start and end platforms. Each simulation experiment uses 5,000 trials, with means and standard deviations reported across three random seeds.

On simulated trapezoids, success rate is 97.1 ± 0.7% for Echo in the Steps versus 64.2 ± 0.3% for Hiking. Foothold accuracy is 65.5 ± 0.5% versus 49.7 ± 0.7%.

The paper's Table 1, a simulation comparison of success rate (%) and foothold accuracy (%) for Hiking and Ours across boxes, stakes, beams, wedges, and trapezoids. Ours is higher on both metrics for all five listed terrain categories.

The real-robot results use 10 trials per terrain setting, with success rate as the primary measure. The authors report two limitations. Staggered foothold layouts can still produce poor support-leg choices and cross-stepping. A restricted camera field of view can also hide upcoming footholds during rapid heading changes.

### 4. Memory is not a plan

Retaining useful views can help a humanoid make more reliable local foothold decisions, as the ablation and the simulated comparison indicate. Remembering visual cues is still different from planning a whole sequence of steps. The paper itself reports failures on some staggered layouts and visibility problems during fast turns.
