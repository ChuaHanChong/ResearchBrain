# TAO-Force: Unifying Force-Aware Perception and Fast-Slow Control for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TAO-Force adapts a frozen GR00T N1.5 VLA for contact-rich manipulation by injecting wrist force/torque feedback through force-conditioned FiLM modulation, jointly generating action and desired-force chunks plus a contact probability. A contact-gated fast-slow controller tracks nominal trajectories in free space and switches to 1000 Hz admittance control during contact. On an AgiBot G1, it averages 80.0% in-domain and 64.2% out-of-domain success across four contact-rich tasks, beating GR00T N1.7 and π0.5.

## Problem

- Vision-centric, position-controlled VLAs see little evidence of contact onset or force magnitude and cannot respond compliantly to fast-changing contact dynamics in tasks like peeling or wiping.
- Force lacks a feature extractor pretrained at vision's scale, so during post-training pretrained visual representations dominate optimization and existing force-aware VLAs underuse force signals.
- Force-as-input methods execute position commands directly, while joint motion-force predictors emit targets without estimating contact state or coordinating transitions between non-contact and contact phases.

## Method

- **F-FiLM**: MLP and 1D-CNN encoders embed current force, force history, and robot state; a ==zero-initialized network== predicts residual scale and shift that modulate frozen Eagle visual-language features, preserving pretrained priors at initialization.
- A shared DiT flow-matches action and desired-force chunks via separate token streams, a classifier head predicts contact probability; ==contact-causal supervision== zeroes pre-contact force targets; ==force-attentive modality dropout== masks vision and proprioception in force-critical segments.
- **Contact-gated fast-slow controller**: thresholded contact probability switches between position tracking of densified references and 1000 Hz ==admittance control==; force-chunk blending and ==quintic smoothstep fusion== smooth phase transitions.

## Results

- Across four contact-rich tasks on AgiBot G1, TAO-Force averages **80.0%** ID and **64.2%** OOD success versus **63.3%**/**20.0%** for GR00T N1.7 and **61.7%**/**29.2%** for π0.5.
- Counterfactual force swapping on Blind Box Sorting flips routing in **90%** and **100%** of trajectories with F-FiLM, versus **20%**/**90%** for DePost and **0%**/**10%** for FVLMoE; real sorting succeeds **100%** ID and OOD.
- Ablations: F-FiLM alone lifts Peeling Cucumber OOD from **3.3%** to **23.3%**, force control alone to **60.0%**, both together to **86.7%**; F-FiLM cuts contact-phase force MSE from **35.1** to **4.1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Zero-initialized FiLM adds a low-data modality to a frozen backbone without erasing priors; counterfactual input swaps are a cheap test that the modality truly drives actions.
> - Supervising future force before contact risks teaching anticipatory shortcuts from visual or timing cues; gating labels by current contact state keeps force prediction grounded in actual interaction evidence.
> - Force-aware perception and compliant execution are complementary, not substitutes: each alone helps inconsistently across tasks, and regulating desired force tolerates contact-timing shifts better than tracking positions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have become a major approach for general-purpose robotic manipulation. Models such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, π0, and GR00T use large-scale visual and language pretraining to connect semantic instructions with robot actions. Their principal advantage is the ability to generalize across objects, tasks, and environments using visual and linguistic information rather than relying exclusively on task-specific controllers.

However, most VLA systems remain primarily vision-centric and typically produce position or motion commands at relatively low frequencies. This creates difficulties for contact-rich tasks, including peeling, pressing, wiping, and cutting. In these tasks, the robot must detect contact onset, regulate interaction force, and adapt to variations in object geometry, tool properties, and contact timing. These factors may not be visible or may produce only small changes in the image. A robot can therefore appear visually aligned with an object while applying insufficient or excessive force.

Recent research has introduced joint torque, force/torque, or tactile sensing into VLA policies. Some approaches add force signals as policy inputs, while others predict both motion and force targets for hybrid controllers. Another line of work separates slow semantic or trajectory generation from fast tactile or force-based action correction. TAO-Force is situated at the intersection of these directions. Its contribution is to address both the perception problem—making the policy use physical-interaction information—and the control problem—converting low-rate policy predictions into high-rate compliant execution.

The paper distinguishes itself through two linked mechanisms. First, Force-conditioned Feature-wise Linear Modulation, or F-FiLM, modifies the pretrained visual-language representation using current force, force history, and robot-state information. Second, a contact-gated fast–slow controller switches between nominal position control and high-frequency admittance control according to a predicted contact probability. The framework therefore treats force as both an input for policy reasoning and a signal for execution-time regulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Addressing the perception gap

The first objective is to provide the policy with direct information about physical interaction. A standard VLA policy receives images, language instructions, and robot state, but does not directly observe the external wrench acting at the end effector. The paper defines force as a six-dimensional quantity consisting of three translational force components and three rotational torque components.

The authors argue that force information is especially important at transition points. Contact onset may occur before a substantial visual change is visible. Similarly, two visually similar states can involve different levels of friction, penetration, pressure, or support. Without force feedback, the model may infer contact indirectly from visual or temporal correlations that do not transfer reliably when the environment changes.

A further motivation is that force lacks a pretrained feature extractor comparable to a visual encoder. If force features are simply appended to a large pretrained visual-language representation, optimization may favor the already well-developed visual pathway. TAO-Force therefore seeks to inject force information directly into the representation while preserving the pretrained semantic capabilities of the base model.

#### 2.2. Addressing the control gap

The second objective is to improve the robot’s response to rapidly changing contact dynamics. VLA policies usually generate action chunks at a lower frequency than the robot’s low-level control loop. Directly executing position commands generated by such a policy can result in rigid behavior, delayed reaction, or excessive contact force.

TAO-Force addresses this issue by separating nominal motion generation from physical regulation. During non-contact motion, a slow position-control branch follows the VLA-generated trajectory. During contact, a fast admittance-control branch adjusts motion in response to force error. The design is intended to preserve efficient trajectory execution in free space while providing compliance during interaction.

#### 2.3. Maintaining pretrained knowledge

The authors also aim to adapt an existing VLA model without retraining its entire visual-language backbone. TAO-Force uses GR00T N1.5 as its base model and freezes the pretrained backbone. Newly added force encoders, modulation networks, and prediction heads are trained for contact-rich manipulation. This design is motivated by the need to retain semantic and action-generation priors while adding physical-interaction capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Base model and inputs

TAO-Force is built on GR00T N1.5. The backbone combines a SigLIP visual encoder with a Qwen-based language model and uses a diffusion transformer action head. At time \(t\), the system receives visual observations, a language instruction, robot state, current external force, and a recent history of force measurements.

The policy produces three outputs:

1. A nominal action chunk.
2. A desired-force chunk.
3. A scalar contact probability representing the estimated current contact state.

The force signal is measured using wrist-mounted six-axis force/torque sensors. The measured wrench is compensated for the tool’s gravitational effects and expressed in the controller frame.

#### 3.2. F-FiLM force-conditioned representation

The visual-language backbone first generates a multimodal representation from the image and language input. Separately, three encoders process the current force, recent force history, and robot state. The current-force and state encoders are multilayer perceptrons, while the history encoder is a lightweight one-dimensional convolutional network designed to capture short-term dynamics.

These encoded features are concatenated and passed to a modulation network. The network predicts a residual scale and a shift that modify the visual-language representation through a feature-wise affine transformation:

\[
\tilde{z}^{vl}_t=(1+\Delta\gamma_t)\odot z^{vl}_t+\beta_t.
\]

The modulation network is initialized so that the residual scale and shift are zero. Consequently, the initial representation is identical to that of the pretrained model, and force conditioning is learned progressively rather than imposed abruptly.

#### 3.3. Joint action and force generation

The force-modulated representation conditions a shared diffusion transformer decoder. The decoder receives separate noised action and force tokens, allowing the two modalities to have distinct generation pathways while still interacting through shared attention.

Two output heads estimate velocity fields for the action and force chunks. At inference time, these velocity fields are integrated to produce the nominal action sequence and desired-force sequence. The action output provides the intended motion, while the force output provides a target for the low-level contact controller.

A separate classification head estimates the instantaneous contact probability directly from the force-conditioned representation. This separation allows contact estimation to respond to the current state, while the diffusion decoder focuses on temporally coherent chunk generation.

#### 3.4. Training objectives

The training loss combines three components: action flow matching, force flow matching, and binary cross-entropy for contact classification.

The force target is modified through contact-causal supervision. If \(c_t\) indicates whether the current state is in a task-relevant contact interval, the target force chunk is defined as:

\[
\bar{F}^{\star}_{t:t+K}=c_tF^{\star}_{t:t+K}.
\]

Thus, force targets are set to zero when the current anchor is pre-contact, even if future frames contain contact forces. This prevents the policy from predicting contact force prematurely based only on visual anticipation or demonstration timing.

The authors also use force-attentive modality dropout. During annotated force-critical segments, selected visual and proprioceptive features are partially masked. This encourages the model to rely on force-conditioned information instead of exploiting visual or state-based shortcuts.

#### 3.5. Fast–slow control

At execution time, the action chunk is converted into a smooth joint-reference trajectory. The system densifies the predicted action using polynomial fitting and applies minimum-jerk transitions between chunks. Desired-force references are similarly aligned and smoothed across chunk boundaries.

The predicted contact probability is thresholded. Below the threshold, the robot uses the nominal position reference. Above the threshold, it applies admittance control based on the difference between measured and desired external force:

\[
M(\ddot{x}-\ddot{x}_d)+D(\dot{x}-\dot{x}_d)+K(x-x_d)=F_{ext}-F_d.
\]

The resulting Cartesian correction is converted into joint velocity and integrated at a high control frequency. A small stiffness term anchors the robot to the nominal trajectory without dominating force regulation.

When contact ends, the force-controlled command may differ from the nominal trajectory. Rather than switching abruptly, the controller uses a quintic smoothstep function to remove the command residual gradually. If contact resumes during this recovery, force control is immediately reactivated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Force-conditioned trajectory generation

The authors first evaluate whether force feedback actually changes trajectory generation. In the Blind Box Sorting task, visually identical cans must be routed according to weight. Twenty trajectories are divided equally between empty and full cans. At a decision anchor, the visual input is held fixed while either factual or counterfactual force input is supplied.

F-FiLM responds consistently to force swapping in both directions. When the force signature is changed, the generated trajectory shifts toward the route associated with the injected force. The reported trajectory errors are 0.0186 and 0.0302 under the two counterfactual conditions, with flip rates of 90% and 100%. DePost obtains flip rates of 20% and 90%, while FVLMoE obtains 0% and 10%. These results support the claim that F-FiLM produces stronger and more symmetric force-dependent trajectory changes in this test.

#### 4.2. Contact-force prediction

On cucumber-peeling trajectories, the authors evaluate force prediction during intervals identified as contact-dominant using a contact-probability threshold of 0.8. F-FiLM reduces translational force MSE from 35.097 to 4.098 and rotational torque MSE from 0.653 to 0.070. The predicted trajectories reportedly follow rapid changes in the measured force and torque, particularly in the \(F_y\) and \(F_z\) components.

#### 4.3. Real-world task performance

Experiments are conducted on an AgiBot G1 dual-arm humanoid robot using three RGB cameras and wrist force/torque sensors. The VLA model runs at 5 Hz, action and force references are processed at 267 Hz, and the admittance controller operates at 1000 Hz. Each of five tasks is evaluated with 30 trials under both in-domain and out-of-domain conditions.

TAO-Force achieves 100% success in both conditions for Blind Box Sorting. For the four contact-rich execution tasks, the average success rates are 80.0% in-domain and 64.2% out-of-domain. The individual results are:

- Peeling Cucumber: 96.7% ID and 86.7% OOD.
- Pressing Pump Head: 80.0% ID and 70.0% OOD.
- Wiping Whiteboard Marks: 60.0% ID and 53.3% OOD.
- Cutting Cucumber: 83.3% ID and 46.7% OOD.

The evaluated baselines, GR00T N1.7 and π0.5, achieve average rates of 63.3% and 61.7% in-domain, and 20.0% and 29.2% out-of-domain, respectively. The baseline models are not evaluated on Blind Box Sorting because they lack force input.

The largest performance degradation for TAO-Force occurs in Cutting Cucumber when the knife is changed. Tool replacement changes contact timing and interaction forces. Wiping also shows reduced OOD performance when a different eraser is used, indicating that tool geometry remains an important source of distribution shift.

#### 4.4. Ablation findings

The ablation study separates F-FiLM from force-controlled execution. Adding F-FiLM to GR00T N1.5 improves peeling success from 56.7% to 70.0% in-domain and from 3.3% to 23.3% out-of-domain. Force control alone improves peeling to 63.3% ID and 60.0% OOD. On pressing, F-FiLM mainly improves OOD performance, while force control alone is inconsistent.

The complete TAO-Force system reaches 96.7% and 86.7% on peeling, and 80.0% and 70.0% on pressing under ID and OOD conditions. The results indicate that force-aware representation learning and closed-loop force regulation address different failure modes and are more effective when combined.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TAO-Force provides an integrated approach to a central limitation of general-purpose VLA systems: the disconnect between semantic trajectory generation and physical contact regulation. Its architecture connects force sensing to both policy representation and low-level control. The experiments suggest that force feedback can influence high-level action selection, while admittance control can compensate for errors and variations during execution.

Potential applications include robotic food preparation, cleaning, pressing, tool use, assembly, and other tasks in which successful behavior depends on interaction force rather than appearance alone. The frozen-backbone strategy may also make adaptation more practical than retraining a complete VLA model.

Several limitations remain. The evaluation uses one robot platform, five tasks, and 30 trials per condition. Force-attentive modality dropout and contact annotations require additional supervision and design choices. The method also depends on wrist force/torque sensors and calibrated admittance-control parameters. Performance decreases under tool changes, particularly for cutting, showing that force feedback does not eliminate all forms of distribution shift. Broader testing across robots, sensor configurations, contact materials, and more diverse tasks would be needed to establish the generality of the approach. Nonetheless, the paper presents evidence that combining force-conditioned perception with contact-gated fast–slow control is a viable direction for improving VLA-based manipulation in physically interactive environments.
