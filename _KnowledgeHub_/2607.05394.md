# Weak-to-Strong Generalization via Direct On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Direct On-Policy Distillation (Direct-OPD), developed by researchers at Tsinghua University and ByteDance Seed, extracts and transfers the policy shift induced by reinforcement learning in a weak model as a dense, implicit reward signal to a stronger student. This method enables efficient weak-to-strong generalization for large language models, reducing post-training computational costs by approximately 50% and improving reasoning performance, for instance, by 10 percentage points for Qwen3-1.7B on AIME 2024.

## Problem

- The escalating computational cost of applying Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to increasingly larger Large Language Models (LLMs).
- Scalability bottlenecks in LLM post-training due to expensive and time-consuming rollouts required for each RLVR iteration.
- Directly distilling a weaker teacher's final policy can degrade a stronger student's performance, as the teacher's inherent limitations are transferred alongside beneficial RL-induced changes.

## Method

- Defining a ==teacher policy shift== as the sequence-level log-ratio between a post-RL weak teacher and its pre-RL reference, which isolates the RL-induced change.
- Interpreting this policy shift as a dense, immediate per-token ==implicit reward== signal that directly trains the stronger student model via **Direct-OPD** (**Direct On-Policy Distillation**), bypassing explicit reward modeling.
- Utilizing an ==on-policy, top-k distillation== framework with an ==adaptive KL controller== to robustly transfer this implicit reward while regularizing the student towards its own initialization.

## Results

- Direct-OPD consistently improved the reasoning abilities of stronger target models (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B by **10 percentage points** on AIME 2024), even when they initially outperformed the weak teacher.
- The method achieved comparable or superior performance with approximately **50% less** computational cost compared to direct RL on a large target model (e.g., R1-Distill-7B).
- Demonstrated the successful sequential composition of multiple, independently learned policy shifts on a single student model, indicating cumulative transfer of distinct abilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The policy shift (log-ratio) resulting from RL training in a weak model can be directly leveraged as an implicit reward function, enabling its transfer to stronger models.
> - Weak-to-strong generalization is effectively achieved by transferring the 'direction of improvement' from a weak teacher, rather than its absolute policy, preventing performance degradation in stronger students.
> - An adaptive KL control mechanism is crucial for maintaining a balanced and reliable implicit reward signal, preventing the student from exploiting noisy or unstable log-ratios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of large language model (LLM) post-training, particularly concerning reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). RLVR has emerged as a robust method for enhancing reasoning capabilities in LLMs. However, its application to increasingly larger models presents a scalability bottleneck. Each iteration of RLVR training necessitates generating numerous rollouts from the target model, which becomes computationally expensive and time-consuming as model size increases.

Existing approaches to transfer knowledge between models primarily involve knowledge distillation, where a smaller "student" model learns to imitate a larger "teacher" model. On-policy distillation (OPD) is a prominent variant where the student is trained on trajectories it generates itself, supervised by the teacher. While effective for knowledge transfer, direct imitation of a teacher's final policy can be problematic in a "weak-to-strong" scenario, where the student model is already stronger than, or has different intrinsic capacities than, the post-RL teacher. In such cases, the teacher's final policy may incorporate both useful RL-induced improvements and the limitations inherent to its smaller scale, potentially degrading the stronger student's performance if directly imitated.

This paper positions itself by proposing an alternative to direct policy imitation. It draws inspiration from "weak-to-strong generalization," a research area exploring how weak supervision can elicit capabilities from stronger models, and from the "policy-as-reward" view in KL-regularized reinforcement learning, which connects policy ratios to implicit reward signals. The authors propose to leverage the *implicit reward* that shaped the weak model's RL process, rather than the weak model's final policy itself, for transfer. This approach aims to extract and transfer the *direction of improvement* learned by the weak model without imposing its inherent performance ceiling on a stronger student. This differentiates it from traditional distillation methods that focus on mimicking the teacher's output distribution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work stems from the escalating computational cost of applying Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to large language models (LLMs). As LLMs continue to grow in size and complexity, the process of post-training them with RLVR becomes prohibitively expensive. Each training update requires the current policy to generate a large number of rollouts, receive verifiable outcome scores, and update based on these trajectories. The larger the model, the slower and more resource-intensive each rollout and subsequent RL iteration becomes, leading to post-training itself becoming a significant bottleneck in the development lifecycle of advanced reasoning models.

To address this challenge, the authors propose an alternative paradigm: weak-to-strong post-training. The core idea is to run RLVR on a smaller, "weak" model, where rollouts and training are less expensive. The key objective is then to effectively reuse the "knowledge" or "improvements" gained from this inexpensive RL run to enhance a stronger target model.

The authors identify a critical limitation with a straightforward approach to this weak-to-strong transfer: directly distilling the post-RL weak teacher's final policy. This is motivated by the observation that the weak teacher's final policy intertwines the beneficial shifts induced by RL with the inherent capacity constraints of the smaller model. If a student model is already stronger than the weak teacher, imitating the weak teacher's complete policy can lead to a degradation of the student's existing, superior behavior. This phenomenon is empirically demonstrated in preliminary experiments, where a stronger student model's performance decreases when attempting to distill directly from a weaker post-RL teacher.

Therefore, the specific objectives and motivations for developing Direct On-Policy Distillation (Direct-OPD) are:

- **To isolate and transfer the RL-induced policy shift:** Instead of the weak model's absolute policy, the objective is to transfer only the *change* that RL brought about in the weak model's behavior. This change is conceptualized as a policy shift, defined as the log-ratio between the post-RL weak teacher and its pre-RL reference.
- **To interpret this policy shift as an implicit reward:** Mathematically, under KL-regularized RL objectives, this policy shift can be shown to be equivalent to the reward signal that trained the weak model. The motivation is to leverage this implicit reward signal directly on the stronger student, thereby bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or costly sparse-reward RL on the target model.
- **To enable weak-to-strong generalization without performance degradation:** The aim is to allow stronger student models to benefit from the RL-learned directions of improvement from a weaker teacher, without being constrained or negatively impacted by the teacher's inherent capacity limitations.
- **To develop a computationally efficient post-training method:** By running the expensive RL process on a smaller model and then transferring the learned policy shift, the method seeks to reduce the overall computational resources and time required to enhance large reasoning models.
- **To demonstrate consistency and robustness:** The research aims to show that Direct-OPD consistently improves stronger target models across various teacher pairs and student families, even when students initially surpass the weak teachers.
- **To explore the compositionality of policy shifts:** An additional objective is to investigate whether multiple, independently learned policy shifts can be sequentially applied to a single student model, potentially allowing for the accumulation of different learned abilities.
- **To analyze the conditions for reliable signal transfer:** Understanding when and why the implicit reward signal is reliable is a key analytical objective, particularly concerning factors like token overlap and adaptive KL control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Direct On-Policy Distillation (Direct-OPD) is designed to transfer the *effect* of a weak model's RL training rather than its final policy. The method operates on autoregressive language models and relies on four policies: the student policy being trained (π_θ), its initial checkpoint (π_S), a pre-RL weak teacher reference (π_T_ref), and the corresponding post-RL weak teacher (π_T).

**Core Principle: Policy Shifts as Implicit Rewards**
The central tenet of Direct-OPD is to define and transfer the "teacher policy shift," ∆_T(y | x), as the sequence-level log-ratio between the post-RL teacher and its pre-RL reference:
∆_T(y | x) = log π_T(y | x) − log π_T_ref(y | x)
This formulation isolates the direction of change induced by RL in the weak model. The paper mathematically substantiates this by demonstrating that, under a KL-regularized RL objective, this policy shift is proportional to the reward function that optimized the weak model, up to a positive scale and a per-prompt constant. This allows ∆_T to be interpreted as the teacher's implicit reward.

**Direct-OPD Objective**
The idealized sequence-level objective for Direct-OPD aims to optimize the student against this teacher-shift signal while regularizing the student towards its own initialization:
J_Direct-OPD(θ) = E_x~D [E_y~π_θ(·|x) [∆_T(y | x)] − α D_KL [π_θ(· | x) || π_S(· | x)]]
The term α controls the KL penalty, ensuring the student does not drift too far from its initial policy. This objective implicitly trains the student as if it were performing KL-regularized RL with the small teacher's implicit reward, anchored by its own initial policy.

**From Sequence to Token Level**
The sequence-level policy shift ∆_T(y | x) can be exactly decomposed into a sum of token-level shifts, r_t(v) = log π_T(v | s_t) − log π_T_ref(v | s_t), where s_t = (x, y_<t) is a prefix. These r_t(v) values are treated as dense immediate per-token rewards, indicating whether RL encouraged (r_t > 0) or suppressed (r_t < 0) a specific token.

**On-Policy, Top-k Training Interface**
Direct-OPD inherits the on-policy, top-k interface common in OPD. The student samples its own states (rollouts), and at each visited prefix s_t, the dense per-token rewards r_t(v) are computed. To focus the signal on actions the student considers plausible, the computation is restricted to the student's top-k support S_t (the top-k most probable tokens according to the student's policy π_θ). The student probabilities within S_t are renormalized (¯p_t).

**Analytical Top-k Policy Gradient**
To estimate the policy gradient for optimization, the immediate per-token reward r_t(v) is used directly as a local advantage. Instead of a high-variance single-token Monte Carlo estimate, the method employs a Rao-Blackwellized per-step estimator. This involves taking the expectation of the gradient over the restricted top-k distribution ¯p_t(v) while still sampling trajectories from π_θ. To avoid injecting extra Jacobian terms from the top-k distribution, the weighted reward (¯p_t(v) · r_t(v)) is detached into a static coefficient, A_w_t(v) = stop_gradient(¯p_t(v) · r_t(v)), for the final local top-k surrogate gradient. The KL regularization term is typically implemented using standard methods.

**Adaptive KL Control**
Recognizing that the implicit reward's scale is dictated by the teacher's training (unobservable parameters like the teacher's reward and KL budget β), a fixed KL coefficient α might not generalize well across different teacher-student pairs. Direct-OPD incorporates an adaptive KL controller. This controller adjusts α based on the batch-level mean of the student-weighted shift ¯r_m. If ¯r_m is positive, α is increased to prevent over-amplification of the local signal; if ¯r_m is negative, α is decreased, weakening the anchor to allow the gradient to move probability away from suppressed tokens. This aims to maintain a balanced dense signal.

**Experimental Setup**
- **Data and Prompt:** The math subset of Skywork-OR1-RL-Data, using a DAPO-style math prompt template, was used for Direct-OPD training.
- **Evaluation:** Models were evaluated on AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 benchmarks, using 32 samples per problem at a temperature of 0.7 and top-p of 0.95.
- **Hyperparameters:** Key Direct-OPD training hyperparameters included a global batch size of 64, rollout n=4, maximum prompt length 1,024, maximum response length 2,048, learning rate of 1 × 10^-6, and 300 training steps. The student top-k support was 16. RL teachers and direct-RL baselines were trained using the GRPO algorithm with specified batch sizes, rollout counts, and learning rates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning the effectiveness, efficiency, and underlying mechanisms of Direct-OPD.

#### 4.1. Weak-to-Strong Generalization Across Teacher Pairs and Student Families (RQ1)

Direct-OPD consistently improved the reasoning abilities of stronger target models, even when these students initially outperformed the post-RL weak teacher.
- **JustRL-1.5B Teacher Shift:** Transferring the policy shift from R1-Distill-1.5B (reference) to JustRL-1.5B (post-RL teacher) enhanced Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and R1-Distill-7B. Notably, R1-Distill-7B and Qwen3-4B, which initially surpassed the JustRL-1.5B teacher (e.g., Qwen3-4B at 72.5% vs. JustRL at 51.3% on AIME 2024), still saw performance increases (e.g., Qwen3-4B to 77.6% and R1-Distill-7B to 63.1%). Qwen3-1.7B improved from 48.3% to 58.3% on AIME 2024.
- **QuestA-Nemotron-1.5B Teacher Shift:** Similar gains were observed with a different teacher pair (Nemotron-1.5B to QuestA-Nemotron-1.5B), transferring to R1-Distill-7B and Qwen3-1.7B. This suggests the approach is not specific to a single teacher family or training pipeline.
These results support the interpretation that Direct-OPD transfers a reusable "direction of improvement" rather than requiring imitation of the weak teacher's absolute policy.

#### 4.2. Computational Efficiency and Performance Against Direct RL (RQ2)

Direct-OPD demonstrated a more efficient training path than running RL directly on a large target model.
- **Performance Advantage:** When comparing R1-Distill-7B trained via direct RL versus via Direct-OPD (using R1-Distill-1.5B for initial RL), Direct-OPD consistently yielded better R1-Distill-7B performance for the same number of RL steps.
- **Compute Advantage:** An RL run of 1500 steps on R1-Distill-1.5B took approximately 160 hours on 32 A100 GPUs, while the subsequent Direct-OPD transfer to R1-Distill-7B required only about 4 hours on 8 A100 GPUs. In contrast, direct RL on R1-Distill-7B for a comparable number of steps took roughly 320 hours. This indicates a substantial reduction in overall compute cost while achieving superior results.
- **Generalization to other models:** Similar patterns were observed with Qwen3 nonthinking models, where transferring a Qwen3-1.7B RL shift to Qwen3-4B-nonthinking matched the performance of direct Qwen3-4B RL.

#### 4.3. Sequential Composition of Policy Shifts (RQ3)

The paper demonstrated that multiple, independently learned policy shifts can be sequentially composed on the same student model.
- A Qwen3-1.7B student first trained with the R1-Distill-1.5B → JustRL-1.5B signal was then further trained with the Nemotron-1.5B → QuestA-Nemotron-1.5B signal. The results showed cumulative performance gains, indicating that different RL training runs can impart distinct abilities that can be combined.

#### 4.4. Cross-Pattern Transfer Without Token-Overlap Imitation (Analysis)

Unlike traditional OPD, which often correlates success with high teacher-student top-k token overlap, Direct-OPD did not always require this.
- In cross-pattern transfers (e.g., between different model families), the student's top-k overlap with the post-RL teacher remained low, yet performance improved. This suggests that Direct-OPD transfers a local ranking signal or "direction" within the student's own token distribution, rather than forcing imitation of the teacher's exact high-probability tokens.
- Actor entropy remained controlled, indicating that the gains were not due to a degenerate collapse of the student's output distribution. The narrowing of the teacher/reference entropy gap suggested the student learned to sample regions differently based on the teacher's RL-induced shift.

#### 4.5. Short-Horizon Training Generalizes to Longer Rollouts (Analysis)

Direct-OPD training, performed with a short 2k-token response horizon, successfully generalized behavioral changes to much longer rollouts (up to 16k tokens) at inference time.
- Tracking the cumulative gap (G_T) between the post-RL teacher and reference log-probabilities, weighted by the actor's likely tokens, revealed that short training shifted the actor's behavior in the desired direction across the full length of the responses.
- A 2k-token training horizon performed better on validation metrics compared to shorter (512-token) or longer (4k/6k-token) horizons, suggesting an optimal balance where early, reliable signals are captured without incorporating noise from later, potentially off-distribution parts of very long responses.

#### 4.6. KL Coefficient Controls Teacher-Shift Reward Reliability (Analysis)

The study found that the optimal fixed KL coefficient for Direct-OPD was highly dependent on the specific teacher-student pair.
- Different fixed KL values yielded varying performance, and a larger positive dense token reward did not universally correlate with better validation accuracy.
- The adaptive KL control mechanism, designed to adjust the KL coefficient dynamically, empirically steered the batch-level mean of the dense token reward towards zero after an initial correction phase. This "balanced regime" was interpreted as maintaining a robust signal for the student, preventing it from exploiting potentially unreliable or large log-ratios when drifting far from the teacher's original distribution. This highlights that the implicit reward is context-dependent and should not be optimized in isolation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Direct On-Policy Distillation (Direct-OPD) carries several significant implications for the training and scaling of large language models (LLMs):

- **Paradigm Shift in RL Outcome Utilization:** This work redefines how the outcomes of reinforcement learning (RL) on language models can be utilized. Instead of viewing the post-RL model as the sole valuable artifact to be imitated, Direct-OPD proposes that the *policy shift*—the directional change induced by RL in a model's behavior—can be extracted and reused as a dense implicit reward signal. This reframing allows for a more granular and transferable form of knowledge.

- **Enhanced Scalability and Cost Efficiency in LLM Post-Training:** One of the most critical impacts is addressing the escalating computational cost of applying RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) to increasingly large LLMs. By enabling the expensive RL training to be performed on smaller, cheaper models, and then efficiently transferring the learned improvements to stronger, larger target models, Direct-OPD offers a significantly more scalable and cost-effective post-training paradigm. This can accelerate the development and refinement of advanced reasoning capabilities in frontier models.

- **Robust Weak-to-Strong Generalization:** Direct-OPD successfully enables weak-to-strong generalization without the common pitfall of stronger student models being capped or degraded by the limitations of a weaker teacher. By transferring only the *direction of improvement* inherent in the teacher's RL training, rather than its absolute final policy, Direct-OPD allows students to leverage useful signals even when they intrinsically possess superior reasoning capacities. This enhances the utility of smaller, readily available models as generators of valuable training signals for more powerful systems.

- **Improved Transferability Across Model Families:** The demonstration of successful transfer across different teacher pairs and student families (e.g., R1-Distill to Qwen3 models) suggests broad applicability. This indicates that the extracted policy shift signal is not highly specific to the architectural nuances or exact training data of the source models, facilitating wider adoption in diverse LLM ecosystems.

- **Compositionality of Learned Abilities:** The finding that multiple, independently learned policy shifts can be sequentially composed on a single student implies a potential pathway for accumulating various specialized abilities or alignment objectives. This modular approach could simplify the process of endowing LLMs with a rich repertoire of behaviors by combining insights from different RL training runs.

- **Advancement in Understanding Distillation Mechanisms:** The analysis regarding top-k overlap, actor entropy, and adaptive KL control contributes to a deeper understanding of how distillation, particularly on-policy variants, functions in complex scenarios. The observation that successful transfer does not always necessitate high token overlap challenges prior assumptions and highlights the nuanced nature of transferring "thinking patterns" rather than literal token distributions.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges certain limitations. The effectiveness of the transferred signal is conditional; Direct-OPD may fail if the teacher/reference improvement direction is not meaningful within the student's visited states. Furthermore, optimal response lengths for training and the appropriate strength of the KL regularization remain somewhat dependent on the specific teacher-student pair, requiring empirical tuning. These factors indicate that while Direct-OPD offers a significant advance, it is not a universally plug-and-play solution and requires careful application.
