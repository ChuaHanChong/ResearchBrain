# Reasoning Boosts Opinion Alignment in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework applying reinforcement learning and explicit reasoning, specifically using Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) and Group-relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), improves Large Language Model alignment with individual political opinions derived from survey data. The SFT+GRPO method demonstrated an average macro-F1 score of up to 70.73% on the smartvote dataset, outperforming baseline methods and revealing challenges with neutral stances and ideological biases.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) often exhibit biased or inconsistent political opinions when prompted, failing to accurately reflect the diversity of human viewpoints.
- Current LLM-based opinion modeling methods, primarily relying on demographic prompting, struggle with accurately capturing opinion distributions and show instability across different prompts and groups.
- A gap exists in effectively leveraging scalable political survey data to train individual-level political agents that can reason towards consistent preferences.

## Method

- Trains LLM agents to produce structured outputs, including a reasoning trace and a ==policy stance==, for political questions.
- Utilizes Group-relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a composite reward function that incentivizes correct output format, appropriate reasoning length, and alignment with ground-truth survey answers.
- Incorporates optional Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) as a warm-start, using Chain-of-Thought demonstrations to pre-train models on the desired reasoning structure and accelerate subsequent GRPO training.

## Results

- The SFT+GRPO method consistently outperformed baselines (random, majority, ICL, ORPO), achieving average macro-F1 scores of **70.73%** on smartvote, **53.21%** on WoM, and **45.43%** on ANES using the Magistral **24B** model.
- Performance varied by dataset complexity and "Neutral" response rates, with the "Neutral" class consistently showing the lowest recall and negatively correlating with predictive performance.
- Agents' political positions typically shifted towards the center-right in ideological space, and biased SFT data disproportionately impaired the performance of underrepresented ideological groups.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Incorporating explicit reasoning via Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) followed by Group-relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) enhances LLM alignment with individual political preferences, outperforming multiple baselines.
> - The "Neutral" response option in survey data poses a specific challenge, consistently yielding lower recall and negatively correlating with overall predictive performance.
> - Observed performance disparities indicate that Center and Right-leaning political profiles are generally more difficult for LLMs to learn, pointing to potential ideological biases or intrinsic learnability differences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The modeling of individual or group political preferences, often termed opinion modeling, holds potential for applications in areas such as digital democracies, where accurate simulations could contribute to policy development. Large language models (LLMs) are considered relevant for this task due to their text generation capabilities and generalization performance across various text-to-text applications. However, existing research indicates that LLMs often produce biased or inconsistent opinions when prompted without specific mechanisms to address their statistical nature and limited causal understanding (Santurkar et al., 2023; Qu & Wang, 2024).

Current approaches to opinion modeling with LLMs frequently involve prompting with demographic information and political affiliations. These methods leverage correlations between demographics and opinions, but have been noted to struggle with capturing actual opinion distributions, exhibiting instability, and yielding inconsistent responses across different prompts and demographic groups (Santurkar et al., 2023; Ball et al., 2025). While approaches using richer data, such as interview transcripts, have demonstrated strong performance in modeling individual personas (Park et al., 2024), they face limitations due to high data collection costs and scalability challenges. Conversely, large-scale political survey data (e.g., American National Election Studies, Voting Advice Applications) offer structured representations of political opinions across populations and are widely used for bias evaluation (Santurkar et al., 2023; Argyle et al., 2023). However, these survey datasets have not been extensively adopted for training individual-level political agents.

Concurrently, reinforcement learning (RL) has driven advancements in LLM reasoning capabilities, with models achieving notable results in mathematical reasoning benchmarks (OpenAI, 2025; Guo et al., 2025). This paper integrates these two areas by proposing an RL-based method to enhance opinion alignment from survey data, framing opinion formation as an explicit reasoning problem. This approach aims to move beyond demographic-based prompting by directly leveraging known individual opinions and incorporating structured reasoning to improve consistency and representativeness, addressing a gap in the application of advanced reasoning techniques to individual-level political opinion modeling from scalable survey data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether the incorporation of explicit reasoning can enhance the alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs) with individual political preferences, particularly when trained on structured survey data. This objective is motivated by several key challenges in current LLM-based opinion modeling.

Firstly, the broader aspiration for LLMs to serve as "digital twins" in contexts like digital democracies necessitates that these models accurately and faithfully reflect human opinions on complex political issues. Existing LLMs, despite their advanced language generation abilities, have demonstrated limitations in capturing the diversity and nuances of human political viewpoints, often defaulting to biased or inconsistent responses when instructed naively. This raises a fundamental question about how to design AI agents that can reason about politics while authentically representing a range of human political diversity.

Secondly, prevailing methods for modeling opinions with LLMs predominantly rely on demographic prompting. While these methods have evolved, they consistently exhibit issues such as a failure to reflect true opinion distributions, insufficient steerability over model outputs, and unpredictable consistency of political biases across topics and contexts (Santurkar et al., 2023). These limitations manifest as systematic biases, impacting accuracy unevenly across different demographic groups. The research is motivated to seek a preference-consistent approach that directly utilizes an individual’s known opinions from survey data rather than relying on demographic proxies.

Thirdly, while methods using rich data sources like interview transcripts can achieve strong performance in modeling individual personas, their data collection costs are prohibitive for large-scale application. Political survey data, such as those from the American National Election Studies (ANES) or Voting Advice Applications (VAAs), offer a structured and more scalable alternative. The motivation is to leverage these widely available datasets, which capture how people position themselves on political issues, for training individual-level political agents.

Finally, the recent advancements in mathematical reasoning capabilities of LLMs, significantly propelled by reinforcement learning (RL) techniques, provide a critical motivation. By drawing inspiration from these successes, the researchers propose to apply RL, specifically Group-relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), to train LLMs. The core idea is to treat opinion formation as a reasoning problem, where the model is trained to generate a rationale and a final decision, receiving rewards when its decision aligns with the survey respondent’s answer. This approach aims to teach the LLM to reason towards survey-consistent positions on previously unseen questions, thereby improving opinion alignment and potentially mitigating biases observed in simpler prompting strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates individual-level opinion alignment using survey data by training LLM agents to generate structured reasoning traces before providing policy stances. The core method employed is Group-relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), often preceded by Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT).

**A. Political Reasoning with GRPO**
The approach trains agents to produce outputs in a fixed schema: `<reasoning>[justification text]</reasoning><answer>[stance]</answer>`. The training data, `D = {(p_j, q_i, y*ij)}`, consists of personas (`p_j`), policy questions (`q_i`), and their observed stances (`y*ij`), but lacks explicit reasoning traces. The model is therefore tasked with learning to generate reasoning that adheres to the format and leads to the correct ground-truth stances.

To achieve this, a composite reward function evaluates each generated output:
- **Format Reward (R_format):** Assigns a score based on the correct placement of the four required tags in the schema (maximum of 4 points), with a weighting factor `α1 = 0.25`.
- **Length Reward (R_length):** Imposes a symmetric penalty, `-|L - L*|`, where `L` is the token length of the generated reasoning trace and `L*` is a desired length. This encourages reasoning traces of an appropriate length, with a weighting factor `α2 = 0.01`.
- **Correctness Reward (R_correct):** The primary signal, awarding `1` if the model’s predicted answer (`y_i`) matches the ground-truth survey response (`y*i`), and `0` otherwise. This has the highest weighting factor `α3 = 1`.
The total reward is calculated as: `R(r_i, y*i) = α1*R_format + α2*R_length + α3*R_correct`.

The agents do not use an explicit persona representation; instead, a country label is included in the system prompt. Alignment with individuals is achieved purely through the model's ability to answer questions correctly according to their stated preferences.

**B. Optional Initialization via Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)**
To facilitate faster convergence and improve the initial quality of reasoning, the models are optionally pre-trained with SFT. This involves creating a dataset of Chain-of-Thought demonstrations, where each example explicitly follows the reward-incentivized format. This SFT phase serves to familiarize the model with the desired output structure and provide a preliminary understanding of political reasoning before the GRPO stage.

**C. Datasets**
Three distinct political datasets were utilized for evaluation:
- **German party positions (Wahl-O-Mat, WoM):** Aggregates party stances (agree/neutral/disagree) on federal, state, and European elections (2021–2025) for six major parties. Responses are mapped to {Yes, Neutral, No}. A total of 30 items from EU&I (2024) served as an out-of-domain test set.
- **American National Election Studies (ANES 2020):** Comprises 79 policy items (67 for training, 12 for testing) recoded to {Yes, Neutral, No}. Respondents (21 in total, 3 per 7-point liberal–conservative self-placement) were sampled to analyze ideological variation. A "conservative" mapping scheme (treating moderate options as neutral) was used by default, with an "aggressive" scheme for robustness analysis.
- **Swiss candidates (smartvote):** Includes 60 policy questions (48 for training, 12 for testing, topic-stratified) from the 2023 Swiss national elections for 18 candidates (three from each of six parties). Likert scale responses were collapsed to {Yes, No}.
Political groups (Left, Center, Right) were assigned to parties and ideologies for comparative analysis.

**D. Experimental Setup**
- **Model Backbones:** Three open-weight LLMs were used: Llama 3.1 8B (non-reasoning focus), Qwen3 8B (reasoning-pretrained), and Magistral 24B (reasoning-pretrained). All models were used with 4-bit quantization. One distinct model was trained for each individual unit (candidate/party/respondent).
- **Methods Evaluated:** GRPO and SFT+GRPO. Synthetic arguments for SFT (and some later bias analyses) were generated using Llama 3.1 70B.
- **Baselines:**
 - `random`: Randomly selects an answer.
 - `majority`: Always selects the most frequent answer from the unit’s training set.
 - `icl` (in-context learning): The model is prompted with question-answer pairs from the training set before answering test questions.
 - `ORPO`: Odds Ratio Preference Optimization, using hyperparameters consistent with prior work.
- **Evaluation Metric:** The primary metric was macro-F1 score, averaged across units, complemented by accuracy scores. Experiments were run 8 times to account for stochasticity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of reasoning in LLM opinion alignment and the factors influencing its performance.

**A. Reasoning Enhances Opinion Alignment**
SFT+GRPO generally outperformed naive baselines (random, majority) and other methods including SFT-only, GRPO-only, in-context learning (ICL), and ORPO across all three datasets (smartvote, WoM, ANES). For example, Magistral 24B with SFT+GRPO achieved an average macro-F1 score of 70.73% on smartvote, 53.21% on WoM, and 45.43% on ANES. This suggests that incorporating explicit reasoning steps through a reinforcement learning framework, particularly when initialized with supervised fine-tuning, improves the ability of LLMs to align with individual political preferences.

**B. Performance Variation Across Datasets and Models**
Performance varied significantly by dataset. Smartvote, which uses binary {Yes, No} labels, showed the highest F1 scores, while ANES, featuring {Yes, Neutral, No} options, proved most challenging. Datasets including a "Neutral" class generally resulted in lower performance. While Magistral 24B, a reasoning-pretrained model, performed best overall, reasoning-pretrained backbones (Qwen3 8B and Magistral 24B) did not consistently outperform the non-reasoning focused Llama 3.1 8B at comparable scales on all datasets. GRPO alone generally underperformed SFT+GRPO, indicating that the supervised warm-start significantly aids training dynamics.

**C. Characteristics of Agent Reasoning**
Agents trained with SFT+GRPO demonstrated the ability to generate coherent arguments that aligned with their final answers. However, two primary failure modes were observed: arguments sometimes contained illogical or hallucinated elements, and agents occasionally failed to maintain consistency across different topics within an individual's opinion profile.

**D. Influence of Political Ideology**
Analysis of F1 scores by ideological group revealed performance disparities. On smartvote, a clear difference was observed between the Left group and the Center and Right groups, with the latter showing lower performance. Similar, albeit weaker, patterns were noted on Wahl-o-Mat and ANES, where Center and Right-leaning profiles were generally harder to learn. This phenomenon might be attributable to known left-leaning biases in general-purpose LLMs or inherent differences in the learnability of certain political preferences from survey data.

**E. Challenges with Neutral Stances**
The "Neutral" class presented a particular challenge, consistently exhibiting the lowest recall across all individuals in the ANES dataset. A strong negative correlation was identified between predictive performance (F1 and accuracy) and the "Neutral" response rate in the test set. Individuals in the Right and, to some extent, Center groups tended to respond with "Neutral" more frequently. Removing "Neutral" instances from the dataset led to a considerable improvement in F1 scores across all groups, with the gap between Left and Center narrowing, though the overall Left-Right performance disparity persisted. This suggests that the "Neutral" class, possibly aggregating various behaviors (uncertainty, social desirability), complicates the learning process, but is not the sole cause of ideological performance differences.

**F. Agent Representativity in Ideological Space**
A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of Swiss candidates (smartvote) and their corresponding agents revealed that agents did not perfectly mirror their human counterparts. Contrary to common observations of left-libertarian biases in many LLMs, the agents' positions generally shifted towards the center-right of the left-right spectrum and towards the center of the conservative-liberal spectrum. Specifically, Right individuals' agents became more left-wing, while Left and Center individuals' agents became more right-wing and conservative.

**G. Impact of Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) Data Bias**
Experiments with progressively and conservatively biased SFT data indicated that ideological bias in SFT data primarily impaired the performance of *underrepresented* groups, rather than consistently benefiting the overrepresented ones. Right candidates were more severely affected by adverse bias than Left candidates. Analysis in PCA space also showed that biased SFT data predominantly shifted the agents of underrepresented groups. Interestingly, the positional shifts under conservatively-biased SFT data were similar to those under default SFT data, suggesting that the observed centerward shift in agent positions might stem from the base model's inherent alignment rather than the SFT data itself.

**H. Performance on Inverted Synthetic Positions**
By inverting all smartvote answers (effectively creating counterfactual political profiles), it was observed that Right-leaning groups benefited from inversion (improved F1), while Left and Center groups experienced a decline in performance. However, even after inversion, the F1 scores of the (now-inverted) Right group did not reach the original levels of the Left group, and the (now-inverted) Left group dropped to approximately the original Right group's level. This suggests that factors beyond simple ideological bias in the model, possibly an intrinsic difficulty in modeling certain preference profiles, contribute to the observed performance disparities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of AI-driven opinion modeling by demonstrating that an explicit reasoning framework, implemented via reinforcement learning, can enhance the alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs) with individual political preferences derived from survey data.

**A. Significance**
The work introduces a method that encourages explicit reasoning through Group-relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to improve opinion alignment. This represents a step beyond purely prompting-based or demographic-driven approaches by treating opinion formation as a structured reasoning task. The evaluation across three distinct real-world political datasets (Swiss candidates, German parties, U.S. voters) provides a robust benchmark and baseline for future research in this domain. The consistent outperformance of the SFT+GRPO method over several baselines, including in-context learning and other preference optimization techniques, highlights the value of combining supervised fine-tuning with reinforcement learning for this specific task. Furthermore, the detailed analysis of ideological effects and the specific challenges posed by "Neutral" responses contribute valuable insights into the complexities of political opinion modeling with LLMs. The observation that political ideology influences learnability, with Center and Right-leaning profiles often performing less well, points to potential biases that warrant further investigation.

**B. Potential Impact**
The ability to train persona-conditioned agents directly on known opinions, and to frame answer generation as a structured reasoning task, could have implications for developing more nuanced and accurate simulations of political behavior. Such advancements might eventually contribute to more sophisticated tools for understanding election outcomes and improving policy-making processes in hypothetical "digital democracies." By releasing both the methodology and the datasets, the authors establish a foundation for subsequent research on LLM opinion alignment.

**C. Limitations**
The research acknowledges several limitations. The inherent noise in human opinions, where responses can vary over time, sets a natural upper bound on achievable performance. The computational intensity of training one distinct model per individual profile restricts the number of individuals or parties that can be studied, posing a scalability challenge for large-scale simulations. Data availability is another constraint; suitable survey datasets with sufficient items for individual agent training are scarce, and question sets often lack cross-country alignment, limiting comparability.

**D. Future Work**
Addressing the observed performance disparities between political groups is identified as a key area for future research, particularly focusing on bias mitigation strategies. Moving towards a single model capable of conditioning on compact persona representations, rather than training individual models per agent, could significantly reduce computational costs and improve data utilization. Developing confidence estimates for LLM answers, possibly through extensions of the reasoning schema or dedicated value heads, would be crucial for real-world deployments. Additionally, designing purpose-built questionnaires and refining uncertainty handling mechanisms could mitigate the difficulties observed with the "Neutral" class.

**E. Ethical Considerations**
The authors explicitly address the ethical implications, recognizing the potential for both benefit and harm in modeling individual opinions, especially in the context of misinformation. They advocate for responsible research to improve opinion alignment. The use of anonymized survey data and public figures' positions addresses immediate privacy concerns. The transparent reporting of systematic performance disparities across ideological groups and difficulties with neutral positions underscores potential risks, such as the disenfranchisement of certain political viewpoints if biased systems are deployed prematurely. The research cautions against real-world applications until these fairness issues are resolved, emphasizing that proper bias mitigation is essential for opinion modeling to realize its potential in fostering fairer and more popular policies.
