# Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filtering for Data-Efficient Joint Tracking and Classification

> [!summary] Summary
> A Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF) is introduced to learn structured corrections for system dynamics and process noise covariance directly from measurement data in a self-supervised manner. This method significantly enhances state estimation accuracy and statistical consistency, reducing RMSE by approximately 34% on a real-world dataset, and enables robust probabilistic model classification, outperforming purely learning-based methods in low-data settings.

## Problem

- Classical Kalman Filters require precise system models and noise covariances, which are often unknown or difficult to tune, leading to suboptimal performance and inconsistent uncertainty estimates.
- Existing learning-based filtering approaches commonly need large datasets of ground-truth states for supervised training, which are frequently unavailable in real-world, safety-critical applications, and often neglect statistical consistency.
- Traditional adaptive filtering methods struggle to correct biases and errors arising from fundamental structural modeling inaccuracies, assuming the underlying system model is largely correct.

## Method

- Developed a Hybrid Adaptive ==Kalman== Filter (**HAKF**) that integrates a neural network to learn structured corrections to the nominal dynamics and process noise covariance, augmenting the standard Kalman prediction step.
- Employed a ==self-supervised== training objective by minimizing the innovation Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL), enabling the filter to learn from measurement data alone without requiring ground-truth states.
- Utilized Generalized Bayesian Inference (GBI) with model-dependent tempered likelihoods to provide robust probabilistic model classification concurrently with state tracking.

## Results

- HAKF improved state estimation accuracy, reducing RMSE on the real-world DPJAIT dataset from 311.44 (classical KF) to 206.39, while significantly enhancing statistical consistency (NEES improved from **60.12%** to **86.32%**).
- On the AirSim simulated dataset, HAKF achieved an RMSE of 83.07 compared to 160.54 for the classical KF, and demonstrated strong consistency with a NEES of **89.88%** (vs. **66.12%**), outperforming all other methods.
- The method demonstrated superior model classification accuracy in low-data regimes, surpassing purely learning-based RNN models and Random Forest classifiers, and remained competitive in high-data scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured corrections to filter parameters (dynamics and process noise covariance) can be effectively learned in a self-supervised manner from innovation sequences, leading to improved estimation accuracy and statistical consistency.
> - Integrating model-based inductive bias with data-driven neural network adaptation allows for data-efficient learning, enabling robust performance even with limited training data where purely data-driven models struggle.
> - The probabilistic framework of the Kalman Filter, combined with Generalized Bayesian Inference, offers a principled way to perform joint state tracking and model classification, leveraging innovation likelihoods for robust inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses challenges at the intersection of classical state estimation and modern machine learning techniques, specifically in the context of Kalman filtering.

**Classical Kalman Filters (KFs)** and their variants are foundational in state estimation for domains such as robotics, navigation, and autonomous systems. Their strength lies in a principled probabilistic framework and computational efficiency. However, their performance is contingent upon accurate system models and precisely tuned noise covariances. Obtaining these parameters typically requires domain expertise, system identification, and manual tuning, and inaccuracies lead to biased estimates and inconsistent uncertainty.

To mitigate these limitations, **learning-based approaches** have emerged, integrating neural networks to learn system dynamics, filter parameters, or correction terms from data. While these methods can enhance point-estimation accuracy, they commonly face several drawbacks:
- They often necessitate supervised training with ground-truth states, which may be unavailable in many real-world, safety-critical applications.
- They typically require large-scale training datasets, frequently generated in simplified simulation environments, which may not translate to real-world data scarcity.
- Their optimization often prioritizes accuracy-based loss functions, potentially neglecting the statistical consistency of estimated uncertainty, a crucial aspect for assessing filter reliability.

In parallel, **classical adaptive filtering methods**, developed since the 1970s, aim to address model mismatch by adapting filter parameters directly from measurements, without requiring ground-truth state information. Techniques like covariance matching, correlation analysis, and likelihood-based estimation fall into this category. A key limitation of these methods is their assumption that the underlying system model is largely correctly specified; they struggle to correct biases and errors arising from fundamental structural modeling inaccuracies.

More recently, **hybrid Kalman Filter methods** have combined KFs with learned neural components. These can be broadly categorized into:
- **State-correction approaches:** Learn residuals to correct predicted states or innovation terms without modifying the system model. While improving accuracy, they may not explicitly enforce statistical consistency.
- **Model-learning methods:** Parameterize parts of the state-space model with neural networks to represent complex dynamics, potentially sacrificing interpretability.
- **Gain-learning methods:** Replace or augment the analytical Kalman gain computation with neural networks.

The proposed **Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF)** positions itself within this landscape by drawing inspiration from classical adaptive filtering but augmenting it with data-driven learning in a novel way. Unlike many existing learning-based and hybrid methods, HAKF aims to learn *structured corrections* to the nominal dynamics and process noise covariance *directly from measurement data alone (self-supervised)*, rather than requiring ground-truth states or solely focusing on gain learning. A primary distinction is its emphasis on preserving the probabilistic structure of the filter, enabling both accurate state estimation with statistically consistent uncertainty and probabilistic model classification through Generalized Bayesian Inference (GBI). This approach seeks to provide data efficiency by leveraging inductive bias from physics-based models, making it suitable for scenarios with limited training data, while also maintaining performance in larger data regimes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for developing the Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF) stem from several limitations inherent in existing state estimation methodologies:

1. **Addressing Kalman Filter Sensitivity to Model Mismatch and Noise Tuning:** Classical Kalman Filters (KFs) are highly dependent on accurate system models and well-tuned noise covariances (process noise $Q$ and measurement noise $R$). In practical applications, these parameters are often unknown, difficult to model precisely, or vary over time. Manual tuning is a labor-intensive process requiring expert knowledge and can lead to suboptimal performance and inconsistent uncertainty estimates if not performed meticulously. The paper seeks to overcome this sensitivity by introducing an adaptive mechanism.

2. **Overcoming Limitations of Supervised Learning-Based Filters:** Current learning-augmented filtering approaches, while promising for improving estimation accuracy, typically rely on supervised training. This necessitates access to large datasets of ground-truth states, which are frequently unavailable or prohibitively expensive to collect in real-world, safety-critical systems (e.g., autonomous navigation, aerospace applications). Furthermore, these methods often prioritize point-estimation accuracy, leading to a neglect of statistical consistency in the estimated uncertainty. This deficiency is critical, as reliable uncertainty quantification is essential for assessing the trustworthiness of filter outputs and for downstream decision-making in sensitive applications. The motivation is to develop a method that does not require ground-truth state supervision.

3. **Enhancing Data Efficiency in Learning-Augmented Filters:** Many learning-based and hybrid filtering methods employ expressive neural network architectures that assume and require abundant training data, often generated in simplified simulations. This assumption frequently does not hold in real-world scenarios where data collection can be sparse or difficult. The research aims to develop a data-efficient approach that performs robustly even with limited measurement data, by integrating model-based inductive bias.

4. **Preserving Probabilistic Structure and Statistical Consistency:** A core motivation is to maintain the probabilistic foundation of the Kalman Filter. While learning-based methods can improve accuracy, many do so at the expense of consistent uncertainty estimates. The goal is to develop a hybrid filter that provides both improved estimation accuracy and statistically consistent uncertainty, enabling reliability assessment. Violations of dynamical consistency conditions (e.g., unbiased and uncorrelated innovation sequences) provide informative signals of model mismatch, which the proposed method intends to exploit.

5. **Enabling Joint Tracking and Model Classification:** Beyond state estimation, the paper is motivated to utilize the probabilistic outputs of the filter for model classification. Specifically, the innovation likelihood produced by a well-calibrated filter can serve as a natural basis for inferring the underlying model or system type (e.g., identifying different drone platforms). The objective is to integrate this classification capability within the same filtering architecture, leveraging Generalized Bayesian Inference (GBI) for robust performance, particularly in scenarios with varying degrees of model misspecification.

In summary, the key motivation is to develop a self-supervised Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF) that can learn structured corrections to system dynamics and noise covariances from measurements alone, thus improving estimation accuracy and statistical consistency, while simultaneously enabling robust model classification without requiring ground-truth state supervision or large datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a self-supervised Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF) that integrates model-based filtering with data-driven learning. The HAKF augments a classical Kalman Filter (KF) by incorporating a neural network to learn structured corrections to the system dynamics and process noise covariance directly from measurement data, thus operating in a self-supervised manner.

**A. HAKF Architecture:**
The HAKF architecture preserves the standard Kalman prediction and measurement correction recursion. The observation model (i.e., observation matrix $H_k$ and measurement noise covariance $R_k$) is assumed to be known.

1. **Adaptive Correction Module:** At each time step $k$, a feature vector $\phi_k$ is constructed. This vector comprises two main components:
 - A finite window of recent innovations: $\nu_k^{(K)} = \{\nu_{k-K+1}, \ldots, \nu_k\}$. Innovations represent the difference between the actual measurement and the predicted measurement.
 - The state correction term: $\Delta \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_{k|k} - \hat{x}_{k|k-1}$, which is the difference between the posterior and prior state estimates.
2. **Neural Network:** A multilayer perceptron (MLP) is employed to map the feature vector $\phi_k$ to structured corrections for the system dynamics and process noise covariance: $(\Delta F_{k}^{ML}, Q_{k}^{ML}) = f_\theta(\phi_k)$. The relatively simple MLP architecture is chosen for data-efficient adaptation under limited training data.
3. **Prediction Step Augmentation:** The learned corrections are integrated into the KF's prediction step:
 - The predicted state mean is computed using the augmented transition matrix: $\hat{x}_{k|k-1} = (F_{k}^{MB} + \Delta F_{k}^{ML}) \hat{x}_{k-1|k-1}$.
 - The predicted state covariance incorporates both the augmented transition matrix and the learned process noise covariance: $P_{k|k-1} = (F_{k}^{MB} + \Delta F_{k}^{ML}) P_{k-1|k-1} (F_{k}^{MB} + \Delta F_{k}^{ML})^\top + Q_{k}^{ML}$.
4. **Measurement Update:** The standard KF measurement update step is then performed using the predicted state and covariance, generating a new innovation $\nu_k$ that is appended to the window for the next iteration, closing the feedback loop.

**B. System Model and Structured Corrections:**
For experimental evaluation, a constant-velocity (CV) motion model is adopted, with a 6-dimensional state vector representing 3D position and velocity.
- The nominal model-based transition matrix $F^{MB}$ and observation matrix $H$ are standard for a CV model.
- A key aspect of the HAKF is learning *structured corrections* to the velocity dynamics. The corrected transition matrix $F_k$ blends the nominal CV dynamics with the learned correction via an interpolation parameter $\lambda$: $F_k = \begin{pmatrix} I_3 & dt I_3 \\ 0 & (1-\lambda)I_3 + \lambda \Delta F_{k}^{ML} \end{pmatrix}$.
- Under limited data, $\Delta F_{k}^{ML}$ is constrained to a diagonal matrix, learning scalar correction factors for each velocity component. With sufficient data, it can represent a full $R^{3 \times 3}$ matrix.
- The learned process noise covariance $Q_{k}^{ML}$ is parameterized using a Cholesky LDL decomposition ($Q_{k}^{ML} = L_k D_k L_k^\top$) to ensure positive semi-definiteness and numerical stability during training.

**C. Cost Function (Self-Supervised Training):**
The HAKF is trained using a self-supervised objective derived from the marginal maximum likelihood estimation. This is achieved by minimizing the innovation Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) function:
$L(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^T (\nu_k^\top S_k^{-1} \nu_k + \log \det S_k)$
Minimizing this NLL implicitly enforces covariance-matching on the innovation sequence, connecting the approach to classical adaptive filtering methods. The use of a finite window of innovations as input features allows the MLP to capture temporal correlation structure in the innovations, further integrating principles from correlation-based adaptive filtering.

**D. Model Classification via Generalized Bayesian Inference (GBI):**
The HAKF framework also enables model classification. Since the KF can be interpreted as a generative model producing a predictive measurement likelihood, Bayesian inference can be used.
- To address potential overconfidence or inconsistency under model misspecification, a Generalized Bayesian Inference (GBI) approach is adopted, using a model-dependent tempered likelihood: $p(M_j | z_{1:k}) \propto p(z_{1:k} | M_j)^{\alpha_j} p(M_j)$.
- The tempering parameter $\alpha_j \in (0, 1]$ controls the influence of the likelihood for each model $M_j$. It is determined through a grid-based search that minimizes the Brier score on a validation dataset, ensuring per-class calibration of the predicted model posterior.

**E. Implementation Details:**
- **Covariance Update:** The Joseph form for the posterior covariance update is used to guarantee symmetry and positive definiteness of $P_{k|k}$ in the presence of numerical errors.
- **Initial State Estimation:** The initial state ($\hat{x}_0, P_0$) is obtained via a weighted least-squares estimate using a minimum number of measurements required for an observable batch estimate.
- **Optimization:** The network parameters are optimized using the gradient-based Adam optimizer.

**F. Datasets and Evaluation:**
- **Datasets:** The HAKF is evaluated on both a real-world drone trajectory dataset (DPJAIT) and a high-fidelity simulated dataset (AirSim) with varying numbers of drone platforms and data availability.
- **Estimation Metrics:** Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for accuracy, and Normalized Estimation Error Squared (NEES) and Normalized Innovation Squared (NIS) for statistical consistency, assessed via $\chi^2$ hypothesis tests.
- **Classification Metrics:** Trajectory-level accuracy.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against classical KFs, purely recurrent neural network (RNN) models, and Random Forest classifiers (with and without KF-derived features).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF) across both real-world and simulated datasets provided several key findings regarding its estimation accuracy, statistical consistency, and classification performance.

**A. Estimation Performance (Table II):**

1. **Improved Accuracy and Consistency over Classical KF:**
 - On the **DPJAIT real-world dataset** (low-data regime, approx. 5500 samples/drone), the classical KF exhibited poor statistical consistency, with a NEES value of 60.12% (target 95%) and an RMSE of 311.44. In contrast, all HAKF variants significantly improved both RMSE and statistical consistency. The HAKF configuration learning both the transition correction ($\Delta F$) and process noise covariance ($Q$) using the NLL loss (HAKF (∆F, Q)*) achieved the best overall performance, reducing RMSE to 206.39 while achieving NEES of 86.32% and NIS of 93.56%. Importantly, this NLL-trained HAKF achieved estimation accuracy comparable to an HAKF variant trained with RMSE loss, despite not requiring ground-truth state supervision.
 - On the **AirSim simulated dataset** (high-data regime, approx. 100,000 samples/drone), similar trends were observed. The classical KF still showed limited consistency (NEES 66.12%, RMSE 160.54). HAKF again demonstrated superior performance. The HAKF (∆F, Q)* variant achieved an RMSE of 83.07 and strong consistency (NEES 89.88%, NIS 94.03%), outperforming all other methods.

2. **HAKF Outperforms Purely Learning-Based RNN in Low-Data, Competitive in High-Data:**
 - On the **DPJAIT dataset**, the purely learning-based RNN model exhibited an RMSE of 327.92, performing worse than all HAKF variants and even the classical KF. This suggests that without sufficient training data, purely data-driven models struggle to generalize effectively.
 - On the **AirSim dataset**, with a large volume of training data, the RNN model improved substantially, achieving an RMSE of 88.39, significantly better than the classical KF. However, the proposed HAKF still achieved better or comparable RMSEs (e.g., 83.07 for HAKF (∆F, Q)*) while uniquely providing probabilistic state estimates and allowing for statistical consistency evaluation, which the RNN does not.

3. **Statistical Consistency is a Key Advantage:** HAKF consistently delivered substantially better statistical consistency (NEES and NIS values closer to the 95% target) compared to the classical KF on both datasets. This indicates that HAKF provides more reliable uncertainty estimates, which is crucial for safety-critical applications.

**B. Classification Performance (Fig. 4):**

1. **Superiority in Low-Data Regimes:**
 - On the **AirSim dataset**, specifically in the low-data regime (fewer than $10^4$ training samples per drone), HAKF consistently achieved higher classification accuracy than the purely learning-based RNN model and the Random Forest baselines (both raw and KF-augmented). This finding supports the advantage of incorporating model-based structure and probabilistic inference (through GBI) when training data are limited for classification tasks.
 - On the **DPJAIT real-world dataset** (approx. 5500 training samples per drone, representing a low-data scenario), HAKF achieved the highest classification accuracy among all tested methods. In contrast, the RNN model exhibited the lowest performance, further underscoring the challenge for purely data-driven models with limited real-world training data.

2. **Competitive Performance in High-Data Regimes:**
 - As the amount of training data increased in the **AirSim dataset**, the RNN model's performance improved substantially and eventually surpassed HAKF and Random Forest approaches. This outcome is attributed to the capacity of deep neural networks to learn highly expressive nonlinear dynamics when abundant training data are available.
 - Despite this, the proposed HAKF maintained strong performance in the high-data regime of AirSim, achieving approximately 84% classification accuracy, indicating its robustness even when purely learning-based methods thrive. HAKF and Random Forest classifiers, relying on structured features, introduce useful inductive bias in low-data settings but may have limited representational capacity compared to deep networks in vast data environments.

Overall, the results indicate that HAKF provides a robust solution for state estimation and model classification, particularly excelling in scenarios with limited training data where uncertainty quantification is paramount.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Hybrid Adaptive Kalman Filter (HAKF) framework presented in this paper carries several significant implications and potential impacts across various domains, particularly in state estimation and system identification.

1. **Enhanced Reliability for Safety-Critical Systems:** A primary impact is the improvement in statistical consistency alongside estimation accuracy. By providing more reliable uncertainty estimates (as indicated by improved NEES and NIS metrics), HAKF enhances the trustworthiness of filtering outputs. This is crucial for safety-critical applications in robotics, autonomous vehicles, and aerospace, where accurate state estimation, robust uncertainty quantification, and reliable decision-making are paramount, especially when ground-truth information is scarce.

2. **Addressing Data Scarcity Challenges:** The self-supervised nature of HAKF, learning from measurements alone without requiring ground-truth states, directly tackles the common challenge of data scarcity in real-world deployments. Its demonstrated data efficiency, particularly in low-data regimes where it outperforms purely learning-based methods, makes it a viable solution for applications where collecting large, labeled datasets is costly or infeasible. This can accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous systems in diverse environments.

3. **Bridging Model-Based and Data-Driven Approaches:** The HAKF framework successfully integrates the strengths of classical model-based Kalman filtering (principled probabilistic structure, interpretability) with the adaptability of data-driven neural networks (learning complex corrections). This "best of both worlds" approach offers a structured way to leverage prior system knowledge while allowing for data-driven refinement of models and noise parameters that are often difficult to characterize precisely. This could inspire further hybrid methodologies in other control and estimation problems.

4. **Joint Tracking and Classification Capability:** The ability of HAKF to perform both probabilistic state estimation (tracking) and model classification within a single, coherent framework is a notable contribution. By leveraging the innovation likelihood and Generalized Bayesian Inference, the system can not only track an object but also identify its type or operational mode. This dual functionality is valuable for applications such as multi-target tracking, reconnaissance, or adaptive control systems that need to tailor their behavior based on the identified characteristics of tracked entities.

5. **Improved System Identification and Adaptive Control:** The learned structured corrections to system dynamics and process noise covariance can offer insights into unmodeled dynamics or time-varying system characteristics. This adaptive learning capability has potential implications for online system identification, allowing the filter to continuously refine its understanding of the system it is tracking. In turn, this refined model could be fed back into adaptive control strategies for more robust and high-performance operation.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies limitations such as the structured nature of dynamics correction and reliance on Gaussian noise assumptions. This opens avenues for future work, including extending the framework to more expressive nonlinear dynamics and non-Gaussian noise models, as well as exploring how to dynamically adjust the degree of model expressiveness based on data availability. The approach of preserving the analytical filter structure while learning specific components offers a promising direction for developing more advanced, yet still interpretable, filtering solutions.

In conclusion, the HAKF represents a step towards more robust, data-efficient, and statistically consistent state estimation and classification, particularly beneficial for critical applications where traditional methods falter due to model mismatch or data limitations.
