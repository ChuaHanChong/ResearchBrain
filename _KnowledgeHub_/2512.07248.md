# Distinguishing Imitation Error from Intrinsic Motion Learning Difficulty

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Xiamen University and Cardiff University introduced the Motion Difficulty Score (MDS), a physics-grounded metric that quantifies the intrinsic imitation difficulty of human motions, disentangling it from policy performance. This metric was found to strongly correlate with the tracking error of state-of-the-art imitation learning policies, providing a novel framework for benchmarking and analysis.

## Problem

- Existing evaluation metrics for humanoid imitation learning conflated policy performance with the inherent difficulty of the motion, making it challenging to diagnose specific policy limitations.
- There was no quantitative, physics-grounded metric to define and measure the 'imitation difficulty' of human motions, which hindered effective curriculum design and fine-grained analysis.
- Current motion datasets primarily categorized data semantically, lacking an explicit measure of dynamic imitation difficulty for systematic benchmarking.

## Method

- Defined motion imitation difficulty based on ==rigid-body dynamics==, specifically as the magnitude of ==torque variation induced by small pose perturbations==.
- Developed the **Motion Difficulty Score (MDS)** by combining three complementary diversity metrics: ==Spectral Diversity== (overall torque volume), ==Variance Diversity== (geometric spread in per-joint torque variation), and ==Segment Diversity== (temporal variability of torque changes).
- Created **MD-AMASS**, a repartitioned version of the AMASS dataset augmented with pre-computed MDS values for approximately 30,000 motion clips, facilitating ==difficulty-aware benchmarking==.

## Results

- MDS demonstrated strong positive correlations with imitation error (Mean Per-Joint Position Error, MPJPE-G), with Pearson's **r up to 0.6478** and Spearman's **ρ up to 0.8203** for state-of-the-art policies like PHC+.
- Two new MDS-based benchmarking metrics were introduced: Maximum Imitable Difficulty (MID), showing PHC+ could imitate more challenging motions (**MID 320.50**) than UHC (**MID 308.22**), and Difficulty-Stratified Joint Error (DSJE), revealing UHC had lower error on the easiest motions (**27.79 mm** vs. PHC+'s **30.38 mm** for MDS < 200).
- MDS was shown to be effective for difficulty-guided curriculum learning, leading to superior overall policy performance, and capable of detecting physically implausible or corrupted motions by assigning them significantly higher scores.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The intrinsic difficulty of imitating a motion can be quantitatively derived from how strongly required torques respond to minor pose deviations, which characterizes the 'flatness' of the reinforcement learning reward landscape.
> - A comprehensive measure of motion difficulty necessitates accounting for the overall volume of the perturbation-induced torque space, the variance across different joint dimensions, and the temporal variability of these torque changes.
> - Disentangling motion difficulty from policy performance enables more precise diagnostic insights into policy capabilities, revealing nuanced strengths and weaknesses across different difficulty strata that are obscured by aggregate metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Physics-based motion imitation learning is a foundational area in humanoid robotics, enabling the creation of physically plausible human-like motions for simulated characters and real-world robot deployment. Current approaches, often relying on reinforcement learning (RL), have achieved considerable success in replicating human motion data, with policies like UHC and PHC achieving high success rates on large-scale datasets such as AMASS. These policies typically rely on similarity-to-reference metrics (e.g., joint position error) for evaluation and reward design.

However, a recognized limitation in the existing landscape is the absence of metrics that quantify the intrinsic difficulty of a motion itself. Current evaluation conflates policy performance with motion difficulty; a high error could stem from a less capable policy or an inherently challenging motion. This ambiguity hinders precise diagnosis of policy limitations, effective curriculum design for training, and targeted motion optimization. Existing motion datasets primarily categorize data based on semantic labels (e.g., "dance," "locomotion") rather than an explicit measure of dynamic imitation difficulty. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a quantitative, physics-grounded metric for motion difficulty, aiming to provide a more nuanced understanding and evaluation framework for humanoid imitation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel metric, the Motion Difficulty Score (MDS), that defines and quantifies the intrinsic imitation difficulty of human motions, independent of policy performance. This objective is motivated by several challenges and limitations in the current state of physics-based humanoid imitation learning:

- **Conflation of Policy Performance and Motion Difficulty:** Existing evaluation metrics, such as joint position error, indicate how well a policy imitates a motion but do not provide information about how difficult the motion itself is to imitate. This makes it difficult to ascertain whether a policy failure or poor performance is due to insufficient learning capability or the inherent challenge presented by the target motion.
- **Lack of Intrinsic Difficulty Quantification:** While researchers intuitively understand that some motions (e.g., ballet sequences, single-leg stances) are more challenging than others (e.g., simple hand waves), there has been no explicit, quantitative definition or metric for "imitation difficulty" grounded in physics.
- **Obscured Diagnostic Insights:** Without a difficulty metric, it is challenging to conduct fine-grained analyses of policy behavior. For instance, a policy might perform well on average but fail catastrophically on specific difficult motions, or conversely, a policy might excel on easy motions but struggle with moderate ones.
- **Limitations in Benchmarking and Training:** The absence of a difficulty metric restricts the development of difficulty-aware benchmarks, which could enable more systematic and fair comparisons between different imitation learning policies. It also impedes the design of effective curriculum learning strategies, where agents could progressively learn from easier to harder motions.
- **Potential for Broader Applications:** An explicit measure of motion difficulty could open new avenues for research beyond evaluation, such as guiding policy training, optimizing motions for easier imitation, and reconstructing physically plausible motions from noisy data by identifying intrinsically difficult or "flawed" segments.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. **Define Motion Difficulty:** Establish a definition of imitation difficulty derived from rigid-body dynamics, specifically relating it to torque variations induced by small pose perturbations.
2. **Quantify Motion Difficulty:** Develop the Motion Difficulty Score (MDS) as a metric that captures this definition through complementary components related to the volume, variance, and temporal variability of the perturbation-induced torque space.
3. **Construct a Difficulty-Aware Dataset:** Create MD-AMASS, a repartitioned version of the AMASS dataset augmented with MDS values for each motion clip.
4. **Validate MDS:** Empirically demonstrate that MDS accurately reflects observed performance trends of state-of-the-art imitation learning policies.
5. **Derive New Benchmarking Metrics:** Introduce novel MDS-based metrics, such as Maximum Imitable Difficulty (MID) and Difficulty-Stratified Joint Error (DSJE), to provide deeper insights into policy capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on defining and quantifying motion difficulty based on rigid-body dynamics and then applying this metric to benchmark existing imitation learning policies.

#### 3.1. Motion Difficulty Definition

The core insight of the work is that the difficulty of imitating a motion is related to how strongly the required torques vary under small motion perturbations. Drawing from rigid-body dynamics (specifically, the equation $M(q)\ddot{q} + h(q, \dot{q}) = \tau + f_{ext}$), the authors propose that when small pose deviations necessitate large torque adjustments, reinforcement learning policies encounter "flat" or "plateau-like" reward landscapes. In these regions, substantial torque changes yield nearly identical poses and, consequently, similar rewards, providing weak improvement signals. This condition is defined as high imitation difficulty.

Specifically, for a motion sequence S, the imitation difficulty is defined as the magnitude of torque variation induced within a bounded pose-error neighborhood. This magnitude is characterized by three factors:
1. **Overall Volume:** The total torque variation induced by pose perturbations.
2. **Variance across Joint Dimensions:** The geometric spread in per-joint torque variation.
3. **Temporal Variability:** How torque variations evolve over the entire motion sequence.

#### 3.2. Motion Difficulty Score (MDS) Computation

The MDS aggregates three complementary diversity metrics, each corresponding to one of the factors identified in the definition of motion difficulty:

- **Spectral Diversity ($d_1$):** This component models the overall volume of the torque space (vol_Y(T)) induced by pose perturbations. It is derived from the coarea formula and relates to the product of singular values of the Jacobian matrix of the torque function ($F$). Specifically, $d_1 = \sum \log \sigma_i$, where $\sigma_i$ are the singular values of the Jacobian. Higher spectral diversity indicates a larger effective dimensionality of the feasible torque space, implying higher torque variation and thus greater difficulty.

- **Variance Diversity ($d_2$):** This component captures the variance of the Jacobian across different joint dimensions. It is defined as $d_2 = \sum_j \log(\text{Var}_j)$, where $\text{Var}_j$ is the variance of the Jacobian entries for joint $j$ across time and rotation directions. Smaller variance in Jacobian entries (meaning similar torque changes for perturbations across a joint) signifies higher motion difficulty because the reward landscape becomes flatter, providing less precise guidance.

- **Segment Diversity ($d_3$):** This component models the temporal variability of torque variations. The motion sequence is partitioned into $K$ non-overlapping segments, and segment diversity is computed as the average spectral diversity over these segments. $d_3 = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K d_1(J_k)$. A motion with highly similar spectral diversity across its segments (i.e., a consistently difficult or consistently easy motion) is considered more difficult to learn than one with a mix of easy and difficult segments that can facilitate progressive learning. The upper bound of $d_3$ is attained when all segments have equal volume. The authors set $K=4$ in their experiments.

The final MDS is a weighted sum of these three components: $MDS = \sum_{i=1}^3 w_i d_i$, where the weights $w_i$ are empirically derived.

#### 3.3. Motion Difficulty-Aware Database (MD-AMASS)

To facilitate benchmarking, the widely used AMASS dataset is repartitioned and augmented to create MD-AMASS. The AMASS dataset is segmented into fixed-length clips of 100 frames (approximately 3.3 seconds at 30 FPS) to mitigate intra-sequence difficulty variation. For each of these approximately 30,000 short motion sequences, an MDS is computed using the defined methodology. The per-frame inertial matrices (M) and nonlinear dynamics terms (h) are calculated using the Rigid Body Dynamics Library (RBDL), with pose perturbations applied iteratively.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides several findings demonstrating the effectiveness and utility of the Motion Difficulty Score (MDS).

#### 4.1. Consistency with Imitation Results

The MDS was validated by assessing its correlation with the performance of state-of-the-art imitation policies, UHC and PHC+, on the MD-AMASS dataset.

- **Statistical Visualization:** Scatter plots showed a strong positive correlation between MDS scores and imitation error (Mean Per-Joint Position Error, MPJPE-G). As MDS increased (indicating higher difficulty), the tracking error generally rose for both UHC and PHC+ policies, confirming that MDS effectively quantifies motion difficulty.
- **Quantitative Results:** This correlation was further substantiated by standard statistical metrics. For UHC, Pearson's r was 0.4823, Spearman's $\rho$ was 0.6586, and Kendall's $\tau$ was 0.4747. For PHC+, the correlations were even stronger: Pearson's r = 0.6478, Spearman's $\rho$ = 0.8203, and Kendall's $\tau$ = 0.6293. These consistently high correlation coefficients across both models validate MDS as a reliable measure of motion difficulty.
- **Qualitative Results:** Visual inspection of motion samples confirmed that low-MDS motions were reproduced with high fidelity, while high-MDS motions exhibited noticeable degradation, such as over-bending of limbs to maintain balance or incomplete leg flexion, which the authors interpreted as the policy settling for simpler, stable but less accurate torque profiles due to extreme torque variation demands.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study was performed to evaluate the contribution of each of the three diversity components (Spectral Diversity, Variance Diversity, Segment Diversity) to the overall MDS. The results (Table 2) indicated that any partial combination of these components yielded lower correlation coefficients with policy performance compared to the full MDS formulation. This suggests that all three components are necessary for a comprehensive and accurate quantification of motion difficulty. The robust monotonic relationship (Spearman's $\rho$ generally above 0.5) observed even in partial configurations validated the underlying principle of diversity aggregation.

#### 4.3. Benchmarking Motion Imitation with MDS

Beyond validation, MDS was used to introduce two new metrics for benchmarking, providing novel insights into policy capabilities.

- **Maximum Imitable Difficulty (MID):** This metric identifies the critical difficulty level beyond which a policy's performance degrades substantially. MID is defined as the MDS threshold that maximizes the disparity between the mean imitation error of low-difficulty and high-difficulty motion partitions. The calculated MID for UHC was 308.22, while for PHC+, it was 320.50. This quantitative finding confirmed PHC+'s superior capacity to imitate more challenging motions before performance collapse, a distinction visible in the scatter plots by the later onset of outliers for PHC+.

- **Difficulty-Stratified Joint Error (DSJE):** This metric provides a fine-grained evaluation of policy performance across different difficulty regimes. DSJE is calculated as the mean MPJPE-G within specific MDS thresholds (e.g., DSJE-200 for MDS < 200, DSJE-300 for MDS < 300, DSJE-350 for MDS < 350). The analysis revealed that while PHC+ generally outperformed UHC in aggregate and on moderate-to-high difficulty motions, UHC exhibited a lower error on the easiest motions (DSJE-200: UHC 27.79 mm vs. PHC+ 30.38 mm). This finding highlights a counter-pattern previously obscured by overall average performance metrics, demonstrating the diagnostic value of MDS in characterizing policy strengths and weaknesses across difficulty levels.

#### 4.4. Additional Applications (from Appendix)

The paper also briefly explores the broader potential applications of MDS:

- **Difficulty-Guided Curriculum Learning:** A preliminary experiment using a curriculum learning strategy guided by MDS showed that training policies from easy to hard motions resulted in superior overall performance and improvements across all difficulty subgroups compared to a baseline with random motion sampling. This suggests MDS can enhance learning efficiency and robustness.
- **Generalization to Other Humanoid Robots:** Qualitative results demonstrated that motion difficulty scores derived for an SMPL-based humanoid model translated consistently to the Unitree G1 robot. Motions rated as easy for SMPL were successfully imitated by the G1, while high-MDS motions led to noticeable imitation failures, suggesting the generalizability of the metric across different humanoid morphologies.
- **Flawed Motion Detection:** Experiments showed that introducing random, physically implausible rotations into natural motion sequences drastically increased their MDS scores. Similarly, visually anomalous or unstable motions selected from an IMU-based mocap dataset exhibited substantially higher MDS values. This indicates MDS's potential as an automated tool for detecting physically unrealistic or corrupted motions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work significantly contributes to the field of physics-based humanoid imitation learning by introducing a novel, principled, and quantitative metric for motion difficulty.

- **Enhanced Evaluation and Benchmarking:** MDS provides a method to evaluate imitation policies that disentangles policy capability from the intrinsic challenge of the target motion. This enables fairer comparisons between policies and offers a more nuanced understanding of their strengths and weaknesses across different difficulty regimes. The derived metrics, MID and DSJE, offer specific tools for this purpose, moving beyond aggregate performance scores.
- **Improved Training Methodologies:** The demonstrated utility of MDS in guiding curriculum learning suggests its potential to accelerate training convergence and improve policy robustness by allowing agents to learn progressively from easier to more challenging motions. This could lead to more efficient and effective development of humanoid controllers.
- **Deeper Diagnostic Insights:** MDS allows researchers to diagnose specific failure modes of policies. Identifying where a policy struggles (e.g., on specific high-difficulty motions or certain difficulty strata) can guide targeted improvements in policy architecture, reward design, or data augmentation.
- **New Avenues for Motion Research:** The concept of quantifying motion difficulty opens new research directions. It could be used to optimize motion data for easier imitation, identify and correct physically implausible motions in datasets, and potentially inform the design of more robust and generalizable humanoid control systems. Its potential generalization to other robotic systems further broadens its impact.

The primary limitation, as acknowledged by the authors, is the computational bottleneck associated with calculating inertial matrices and nonlinear dynamics terms using RBDL on the CPU. However, this is an engineering challenge that could be addressed by integrating more efficient or GPU-accelerated libraries in the future. Despite this, the theoretical grounding and empirical validation of MDS establish it as a valuable tool for advancing the understanding and development of physics-based humanoid control.
