# Learning Meta-Skills for Agent Harness Design in Test-Time AI4AI

> [!summary] Summary
> MetaSkill teaches a Builder LLM with frozen weights to build better execution harnesses for a Target agent, also with frozen weights. It does this by learning meta-skills: reusable support principles in when/provide/use form, distilled from the Target's execution feedback on a small development set. The frozen bank then guides a fresh harness for each test task. Full-bank meta-skills lift macro-average score by 8.95 points over no-skill construction and 12.02 points over direct delivery.

## Problem

- Agent skill methods such as Voyager, ExpeL and SkillRL store task skills describing how to solve tasks, not principles for designing the support environment that helps an agent succeed.
- Automated agent-design and harness-search methods like ADAS, AFlow and Meta-Harness optimize programs per setting but do not accumulate reusable construction knowledge that transfers across tasks.
- Prior strong-to-weak harness construction lets a stronger Builder supply executable support, yet the Builder never learns from how its harnesses actually performed during Target execution.

## Method

- ==Meta-skill representation==: each principle is a (when, provide, use) triple naming an observable trigger, the capability or resource to supply, and the judgment the Target keeps responsibility for.
- ==Construction-execution-reflection loop==: starting from an empty bank, the Builder builds harnesses on development tasks, reviews Target traces and scores, and keeps, adds or revises at most one evidence-cited skill per batch.
- Test-time harness construction: the frozen bank, given in full or through ==BM25== top-2 retrieval, guides fresh per-task harnesses across seven component families, including memory, composed tools, execution controllers and verification.

## Results

- With GPT-**5.6**-Sol as Builder, full-bank meta-skills reach a **65.31%** macro-average, beating the no-skill Builder by **8.95 points** and independently learned Target skills by **10.93 points**.
- Having the Builder enact the bank beats giving the same bank directly to the Target in all six settings, by **12.02 points** on average and up to **25.43 points**.
- Same-model self-improvement gains **18.71 points** over no-skill construction. On NewtonBench, no-valid-submission cases drop **35.5%** and correct discoveries rise **21.9%**. Removing the controller costs Gemini **13.36 points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Experience is worth more when compiled into tools, state and control before execution than when handed to the acting agent as advice it must interpret.
> - Environment design is a separate axis of self-improvement: a model with frozen weights can improve its own results by learning to build better support for itself.
> - Repeated skill refinement does not always improve results, and transfer depends on which model implements the skills, so banks need rollback criteria and recipient-side feedback rather than blind reuse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

An AI agent can be capable yet lose effectiveness when its environment makes it hard to track evidence, use tools, or finish reliably. The authors compare this to a PhD student who repeats experiments because notes and configurations are scattered. An advisor can help by creating a shared log, reproducible tools, and a validation workflow. The paper's counterpart is an AI Builder that learns to provide useful support to an AI Target. The Builder does more than give the Target extra advice: it learns what support is needed and prepares that support in the Target's environment. In the main tests, a Builder with its full learned bank outperformed two alternatives: a Builder constructing support with no learned skills, and direct delivery of the same bank to the Target.

During development, the Builder starts with an empty skill bank, makes a harness, receives public Target traces and scores, and keeps, adds, or revises skills. Each skill card spells out "when," "provide," and "use." At test time, the bank is frozen, skills are supplied in full or by BM25 retrieval, and the Builder constructs a fresh harness for each test task before the Target acts. The framework equips the Builder rather than teaching the Target directly.

### 2. From execution to support

The Target's task-specific working environment is its **harness**: instructions, resources, and executable support. The Builder learns **meta-skills**. A meta-skill is a reusable support-design principle, not advice on how to solve a task. Each one has three fields:

- **when** to intervene;
- **what** capability or resource to provide;
- **how** the Target should use it, while the Target retains responsibility for judgment.

Learning starts with an empty skill bank. On development tasks, the Builder constructs a harness and the Target executes. The Builder then reviews the generated harness, the public execution evidence, and the score. It may keep the bank unchanged, revise a principle, or add one; an addition or revision must be grounded in evidence. The study uses two development passes, then freezes the bank before testing.

At test time, the Builder receives the frozen bank in one of two ways. It gets either the full bank or a BM25 retrieval, which selects at most two positively matching skills using the initial public prompt. The Builder then constructs a new task-specific harness in a fresh environment, and the Target still reasons and submits the answer. The idea is therefore to let the Builder turn a reusable support principle into task-specific infrastructure, rather than to give the Target a better prompt.

This distinguishes the work from two earlier lines of research. [[2603.28052|Meta-Harness]] searches executable harness implementations using prior code, scores, and execution traces. [AI4AI at Test-Time: Strong-to-Weak Capability Transfer via Harnesses](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.12307) studies a stronger Builder constructing scaffolds for a weaker Target. In contrast, this paper has the Builder learn reusable principles for support design.

### 3. Does enactment matter?

A skeptic could ask where the gain comes from. The learned bank might help because it contains useful knowledge, or because a Builder enacts that knowledge in the environment. Two controls address this:

- **Direct delivery** holds the skill content fixed. The Target receives the bank as instructions, and no Builder-generated harness components are installed.
- **The no-skill Builder** keeps the Builder's construction capability, with the same construction space and budget, but uses no learned skills.

The test sets contain 95 Harness-Bench tasks and 292 NewtonBench tasks. Harness-Bench uses a deterministic completion-oracle score. NewtonBench measures symbolic-structure accuracy. Both are reported as percentages, and invalid harness-construction outcomes receive zero while remaining in the denominator. Builder and Target weights stay fixed.

All conditions used the same Builder, GPT-5.6-Sol, and fixed Target execution budgets. Across six model–benchmark settings (three Targets × two benchmarks), full-bank meta-skills averaged **65.31%**. That is **8.95 pp** above the no-skill Builder and **12.02 pp** above direct delivery of the same bank. This macro-average is the arithmetic mean of the six benchmark scores, not a pooled per-task score. On Harness-Bench, for example, Gemini-3.6-Flash scored **67.81%** with the full-bank Builder, **53.29%** with the no-skill Builder, and **42.38%** with direct delivery.

These comparisons support the value of Builder enactment in this framework. They do not prove that enactment is the only source of the gains, and they do not isolate which individual support mechanism caused them.

The gains also need careful accounting. Target turns, tool calls, and tokens are fixed within a benchmark, but Builder learning and construction are excluded from that execution budget. The results therefore measure task performance under fixed Target execution limits. They do not establish fewer generated tokens, fewer attempts, lower elapsed time, or lower total compute.

The paper's contribution is to make support design itself something a fixed model can learn. Across three evaluated same-model Builder–Target settings, meta-skills averaged **18.71 pp** above no-skill construction while model weights remained fixed. The evidence has clear bounds. It spans one main Builder, GPT-5.6-Sol, two benchmarks, and one adopted execution per task and condition. Broad applicability and performance relative to construction cost remain open questions.
