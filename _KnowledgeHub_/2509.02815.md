# Multi-Embodiment Locomotion at Scale with extreme Embodiment Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed URMAv2, an enhanced learning architecture combined with extreme embodiment randomization, to train a single locomotion policy capable of controlling 50 diverse legged robots simultaneously. This policy demonstrates improved learning efficiency and achieves zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to real-world quadrupeds and stable locomotion on a real humanoid.

## Problem

- Developing a specific control policy for each robot morphology is inefficient given the rapid proliferation and evolution of diverse robot hardware platforms.
- Existing Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approaches for locomotion often lack explicit awareness of a robot's specific physical characteristics, impeding policy transfer across different embodiments.
- Scaling multi-embodiment learning to a large number of diverse robots while maintaining generalization and stability in training presents significant challenges.

## Method

- Introduced URMAv2, an improved embodiment-aware neural network architecture with increased capacity, enhanced training stability via WeightNorm, and a more efficient attention-based action decoder.
- Applied extreme online Embodiment Randomization (ER) during training, exposing the policy to millions of morphological variations to foster robust generalization to unseen physical properties.
- Implemented a performance-based curriculum learning strategy that dynamically adjusts training difficulty based on the policy's performance across 50 diverse legged robots.

## Results

- URMAv2 achieved a higher final curriculum coefficient (beta=0.88) and faster learning speed compared to URMA (beta=0.82) when trained on 50 diverse robot embodiments.
- The policy demonstrated successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to real Unitree Go2 and MAB Silver Badger quadruped robots, exhibiting robustness to external disturbances.
- A policy trained on all 50 robots achieved stable locomotion in all directions on the real Unitree H1 humanoid, while zero-shot transfer to the Booster T1 humanoid allowed reliable forward and sideward motion, but faced challenges with turning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Embodiment-aware architectures, when properly scaled and refined, are effective for learning general locomotion policies that can control a massively diverse set of robot morphologies.
> - Combining extreme online embodiment randomization with a performance-based curriculum is crucial for stable and efficient training when dealing with a vast and varied embodiment space.
> - Achieving stable locomotion on complex real-world humanoids with a single general policy is feasible, though robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for humanoids under all conditions remains an area for further research.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot locomotion has seen significant advancements, particularly through the application of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). Utilizing high-fidelity simulators such as Isaac Gym/Sim and MuJoCo XLA (MJX), coupled with Domain Randomization (DR) and scalable DRL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), researchers have developed robust locomotion policies for various quadruped and humanoid robots. However, the prevailing paradigm involves training a specific control policy for each robot morphology.

With the proliferation of diverse robot hardware platforms, including advanced humanoids, this robot-specific training approach becomes increasingly inefficient. Robot designs evolve, requiring repeated training, and current methods often do not explicitly account for the unique characteristics of a robot's embodiment. This limitation makes transferring policies across different robot morphologies challenging or impossible.

This research addresses the challenge of cross-embodiment generalization in robot locomotion by building upon the concept of embodiment-aware learning. Prior work in this area includes Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (MTRL) which often resorts to simple observation/action padding or separate input/output heads, potentially limiting generalization. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Transformers have been explored to better capture robot structure and improve scalability in multi-embodiment settings. The Unified Robot Morphology Architecture (URMA) was a recent development demonstrating the applicability of multi-embodiment learning to real-world robots, initially tested on 16 robots. Subsequent work explored URMA's scaling laws with up to 1000 offline-generated robots and showed transfer to a real humanoid.

This paper extends these efforts by pushing the boundaries of embodiment-aware learning to a much larger scale and diversity, aiming for a single, general policy capable of controlling a wide range of legged robots. It focuses on improving the architectural robustness and training efficiency required to achieve zero-shot transfer to previously unseen real-world robots, particularly humanoids, which have historically presented greater challenges for generalization. This work aims to contribute to the development of "foundation models" in robotics, where a single policy can adapt to numerous robot designs without extensive retraining.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the rapid development and increasing availability of diverse robot hardware, particularly legged robots such as humanoids. The traditional approach of developing and training a distinct control policy for each specific robot morphology is becoming unsustainable and inefficient. As robot platforms frequently change or are adapted, this paradigm necessitates continuous retraining, consuming significant computational resources and time. Furthermore, many existing DRL approaches for locomotion are "agnostic" or "unaware" of a robot's specific physical characteristics and capabilities, which are crucial for effective control. This lack of embodiment awareness severely impedes the ability to transfer learned policies from one robot to another, even those with similar functionalities. The inability to generalize across embodiments limits the practicality and scalability of deploying learned locomotion skills on new or modified robot platforms.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a single, general locomotion policy:** The central goal is to create a unified policy that can effectively control a highly diverse collection of legged robots, rather than requiring individual policies for each. This policy aims to be robust and adaptive across a wide spectrum of morphologies.
2. **Scale embodiment-aware learning:** The research seeks to significantly increase the scale of multi-embodiment learning by training on a diverse set of 50 different legged robots simultaneously, encompassing various types such as quadrupeds, humanoids, bipeds, and hexapods.
3. **Enhance generalization through extreme Embodiment Randomization (ER):** A critical objective is to leverage a sophisticated online ER strategy during training. This involves exposing the policy to millions of morphological variations, aiming to foster extreme robustness and adaptability to unseen physical properties.
4. **Improve learning architecture for scalability and stability:** The paper aims to introduce an improved embodiment-aware neural network architecture, URMAv2, designed to be more scalable, stable during training, and empirically more performant than its predecessor, URMA, in a massively multi-embodiment context.
5. **Implement a performance-based curriculum:** To manage the increased complexity of training across numerous robots and variations, a specific objective is to integrate a performance-based curriculum learning strategy. This curriculum dynamically adjusts training difficulty based on the policy's current performance, aiming to speed up and stabilize the learning process.
6. **Achieve zero-shot transfer to unseen real-world robots:** The ultimate objective is to demonstrate that the learned general policy can be directly transferred, without any further training or fine-tuning (zero-shot), to previously unseen real-world humanoid and quadruped robots, validating its practical utility and generalization capabilities.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to contribute towards a more efficient and adaptable paradigm for robot control, moving closer to the vision of foundation models in robotics that can universally control diverse hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on extending and refining the Unified Robot Morphology Architecture (URMA) to enable learning a single, general locomotion policy across a massively diverse set of robot embodiments. This involves architectural modifications, extreme embodiment randomization, and a performance-based curriculum learning strategy.

**A. URMAv2 Architecture**
The Unified Robot Morphology Architecture v2 (URMAv2) builds upon the original URMA by enhancing its scalability, learning stability, and empirical performance.

- **Inputs:** The input structure is maintained with three categories:
 - **Per-joint description vectors ($d_j$):** Static properties unique to each joint (e.g., rotation axis, torque limits).
 - **Per-joint observations ($o_j$):** Dynamic state information for each joint (e.g., position, velocity). URMAv2 introduces an additional per-joint observation to indicate whether a joint should track a nominal position or can be freely controlled, allowing for task-specific joint conditioning. Feet-specific observations and their encoding are removed from the policy network due to their absence on many considered robots, though the critic network retains them and receives noise-free observations for improved value estimation.
 - **General robot observations ($o_g$):** Global robot state (e.g., trunk velocity, gravity vector).

- **Joint Encoder:** This component uses an attention-based mechanism to process per-joint data. It encodes joint descriptions ($d_j$) as attention keys and joint observations ($o_j$) as attention values. These are then aggregated into a combined fixed-size joint latent vector ($\bar{z}_{joints}$). For URMAv2, the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) $f_\psi$ responsible for encoding joint observations is made wider (2x 256 units) to increase the model's capacity for the expanded number of robots and variations.

- **Core Network:** The fixed-size joint latent vector ($\bar{z}_{joints}$) is concatenated with the fixed-size general observations ($o_g$) and processed by the core network ($h_\theta$) to generate an action latent vector ($\bar{z}_{action}$). URMAv2 deepens this network from 3 to 5 hidden layers to further increase model capacity. To enhance training stability, WeightNorm layers are applied around every Dense layer, which reparameterizes weights to separate their magnitude from their direction, facilitating more stable gradient updates.

- **Action Decoder:** This represents the most significant architectural change. URMAv2 replaces URMA's original universal decoder with a streamlined attention-based decoding mechanism. Instead of concatenating the action latent vector with encoded joint description latents in batch, URMAv2 computes the mean action ($\mu_j$) for each joint through a simple dot product between the action latent vector ($\bar{z}_{action}$) and the corresponding joint's attention weights ($\alpha_j$), which were generated during the encoding phase. The per-joint standard deviations ($\sigma_\upsilon$) are predicted by a linear layer based on the same joint description encoding used for attention weights. This approach results in actions being sampled from a Gaussian distribution $N(\mu_j, \sigma_\upsilon(f_\phi(d_j)))$. This revised decoder is designed to be conceptually simpler, computationally more efficient, and empirically more performant.

**B. Embodiment Randomization (ER)**
To foster robust generalization, extreme ER is applied online during training. Unlike standard Domain Randomization (DR) where randomized parameters are hidden from the policy, ER ensures that the policy "sees" and can condition on the randomized values through the description vectors. After ER sampling, DR is used to further modify parameters in a hidden manner, enhancing sim-to-real transfer.
The randomized parameters include: body part size and position (all dimensions), coupled and decoupled mass and inertia, center of mass, inertia, body part and joint axis orientation, IMU position, motor torque/velocity/position limits, joint damping/friction/armature/stiffness, joint nominal position, PD gains, and action scaling factor. A new embodiment is sampled during every episode step with a 0.2% probability, leading to the exposure of up to 10 million distinct embodiments per training run at the highest curriculum level.

**C. Performance-based Curriculum**
To manage the increased learning difficulty arising from a vast number of robots and variations, a performance-based curriculum learning strategy is implemented. All components of the training framework are tied to a single curriculum coefficient $\beta \in$. Initially $\beta = 0$, it is increased by $n\Delta\beta$ (where $n$ is consecutive successful episodes and $\Delta\beta$ is a small step size) when an episode meets success criteria (e.g., minimum tracking error, episode length, return). Conversely, $\beta$ can be decreased for unsuccessful episodes. This dynamic adjustment allows the curriculum to adapt to the policy's current performance.
This coefficient controls: domain and embodiment randomization ranges, perturbations, sampling probabilities, terrain attributes, termination conditions, and reward penalty coefficients. Training parameters are defined in a percentage-based manner relative to a robot's nominal URDF parameters. This standardization allows for a fully shared training framework across all robots, requiring only the nominal joint positions and PD gains to be specified for each.

**D. Experimental Setup**
- **Robot Dataset:** Training was conducted on a diverse collection of 50 legged robots, comprising 15 quadrupeds, 23 humanoids, 8 bipeds, and 4 hexapods, sourced from freely available URDFs.
- **Physics Engine and DRL Algorithm:** MJX served as the physics engine, and PPO was used as the DRL algorithm, implemented with the RL-X library.
- **Parallelism:** A total of 25,600 parallel environments were utilized (512 per robot).
- **Training Details:** 1.6 million samples were collected (32,768 per robot), with 16 minibatches of size 102,400 (2,048 per robot) used for 10 epochs per policy update.
- **Total Training:** The policy was trained for 5 billion environment steps in total (100 million per robot).
- **Computational Resources:** Training required approximately 40 hours on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings concerning the performance of URMAv2, its generalization capabilities, and its ability to transfer to real-world robot systems.

**A. Training Performance**
A comparison of URMAv2 against the original URMA architecture, a zero-padding baseline, and a multi-head baseline (one head per robot) was conducted, measuring performance by the average curriculum coefficient ($\beta$) across all robots.
- URMA and URMAv2 demonstrated significantly superior performance compared to both the zero-padding and multi-head baselines, indicating the effectiveness of the embodiment-aware architecture in handling multiple robot morphologies.
- URMAv2 exhibited improved learning speed and achieved a higher final curriculum coefficient ($\beta = 0.88$) compared to URMA ($\beta = 0.82$). This suggests that the architectural enhancements in URMAv2 contributed to more efficient and stable learning, allowing the policy to progress further through the curriculum.

**B. Zero-shot Transfer in Simulation**
The zero-shot transfer performance of URMAv2 was evaluated by training on 49 robots and testing on a single left-out robot.
- **Quadrupeds:** URMAv2 demonstrated strong zero-shot performance on quadruped robots, specifically the Unitree Go2 and the MAB Silver Badger. Notably, the MAB Silver Badger, which features an additional spine joint not present in other training robots, was successfully controlled, addressing a challenge identified in prior URMA studies.
- **Humanoids:** While zero-shot performance for humanoids (Unitree H1, Unitree G1, Booster T1) was lower than for quadrupeds, it represented a significant improvement over the reported near-zero return for the Unitree H1 in the original URMA study. In simulation, the policy controlled the Booster T1 and Unitree G1 effectively, but occasionally experienced falls under severe perturbations or on rough terrain.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer**
The ultimate evaluation involved deploying the trained policies on real-world robots to assess their practical transferability.
- **Quadrupeds:** The zero-shot policy transferred successfully to the real Unitree Go2 and MAB Silver Badger. The policy demonstrated robust control, even when subjected to external disturbances like pushes or pulling on the legs.
- **Humanoids:**
 - For the Booster T1 humanoid, the zero-shot policy achieved reliable forward and sideward locomotion. However, it exhibited difficulties with turning and walking backward, which sometimes resulted in falls.
 - Direct zero-shot transfer to the Unitree H1 proved to be unstable. Instead, the URMAv2 policy that had been trained on *all 50 robots* (not a zero-shot left-out scenario) was transferred to the Unitree H1. This policy was capable of stable locomotion in all directions on the real Unitree H1. This result indicates that while zero-shot transfer to humanoids remains challenging, a policy exposed to a broader range of similar morphologies during training can achieve successful real-world deployment.

In summary, the research demonstrated that URMAv2, combined with extreme ER and a performance-based curriculum, facilitates robust training and significant generalization across a large number of diverse legged robots. While sim-to-real transfer to unseen humanoids in a strict zero-shot context still presents difficulties, substantial progress was made in enabling a single policy to control a wide array of physical robots.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of robotics by addressing the fundamental challenge of generalization across diverse robot morphologies. The development of URMAv2 and its successful application to a large-scale, multi-embodiment locomotion problem has several key implications:

1. **Advancement in General Robot Control:** The most significant impact is the demonstration of a single locomotion policy capable of controlling 50 different legged robots, including humanoids, quadrupeds, bipeds, and hexapods. This represents a substantial increase in scale and complexity compared to previous multi-embodiment efforts, moving closer to the vision of truly generalist robot controllers. It suggests a paradigm shift from robot-specific policy design to more universal learning approaches.

2. **Architectural and Methodological Innovations:** The improved URMAv2 architecture, with its increased capacity, enhanced stability through WeightNorm, and an attention-based action decoder, provides a more efficient and effective framework for embodiment-aware learning. The combination of extreme Embodiment Randomization (ER) and a performance-based curriculum is crucial for managing the complexity of training on such a diverse dataset, offering a robust methodology for future research in generalization.

3. **Feasibility of Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer to real-world quadrupeds (Unitree Go2, MAB Silver Badger) and partial success with humanoids (Booster T1) validates the practical utility of the approach. While zero-shot transfer to humanoids remains a challenge, the ability to transfer a policy trained on all 50 robots to the Unitree H1 indicates that with sufficient training diversity, real-world deployment on complex platforms is achievable without extensive robot-specific fine-tuning. This can significantly reduce development time and costs for new robot platforms.

4. **Foundation for Robotics Foundation Models:** This work contributes to the ongoing effort to create "foundation models" for robotics – large, pre-trained models capable of performing a wide range of tasks on various robot hardware. By showing that a single policy can learn robust locomotion across vastly different robot bodies, the research lays groundwork for more general and adaptable robotic systems that can operate in diverse environments with minimal customization.

5. **Reduced Barrier to Entry and Increased Research Efficiency:** If general locomotion policies become widely available, researchers and developers may no longer need to train bespoke controllers for every new robot or minor morphological change. This could lower the barrier to entry for robotics research and accelerate the development and deployment of new robotic applications.

6. **Identification of Future Research Directions:** The paper openly acknowledges remaining challenges, particularly regarding robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for humanoids under all conditions. This highlights the need for further exploration into embodiment diversity and more effective curriculum learning strategies that can thoroughly explore the vast embodiment space, guiding subsequent research efforts towards achieving even more robust and universally applicable robot control.
