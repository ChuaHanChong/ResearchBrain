# Difference-Based Relational Learning for Zero-Shot Object-Goal Visual Navigation With Direct Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> T-DRN is a lightweight end-to-end DRL policy for zero-shot object-goal visual navigation. A Siamese feature extractor computes absolute element-wise differences between the target embedding and every detected object, mean-pooled into a domain-independent scene vector, while a two-frame temporal buffer preserves object continuity under narrow field-of-view. It reaches 71.9% success in AI2-THOR and 64.1% on a real TurtleBot4 without fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Simulated objects differ from real ones in appearance, lighting and texture, so end-to-end RGB navigation policies degrade sharply when transferred directly to physical robots.
- Graph-based methods depend on predefined object-relation graphs covering few categories, and transformer relational models cost too much compute for resource-constrained mobile platforms.
- Simulators assume wide-angle observation while real robots have restricted field-of-view; single-frame policies suffer perceptual aliasing and lose targets during rotation.

## Method

- ==Difference-based relational learning==: a ==Siamese encoder== maps 303-dim spatial-plus-GloVe object vectors, takes absolute element-wise target-object differences, applies residual FFN with LayerNorm, then mean-pools over all detected objects.
- ==Dual-frame temporal buffer== of size two stores current and previous scene representations, concatenated with embedded previous-action probabilities and fed to an ==LSTM== for sequential conditioning.
- ==A3C== actor-critic heads on the LSTM state, trained with a reward combining target reward 5, parent-object partial reward scaled by conditional probability and factor 0.1, and a -0.01 step penalty.

## Results

- On the 18/4 unseen-class split **T-DRN** reaches **71.9%** success versus TDANet's **62.5%** for L≥1, and **57.2%** versus **47.7%** for L≥5; 14/8 split gains are **9.3%** and **1.5%**.
- Real TurtleBot4 deployment over **39** trials across office, bedroom and living room gives **64.1%** success against TDANet **53.8%** and MJOLNIR **20.5%**, with no fine-tuning.
- Ablation: difference module alone lifts success from **7.7%** to **51.3%**, adding the temporal buffer reaches **64.1%**; 2-step memory beats 3-, 5-, 10-step and attention-fused variants; all-object pooling scores **62.7%** versus **39.2%** for top-7.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning relative differences rather than absolute appearance features yields representations that survive domain shift, since discriminative gaps are less tied to simulator-specific texture and lighting.
> - More memory is not better under partial observability: two frames outperformed longer histories and learnable attention, as extra history injects noise when the view is restricted.
> - Aggregating every detected object beats selecting the most target-similar ones, because discarded objects act as spatial anchors that ground the agent's position and orientation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on object-goal visual navigation for mobile robots using deep reinforcement learning (DRL). The broader objective in this domain is to enable agents to locate a specified target object using only egocentric RGB observations, particularly in novel and unmapped environments.

Traditional map-based navigation methods often experience performance degradation in previously unseen environments, which limits their practical deployment. This challenge has motivated the development of zero-shot object-goal navigation (ZSON), where a robot must navigate towards object categories not encountered during its training phase.

Existing end-to-end DRL approaches have shown promise for ZSON in simulation environments such as AI2-THOR. However, their direct transfer to real-world scenarios faces significant hurdles, collectively known as the "sim-to-real gap". This gap manifests in several ways:
- **Perceptual Discrepancies:** Simulated objects often differ significantly from real-world objects in appearance, lighting, and texture.
- **Scalability for Real-World Objects:** The vast number of object categories in real environments makes it impractical to rely on explicit knowledge graphs or predefined object relationships, which are static and can limit generalizability.
- **Hardware-induced Sensing Limitations:** Standard simulation platforms typically offer wide-angle, ideal observations, contrasting with the restricted camera field-of-view (FoV) of physical robots (e.g., Turtlebot4). This FoV mismatch can lead to increased partial observability and temporary object loss during navigation, thereby degrading the performance of single-frame end-to-end models.

Previous efforts in ZSON have explored various strategies, including Siamese architectures for image-goal navigation, leveraging pre-trained vision-language models like CLIP for semantic embeddings, integrating knowledge graphs with visual perception, and using large language models (LLMs) for open-vocabulary navigation. Attention-based mechanisms, such as TDANet, have also been proposed to focus on relevant objects. However, many of these approaches either face high computational costs (e.g., Transformer-based models,), rely on static knowledge graphs with limited generalization, or may neglect broader contextual information by overly prioritizing a small subset of salient objects.

This work contributes to the landscape by addressing the sim-to-real gap more directly through two primary mechanisms: a difference-based relational learning approach designed to produce domain-independent representations, and a temporal memory mechanism specifically engineered to mitigate the challenges posed by restricted camera FoV in physical robots. By focusing on lightweight, object-centric design and relational cues, the proposed method aims to achieve robust zero-shot generalization and practical direct sim-to-real transferability, distinguishing itself from more computationally intensive or context-limiting prior approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, end-to-end deep reinforcement learning framework for zero-shot object-goal visual navigation (ZSON) that facilitates direct transfer from simulation to physical robots without requiring fine-tuning in the real world. This overarching goal is motivated by several fundamental challenges inherent in deploying embodied AI agents:

1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Perceptual Generalization:** A significant barrier to real-world deployment is the "sim-to-real gap." This includes substantial perceptual discrepancies, such as variations in object appearance, lighting, and texture between simulated and real environments. Existing DRL models often struggle to generalize when confronted with these domain shifts, limiting their effectiveness when trained in simulation and deployed in reality. The authors draw inspiration from human cognitive learning, where novel objects are often identified by comparing them to familiar ones and highlighting discriminative features (e.g., recognizing a zebra by its stripes relative to a horse). This observation motivates the development of a mechanism that learns *relational differences* rather than absolute object representations, aiming to produce domain-independent features that are more robust to variations in object appearance and better support zero-shot recognition.

2. **Addressing Restricted Camera Field-of-View (FoV) on Physical Robots:** A critical yet frequently overlooked aspect of the sim-to-real gap is the discrepancy in camera FoV. Simulation platforms like AI2-THOR typically offer wide-angle or panoramic observations, which provide agents with a comprehensive view of their surroundings. In contrast, physical robots, such as the Turtlebot4 used in this study, are constrained by much narrower sensing hardware. For end-to-end models that rely solely on instantaneous RGB input, this limited FoV leads to increased partial observability, frequent temporary loss of visual contact with objects during movement (especially rotations), and severe perceptual aliasing. These issues significantly degrade navigation performance during direct sim-to-real transfer. The motivation here is to introduce a lightweight memory mechanism that can preserve short-term object continuity, thereby adapting the policy to narrow and variable FoVs without requiring modifications to the offline training data or computationally expensive global map building.

3. **Achieving Computational Efficiency for Real-Time Deployment:** Many advanced navigation models, particularly those leveraging Transformer-based architectures, or explicit semantic map construction, entail high computational costs. For deployment on resource-constrained mobile robots, maintaining low inference latency and model complexity is crucial for real-time operation. This motivates the design of a lightweight, object-centric architecture that can process detected objects efficiently while still capturing rich scene information relevant for navigation.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Propose an object-centric relational difference architecture (T-DRN) to improve zero-shot generalization by modeling relational disparity between the target and observed objects, thereby becoming more robust to appearance variations.
- Introduce a dual-frame temporal memory mechanism to explicitly address the FoV discrepancy between simulation and reality, ensuring navigation stability under restricted sensing conditions.
- Validate the proposed method through extensive experiments in both simulation and on a physical robot, demonstrating strong overall success rates and practical direct sim-to-real transferability without fine-tuning, while maintaining computational efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed **Temporal Difference-Relational Network (T-DRN)** is an end-to-end deep reinforcement learning framework designed for zero-shot object-goal visual navigation, built upon an Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C) algorithm. The network takes egocentric RGB observations and the word embedding of a target object as input and outputs discrete navigation actions.

**A. Problem Formulation and Action Space**
The ZSON task requires an agent to locate a specified target object from an unseen class set `U`, having been trained on a seen class set `S` (`S ∩ U = ∅`). The agent operates on a physical TurtleBot4 robot, using only RGB observations without prior maps.
The discrete action space includes:
- `MoveAhead`: Move forward by approximately 0.25 meters.
- `RotateLeft` / `RotateRight`: Rotate by 45° counter-clockwise or clockwise.
- `LookUp` / `LookDown`: Adjust camera tilt by 30°.
- `Done`: Signal episode end when the target is believed to be found.
An episode is successful if the `Done` action is triggered when the target is visible and within a 1.5-meter radius of the robot.

**B. Difference-Based Relational Learning**
This module is inspired by human cognition and aims to learn domain-independent representations by focusing on distinctions rather than absolute object features.
1. **Feature Vector Construction:** For each object `i` detected in the current observation, and for the target object `T`, a 303-dimensional feature vector `V` is constructed. This vector concatenates:
 - `p_i = [x_i, y_i, a_i]`: Normalized spatial properties (center coordinates `x, y` and bounding box area `a`).
 - `s_i ∈ R^300`: Semantic embedding obtained from a pre-trained GloVe model.
2. **Siamese Feature Extractor:** A Siamese-style architecture processes the feature vectors. For an observed object `V_i` and the target object `V_T`:
 - `X_ri = σ(W_v V_i + b_v)`
 - `X_t = σ(W_v V_T + b_v)`
 where `σ` is the ReLU activation function, and `W_v`, `b_v` are shared weights and biases.
3. **Relational Difference Computation:** To explicitly capture the relationship, the absolute element-wise difference between the transformed features is computed:
 - `d_i = |W_L X_ri − W_L X_t|`
 where `W_L` is a learnable linear transformation. This `d_i` represents the discriminative gap between the observed object and the target.
4. **Individual Difference Vector Processing:** A residual transformation followed by Layer Normalization (LN) is applied:
 - `V_di = LN(FFN(d_i) + d_i)`
5. **Scene-Level Aggregation:** To handle a variable number of detected objects `n`, all individual difference vectors `V_di` are aggregated using mean-pooling to produce a single scene-level representation `X_t = (1/n) Σ V_di`. This approach considers *all* detected objects in the field of view, preserving broader scene context.

**C. Dual-Frame Temporal Buffer**
This mechanism addresses the challenges of restricted FoV and temporary object loss by incorporating short-term memory.
1. **Temporal Buffering:** A memory buffer of size `M=2` stores the scene-level representations from the current (`X_t`) and previous (`X_t-1`) timesteps.
2. **Sequential Conditioning:** The temporal sequence from the buffer is concatenated with the embedded probabilities of the previous action `a_t-1`:
 - `E_t = [X_τ , σ(W_a a_τ-1 + b_a)]_{τ=t-1}^t`
 This combined feature `E_t ∈ R^(2×522)` serves as the input to the temporal processing unit.
3. **LSTM Processing:** `E_t` is fed into a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. The LSTM's hidden state `h_t` then drives two separate linear heads:
 - **Actor:** Predicts the navigation policy `π(a_t|h_t)`.
 - **Critic:** Predicts the state-value `V(h_t)`.

**D. Reward Design**
The reward function `R` is designed to promote efficient navigation, target acquisition, and an understanding of object relationships:
- **Target Reward (`R_t`):** `R_t = 5` awarded for successfully performing the `Done` action when the target object `t` is visible.
- **Partial Reward (`R_p`):** Encourages considering object relationships, defined as `R_p = R_t · Pr(t | p) · k`, where `k = 0.1`. `Pr(t | p)` is the conditional probability of finding `t` near its parent object `p` (a larger, spatially/semantically associated object, as defined in), calculated based on relative spatial distances. `R_p` is awarded the first time a parent object `p` becomes visible.
- **Step Penalty:** `-0.01` for every timestep to encourage shorter paths and efficient navigation.
The total reward is `R_t + R_p` if both target `t` and parent `p` are visible when `Done` is executed; `R_t` if only `t` is visible; `R_p` if `p` is visible for the first time; and `-0.01` otherwise.

**E. Training and Evaluation**
- **Simulation Environment:** AI2-THOR virtual environment (kitchen, living room, bedroom, bathroom scenes), with 20 rooms for training and 10 for testing per scene type. Target set of 22 object classes.
- **Training:** 6,000,000 episodes using offline data from AI2-THOR v1.0.1, learning rate 0.0001, A3C framework.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) and Success Weighted by Path Length (SPL).
- **Zero-Shot Splits:** Performance evaluated on "18/4" and "14/8" unseen-class splits, referring to the number of training classes vs. unseen test classes.
- **Real-World Deployment:** Policy trained in simulation deployed directly onto a TurtleBot4 robot. YOLOv7 used for object detection from robot's RGB observations, ROS2 for communication. Evaluated in office, bedroom, and living room settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in both simulation (AI2-THOR) and on a physical robot (TurtleBot4) to evaluate the T-DRN's performance in zero-shot object-goal visual navigation and its sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

**A. Simulation Results (AI2-THOR)**
T-DRN was compared against several baselines, including random exploration, early target-driven DRL (Zhu et al.), graph-based reasoning (MJOLNIR), and recent ZSON methods (SSNet, TDANet).
- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** T-DRN consistently achieved the highest Success Rate (SR) across all tested scenarios and splits.
 - On the 18/4 unseen-class split, T-DRN achieved an SR of 71.9% for optimal path length `L ≥ 1` and 57.2% for `L ≥ 5`. This represented an improvement of 8.2% and 9.5% respectively over TDANet, the strongest competing baseline.
 - On the 14/8 unseen-class split, T-DRN achieved an SR of 62.7% for `L ≥ 1` and 42.7% for `L ≥ 5`, improving over TDANet by 9.3% and 1.5%.
- **Navigation Efficiency:** T-DRN's Success Weighted by Path Length (SPL) was comparable to or slightly higher than TDANet. For instance, on the 18/4 split, T-DRN achieved 25.3% SPL (`L ≥ 1`) and 25.4% SPL (`L ≥ 5`), while TDANet achieved 25.3% and 23.8% respectively.
- **Qualitative Observation:** A qualitative example demonstrated that T-DRN was able to successfully navigate to an unseen target (toilet paper) without being distracted by visually similar objects (towel), unlike TDANet which exhibited misdirection. The authors attribute T-DRN's improved performance to its aggregation of feature differences across all detected objects, providing a more complete scene representation compared to attention-based methods that prioritize a subset of objects.

**B. Model Efficiency**
- **Parameter Count:** T-DRN is a lightweight model, comprising 2.96 million parameters. This is fewer than MJOLNIR-o (3.42M) and TDANet (3.08M).
- **Inference Latency:** T-DRN achieved an inference latency of 0.5 ms per frame on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU, matching the fastest baseline (SSNet) while delivering superior navigation performance. This indicates its suitability for real-time deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms.

**C. Real-World Deployment (TurtleBot4)**
The policy trained in simulation was directly transferred and deployed on a physical TurtleBot4 robot without any fine-tuning.
- **Sim-to-Real Transferability:** T-DRN achieved an SR of 64.1% in real-world zero-shot navigation tasks, representing a 10.3% improvement over TDANet (53.8%). MJOLNIR performed significantly worse (20.5%) due to its reliance on predefined knowledge graphs that did not generalize well to real-world objects.
- **Robustness under Real Constraints:** The real-world experiments demonstrated that T-DRN's representation transfers more reliably under real sensing and actuation constraints, particularly with the robot's narrow camera FoV. The dual-frame temporal buffer was shown to be crucial for maintaining short-term memory and object permanence, enabling the agent to persist in promising search directions despite temporary visual loss during rotations.
- **Failure Modes:** The primary failure mode identified for T-DRN was its RGB-only nature, leading to occasional collisions due to a lack of explicit depth perception for accurate obstacle proximity estimation.

**D. Ablation Study (Real-World)**
An ablation study was conducted in the real-world environment to quantify the contribution of T-DRN's core components: Difference-Based Relational Learning and the Dual-Frame Temporal Buffer.
- **Baseline (no specific modules):** SR of 7.7%.
- **Difference-Only:** Achieved an SR of 51.3%, showing a substantial 43.6% improvement over the baseline. This supports the hypothesis that difference-based relational features are more robust to domain shifts and generalize better to unseen categories.
- **Temporal-Only:** Achieved an SR of 18.0%, an improvement of 10.3% over the baseline. This indicates the temporal buffer's effectiveness in providing short-term spatial continuity and reducing policy collapse due to restricted FoV.
- **T-DRN (Both Modules):** Achieved the highest SR of 64.1%, demonstrating that both modules contribute synergistically to the overall performance.

**E. Temporal Memory Analysis**
The study evaluated different temporal memory lengths and an attention mechanism for fusing historical features.
- The dual-frame (2-step) temporal memory achieved the best performance (71.9% SR for L>=1, 57.2% for L>=5) compared to longer histories (3, 5, 10 steps) or a 5-step with attention mechanism. This suggests that shorter temporal history is more effective, potentially because longer memory spans might introduce noise under restricted FoV conditions.

**F. Object Aggregation Analysis**
The aggregation strategy for detected objects was investigated, comparing mean-pooling of *all* detected object differences versus a Top-k selection (only the `k` most target-similar objects).
- Aggregating differences from *all* detected objects (`X_t = (1/n) Σ V_di`) consistently outperformed Top-k selection for various `k` values (2, 3, 5, 7). For instance, "All Objects" achieved 62.7% SR, while the best Top-k (k=7) achieved 39.2%.
- This finding suggests that preserving contextual navigation cues from all objects (even those not directly target-like) and maintaining information robustness is crucial, especially under restricted FoV conditions where object counts are already limited. Narrowing the focus negatively impacts spatial awareness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that makes notable contributions to object-goal visual navigation, particularly concerning zero-shot generalization and practical deployment on physical robots.

1. **Advancement in Zero-Shot Generalization:** The core contribution of the Difference-Based Relational Learning module is its ability to enable robust generalization to unseen target categories. By learning what makes an object *distinct* relative to others, rather than relying on absolute visual features, the system becomes less susceptible to domain-specific appearance biases. This approach is significant because it allows robots to recognize and navigate towards novel objects without prior training examples for those specific categories, which is critical for operating in dynamic and open-ended real-world environments.

2. **Demonstrated Direct Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A key challenge in robotics is the sim-to-real gap, which often necessitates extensive fine-tuning in the real world after simulation training. This work demonstrates the feasibility of *direct sim-to-real transfer* for zero-shot object-goal navigation without any real-world fine-tuning. The robust performance of T-DRN on a physical TurtleBot4 robot, trained solely in AI2-THOR, highlights a practical pathway for deploying learned policies, reducing the significant time and resource investment associated with real-world data collection and training.

3. **Mitigation of Hardware-Induced Sensing Limitations:** The dual-frame temporal buffer specifically addresses a common hardware constraint in mobile robotics: restricted camera field-of-view. By maintaining short-term memory of scene information, the system compensates for temporary object loss during robot movements and narrow sensing windows. This enhances the policy's stability and robustness, making it more reliable in real-world scenarios where full panoramic views are often unavailable.

4. **Computational Efficiency:** T-DRN's lightweight architecture, characterized by a low parameter count and fast inference latency, is crucial for its practical applicability. Resource-constrained mobile robotic platforms can execute the policy in real-time, enabling responsive navigation behavior without requiring powerful onboard computing resources. This efficiency broadens the range of potential robotic applications.

5. **Implications for Embodied AI Research:** The findings regarding the effectiveness of difference-based features and the importance of aggregating information from *all* detected objects (rather than a focused subset) provide valuable insights for future embodied AI research. They suggest that comprehensive contextual awareness and relational understanding are paramount for robust navigation, especially in partially observable and novel environments. The analysis of temporal memory also indicates that an optimal, shorter memory span might be more effective in certain constrained sensing conditions, guiding future architectural designs.

The potential impact of this work extends to various applications, including household service robots, logistics automation, and exploration tasks, where robots need to operate autonomously in previously unseen environments and locate specific objects. By offering a robust, efficient, and directly transferable solution, this research contributes to bridging the gap between simulated robotic capabilities and their practical deployment in complex real-world settings. Future work on integrating long-term memory and handling dynamic obstacles could further enhance the system's capabilities for even more complex, multi-room environments.
