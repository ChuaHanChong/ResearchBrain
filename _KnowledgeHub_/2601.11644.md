# Predicting When to Trust Vision-Language Models for Spatial Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> A vision-based framework was developed at the University of Missouri Kansas City to predict the trustworthiness of Vision-Language Model (VLM) spatial reasoning outputs. This approach uses independent geometric verification through object detection, demonstrating a 34% relative improvement in AUROC for BLIP-2 predictions (0.674) compared to text-based confidence estimation methods.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) exhibit systematic failures and low accuracy (49-54%) in basic spatial reasoning tasks, making them unsuitable for critical applications.
- Existing confidence estimation methods for VLMs rely on text-based signals, which are ineffective for spatial reasoning and often yield AUROC scores near chance level due to VLM overconfidence.
- The lack of a robust method to predict VLM prediction trustworthiness for spatial reasoning hinders their safe and reliable deployment in high-stakes real-world systems.

## Method

- A ==four-stage pipeline== performs VLM spatial prediction, ==independent geometric validation== via object detection, ==multi-signal feature extraction==, and ==learned confidence fusion==.
- ==GroundingDINO-base== localizes objects to determine geometric relations and calculate ==geometric confidence (α_geo)==, providing an objective, external validation.
- An ==XGBoost classifier== combines four features (geometric confidence, separation confidence, detection quality, VLM token confidence) to output a final ==trustworthiness score== for VLM spatial predictions.

## Results

- Achieved AUROC scores of **0.674** for BLIP-2 and **0.583** for CLIP, representing **34.0%** and **16.1%** relative improvements over text-based baselines, respectively.
- At a **60%** target accuracy, the method achieved **61.9%** coverage for BLIP-2 predictions, outperforming geometric-only baselines by **2.2x** and text-based methods by approximately **19x**.
- Vision-based features contributed over **80%** of model importance (geometric confidence **37.5%** for BLIP-2), improving scene graph construction: BLIP-2 edge precision rose **52.1%** to **78.3%** while retaining **68.2%** of edges, at **87ms**/image overhead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - External, vision-based geometric verification provides a more reliable signal for VLM spatial reasoning trustworthiness than the VLM's internal self-assessment, which struggles with systematic overconfidence.
> - Fusing multiple complementary confidence signals (geometric, separation, detection quality, and VLM internal confidence) via a learned model significantly improves the accuracy of trustworthiness prediction.
> - Spatial relationships possess inherent objective verifiability, which can be leveraged through independent geometric analysis to effectively gauge VLM prediction reliability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated a range of capabilities in multimodal tasks, integrating visual and linguistic information for applications such as image captioning, visual question answering, and image generation. However, a documented limitation of these models is their performance in spatial reasoning. Prior research, including works by Liu et al. (2023) and Wang et al. (2025), indicates that state-of-the-art VLMs often exhibit systematic failures in basic spatial relationships, achieving accuracies as low as 49% to 54% on directional tasks. This deficit poses a challenge for their deployment in applications requiring precise spatial understanding, such as robotic navigation, autonomous driving, and advanced image editing, where errors could have significant consequences.

Efforts within the research community to address this spatial reasoning gap have primarily focused on improving the models' predictive accuracy. These include the development of specialized architectures (e.g., Kamath et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025) and the introduction of structured frameworks (e.g., Huang et al., 2025) designed to enhance how VLMs process and infer spatial relationships. While these approaches aim to make VLMs *more correct*, they generally do not address the equally critical need for quantifying the uncertainty of their predictions.

The ability to predict when a VLM's output is trustworthy is a distinct requirement, particularly for high-stakes real-world systems. Existing confidence estimation methods for VLMs, as explored by Khan et al. (2023), Manakul et al. (2023), and Kadavath et al. (2022), largely rely on text-based signals, such as verbalized confidence, multi-prompt consistency, or token probabilities. These methods have shown limitations, particularly for spatial reasoning tasks. For example, text-based approaches have been observed to yield an AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) around 0.503 on BLIP-2 for spatial tasks, which is only marginally better than random guessing. This is attributed to VLMs exhibiting systematic overconfidence, where they generate consistent but incorrect predictions with high internal probabilities, even when fundamentally mistaken about a spatial relation.

This research distinguishes itself by moving beyond self-assessment and proposing a vision-based confidence estimation framework. It leverages the objective verifiability of spatial relationships through independent geometric analysis, offering an external mechanism to validate VLM predictions. This approach addresses the limitation of internal, text-based confidence metrics by introducing a modality-specific oracle for spatial reasoning, aiming to provide a more reliable indicator of prediction trustworthiness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust method for predicting the trustworthiness of Vision-Language Model (VLM) predictions specifically for spatial reasoning tasks. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing VLM Spatial Reasoning Deficiencies:** VLMs, despite their advancements in general multimodal understanding, exhibit systematic failures and low accuracy (e.g., 49-54%) in basic spatial reasoning tasks, such as distinguishing "left" from "right." This performance level renders them unsuitable for applications where spatial precision is critical.

2. **Enabling Safe and Reliable Deployment:** For VLMs to be safely and effectively deployed in real-world, high-stakes applications like robotic navigation, autonomous driving, and image editing, it is essential to move beyond merely improving their average accuracy. A critical requirement is the ability to predict *when to trust* a VLM's spatial prediction and, conversely, when to abstain or defer to human review. Without this capability, the risk of consequential errors in safety-critical systems remains high.

3. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Confidence Estimation Methods:** Current confidence estimation strategies for VLMs predominantly rely on text-based signals, such as the model's self-expressed confidence or the consistency of its responses to multiple prompts. These methods have been shown to be ineffective for spatial reasoning, achieving AUROC scores near chance level. This failure stems from VLMs' tendency to exhibit "systematic overconfidence"—producing incorrect spatial predictions with high internal probabilities, thereby undermining self-assessment mechanisms.

4. **Leveraging Objective Verifiability of Spatial Relations:** The motivation for a novel approach is rooted in the insight that spatial relationships possess an inherent objective verifiability. Unlike more subjective or abstract reasoning tasks, a statement like "the dog is left of the person" can be externally verified by physically locating the objects in an image and computing their relative geometric positions. This offers a robust, independent ground truth that VLM internal states cannot consistently provide.

5. **Developing a Vision-Based External Validation Mechanism:** The specific objective is to implement this insight by proposing a confidence estimation framework that performs independent geometric verification of VLM spatial claims using object detection. This framework aims to fuse external vision-based signals with the VLM's internal uncertainty to produce a more reliable trustworthiness score.

In summary, the research is motivated by the critical need for VLM reliability in spatial reasoning for real-world applications, identifying a gap in current VLM capabilities and confidence estimation techniques. It proposes an external, vision-based validation approach as a solution to this problem.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology for vision-based confidence estimation in VLM spatial reasoning involves a four-stage pipeline designed to objectively validate VLM predictions through geometric verification.

#### 3.1. VLM Spatial Prediction

The initial stage involves querying the VLM to obtain a spatial prediction.
- For **generative VLMs** like BLIP-2 (opt-2.7b), natural language prompts such as "Where is the [object1] relative to the [object2]?" are used to elicit a spatial relation.
- For **classification-based models** like CLIP (vit-large-patch14), all possible spatial relations (e.g., "left", "right", "above", "below", "near") are scored. This is done by computing the image-text similarity between the image embedding and text embeddings generated from prompts like "The [object1] is [relation] of the [object2]". The relation with the highest similarity score is chosen as the predicted relation, denoted as $\hat{r}$.

#### 3.2. Geometric Validation via Object Detection

This stage provides an independent, vision-based verification of the VLM's prediction.
- **Object Localization:** GroundingDINO-base, an open-set object detector, is employed to localize the two objects (o1, o2) specified in the spatial query. This process yields bounding boxes (b1, b2) and associated detection confidences (c1, c2). If an object's detection confidence falls below a threshold (τ = 0.3), it is considered not detected, and a zero confidence is assigned for subsequent steps.
- **Center Point Calculation:** From the detected bounding boxes, the center coordinates (px, py) for each object are computed.
- **Geometric Relation Determination:** The geometric relation (r_geo) between the two objects is determined by comparing the absolute displacements along the x-axis (∆x = p_x2 - p_x1) and y-axis (∆y = p_y2 - p_y1). For instance, if |∆x| > |∆y| and ∆x > 0, the relation is "left."
- **Geometric Confidence Calculation (α_geo):** A geometric confidence score, α_geo ∈, is assigned based on the alignment between the VLM's predicted relation (ˆr) and the geometrically derived relation (r_geo). If ˆr contradicts r_geo, a low baseline confidence (0.2) is assigned. If they match, the confidence is set to 0.5 plus an additional term proportional to the displacement in the primary direction (min(1, d_primary/100)), up to a maximum of 1. This α_geo is then adjusted by the average detection quality (¯c = (c1 + c2)/2) using a sigmoid-like function: α_adj_geo = α_geo * (0.5 + 0.5 * 1/(1 + e^(-10*(¯c - 0.3)))).

#### 3.3. Multi-Signal Feature Extraction

Four complementary features are extracted to capture different aspects of confidence and ambiguity. This minimal set was selected to balance model capacity and generalization, aiming to reduce the train-validation AUROC gap.
- **Geometric Confidence (α_geo):** This feature directly quantifies the alignment between the VLM's prediction and the coordinate-based geometric verification.
- **Separation Confidence (α_sep):** Defined as 1 minus the Intersection over Union (IoU) of the two object bounding boxes. A low separation confidence (high IoU) indicates significant overlap, suggesting spatial ambiguity where relationships might be unclear.
- **Detection Quality (¯c):** The average confidence of the two object detections. Low scores signal challenging visual conditions or unreliable object localizations, which can affect the validity of geometric verification.
- **VLM Token Confidence:** For generative models (BLIP-2), this is the maximum token probability from the first generated token. For contrastive models (CLIP), it is the maximum similarity score among all candidate relations. This represents the VLM's internal assessment of its own prediction.

#### 3.4. Learned Confidence Fusion

The four extracted features are combined to generate a final confidence score indicating the probability that the VLM's prediction is correct.
- **Model:** An XGBoost classifier (gradient boosting) is used for this fusion. The classifier, h: R^4 →, is trained to predict P(VLM correct | f), where f is the vector of the four features.
- **Training:** The model is trained on 70% of 705 samples from the VSR benchmark, with hyperparameters (100 trees, learning rate 0.03, max depth 3, strong regularization α=0.5, λ=2.0) selected to promote generalization. The training achieved a train AUROC of 0.77 and a validation AUROC of 0.67.
- **Decision Threshold:** A confidence threshold (θ*) for classifying predictions as "trustworthy" or "untrustworthy" is determined using Youden's index on the validation set (θ* = arg max_θ [TPR(θ) - FPR(θ)]). For BLIP-2, θ* = 0.502; for CLIP, θ* = 0.380.

#### 3.5. Deployment Strategies

The framework supports two main deployment strategies:
- **Selective Prediction:** By ranking predictions based on their confidence score h(f), a system can choose to deploy only the top-k predictions that meet a desired accuracy target (1-ϵ), abstaining from uncertain cases.
- **Scene Graph Construction:** The pairwise confidence estimates can be used to prune unreliable edges from scene graphs, adding an edge (oi, rij, oj) only if its confidence h(fij) ≥ τ, thereby improving graph precision.

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

The models evaluated are BLIP-2 (opt-2.7b) and CLIP (vit-large-patch14). Object detection is performed by GroundingDINO-base with a threshold of τ = 0.3. The computational overhead of the confidence estimation framework is approximately 87ms per image (80ms for GroundingDINO, 5ms for feature extraction, 2ms for XGBoost inference), representing a 46% increase over VLM inference time, which is substantially lower than multi-prompt text-based methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results that indicate the efficacy of the proposed vision-based confidence estimation framework.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

The method demonstrates substantial performance improvements in discriminating correct from incorrect VLM spatial predictions compared to baselines.
- **AUROC:** For BLIP-2, the framework achieved an AUROC of 0.674, representing a 34.0% relative improvement over the text-based Khan et al. baseline (0.503 AUROC). For CLIP, it achieved an AUROC of 0.583, a 16.1% improvement over Khan et al. (0.502 AUROC). The geometric-only baseline showed AUROC scores of 0.493 (BLIP-2) and 0.509 (CLIP), indicating that the learned fusion of multiple signals is critical.
- **Coverage at Target Accuracy:** At a target accuracy of 60%, the method achieved a coverage of 61.9% for BLIP-2 predictions, which is 2.2 times higher than the geometric-only baseline (27.6%) and approximately 19 times higher than Khan et al.'s method (3.2%). For CLIP, coverage at 60% accuracy reached 54.5%, a 4.5 times improvement over Khan et al. (12.2%). At a more permissive 50% accuracy target, BLIP-2 achieved 91.7% coverage.
- **Baseline Limitations:** Text-based methods, specifically Khan et al.'s approach, exhibited imbalanced precision-recall trade-offs. For BLIP-2, it showed 100% recall with only 51.6% precision, indicating over-trusting nearly all predictions. For CLIP, it showed 1.8% recall, rejecting most predictions. This suggests that textual self-assessment struggles with systematic errors in spatial reasoning.

#### 4.2. Feature Importance Analysis

An analysis of XGBoost's learned feature importances revealed the relative contributions of the four input signals:
- **Vision-Based Dominance:** Vision-based features collectively contributed 87.4% of the model importance for BLIP-2 and 80.1% for CLIP.
- **Geometric Confidence:** This feature, reflecting the alignment between the VLM's prediction and geometric verification, was the most significant, contributing 37.5% for BLIP-2 and 38.4% for CLIP.
- **Separation Confidence:** This feature, accounting for spatial ambiguity due to object overlap, contributed 32.7% for BLIP-2 and 20.0% for CLIP.
- **Detection Quality:** The average confidence of object detections contributed 17.2% for BLIP-2 and 21.7% for CLIP, indicating its role in signaling unreliable geometric data.
- **VLM Token Confidence:** The VLM's internal confidence was the least impactful feature, contributing 12.7% for BLIP-2 and 20.0% for CLIP. This finding supports the argument that external geometric verification provides a more reliable signal than the VLM's self-assessment for spatial reasoning.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study confirmed that all four features contribute to the overall performance of the confidence estimator:
- Removing geometric confidence resulted in the largest performance drop, reducing AUROC by 0.171 points (from 0.674 to 0.503), underscoring its foundational role.
- Removing separation confidence caused a 0.062 AUROC drop.
- Removing detection quality caused a 0.033 AUROC drop.
- Even removing VLM token confidence, the weakest feature, resulted in a slight AUROC drop of 0.006.
- The full model with learned fusion achieved a 0.181 AUROC improvement (+36.7%) over the geometric-only baseline (0.493 AUROC), demonstrating the value of optimally combining complementary signals.

#### 4.4. Application: Scene Graph Construction

The confidence scores enabled more reliable scene graph construction.
- By filtering edges based on confidence, precision for BLIP-2 could be increased from a baseline of 52.1% to 78.3% while retaining 68.2% of edges (F1 score of 72.8%).
- At a 60% target accuracy, the method retained 61.9% of BLIP-2 edges with 69.5% precision, significantly outperforming baselines (27.6% coverage for geometric, 3.2% for Khan et al.).
- The framework allowed for flexible operating points: at 70% accuracy, BLIP-2 retained 23.7% of edges with 76.8% precision; at 80% accuracy, it retained 12.2% coverage with 84.3% precision.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative examples illustrated the method's behavior:
- **Success Cases:** When VLM and geometric validation agreed, the framework produced high confidence scores, correctly trusting the VLM.
- **Contradiction Handling:** A key strength was demonstrated when the VLM's prediction contradicted geometric verification (e.g., VLM predicts "left" but geometry indicates "right"). In such cases, the method correctly assigned a very low confidence (e.g., 0.08), while baselines might have incorrectly accepted the prediction.
- **Detection Failure:** When object detection failed completely, the method appropriately expressed uncertainty, highlighting a limitation that spatial verification depends on reliable object localization.

#### 4.6. Computational Cost

The method introduced a modest computational overhead of 87ms per image (46% relative to VLM inference), which is significantly more efficient than text-based multi-prompt methods (950ms, or 500% overhead).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by addressing a critical challenge: the reliability of spatial reasoning in real-world applications. The proposed vision-based confidence estimation framework has several implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Responsible AI Deployment:** By providing a mechanism to predict when VLM spatial predictions are trustworthy, the framework directly supports the safe and responsible deployment of AI in high-stakes environments. Applications such as autonomous driving, robotic navigation, and medical image analysis, which require precise spatial understanding, can leverage this system to identify and defer uncertain predictions to human oversight, thereby mitigating risks associated with VLM errors.

2. **Addressing a Core VLM Limitation:** The work directly tackles the documented weakness of VLMs in spatial reasoning, demonstrating that while VLMs may struggle with accuracy, their outputs can be effectively filtered for reliability. This transforms unreliable baseline performance (49-54% accuracy) into a practical tool through selective prediction, achieving high coverage at desired accuracy levels (e.g., 61.9% coverage at 60% accuracy for BLIP-2).

3. **Validation of External Verification as a Design Principle:** A key finding is the dominance of vision-based features (87.4% model importance) over VLM internal confidence (12.7%) for spatial reasoning. This validates a general principle: for tasks with objective, verifiable ground truth (an "oracle"), external verification mechanisms are superior to internal model self-assessment for confidence estimation. This principle has broader implications for building reliable multimodal systems in other structured prediction tasks, such as temporal reasoning, numerical reasoning, physical reasoning, and factual claims, where similar external verification can be applied.

4. **Improved Scene Graph Construction:** The ability to assign confidence at the pairwise relation level facilitates the construction of more precise and reliable scene graphs. This is crucial for applications requiring structured spatial representations, such as visual question answering, knowledge representation, and complex image understanding, where high-quality relational information is paramount. The framework allows for flexible trade-offs between precision and coverage, enabling applications to select operating points based on their specific reliability requirements.

5. **Computational Efficiency for Real-time Applications:** The modest computational overhead (46% relative to VLM inference) makes the proposed method practical for integration into systems requiring near real-time processing. This efficiency, especially when compared to more resource-intensive multi-prompt text-based methods, enhances its applicability in operational settings.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the dependency on the quality of underlying object detection systems, errors with multi-instance disambiguation, small objects, or occlusion, provide clear directions for future research. This includes exploring keypoint-based validation, uncertainty-aware detection, and extensions to ternary relations or 3D spatial reasoning, which could further enhance the framework's robustness and applicability.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the field by offering a practical and empirically supported solution for increasing the trustworthiness of VLM spatial reasoning. By establishing external geometric verification as a more reliable confidence signal than internal self-assessment, it provides a blueprint for building more dependable and deployable AI systems across various structured prediction tasks.
