# Rethinking Self-Evolving Agent Skills: Feedback Dynamics over Multiple Rounds

> [!summary] Summary
> A controlled framework isolates how feedback composition drives self-evolving agent skills: executor, optimizer, revision operator, validation rule and ten-round budget stay fixed while the optimizer sees successes only, failures only, or both. Across 42 runs, 14 model-benchmark settings and three models, only 55 of 388 candidates set byte-distinct validation bests, and all 11 evolved selections came from feedback containing failed trajectories.

## Problem

- Reported before-and-after scores hide accepted revisions, rejected candidates, rollbacks and stopping decisions, so prior results cannot say when extra evolution rounds still help.
- Each self-evolving skill system evaluates its own native feedback policy under its own revision and selection procedure, so success-driven and failure-driven evidence were never compared fairly.
- It stayed untested whether extra per-instance inference, such as sampling or refinement, recovers the same gains as a persistent change to the skill.

## Method

- A ==feedback-conditioned evolution loop== runs Normal, Fail-only and Success-only views from one shared parent skill, holding executor, optimizer, revision operator and ten-round budget identical.
- A ==validation gate== advances any non-decreasing candidate and rolls back regressions; the best checkpoint updates only on strict improvement plus a ==byte-distinct== skill, separating real edits from rerun noise.
- Frozen skills pass a common evaluation path: released test, three robustness tiers, three transfer tiers, plus GPT-5.5 controls using oracle ==Parallel Sampling== and ==Sequential Refinement==.

## Results

- Only **55** of **388** candidates set byte-distinct validation bests; validation picked an evolved skill in **11** of **14** settings, nine of which improved released test.
- All 11 evolved selections came from failure-containing views (Normal nine, Fail-only two); SpreadsheetBench gained **35.6**, **37.7** and **28.8** test points across the three models.
- Oracle Parallel Sampling landed **0.43** points below the evolved SearchQA skill but **30.96** points behind on SpreadsheetBench; Sequential Refinement recovered neither gain.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Failed trajectories carry the corrective signal; successes alone let an optimizer mistake incidental commonalities for task rules, sometimes collapsing validation scores outright.
> - Self-evolution is validation-filtered search, not monotonic refinement: 38 of 55 new bests arrived early, yet six of 11 selections appeared in rounds 6-9.
> - Test-time sampling substitutes for evolution only when a skill adds answer formatting; multi-step procedural workflows stay far out of reach of resampling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence agents has increasingly focused on self-evolving skill systems. These systems aim to enhance agent capabilities by enabling them to learn and refine reusable behaviors, known as skills, based on feedback from their executions. This refinement occurs without modifying the agent's underlying model parameters, allowing for persistent improvements that generalize across future tasks.

Prior research in self-evolving skill systems has explored various strategies for incorporating feedback. These strategies can generally be categorized into three groups: success-gated methods, which leverage positive execution traces to identify and retain useful discoveries; diagnosis-oriented methods, which focus on observed failures to pinpoint and repair skill defects; and mixed-trajectory methods, which integrate both successful and failed execution evidence.

However, a notable gap in the existing literature is the isolated analysis of feedback composition. Previous studies typically evaluate each self-evolving system under its unique revision and selection procedures, making it difficult to ascertain the specific impact of different feedback types. Consequently, it has remained unclear when additional rounds of evolution are beneficial, how successful versus failed trajectories distinctly influence skill revision, and whether gains from persistent skill updates could be replicated by simply increasing computational effort during inference (test-time computation) for single task instances. Furthermore, factors such as run-to-run variation and verifier choices have complicated direct comparisons and the interpretation of reported score changes.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a controlled evaluation framework. This framework systematically isolates the effects of different feedback compositions (successful trajectories only, failed trajectories only, or both) while maintaining consistent evolution procedures, model configurations, and evaluation criteria. By doing so, the work provides a more granular understanding of the dynamics of skill evolution, its efficiency, its generalization capabilities, and its relationship to alternative forms of computational scaling, thus contributing to a more robust characterization of self-evolving agent skills.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to move beyond aggregate performance metrics in self-evolving skill systems and gain a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics of skill evolution. The authors identify three specific questions that existing literature has not definitively answered:

1. **When further evolution helps, and when the search stops making progress:** Existing reports often present "before-and-after" scores, which obscure the iterative process of skill revision, including accepted revisions, rejected candidates, rollbacks, and decisions to stop. It is unclear whether performance improvements are steady throughout evolution rounds or if progress is sparse and intermittent. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for optimizing the computational cost and duration of skill evolution.

2. **How successful and failed trajectories shape revision under the same procedure and across different models:** Current self-evolving systems employ diverse approaches to feedback, some using only successes, some only failures, and others both. However, these systems are typically evaluated with their own specific revision and selection procedures. This makes it challenging to disentangle the causal effect of different feedback compositions (e.g., "Normal" (both), "Fail-only," or "Success-only") on skill evolution when all other factors (executor, optimizer configuration, revision procedure, validation rule, round budget) are held constant. The motivation is to isolate the impact of different feedback types to determine their relative efficacy and specific contributions to skill refinement across various models.

3. **Whether additional per-instance inference can achieve similar gains without changing the persistent skill:** A practical question in agent development is whether the observed performance gains from persistent skill evolution are truly attributable to a more capable, revised skill, or if they could be achieved by simply applying more computational resources (e.g., multiple attempts, refinement steps) at test time for each task instance using the original, unevolved skill. Distinguishing between persistent skill improvement and transient gains from increased inference-time computation is important for understanding the nature and value of self-evolution.

To address these questions, the research aims to establish a controlled evaluation framework capable of providing transparent, artifact-level analysis of skill evolution. This involves tracking every candidate skill, acceptance/rollback decisions, and validation-best updates, along with comprehensive downstream evaluations on various generalization tasks and comparisons with test-time scaling methods. The overarching goal is to characterize persistent skill self-evolution more accurately as a validation-filtered search process rather than a linear or steady improvement, and to provide empirical insights into the optimal design choices for feedback mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a controlled evaluation framework designed to isolate the effects of feedback composition on skill evolution. This framework is applied across multiple models and benchmarks, systematically tracing the entire evolution trajectory of agent skills.

#### 3.1. Framework Overview

The core of the framework involves an underlying agent model (M) and a persistent skill (s), which evolves over rounds. At each round *r*, the current skill *s(c)r* (where *c* denotes a feedback condition) is used to execute tasks, generating trajectories *τ(c)r*. A benchmark verifier labels these trajectories as successful or failed. Based on this, a feedback component constructs one of three views:
- **Normal:** Includes both successful and failed trajectories.
- **Fail-only:** Includes only failed trajectories.
- **Success-only:** Includes only successful trajectories.

An optimizer (O), instantiated by the same underlying model, proposes a candidate skill *bs(c)r+1* based on the current skill and the condition-specific feedback. This candidate is subjected to a validation gate. If its validation score does not decrease, it becomes the next-round skill *s(c)r+1*; otherwise, the current skill *s(c)r* is retained. A separate validation-best skill *s(c)best* is tracked, updated only upon strict validation improvement and if the candidate is byte-distinct from the incoming skill (to filter out score changes due to execution variability). After the final round, the validation-best skill from each feedback view is frozen for comprehensive evaluation.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup

- **Models and Benchmarks:** The primary study evaluates three underlying models: GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and DeepSeek V4-Pro. These models are tested on five diverse benchmarks: SearchQA (short-answer QA), OfficeQA (grounded QA with tools), SpreadsheetBench (spreadsheet manipulation), LiveMath (mathematical reasoning), and DocVQA (VQA on document images). The DeepSeek–DocVQA setting was excluded due to technical constraints. This setup yields 14 model–benchmark settings, totaling 42 matched feedback runs. An extended analysis for SearchQA covers eight models in total.
- **Evolution Protocol:** For each model–benchmark setting, the three feedback views (Normal, Fail-only, Success-only) start from an identical parent skill. Key parameters are held constant across these views: the executor and optimizer configuration, the revision procedure, the validation rule, and a fixed budget of ten evolution rounds. Each round involves executing 40 training trajectories (36 for LiveMath). If the feedback view is non-empty, the optimizer is called to propose at most four minimal, task-general edits. Validation is performed on a full split. A run stops early if it reaches ten rounds, experiences five consecutive regressions or no-ops, or if the view-specific feedback pool becomes empty.
- **Post-Evolution Evaluation:** Selected evolved skills (chosen based on validation scores alone) are frozen and evaluated on several dimensions:
 - **Released Test:** Performance on a standard test set.
 - **Same-Task Robustness (R):** Assessed across three tiers: R1 (surface form changes), R2 (irrelevant context/distractors), and R3 (output/interface contract changes).
 - **Transfer (T):** Assessed across three tiers: T1 (new official task, same source), T2 (new source, same task subtype), and T3 (new source, subtype/difficulty/distribution shift).
 - **Test-Time Scaling Controls:** For GPT-5.5 settings, evolved skills are compared against two test-time inference strategies:
 - **Parallel Sampling:** Multiple independent attempts are made, and the oracle best outcome is reported.
 - **Sequential Refinement:** Additional attempts are conditioned on the task and previous responses.
 These controls share the same executor, tool interface, test pool, verifier, and call budget.

The methodology emphasizes strict controls, byte-distinct artifact tracking, and multi-faceted evaluation to provide a comprehensive and unbiased characterization of self-evolving skill dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the dynamics of self-evolving agent skills, the impact of feedback composition, and the relationship between persistent skill gains and test-time computation.

#### 4.1. Sparsity and Dynamics of Skill Evolution

- Skill evolution is characterized as a sparse search process rather than a steady, monotonic improvement. Out of 388 candidate skills evaluated across 42 feedback runs, only 55 established byte-distinct validation bests. This indicates that while revision activity is common, validation-confirmed, byte-distinct improvements are less frequent.
- The trajectories of evolution vary significantly, ranging from late discovery of effective skills to early saturation or complete stagnation (no improvements). Although most new-best events (38 out of 55) occurred in early rounds (1-4), a substantial portion of the *finally selected* evolved skills (6 out of 11) emerged in later rounds (6-9), suggesting that prolonged search can be consequential.
- Rollbacks, where a candidate skill performs worse on validation and the incumbent skill is retained, are a common part of the process, indicating that not all proposed revisions are beneficial.

#### 4.2. Impact of Feedback Composition

- Feedback conditions that include failed trajectories (Normal or Fail-only) are crucial for successful skill evolution. In the primary study, all 11 validation-selected evolved skills originated from either Normal (9 settings) or Fail-only (2 settings) feedback views.
- The **Success-only** feedback view was never selected in the primary study. This suggests that while successful traces show what works, they often lack the negative contrast needed to identify and correct skill defects effectively.
- The relative efficacy of Normal versus Fail-only varied by model and benchmark. For instance, on OfficeQA, Normal led to a superior skill by integrating insights from successes (what to preserve) with diagnoses from failures (what to fix). On SpreadsheetBench, Fail-only proved more effective by focusing explicitly on critical, verifier-visible defects, leading to substantial gains.
- An extended analysis on SearchQA across eight models further confirmed these feedback-dependent dynamics, with Normal or Fail-only being selected in six models and Success-only in two.

#### 4.3. Performance Gains and Generalization

- Among the 11 validation-selected evolved skills, 9 demonstrated improved performance on released test sets, with gains ranging from 0.5 to 37.7 percentage points.
- Generalization capabilities varied: 9 of the 11 selected skills improved robustness, and 9 improved transfer performance. Seven skills improved both robustness and transfer simultaneously.
- **SpreadsheetBench** showed particularly strong and consistent gains across all three models (GPT-5.5, Gemini, DeepSeek) for test, robustness, and transfer, indicating significant and broad-ranging improvements.
- **LiveMath** exhibited model-dependent variability; for GPT-5.5, the evolved skill improved robustness but showed decreased test and transfer performance, while for Gemini and DeepSeek, all three measures improved. This highlights that generalization is not solely dependent on validation improvement but on the interaction between the benchmark, model, and the specific retained skill.
- The analysis revealed benchmark-level convergence in the *type* of operational guidance learned (e.g., answer normalization for SearchQA, executable postconditions for SpreadsheetBench, theorem-level option comparison for LiveMath), with model-specific specializations in their clauses.

#### 4.4. Self-Evolution versus Test-Time Scaling

- A key finding from the GPT-5.5 test-time scaling controls is the distinct nature of gains from persistent skill evolution compared to increased per-instance inference.
- On **SearchQA**, oracle Parallel Sampling nearly matched the performance of the evolved skill, coming within 0.43 points. This suggests that for tasks primarily requiring better answer formulation or canonical naming, increased response diversity can partially replicate evolutionary gains.
- However, on **SpreadsheetBench**, the evolved skill significantly outperformed Parallel Sampling by 30.96 points. This indicates that tasks requiring complex, multi-step procedural workflows cannot be effectively improved by merely sampling more responses from the unevolved parent skill; they necessitate fundamental, persistent policy changes.
- Sequential Refinement, which conditions additional attempts on preceding responses, reproduced neither the SearchQA nor SpreadsheetBench gains, suggesting that this form of corrective feedback within a single instance is insufficient for major skill improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a more nuanced and empirically grounded understanding of self-evolving agent skills, challenging the perception of skill evolution as a steadily improving process. The findings have several implications and potential impacts:

- **Rethinking Agent Skill Development:** The characterization of self-evolution as sparse, validation-filtered search, rather than continuous monotonic improvement, suggests that resource allocation for agent skill development should account for intermittent gains and potential plateaus. This can inform more efficient strategies for training duration and iteration management.

- **Guidance for Feedback Mechanism Design:** The finding that feedback including failed trajectories (Normal or Fail-only) is critical for effective skill evolution, while Success-only feedback is often insufficient, provides direct guidance for designing more robust and efficient skill revision mechanisms. Future agent systems can prioritize or strategically combine failure analysis with successful examples to maximize learning potential. This moves beyond intuitive assumptions to evidence-based design.

- **Enhanced Evaluation Methodologies:** The paper highlights the inadequacy of relying solely on endpoint scores. It advocates for comprehensive evaluation protocols that include:
 - **Search Trajectory Analysis:** Reporting the full history of candidates, acceptances, and rollbacks to understand how evolution unfolds.
 - **Skill Identity Tracking:** Using byte-distinctness to confirm actual skill changes versus execution variability.
 - **Downstream Generalization:** Systematically assessing performance on robustness and transfer tasks to determine the breadth and durability of learned skills.
 - **Test-Time Computation Controls:** Explicitly comparing evolved skills against various test-time scaling methods to differentiate persistent policy changes from transient gains through additional inference. This ensures a clearer attribution of performance improvements.

- **Understanding the Nature of Skill Gains:** By distinguishing between gains recoverable by test-time sampling (e.g., SearchQA) and those requiring persistent skill modification (e.g., SpreadsheetBench), the research clarifies the types of problems where self-evolution offers unique value. This informs decisions on when to invest in persistent skill development versus when to simply increase inference-time compute. For tasks demanding complex procedural knowledge, persistent skill evolution is demonstrated to be indispensable.

- **Cross-Model and Cross-Benchmark Insights:** The consistent patterns observed across diverse models and benchmarks, such as the importance of failure feedback and the sparse nature of improvements, suggest general principles for self-evolving agents. Conversely, the model- and benchmark-dependent variability in generalization and specific feedback efficacy highlights the need for tailored approaches and careful consideration of task characteristics.

Overall, this work contributes to the scientific understanding of how agent skills evolve, providing critical insights for the design, evaluation, and practical application of future self-improving AI agents.
