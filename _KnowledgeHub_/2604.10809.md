# WARPED: Wrist-Aligned Rendering for Robot Policy Learning from Egocentric Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> WARPED trains visuomotor policies from monocular egocentric human video alone, skipping teleoperation, depth sensors, or multiview rigs. Vision foundation models plus hand-object interaction optimization track and retarget human motion to a robot gripper; Gaussian Splatting renders photorealistic wrist-view images to train a diffusion policy, matching teleoperation success rates at 5-8x less data-collection time.

## Problem

- Prior human-video-to-robot methods depend on multiview cameras, depth sensors, or custom collection hardware, limiting ease of new-task data collection.
- Human-demonstration approaches typically train policies for third-person or egocentric viewpoints, unable to exploit wrist-mounted cameras that capture fine-grained contact detail at deployment.
- Existing wrist-view synthesis methods (embodiment swapping, inpainting) rely on custom-trained generative models or produce geometrically inaccurate renders lacking true hand-object interaction constraints.

## Method

- Interactive scene initialization builds a ==Gaussian Splat== from a monocular RGB scan, then localizes per-demo hand pose (==HAMER==) and object pose/mesh (==Grounding DINO==, ==SAM2==, ==SAM3D==, ==MegaPose==) within it.
- Hand-object optimization jointly refines ==MANO== hand and object poses per frame using ==occlusion-aware mask, depth, contact, collision, and stable-grasp losses== to resolve interaction ambiguity.
- Retargeting-and-rendering pipeline maps hand keyframes to end-effector trajectories via thumb-index pose mapping and pre-contact trajectory optimization, then renders fisheye wrist views via ==3D Gaussian Splatting== for ==diffusion policy== training with augmentation. **WARPED** is the full pipeline name.

## Results

- WARPED matches or exceeds teleoperation on **4 of 5** tabletop tasks (e.g. **20/20** vs **16/20** Rotate Box, **17/20** vs **16/20** Bottle from Rack) while collecting data **5-8x** faster per Table III.
- Hand-object optimization beats FoundationPose tracking by a wide margin on success rate, **17/20** vs **11/20** on Rotate Box and **17/20** vs **2/20** on Can on Plate.
- Ablations show augmentation is critical, removing it drops success to **0/20** on three of five tasks, and the Alter inpainting baseline fails broadly (**0-8/20**), confirming geometry-accurate rendering matters.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Photorealistic, geometry-grounded rendering of wrist views from monocular human video can substitute for teleoperated data without specialized capture hardware.
> - Natural human hand motion can outperform teleoperated end-effector control on tasks requiring smooth continuous rotation, since teleoperation interfaces struggle with fine rotational precision.
> - Rendered synthetic demonstrations can co-train with real teleoperated data, recovering teleoperation-level behavior while cutting total demonstration collection time.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of imitation learning for robotic visuomotor policies, specifically addressing challenges related to data collection and generalization. Imitation learning is a common approach for training robots to perform a variety of manipulation tasks, from simple pick-and-place to complex, long-horizon operations. However, the efficacy of these policies largely depends on the availability and quality of demonstration data.

Current methodologies for acquiring robot demonstration data often face limitations. Large-scale datasets derived from teleoperated robot demonstrations or internet videos are typically expensive and difficult to collect, especially when scaling to new tasks, environments, or domain-specific applications like agriculture. Alternatively, collecting new teleoperated robot data is a slow, time-consuming, and labor-intensive process, often requiring specialized interfaces such as keyboards, game controllers, 3D SpaceMice, or more advanced VR/AR-based systems and custom hardware. These methods can be constrained in terms of fine-grained manipulation and introduce user learning overhead or additional hardware requirements.

To mitigate these limitations, recent research has explored learning from human demonstrations, which can be collected more rapidly due to humans' natural manipulation abilities. Approaches in this area often involve specialized data collection interfaces, data augmentation strategies, or simplified intermediate representations like hand-pose trajectories, keypoints, or object-centric 3D representations. However, many of these methods still necessitate additional sensing modalities, such as multi-view images or depth sensors for reconstruction and tracking, or rely on custom-trained generative models to convert human demonstrations into robot-compatible observations. Such dependencies can hinder the ease of data collection for novel tasks, as users may not have access to the required components. Furthermore, existing human demonstration-based methods frequently limit policy execution to fixed or egocentric camera viewpoints, precluding the use of wrist-mounted cameras that offer fine-grained detail crucial for robotic manipulation.

The paper aims to bridge the observation gap between human demonstrations and robot execution by synthesizing robot-centric wrist-camera views from alternative viewpoints. Prior work in this area includes methods like WristWorld, Imagination at Inference, and RwoR, which generate wrist-view observations or convert human hand demonstrations into robot end-effector observations. Other approaches involve replacing human embodiment with a robot through image editing or inpainting, such as Phantom and Masquerade, to produce robot-consistent visual observations. Additionally, neural rendering and view synthesis techniques, including Gaussian Splatting, have been employed to render novel views and augment robot demonstrations from limited real data, enhancing data diversity without extensive real-world trajectory collection. WARPED builds upon these advances, specifically focusing on monocular RGB input and the generation of wrist-view observations, while minimizing specialized hardware requirements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, named WARPED (Wrist-Aligned Rendering for Robot Policy Learning from Egocentric Human Demonstrations), that enables the training of robotic visuomotor policies from human demonstrations using solely monocular RGB data. This framework aims to synthesize realistic wrist-view observations and corresponding actions, traditionally associated with robotic end-effectors, from egocentric human demonstration videos.

The key motivations driving this work are:

1. **Reduce Reliance on Specialized Hardware and Sensing**: Existing imitation learning approaches often depend on complex data collection setups, including multi-view camera systems, depth sensors, or custom hardware interfaces. This dependency limits their scalability and ease of adaptation to new tasks or environments, as acquiring and configuring such equipment can be expensive and time-consuming. WARPED seeks to eliminate these requirements, making data collection more accessible and portable.
2. **Enable Wrist-View Policy Execution**: Many current methods for learning from human demonstrations restrict policy execution to fixed or egocentric camera viewpoints, similar to those used during data collection. Wrist-mounted cameras on robots are often advantageous because they provide detailed, close-up views of the interactive scene, which is beneficial for fine-grained manipulation tasks. A key objective is to generate wrist-camera observations from human egocentric demonstrations, thereby allowing policies to be directly trained for and deployed with wrist-mounted robot cameras.
3. **Improve Data Collection Efficiency**: Collecting teleoperated robot demonstrations is a slow, labor-intensive, and costly process. Human demonstrations, in contrast, can be collected much faster and more naturally. By leveraging egocentric human videos, WARPED aims to significantly reduce the time and effort required to acquire sufficient demonstration data for training robust policies.
4. **Enhance Policy Generalization and Robustness**: The framework aims to provide a robust method for transforming human motions into robot-compatible trajectories and visual observations. By integrating vision foundation models and neural rendering techniques like Gaussian Splatting, WARPED seeks to synthesize photorealistic observations that can be augmented, thereby improving the sim-to-real transfer capabilities and generalization of trained policies to novel objects and scenes.
5. **Facilitate Scalability for New Tasks**: The overarching motivation is to create a scalable and adaptable system for training visuomotor policies. By simplifying the data collection process and generating a diverse set of robot-centric observations and actions, WARPED intends to enable rapid adaptation to new manipulation tasks without incurring prohibitive costs or extensive manual effort.

In summary, WARPED is motivated by the need for a more accessible, efficient, and versatile framework for robot imitation learning, one that can translate the speed and naturalness of human demonstrations into deployable robot policies with optimal camera viewpoints, while minimizing hardware and sensing requirements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The WARPED framework transforms egocentric RGB human demonstrations into a wrist-camera robotic observation-action dataset for training visuomotor policies for tabletop manipulation tasks. The pipeline consists of five main stages: data collection, interactive scene initialization, hand-object optimization, wrist-view retargeting and rendering, and policy training and deployment.

#### 3.1. Assumptions

The method operates under the assumptions that all manipulated objects are rigid and that tasks occur in a tabletop setting with no significant scene changes other than the hand-object interaction during the demonstration.

#### 3.2. Data Collection

Data collection involves two steps:
1. **Scene Scan**: The user first records a short monocular RGB video of the workspace *without* the manipulated objects. This video is used for Structure-from-Motion (SfM) with Lightglue feature matching to estimate camera poses and generate a sparse 3D reconstruction of the environment. This reconstruction initializes a 3D Gaussian Splat representation of the scene and localizes subsequent demonstrations. This process typically takes less than one minute.
2. **Demonstration Recording**: The user wears a head-mounted egocentric monocular RGB camera (e.g., GoPro Hero 9 on a helmet) to record multiple demonstrations of tabletop manipulation tasks within the workspace at 30 Hz.

#### 3.3. Interactive Scene Initialization

This stage reconstructs and initializes the geometry of the hand and objects within the scene.
1. **Interactive Scene Reconstruction**: For each demonstration, frames are localized within the static scene using Hierarchical Localization and Light-Glue. SpatialTrackerV2 extracts monocular depth maps for each frame. A scene-level scale alignment is performed between the SfM reconstruction and predicted depth maps, and the scene Gaussian Splat is rescaled.
2. **Hand Pose Initialization**: HAMER is used to obtain initial per-frame hand shape and pose estimates. These parameters are then refined through a two-stage sequence-level optimization that enforces temporal smoothness, consistency with monocular depth estimates, and regularization against original HAMER outputs.
3. **Object Pose Initialization**: A text description of the object is used with Grounding DINO for detection, followed by SAM2 for segmentation mask generation and propagation across the sequence. SAM3D reconstructs an initial object mesh. A custom Gaussian Splat is created for the object by rendering multi-view images of this mesh to achieve higher fidelity. MegaPose provides an initial 6D pose estimate using the reconstructed mesh and first-frame segmentation. The object pose and scale are further optimized by enforcing mask and depth consistency for frames prior to hand contact.

#### 3.4. Hand-Object Optimization

This stage accurately tracks the 3D pose of the object by leveraging complementary geometric constraints from hand and object motion, particularly during occlusions. It involves two steps:
1. **Object Pose Estimation**: The object pose for each frame is sequentially estimated using differentiable rendering. Losses include an occlusion-aware mask loss (between rendered and SAM2 masks, ignoring hand-occluded regions), a depth consistency loss (between rendered and predicted depths), and a DINOv2 feature-based cosine similarity loss (between rendered and masked original images). The pose from the previous frame initializes the current frame's optimization. Contact start and end frames are identified based on changes in object translation or rotation.
2. **Joint Hand-Object Refinement**: All object (rotation, translation, global scale) and MANO hand parameters (global rotation, translation, pose, shape) are jointly optimized across all frames. This optimization minimizes a comprehensive set of losses:
 - **Occlusion-Aware Mask Loss**: Accounts for mutual hand-object occlusions for both hand and object masks.
 - **Depth Loss**: Enforces consistency between rendered and predicted depths for both hand and object.
 - **Contact Loss**: Encourages proximity between frequently contacted hand vertices (thumb/index fingertips) and object vertices during contact frames.
 - **Collision Loss**: Penalizes hand vertices interpenetrating the object volume using a precomputed truncated signed distance field (TSDF) of the object.
 - **Stable Grasp Loss**: Encourages consistent distances between finger tip vertices and object vertices during contact.
 - **Auxiliary Losses**: Includes a scene TSDF loss (prevents hand/object collision with the static scene), a resting-on-scene loss (encourages object contact with the scene when not handled), hand projection loss (keeps projected hand vertices near 2D keypoints), and hand pose regularization (limits deviation from HAMER outputs).

#### 3.5. Retargeting and Rendering

After pose recovery, human hand motion is converted into robot end-effector trajectories, and corresponding wrist-camera observations are rendered.
1. **Keyframe Extraction**: A sparse set of evenly spaced keyframes is extracted from the full hand-object trajectory.
2. **Hand-to-End-Effector Mapping**: For pre-contact frames, the robot end-effector's tool center point (TCP) is mapped to the midpoint of the human thumb and index fingertips. The gripper's orientation is aligned using the vector connecting the thumb and index MCP joints.
3. **Pre-Contact Trajectory Optimization**: An optimization is applied to prevent unintended gripper-object collisions, combining a funnel loss (maintaining trajectory proximity to original motion), a collision loss (preventing gripper penetration into the object TSDF), and a smoothness loss (encouraging temporally smooth motion).
4. **Contact Grasp Refinement**: At the contact start frame, the end-effector pose and gripper width are refined to achieve a physically plausible grasp. This involves identifying contact points on the object mesh and optimizing the gripper position and width to align with these points, minimizing contact loss, maximizing gripper closure, and preventing collision.
5. **Motion Propagation (during contact)**: During contact, the end-effector pose is updated by applying the relative object motion with respect to the contact frame, ensuring a rigid follow of the object while maintaining the grasp.
6. **Rendering**: Keyframe end-effector and object poses are interpolated into continuous trajectories. Wrist-camera views are rendered by combining the Gaussian Splats of the static scene, the object, and the end-effector. Fisheye images are rendered using Nerfstudio's 3DGUT implementation.

#### 3.6. Policy Training

A diffusion policy is trained using the generated wrist-view observations and robot proprioceptive inputs to produce relative pose and gripper action chunks.
1. **Sim-to-Real Mitigation**: Gaussian noise is added to input images during training to reduce the sim-to-real gap between rendered and real robot images.
2. **Data Augmentation**: To increase dataset diversity and robustness, demonstrations are augmented. This includes retexturing object meshes, random object translation, random initial gripper pose, random scene scaling, and perturbation of wrist-camera intrinsics and extrinsics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates WARPED's performance across five tabletop manipulation tasks: Rotate Box, Pour Mug, Take Bottle out of Dish Rack, Wipe Plate with Brush, and Pick and Place Can on Plate.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison with Baselines

Policies trained using WARPED are compared against a teleoperation baseline and the Alter baseline.
- **Comparable to Teleoperation**: WARPED achieves success rates comparable to teleoperation on Pour Mug (18/20), Bottle from Rack (17/20), and Can on Plate (17/20).
- **Outperforms Teleoperation**: WARPED outperforms teleoperation on Rotate Box (20/20 vs. 16/20), which is attributed to the more precise and consistent rotational control achievable with natural human hand motion.
- **Underperforms Teleoperation**: For Wipe Brush, WARPED performs lower than teleoperation (11/20 vs. 15/20). This is attributed to the task involving a small object lying flat, posing challenges for accurate pose estimation and retargeting.
- **Alter Baseline Performance**: The Alter baseline consistently exhibits poor performance across all tasks (e.g., 7/20 for Rotate Box, 0/20 for Bottle from Rack), indicating that naive wrist-view image rendering without accurate hand-object geometry is insufficient for policy learning.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Novel Objects

WARPED’s ability to generalize to novel objects of the same category, not seen during training, was evaluated.
- **Superior Generalization**: WARPED consistently outperforms teleoperation on novel objects for most tasks (Rotate Box, Bottle from Rack, Can on Plate), with the exception of Wipe Brush.
- **Robustness to Variation**: The performance difference is particularly notable on specific novel objects for Rotate Box and Bottle from Rack, suggesting WARPED’s robustness to variations in object geometry and size.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Scenes

For the Can on Plate task, WARPED was evaluated on four unseen, diverse tabletop scenes after training on 50 demonstrations across 20 varied settings.
- **Moderate Generalization**: WARPED achieved a success rate of 16/20. The observed variations in performance are attributed to subtle differences in training data, such as lighting and scene layout.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Background Distractors

The framework’s robustness to background visual distractors not present in the initial scene Gaussian Splat was tested.
- **Minimal Impact**: Background distractors had negligible impact on Can on Plate (17/20) and Bottle from Rack (17/20).
- **Small Degradation**: A small drop in performance was observed for Rotate Box (18/20) and Wipe Brush (9/20).
- **Largest Degradation**: The most significant degradation occurred for Pour Mug (15/20), primarily due to missed grasps.

#### 4.5. Data Collection Efficiency

A comparison of data collection time between WARPED and teleoperation was performed.
- **Significant Time Reduction**: WARPED required approximately 5 to 8 times less data collection time than teleoperation across all tasks (e.g., 3:37 for Rotate Box vs. 22:51 for teleoperation). This includes the initial scene scan for WARPED.

#### 4.6. Complementing Teleoperated Data

The study investigated whether WARPED demonstrations could be co-trained with teleoperated data.
- **Improved or Comparable Performance**: For Rotate Box, Pour Mug, Bottle from Rack, and Can on Plate, co-training with 15 teleoperated and 15 WARPED demonstrations resulted in performance comparable to or better than training with teleoperation alone (e.g., 20/20 for Can on Plate with co-training vs. 19/20 for teleoperation).
- **Inconsistencies for Complex Tasks**: Performance decreased for Wipe Brush under co-training, attributed to the complex motion of the task and dissimilarities between rendered WARPED trajectories and teleoperated executions, leading to data inconsistencies.
- **Behavioral Alignment**: Qualitatively, co-trained policies exhibited behaviors more similar to teleoperated executions, despite a substantial portion of the training data originating from WARPED.

#### 4.7. Comparison with UMI-based Data Collection

WARPED was compared against the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) on Can on Plate and Rotate Box.
- **Can on Plate**: UMI achieved near-perfect success (9/10 train, 10/10 novel), while WARPED showed slightly lower success (8/10 train, 9/10 novel), possibly due to noise in object pose tracking.
- **Rotate Box**: WARPED achieved near-perfect success (10/10 train, 10/10 novel), while UMI performed substantially worse (2/10 train, 0/10 novel). This difference is attributed to the difficulty of demonstrating precise, continuous rotational motion with end-effector tools in UMI, contrasting with the smoother human hand demonstrations and WARPED's augmentation strategy for geometric variation.

#### 4.8. Benefit of Augmented Demonstrations

The impact of data augmentation on WARPED's performance was evaluated.
- **Critical for Performance**: Without data augmentation, WARPED failed completely on three of the five tasks (Rotate Box, Bottle from Rack, Wipe Brush) and experienced a significant performance drop (over 50%) on Can on Plate. This indicates that augmentation is crucial for bridging sim-to-real discrepancies.

#### 4.9. Hand-Object Optimization vs. Object Pose Tracking

A qualitative comparison was made between WARPED's hand-object optimization and object pose tracking using FoundationPose.
- **Improved Reliability**: Hand-object optimization resulted in higher success rates for Rotate Box (17/20) and Can on Plate (17/20) compared to FoundationPose (11/20 and 2/20, respectively).
- **Robustness to Occlusion and Noise**: FoundationPose exhibited poor performance, particularly for small and occluded objects like the can, due to noise in monocular depth estimates, camera intrinsic inaccuracies, and mesh misalignment. Hand-object optimization, by contrast, leveraged interaction constraints to maintain accuracy through occlusions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The WARPED framework represents an advancement in robotic imitation learning by enabling the training of visuomotor policies from human egocentric demonstrations using only a monocular RGB camera. This approach offers several potential impacts:

1. **Increased Accessibility and Reduced Cost**: By eliminating the need for multi-view camera setups, depth sensors, or custom hardware, WARPED significantly lowers the barrier to entry for collecting robot demonstration data. This could make advanced robotic manipulation capabilities more accessible to a wider range of researchers, small businesses, and educational institutions without substantial investment in specialized equipment.
2. **Enhanced Data Collection Efficiency**: The ability to collect human demonstrations 5–8 times faster than traditional teleoperation methods addresses a key scalability challenge in imitation learning. This efficiency can accelerate the development and deployment of robot policies for new tasks and environments, reducing the labor and time costs associated with data acquisition.
3. **Improved Generalization and Robustness**: The synthesis of photorealistic wrist-view observations combined with extensive data augmentation strategies contributes to policies that are more robust to variations in object geometry, size, and potentially scene characteristics. This is particularly valuable for deploying robots in real-world, less-controlled environments.
4. **Leveraging Optimal Robot Viewpoints**: By generating robot-aligned wrist-view observations, WARPED enables policy training directly for the wrist-mounted camera perspective, which is often optimal for fine-grained manipulation tasks due to its close-up and detailed view of the interaction zone. This facilitates the practical application of trained policies on robots equipped with such cameras.
5. **Complementary Data Source**: The demonstrated effectiveness of co-training WARPED-generated data with teleoperated data suggests that WARPED can serve as a valuable supplement to existing data collection pipelines. This hybrid approach could further optimize the balance between data quality and collection efficiency, enabling teleoperation-like performance with less reliance on purely teleoperated data.
6. **Shift towards Automated Data Processing**: The framework shifts the burden from labor-intensive human demonstration collection to automated computational processing, which can be parallelized and scaled more efficiently. This paradigm can support the creation of larger and more diverse datasets for advanced robot learning.

Future developments, such as extending WARPED to articulate and deformable objects, dynamic scenes, and longer-horizon manipulation tasks, could further expand its applicability and impact across a broader spectrum of complex robotic applications.
