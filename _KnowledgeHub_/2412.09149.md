# Student-Informed Teacher Training

> [!summary] Summary
> The Robotics and Perception Group at the University of Zurich developed a "Student-Informed Teacher Training" framework that modifies a reinforcement learning teacher's objective to learn behaviors that are inherently more imitatable by a partially observable student. This approach significantly increases student success rates in tasks like quadrotor obstacle avoidance and robotic manipulation by encouraging perception-aware teacher policies.

## Problem

- Information asymmetry exists between privileged teacher policies (full state access) and partially observable student policies (limited sensor input) in imitation learning, leading to a significant performance gap.
- Teachers, optimized with complete environmental information, often learn behaviors that are difficult or impossible for students with limited sensory inputs (e.g., cameras) to replicate.
- Traditional imitation learning methods primarily focus on making the student more robust, but do not address the root cause of the teacher's potentially un-imitatable behaviors.

## Method

- Augments the teacher's ==reinforcement learning== objective with a penalty term derived from the KL-Divergence between the teacher's and student's action distributions.
- Implements a joint learning framework with three alternating phases: teacher roll-out (applying KL-Divergence penalty in reward), teacher policy update (with a direct KL-Divergence gradient), and student/proxy-student alignment.
- Introduces a "proxy student" network, trained on teacher observations, to efficiently estimate current student capabilities without computationally expensive high-dimensional observation rendering during teacher training.

## Results

- Demonstrated substantially higher student success rates across diverse tasks, including a tabular maze, vision-based quadrotor obstacle avoidance, and robotic manipulation.
- In quadrotor obstacle avoidance, the student success rate increased to **0.46** compared to baselines (ranging from **0.05** to **0.35**), while in manipulation, it reached **0.88** (versus baselines from **0.34** to **0.63**).
- The teacher developed emergent "perception-aware" behaviors, such as orienting a quadrotor camera to keep obstacles in view and a robotic arm avoiding self-occlusion, making policies inherently more imitatable.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively shaping the teacher's policy to be "student-aware" during its training is more effective than solely improving the student's imitation capabilities.
> - Modifying the teacher's objective can lead to the emergence of desirable, student-friendly behaviors, such as perception-aware navigation or self-occlusion avoidance, without explicit reward engineering.
> - Aligning the teacher's policy with the student's capabilities at the source not only improves student performance but can also lead to more robust and efficient teacher policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the crucial intersection of reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), specifically within the context of the teacher-student framework. RL has demonstrated remarkable success in learning complex behaviors for autonomous agents, even surpassing human capabilities in certain domains. However, a significant drawback of RL, particularly when dealing with high-dimensional observations like images, is its inefficiency in terms of required environment interactions and computational expense. This often translates into prohibitive training times and data requirements, especially for real-world robotic applications.

Imitation learning offers a solution by leveraging expert demonstrations to accelerate the learning process, bypassing the extensive exploration phase inherent in pure RL. A popular and effective variant of IL is the teacher-student framework, or privileged imitation learning. In this setup, a "teacher" policy is initially trained using RL, often benefiting from "privileged" information (e.g., precise state, direct distances to obstacles) that is easily accessible in simulation. Subsequently, a "student" policy is trained to imitate the teacher's actions, but critically, the student relies on more limited, realistic observations (e.g., camera images). This approach effectively bridges the gap between efficient simulation-based training and real-world deployment, where only limited sensor data is available.

However, the existing privileged imitation learning paradigm faces a critical challenge: information asymmetry. The teacher, optimized with complete environmental information, may learn behaviors that are inherently difficult or impossible for the student to replicate due to the student's partial observability. The teacher might implicitly "over-rely" on its full information, leading to situations where the student, lacking those critical cues, cannot infer the correct actions. Prior research has primarily focused on making the *student* more robust or better at imitating the teacher, often by augmenting the student's training objective with additional RL components (e.g., Nguyen et al., 2023; Shenfeld et al., 2023) or by employing reward shaping (Walsman et al., 2022). While effective to some extent, these approaches still place the burden of adaptation primarily on the student, attempting to bridge a potentially insurmountable imitation gap.

This paper proposes a paradigm shift by explicitly addressing the teacher-student asymmetry from the teacher's side. Instead of solely improving the student's ability to imitate, this work aims to adapt the *teacher's* policy during its training to be more "student-aware." This means the teacher is encouraged to learn behaviors that are not only optimal for the task but also *inherently more imitatable* given the student's sensory limitations. This novel perspective directly tackles the root cause of the performance gap, making the teacher a more empathetic and effective supervisor. This differentiates it from other works that might attempt to unify teacher and student policies (Fu et al., 2022) or expose teachers to student rollouts (Warrington et al., 2021) by systematically integrating a performance bound for imitation learning into the teacher's objective.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to effectively address the fundamental challenge of information asymmetry between teacher and student policies in privileged imitation learning. Specifically, the authors aim to develop a joint training framework that encourages the teacher to learn behaviors that can be successfully imitated by a student operating with more limited, high-dimensional observations and partial observability.

The motivation for this objective stems from several critical limitations in current imitation learning approaches:
1. **Data Inefficiency of RL for High-Dimensional Inputs:** While powerful, RL algorithms require an enormous number of environment interactions to learn from high-dimensional inputs like images. This makes direct RL often impractical for real-world robotic applications due to simulation costs or real-world exploration dangers.
2. **Accelerating Training with Teacher-Student Framework:** The teacher-student framework offers a compelling solution by generating expert data automatically from a privileged teacher, thereby significantly speeding up the student's training process by providing direct supervision signals.
3. **The Information Asymmetry Problem:** The core issue arises when the teacher, trained with full access to environmental information, develops optimal policies that inadvertently rely on cues unavailable to the student. For instance, a teacher navigating an obstacle course might use precise distance measurements, while a student with only a forward-facing camera might struggle if obstacles are outside its field of view or if the teacher's actions assume global knowledge. This "over-reliance" by the teacher leads to "un-imitatable" behaviors from the student's perspective.
4. **Consequences of Asymmetry:** This fundamental mismatch results in a significant performance gap between the teacher and the student. The student's success rate can be drastically lower, hindering the practical deployment of policies learned through this framework to real robotic systems, where only partial, high-dimensional observations are available.

To overcome this, the authors propose a novel solution: modify the teacher's learning process itself. Instead of merely trying to make the student better at imitation, they aim to make the teacher learn a policy that inherently *considers the student's capabilities*. This involves influencing the teacher to explore and adopt behaviors that are robustly inferable and replicable by the partially observable student, thereby bridging the imitation gap at its source rather than solely at the receiving end.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, "Student-Informed Teacher Training," revolves around extending the teacher's objective function to explicitly account for the student's ability to imitate. This is mathematically motivated by the performance bound in imitation learning, which links the performance gap between teacher and student to the KL-Divergence between their action distributions.

**The Modified Teacher Objective:**
The authors augment the standard RL objective for the teacher, `J(π_T)`, by subtracting an expectation of the KL-Divergence between the teacher's action distribution `π_T(·|s)` and the student's action distribution `π_S(·|s)`. The new objective, `˜J(π_T)`, is formulated as:
`˜J(π_T) = E[r(s, a)] - E[D_KL(π_T(·|s), π_S(·|s))]`
This formulation means the teacher is not only optimizing for the task reward but is also penalized for visiting states where there's a significant action mismatch with the student. Taking the gradient of this modified objective (as shown in Eq. 8) yields two key terms:
1. **Policy Gradient Term:** This is the standard policy gradient applied to a modified reward function, `r(s, a) - D_KL(π_T(·|s), π_S(·|s))`. This term acts as a *penalty* incorporated into the teacher's reward. It encourages the teacher to explore and prioritize states where the student is capable of imitating its actions. This influences the teacher's long-term exploration behavior.
2. **KL-Div Gradient Term:** This is a direct gradient of the KL-Divergence with respect to the teacher's parameters. It functions as a *direct supervision signal* that explicitly pushes the teacher's action distribution to align with the student's at each state.

**Joint Learning Framework:**
To implement this objective practically, the authors adapt the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm within a framework comprising three alternating training phases (depicted in Figure 1):

1. **Roll-out Phase:**
 - The teacher policy (`F_T`) interacts with the environment to collect trajectories.
 - A critical modification occurs in the reward calculation: in addition to the standard task reward (`r_t`), a penalty term `λ_1 * D_KL(π_T(s_t) || ˆπ_S(s_t))` is subtracted. Here, `ˆπ_S` is a "proxy student" network (explained below). This penalty term discourages the teacher from taking actions in states where the student is likely to diverge, influencing its exploration.
 - To avoid computationally expensive high-dimensional student observation rendering at every step, the `ˆπ_S` (proxy student) is used, which takes *teacher observations* as input.
 - A *subset* of collected experiences, including paired teacher and (rendered) student observations, are stored in an "alignment buffer" for later use.

2. **Policy Update Phase:**
 - The teacher network's weights are updated using PPO's clipped policy gradient.
 - The second term from the modified objective's gradient is applied here: `λ_2 * D_KL(π_T(s_t) || ˆπ_S(s_t))`. This directly aligns the teacher's policy with the proxy student's, thereby aligning it with the true student's capabilities.
 - For continuous action spaces, the KL-Divergence simplifies (Eq. 10) to a term related to the mean action difference and covariance. Optimizing this can increase the teacher's action variance in misaligned states, promoting further exploration for student-feasible behaviors.

3. **Alignment Phase:**
 - This phase utilizes the paired teacher and student observations collected in the roll-out phase.
 - **Student (F_S) to Teacher (F_T) Alignment:** The student encoder is trained to align its features with the teacher's encoder features (and activations of the shared action decoder) using an L1 loss. Gradients are *only backpropagated to the student encoder*, effectively training the student to imitate the teacher.
 - **Proxy Student (ˆF_S) to Student (F_S) Alignment:** The proxy student network is aligned with the actual student network using an L1 loss on their encoded features. Gradients are *only backpropagated to the proxy student*. This ensures the proxy student accurately reflects the current student's capabilities without needing full student observation rendering during teacher rollouts.

**Architectural Components:**
- **Teacher Network (F_T):** Encodes privileged observations.
- **Student Network (F_S):** Encodes high-dimensional observations (e.g., images using a frozen DINOv2 encoder).
- **Proxy Student Network (ˆF_S):** Crucially, this network takes *teacher's privileged observations* but is trained to predict *student actions*. This avoids computationally expensive rendering during the teacher's primary learning loops.
- **Shared Action Decoder (A):** All three networks feed their encoded features into this common decoder. It is updated *only by the policy gradient* (task reward). This design choice is critical as it allows the student to leverage the high-level feature correlations learned by the teacher, promoting robust learning even with limited alignment data.

This comprehensive approach systematically guides the teacher to learn behaviors that are not only effective for the task but also inherently more discoverable and imitatable by a partially observable student.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across three diverse environments—a tabular maze, vision-based quadrotor obstacle avoidance, and vision-based robotic manipulation—consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed "Student-Informed Teacher Training" framework. The key finding is that explicitly aligning the teacher's objectives with the student's capabilities leads to substantially higher student success rates and improved overall performance, significantly closing the teacher-student performance gap.

**Color Maze:**
- **Challenge:** The teacher can take a shortcut through a "path" region indistinguishable from "lava" for the partially observable student.
- **Results:** Baselines (Behavior Cloning, DAgger, and even their own method without alignment) show students failing to imitate the teacher, often getting stuck in the "lava" regions (Figure 2e). In stark contrast, their proposed method ("w Align (Ours)") causes the teacher to learn a "sub-optimal" but *imitatable* route around the maze (Figure 2d). The student then successfully imitates this behavior and reaches the goal in all test runs (Figure 2f).
- **Key Insight:** This validates that the KL-Divergence penalty and gradient terms successfully guide the teacher away from behaviors that are difficult for the student, without hindering the discovery of a student-feasible solution.

**Vision-based Obstacle Avoidance with a Quadrotor:**
- **Challenge:** The teacher has access to precise obstacle distances, while the student relies on an RGB camera with a limited field of view. The teacher might not orient the camera optimally for the student's perception.
- **Results:** As shown in Table 1, student success rates for baselines (BC: 0.05, DAgger: 0.08, HLRL: 0.31, DWBC: 0.35, COSIL: 0.30) are very low, despite the teacher achieving over 98% success. This highlights the severe information asymmetry. Their method ("w Align (Ours)") achieves a significantly higher success rate of 0.46, outperforming all baselines.
- **Perception Awareness:** Table A.1.3 provides quantitative evidence: their method significantly reduces the "Velocity Angle" (32.2 deg vs. 60-78 deg for baselines), meaning the quadrotor's camera is better aligned with its velocity direction. It also leads to a higher "Number of Obstacles in View" (3.51 vs. 1.92-2.61).
- **Key Insight:** The teacher learns an emergent "perception-aware" behavior—orienting the camera to keep obstacles in view (Figure 3)—without explicit rewards for camera orientation, making it easier for the student to detect and avoid obstacles. The student's return also closely follows the teacher's (Figure 4), demonstrating effective knowledge transfer.

**Vision-based Manipulation (Drawer Opening):**
- **Challenge:** The teacher has precise relative pose information of the drawer handle, while the student relies on an image. The robot arm can self-occlude the camera's view of the handle.
- **Results:** Table 3 shows their method ("w Align (Ours)") achieving an 0.88 success rate, significantly outperforming all baselines (e.g., DAgger: 0.34, HLRL: 0.61, DWBC: 0.63, COSIL: 0.56, and their own method without alignment: 0.61).
- **Emergent Behaviors:** As visualized in Figure 5, the teacher learns diverse yet student-friendly strategies, such as grabbing the handle from a top-down configuration or lowering the arm to ensure the handle remains visible, preventing self-occlusion.
- **Teacher Performance:** Interestingly, the aligned teacher also achieves consistently higher returns than the non-aligned teacher (Figure 6), suggesting it learns more robust and efficient manipulation strategies that inherently avoid problematic situations for the student.

**Ablation Studies:**
- Ablations on the manipulation task (Table 2) confirm the importance of each proposed component:
 - Removing the KL-Divergence gradient in the policy update ("w/o KL-Grad") drastically drops performance (0.47), highlighting its crucial role in directly aligning the teacher.
 - Removing the penalty term in the reward ("w/o Penalty") also causes a notable drop (0.74), showing its importance in guiding teacher exploration.
 - Removing the shared action decoder ("w/o shared decoder") leads to a substantial performance decrease (0.62), underscoring its role in leveraging teacher features and providing robustness.
 - Sensitivity analysis to `λ_1` (alignment penalty coefficient) shows robustness, with an impressive 0.95 success rate at `λ_1 = 0.025`.

Overall, the findings strongly support the hypothesis that making the teacher "student-informed" during training significantly enhances the student's ability to learn complex behaviors in partially observable environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Student-Informed Teacher Training" framework represents a significant conceptual and practical advancement in the field of imitation learning and robot control. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Fundamental Paradigm Shift:** The work introduces a critical paradigm shift in the teacher-student framework. Instead of primarily focusing on how the student can better imitate a fixed teacher (which might exhibit "un-imitatable" behaviors due to information asymmetry), this research proposes to actively shape the *teacher's* policy. By making the teacher "student-aware," it learns behaviors that are inherently more amenable to imitation by a partially observable student, tackling the problem at its source.

2. **Addressing a Core Limitation in Robotics:** Information asymmetry and partial observability are ubiquitous challenges in real-world robotics. Robots often operate with limited sensor inputs (e.g., a single camera view) compared to the full state information available in simulation. This method directly addresses this critical gap, improving the reliability and performance of policies transferred from simulation to the real world.

3. **Emergent, Desirable Behaviors:** A striking impact of this method is the emergence of intelligent, perception-aware behaviors in the teacher without explicit reward engineering for these specific properties. For instance, the quadrotor learns to orient its camera to ensure obstacles are in view, and the robotic arm learns to avoid self-occlusion. This ability to implicitly derive beneficial, student-friendly behaviors is powerful and reduces the burden of complex reward shaping.

4. **Enhanced Learning Efficiency and Robustness:** By ensuring the teacher provides "imitatable" expert demonstrations, the student's learning process becomes more efficient and robust. The experiments show dramatically improved student success rates across diverse tasks, indicating more effective knowledge transfer. The use of a proxy student also ensures that the method is computationally efficient, avoiding the costly rendering of high-dimensional observations during the teacher's main training loops.

5. **Broad Applicability and Generalizability:** The framework is designed to be agnostic to specific IL algorithms and can be integrated into various existing IL pipelines. Its successful demonstration across tabular, locomotion, and manipulation tasks, utilizing different sensor modalities (state-based and vision-based), showcases its versatility and potential for broad application beyond the presented examples. It could be extended to other modalities, multi-agent systems, or even human-robot interaction where understanding the human's "observability" is crucial.

6. **Unlocking Complex Real-World Tasks:** By reducing the imitation gap for tasks involving large information discrepancies, this framework lowers a significant barrier to deploying advanced AI-driven control policies on real-world robotic platforms. This could accelerate progress in fields such as autonomous navigation, dexterous manipulation, and complex interaction, where robustness to partial observability is paramount.

In conclusion, "Student-Informed Teacher Training" offers a theoretically grounded and empirically validated approach to a long-standing problem in reinforcement and imitation learning. By making the expert teacher a more effective and "empathetic" guide, the method promises to unlock new possibilities for developing highly capable and robust autonomous systems operating in complex, partially observable real-world environments.
