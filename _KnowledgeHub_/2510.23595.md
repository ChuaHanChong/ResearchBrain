# Multi-Agent Evolve: LLM Self-Improve through Co-evolution

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE) enables large language models (LLMs) to improve their reasoning abilities through a co-evolutionary process involving self-generated questions, answers, and evaluations, eliminating the need for human-curated data or external verifiable rewards. The method significantly outperforms existing baselines, with the MAE (half reference) setting achieving an overall average score of 59.87 on diverse benchmarks using a Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model.

## Problem

- Current reinforcement learning methods for LLMs rely heavily on expensive human-curated datasets and domain-specific verifiable rewards, limiting their scalability and generality.
- Self-play paradigms for LLMs often require 'grounded environments' that provide objective feedback, which is difficult to apply in open-ended, general reasoning domains.
- Integrating LLM-as-a-Judge mechanisms into robust, general self-improvement loops for continuous training has faced challenges in stability and implementation complexity.

## Method

- MAE instantiates three interacting roles (==Proposer==, ==Solver==, ==Judge==) from a single, shared LLM backbone, forming a closed self-improving loop for generating training data and reward signals.
- The Proposer generates questions, the Solver answers them, and the Judge evaluates both questions and answers, providing numerical reward signals without human ground truth.
- All three roles are trained simultaneously using ==Task-Relative REINFORCE++==, performing synchronized parameter updates on the uniform shared model to foster co-evolution and stable learning.

## Results

- MAE (zero), starting with only **16** self-generated questions, improved the base model's overall average score from **55.33** to **58.51**, surpassing the strong AZR baseline (**57.72**) without relying on verifiable environments.
- All MAE variants, which do not use ground-truth answers, consistently outperformed Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the same seed dataset, demonstrating the framework's data efficiency and robustness.
- The MAE (half reference) setting achieved the highest overall average score of **59.87**, showing strong performance on both in-distribution (**68.95**) and out-of-distribution (**43.96**) benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A 'beyond-zero-sum' reward design, incentivizing the Proposer to generate challenging yet solvable questions for the Solver while ensuring intrinsic question quality, is crucial for stable and effective co-evolution.
> - Maintaining high-quality training data through a Judge-based question quality filtering mechanism is essential to prevent degradation of the question dataset and ensure sustained learning.
> - A balanced approach that combines exploring novel self-generated questions with referencing an existing, diverse distribution of unlabeled seed questions (MAE (half reference)) is most effective for general reasoning improvement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the rapidly evolving landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) training and self-improvement, specifically at the intersection of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs, Self-Play mechanisms, and Multi-Agent systems.

Historically, RL has shown immense promise in enhancing LLM capabilities, particularly in domains like coding and reasoning. However, its widespread application has been hampered by two significant limitations:
1. **Reliance on human-curated datasets:** Training sophisticated LLMs often requires vast quantities of high-quality, human-annotated data, which is expensive to create, limited in scale, and inherently bottlenecked by human capacity. This issue becomes even more pronounced when aiming for LLMs that surpass human-level intelligence, as the training signals themselves would need to exceed human expertise.
2. **Dependence on verifiable rewards:** Many successful RL applications for LLMs, especially those employing Self-Play, necessitate "grounded environments" (e.g., Python interpreters for code, game engines for games) that can provide clear, verifiable feedback (rewards). This limits their generality, making it challenging to extend these methods to open-ended, general domains like natural language reasoning or abstract knowledge, where objective ground truth and quantifiable rewards are often ambiguous or non-existent.

**Self-Play**, a paradigm proven effective in domains like Go and strategy games, allows models to improve by competing against themselves, generating progressively stronger strategies without human supervision. Recent efforts have extended Self-Play to LLMs, but these too largely depend on domain-specific verifiers. The "Absolute Zero Reasoner" (AZR) is cited as an example, enhancing performance in coding and mathematics through Self-Play but relying on a verifiable environment. Similarly, "SPIRAL" uses Self-Play in zero-sum game settings, which might still be restrictive for truly general tasks.

The emergence of **"LLM-as-a-Judge"** (or LLM-as-a-reward-model) has provided a crucial stepping stone. This paradigm leverages advanced LLMs themselves to evaluate outputs, score, rank, or select, thereby generating self-training signals that reduce the reliance on costly human annotation. However, integrating this into a robust self-improvement loop for general domains remains a challenge.

Existing **Multi-Agent LLM systems** have demonstrated the potential of LLMs interacting as specialized agents. Yet, many prior works deploy these agents without explicit training, limiting their adaptive evolution, or face issues with implementation complexity, cost, and stability. While some explore training multi-agent LLMs (e.g., with voting mechanisms or for safety tasks), a general framework for continuous self-improvement across diverse tasks is largely unexplored.

**MAE addresses these gaps** by proposing a framework that extends the Self-Play paradigm to general, open-ended domains by:
- Eliminating the need for human-annotated data and external verifiable environments.
- Leveraging the LLM itself (instantiated into multiple roles) to generate questions, solve them, and critically, *evaluate both questions and answers*, thereby providing its own domain-agnostic reward signals.
- Integrating sophisticated reward designs and filtering mechanisms to ensure stable and scalable training in this self-referential loop.

Thus, MAE attempts to push the frontier of LLM self-improvement towards true generality and autonomy, reducing the external supervision bottleneck significantly.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central research question driving this work is: **"Can we build an effective RL framework for LLM to self-improve without human annotation in general domains?"**

This overarching question motivates several specific objectives:

- **Overcoming Scalability and Generality Limitations of Current RL for LLMs:** The authors are motivated by the inherent limitations of current RL methods for LLMs, which rely heavily on expensive, human-curated datasets and domain-specific verifiable rewards. They aim to develop a framework that transcends these limitations, allowing LLMs to scale their learning beyond human capacity and generalize across diverse tasks.
- **Extending Self-Play to Open-Ended Domains:** While Self-Play has been highly successful in grounded environments like games, applying it to general reasoning or knowledge domains without clear, objective feedback mechanisms is challenging. A key objective is to design reward signals that enable self-improvement in these ambiguous, open-ended scenarios.
- **Developing a Robust Self-Evolving Multi-Agent Framework:** The paper seeks to create a stable and effective multi-agent system where a single LLM can instantiate different roles (Proposer, Solver, Judge) that cooperatively and competitively co-evolve. The goal is for these interactions to drive continuous enhancement of the base model's problem-solving abilities.
- **Achieving Data-Efficient and Scalable LLM Improvement:** By removing the need for human labels and external verifiers, the authors aim to build a framework that is highly data-efficient (requiring minimal to zero real-world seed data) and scalable, making LLM training more autonomous and less resource-intensive.
- **Enhancing General Reasoning Capabilities:** The ultimate motivation is to improve the general reasoning abilities of LLMs across a wide array of tasks, including mathematics, coding, reasoning, and general knowledge Q&A, purely through internal self-improvement loops.

In essence, the motivation is to unlock a new paradigm for LLM development where models can autonomously generate their own curriculum, evaluate their own performance, and iteratively refine their capabilities across broad intellectual domains, minimizing external human intervention.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE) is a self-evolving reinforcement learning framework that instantiates three interacting roles—**Proposer, Solver, and Judge**—from a *single shared LLM backbone*. This design forms a closed, self-improving loop, allowing the model to generate its own training data and reward signals.

**Core Framework Design (Figure 1 & 2):**
The MAE framework operates in a cycle:
1. **Proposer:** Generates new questions.
2. **Solver:** Attempts to answer these questions.
3. **Judge:** Evaluates both the generated questions and answers, providing reward signals.
All three agents are instantiated from the *same* LLM, meaning improvements in any role feed back into the shared model, enhancing all capabilities.

**Agent Roles and Reward Design:**

- **The Proposer (π_P):**
 - **Objective:** Generate solvable yet challenging and high-quality questions.
 - **Process:** Receives generation instructions (I_g) and optionally a reference question (q_ref) from a valid question pool. It generates a new question (q) with thinking steps.
 - **Reward (R_P):** A weighted sum of three components (λ_quality, λ_difficulty, λ_format all 1/3):
 - **Quality Reward (R_quality):** The Judge evaluates the clarity, solvability, and overall quality of the generated question.
 - **Difficulty Reward (R_difficulty):** This is crucial for co-evolution. It incentivizes the Proposer to generate questions that are *challenging* for the current Solver. It is calculated as `1 - average_solve_score`, where `average_solve_score` is the mean Judge score for N_sample answers generated by the Solver for that question. A lower Solver score means higher difficulty reward for the Proposer.
 - **Format Reward (R_format):** Ensures the Proposer's output adheres to specific formatting (e.g., `<question>...</question>` tags) for reliable parsing.
 - **Quality Filtering:** Only questions with a Judge-assigned quality score above a threshold (0.7) are added to the valid question dataset, preventing degradation of the training data.

- **The Solver (π_S):**
 - **Objective:** Generate accurate and well-reasoned answers to questions.
 - **Process:** Receives general problem-solving instructions (I_S) and a question (q) from the Proposer. It generates an answer (a).
 - **Reward (R_S):** A weighted sum of two components (λ_judge, λ_format both 0.5):
 - **Judge Reward (R_judge):** The Judge directly evaluates the correctness and quality of the Solver's answer (a) for question (q).
 - **Format Reward (R_format):** Ensures the Solver's output is correctly formatted (e.g., `<answer>...</answer>` tags).

- **The Judge (π_J):**
 - **Objective:** Act as a generative reward model, providing objective numerical scores for both questions and answers without ground truth.
 - **Process:** Uses a chain-of-thought process (`<think>...</think>`) before outputting a score to ensure reasoned evaluations. It uses strict rubrics:
 - **Judging Answers (V_J(a,q)):** Evaluates correctness, logical soundness, completeness, and absence of errors (factual, logical, calculation, hallucination). Scores range from 1 (severe errors) to 10 (flawless).
 - **Judging Questions (R_quality(q)):** Assesses intrinsic quality, solvability, logical coherence, clarity, and well-formedness. Scores range from 1 (unsolvable, contradictory) to 10 (perfectly clear, feasible).
 - **Reward (R_J):**
 - **Format Reward (R_format):** Incentivizes the Judge to produce parsable output (e.g., `<score>X</score>` tags), ensuring reliable extraction of numerical scores for the Proposer and Solver.

**Coordination and Training Workflow (Algorithm 1):**
1. **Initialization:** The base LLM is initialized for Proposer, Solver, and Judge roles. A question dataset D (can be zero or small seed data) and a pair dataset P are initialized.
2. **Proposer Phase:** The Proposer generates new questions (q_t), either from scratch or by referencing existing questions in D. The Judge evaluates q_t for quality (R_quality) and difficulty (R_difficulty). If q_t is "Qualified" (R_quality ≥ 0.7), it's added to D. The Proposer receives its composite reward (r_P).
3. **Solver Phase:** The Solver samples a question from D, generates an answer (a_t). The Judge evaluates (q, a_t), providing R_judge for the Solver. The pair (q, a_t) is added to P. The Solver receives its composite reward (r_S).
4. **Judge Phase:** The Judge samples (q, a) from P and generates an evaluation (g), receiving a format reward (r_J) for its output structure.
5. **Synchronized Update:** All three roles (Proposer, Solver, Judge) are trained simultaneously using Task-Relative REINFORCE++. This algorithm computes separate baselines and advantages for each agent's task type before performing a synchronized parameter update on the *uniform shared model*. This ensures co-evolution and stable learning across roles.

**Settings:** The experiments explore four settings: MAE (zero) starting with 16 self-generated questions, and MAE (no reference), MAE (half reference), MAE (with reference) initialized with a seed dataset of ~1K unlabeled questions from diverse benchmarks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted on the Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model, demonstrate the effectiveness and scalability of the MAE framework.

#### 4.1. Evolution without Reference Questions (MAE (zero))

- **Performance:** MAE (zero), starting from a minimal seed of 16 self-generated questions and no real-world data/ground truth, significantly improves over the base model across most benchmarks (Overall Avg. 58.51 vs. 55.33).
- **Comparison with AZR:** MAE (zero) outperforms the strong AZR baseline (58.51 vs. 57.72 Overall Avg.), particularly in complex reasoning domains like BigBench Hard (BBH) (+4.94) and AMC (+9.64), where AZR, despite using Self-Play, still relies on verifiable environments. This highlights MAE's ability to enhance general capabilities with minimal supervision.

#### 4.2. Evolution with Reference Questions

- **SFT Baseline Degradation:** Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the same seed dataset (with ground-truth answers) surprisingly showed performance degradation (Overall Avg. 53.87 vs. Base 55.33). This suggests that for diverse, limited datasets, standard SFT might struggle.
- **MAE Outperforms SFT:** All MAE variants, which *do not* use ground truth answers, significantly outperform the SFT baseline, showcasing the data efficiency and robustness of the self-improvement mechanism.
- **Optimal Strategy: MAE (half reference):**
 - **Balance of Exploration and Exploitation:** This setting, which allows the Proposer to either generate new questions from scratch or reference existing (unlabeled) seed questions, achieved the highest overall average score (59.87). It also led to the best performance on both in-distribution (ID Avg. 68.95) and out-of-distribution (OOD Avg. 43.96) benchmarks.
 - This indicates that a balanced approach, leveraging a diverse initial question distribution while actively exploring novel self-generated challenges, is most effective. MAE (no reference) also performed strongly (Overall Avg. 58.18), emphasizing the efficacy of pure exploration.

#### 4.3. Training Stability and Curve Analysis

- **Enhanced Stability:** Unlike previous LLM interaction methods that often face instability or collapse, MAE demonstrated stable performance improvements over 250 training steps with a batch size of 128. This is a significant improvement over methods like R-Zero, which reported shorter improvement durations. The continuous contribution of high-quality questions to the dataset ensures sustained learning.
- **Desirable Difficulty Effect:** Analysis of training curves revealed that generating feasible yet difficult questions is crucial. As the model gained the ability to create more challenging problems, its performance on benchmarks increased. This aligns with the "Desirable Difficulty Effect" in human learning, where optimally challenging tasks lead to better long-term learning.
- **Agent Diversity:** Maintaining diverse actions and healthy interactions among the Proposer, Solver, and Judge roles prevents the system from collapsing or converging prematurely to suboptimal strategies (e.g., Proposer generating only open-ended writing tasks).

#### 4.4. Ablation Study (Table 2)

- **Necessity of All Agent Roles:** Disabling training for any single role (Solver, Proposer, or Judge) led to a performance drop (2.08%, 1.97%, and 2.63% respectively in Overall Avg. accuracy). This conclusively demonstrates that the comprehensive interactions and co-evolution of all three agents are essential for the framework's success and full potential.
- **Importance of Quality Filtering and Format Reward:**
 - **Question Quality Filtering:** Removing this component caused a "considerable performance drop of 3.72%". Without it, low-quality questions could enter and propagate through the dataset, significantly hampering training. This also supports the "beyond-zero-sum" reward scheme, where the Judge's quality evaluation prevents the Proposer from "hacking" the difficulty reward by generating unsolvable questions.
 - **Format Reward:** While helpful, its removal caused only minor degradation. This is because question quality filtering indirectly covers some of its function; questions with formatting errors are often deemed low-quality by the Judge and filtered out. However, both mechanisms contribute to a more stable and robust training process by ensuring parsable and meaningful interactions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE) framework represents a significant step forward in the pursuit of autonomous and scalable LLM self-improvement, with several profound implications and potential impacts:

- **Breaking the Data and Supervision Bottleneck:** MAE offers a compelling solution to the critical challenges of data scarcity and the need for human supervision in LLM training. By enabling LLMs to generate their own curriculum and reward signals without human-curated ground truth or external verifiers, it dramatically reduces the cost and logistical complexity of developing advanced AI. This could democratize access to high-performance LLM training, allowing smaller teams or researchers with limited resources to develop sophisticated models.

- **Advancing Towards General AI (AGI) in Open-Ended Domains:** The ability of MAE to foster self-improvement in general, open-ended domains (mathematics, reasoning, general knowledge) without domain-specific grounding is a crucial stride towards Artificial General Intelligence. Previous Self-Play successes were often confined to environments with clear rules and outcomes. MAE's approach of using an LLM-as-a-Judge for ambiguous rewards opens new avenues for AI to learn and reason in more human-like, less constrained environments.

- **Enhanced Robustness and Stability in Self-Play:** The framework's demonstrated stability over prolonged training steps, coupled with its effective mechanisms for maintaining dataset quality (quality filtering) and ensuring structured interactions (format rewards), addresses a key weakness of prior LLM self-improvement systems. This robustness is critical for practical deployment and sustained autonomous learning.

- **Inspiration for Curriculum Learning and Adversarial Training:** The "desirable difficulty effect" observed in MAE's training curves provides valuable insights into how AI models can optimally learn. The adversarial co-evolution between the Proposer (seeking challenging questions) and the Solver (improving to answer them) creates a dynamic curriculum that pushes the model's capabilities continually. This paradigm could inspire new forms of curriculum learning where the model itself dynamically adjusts the difficulty and nature of its training tasks.

- **Scalability to Larger Models and Broader Capabilities:** The authors suggest that the framework's stability and ability to generate high-quality data could lead to even greater improvements when applied to larger LLM backbones, which possess more inherent knowledge and reasoning capacity. Future work could expand the number of agents or integrate verifiable environments for a truly unified self-evolving platform capable of mastering all general domains.

- **New Directions in AI Research:** MAE contributes a novel architectural pattern for LLM training. Its success encourages further exploration into multi-agent systems, sophisticated self-rewarding mechanisms, and the delicate balance between cooperation and competition within AI agents. This research could pave the way for entirely new categories of AI systems that are less reliant on external human intervention throughout their lifecycle.

In summary, Multi-Agent Evolve signifies a promising shift towards truly autonomous and scalable LLM development, moving beyond the limitations of human data curation and grounded environments to unlock the potential for general, self-improving artificial intelligence.
