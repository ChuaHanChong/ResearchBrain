# FineART: Fine-Grained Annotated Robotic Trajectory Dataset and Vision-Language-Action Model for Bimanual Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> FineART is a bimanual manipulation dataset of 40,543 teleoperated episodes (1,718 hours) with 533,913 human-written subtask labels covering every episode. It comes with FineART-VLA, a single pi0.5-based policy that predicts its next subtask in language before generating continuous actions, using knowledge insulation. Subtask mid-training lifts spatial-instruction success from 32% to 100%, raises guided long-horizon success from 16% to 76%, and cuts cross-embodiment fine-tuning data tenfold.

## Problem

- Large single-arm datasets such as DROID and Open X-Embodiment are dominated by short pick-and-place episodes, and each trajectory typically carries only one high-level instruction.
- Existing bimanual datasets annotate subtasks for only part of their hours (ABC-130K labels 1,552 of 3,553) and report diversity only in aggregate, hiding contact-rich long-tail coverage.
- Flat VLAs cannot track progress through multi-minute tasks. Hierarchical methods split planning and execution across separate models, and annotation pipelines generate subtasks online rather than as fixed training targets.

## Method

- **FineART dataset**: 40,543 episodes on a four-camera Trossen bimanual ALOHA rig. Independent human annotators segmented each episode into contiguous free-text subtasks, and the data includes a contact-rich long tail of insertion, cable routing, textile and tool tasks.
- **FineART-VLA**: extends LeRobot's pi0.5 (2B Gemma backbone, SigLIP encoder, 300M action expert) to autoregressively predict the next subtask (System 2), then ==FAST tokens== and a 50-step ==flow-matching== action chunk (System 1).
- ==Knowledge insulation== with a mixed objective: the action expert attends to ==stop-gradient== backbone keys and values, so the flow loss never updates the VLM. Batches are split 30% subtask-text loss and 70% FAST plus flow losses.

## Results

- Subtask training lifts success on the left/right/middle-bin spatial task from **32%** to **100%**. With identical human subtask guidance, prepare-breakfast success rises from **16%** to **76%**.
- In the 2x2 ablation, subtask training plus knowledge insulation averages **28.0%** success versus **17.3%** with neither. Removing long-tail data drops success from **24.7%** to **14.0%**.
- On the YAM robot, FineART-VLA reaches **26.4%** success with **25** episodes per task, versus **18.4%** at **250** episodes without mid-training. It rises steadily to **33.6%** at **1,250** episodes and reaches **28%** on unseen prepare-breakfast.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The long-horizon gain comes from dense subtask supervision during training, not from guidance at inference: a policy trained without subtasks still mostly fails when given the identical step-by-step oracle subtasks.
> - Contact-rich long-tail diversity matters beyond category coverage. Dropping it hurt even tasks nominally covered by the retained pick-and-place and sorting data.
> - Subtask-level task structure transfers across embodiments while fine-tuning supplies kinematics. Autonomous long-horizon execution remains brittle, because one misstep with self-proposed subtasks stalls the whole rollout.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot is asked to put a donut into a bin, and the instruction names the bin with a single word: left, right, or middle. The object and task are otherwise the same. The real test is whether the policy learned to act on the actual spatial instruction or only learned a familiar motion. FineART's approach is to teach the policy to say what it should do next and to use that step to guide its movement. In a matched comparison on this task, the subtask-trained policy succeeds in 100% of 25 trials, versus 32% for a policy trained without subtasks. Both policies are evaluated without knowledge insulation. This is evidence for spatial instruction following in that setup. It is not proof that the policy autonomously plans every long task successfully.

Three task conditions: breakfast with human subtask labels, breakfast with autonomous execution, and the donut-to-left/right/middle-bin task. Each compares subtask-trained Run 1.3 with no-subtask Run 1.4. Solid fill shows success rate, and the pale bar behind it shows progress rate. On the donut task, success is 100% versus 32%. Breakfast with human guidance shows 76% versus 16% success. Autonomous breakfast shows zero success for both, despite partial progress.

### 2. A step between goal and motion

A several-minute task may require many coordinated two-handed actions. A single whole-episode command gives a policy little direct supervision for tracking what comes next.

FineART records bimanual demonstrations on one stationary Trossen/ALOHA platform. It labels them at two levels:

- a whole-episode summary
- contiguous, non-overlapping time segments with free-text subtask descriptions

Human annotators made the labels, not a vision-language model. The dataset contains 40,543 teleoperated episodes and 533,913 subtasks across 151 tasks. The subtask labels cover every episode and all 1,718 recorded hours.

Subtask prediction itself is not new. [[2504.16054|π₀.₅: *A Vision-Language-Action Model with Open-World Generalization*]] already describes one model predicting a language subtask and then producing low-level actions conditioned on it. FineART's sharper distinction is the pairing of that kind of policy with a bimanual dataset whose subtask labels persist across every episode and all recorded hours.

FineART-VLA extends the pretrained π₀.₅ model. Given camera images, robot state and the task, the policy can generate the next language subtask. Its action expert then predicts a chunk of continuous robot actions conditioned on that subtask.

Knowledge insulation (KI) controls how training signals reach the backbone. Under KI, the action expert reads the language/vision backbone's representations, but its continuous-action loss does not backpropagate into that backbone. Token-level text and discrete-action losses still train the backbone. Mid-training lasted 300,000 steps, about 10% of an epoch.

Observations and task text feed a SigLIP vision encoder plus a 2B Gemma backbone. The language head predicts a subtask and FAST action tokens. A separate 300M Gemma-style action expert predicts continuous flow-matched action chunks using the subtask and robot state. Under KI, the action expert reads stop-gradient copies of the backbone's keys and values. The figure depicts data paths, not a measured result.

### 3. Does subtask training change behavior?

The donut experiment compares two runs:

- Run 1.3 has subtask training and no KI.
- Run 1.4 has neither.

Each task uses 25 rollouts with prescribed initial conditions. Two metrics are reported. Success rate is the percentage of rollouts completing the task. Progress rate is the average fraction of task stages completed.

On the donut task, success rate is 100% for Run 1.3 and 32% for Run 1.4. All 17 Run 1.4 failures put the donut in the wrong bin.

The breakfast task separates learning to follow steps from planning them autonomously. Its individual subtasks appear in FineART, but its complete sequence does not.

When the policies propose their own subtasks, both have 0% success. Run 1.3 reaches 54.9% progress and Run 1.4 reaches 54.3%.

In the guided condition, a human supplies each next subtask and advances only after the previous one is judged complete. Under this guidance, Run 1.3 reaches 76% success versus 16% for Run 1.4. Training on subtask labels therefore improves the policy's use of guidance. This experiment does not establish that its autonomous subtask predictions reliably complete long-horizon tasks.

### 4. What this establishes

FineART provides bimanual demonstrations with subtask labels across every episode and all recorded hours. In matched tests, training a single policy on those labels helped it act on the spatial word in the donut instruction and make better use of human step-by-step guidance. The main boundary is that without a human advancing the subtasks, both autonomous policies fail to complete the full breakfast task.
