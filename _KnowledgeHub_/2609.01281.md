# EmbodiedSkills: A Unified Framework for Orchestrating, Training, and Deploying VLA Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> EmbodiedSkills is a framework that turns a low-level VLA policy into a closed-loop agent by treating every skill decision as an execution proposal: a guarded runtime checks prerequisites, artifact freshness, and legal transitions before acting, then verifies outcomes afterward. A six-phase AgentLoop coordinates observation, planning, preflight, bounded execution, verification, and recovery. Task-adapted policies reach 86.20% on 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and 97.40% across LIBERO.

## Problem

- End-to-end VLA policies leave intermediate task decisions implicit, so a failure cannot be attributed to grounding, subgoal selection, action execution, verification, or recovery.
- LLM-based robot agents make tool calls explicit, but an explicit decision may still be invalid: incompatible with the phase, built on stale observations, or missing required arguments.
- Prompting can steer skill choice yet cannot enforce execution constraints or verify physical outcomes, which is acute under partial observability, delayed progress feedback, and contact-rich failure.

## Method

- **Executable skill contract**: each skill carries typed input/output schemas, prerequisites, executable operation, state update, and failure mapping, with artifact provenance and freshness driving dependency invalidation.
- **Guarded AgentLoop** over six phases (Observe, Plan, Preflight, Execute, Verify, Recover) with separate skill, advance, and finish guards; verification emits a route among advance, continue, reobserve, replan, recover, finish.
- **Component-level supervision** on deployment-consistent traces trains a ==Qwen3-VL== scheduler and planner with the low-level policy frozen; optional ==group-relative online optimization== uses ==masked clipped objectives== over recorded trajectories.

## Results

- Task-adapted policies average **86.20%** across 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks versus **82.74%** for the reference, improving on **39** tasks, with largest gains on Hanging Mug (**+20**) and Blocks Ranking Size (**+15**).
- LIBERO average reaches **97.40%** against **96.85%** for the official reference, with the biggest gain on LIBERO-Long (**92.4%** to **93.6%**); RMBench memory-dependent tasks average **12.5%** versus **7.3%** best baseline.
- Ablations over **5,000** episodes drop success from **86.20%** to **48.2%** without verification, **34.4%** without subtask conditioning, and **19.5%** with a single 32-step chunk per subtask.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating policy proposals from a runtime that enforces prerequisites and freshness stops invalid decisions from silently becoming physical actions and makes failures traceable.
> - Semantic subtask conditioning matters more than verification for low-level execution quality; a long whole-task instruction forces the policy to infer stage and motion at every chunk.
> - A stable execution interface still cannot catch semantically plausible but wrong subgoals, and per-task specialist policies plus extra VLM calls add cost and latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressively moving towards autonomous systems capable of performing complex, long-horizon tasks in diverse environments. Vision-language-action (VLA) models represent a significant advancement in this direction, directly translating visual observations and natural language instructions into robot actions. Recent VLA policies, such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, and π0.5, have demonstrated increasing versatility across various instructions, multi-object environments, and robot embodiments. Benchmarks like RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO further reflect this expansion in scope by including bimanual manipulation and long-horizon challenges.

However, existing VLA models primarily focus on action prediction. Long-horizon tasks demand more than predicting the next action; an agent must integrate capabilities for perception, planning, execution, progress verification, and recovery as the physical state of the environment evolves. Current VLA policies often treat intermediate task decisions implicitly, making it difficult to diagnose failures (e.g., identifying whether an error stems from object grounding, subgoal selection, or action execution). Conversely, large language model (LLM)-based robotic agents can make explicit tool calls and reasoning steps. Nevertheless, an explicit decision from an LLM does not guarantee its validity or executability within the current physical state, nor does it ensure the intended physical outcome. Challenges such as partial observability, delayed feedback, and contact-rich failures exacerbate these limitations.

This work addresses the gap between advanced VLA policies and the requirements for reliable embodied agents by proposing a framework that explicitly orchestrates these capabilities. It positions itself as a complementary approach to existing VLA models, providing an "agentic layer" that manages when and how low-level VLA policies are invoked, verified, and continued. Unlike purely planning-focused LLM agents, EmbodiedSkills emphasizes runtime enforcement of prerequisites and verification of physical outcomes. It builds upon the advancements in low-level robot control (e.g., diffusion policies, ACT-style action chunking) by exposing them through a stable execution interface, allowing the agentic layer to be developed and adapted independently. The framework also draws on concepts from hierarchical reinforcement learning and behavior tree control, formalizing skill orchestration with a focus on policy-runtime separation. Furthermore, it contributes to the evolving landscape of robot evaluation by providing a unified, diagnosable trajectory structure for analyzing agent behavior and failure modes across benchmarks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for EmbodiedSkills stems from the limitations of current VLA models and LLM-based robotic agents in effectively handling long-horizon, complex manipulation tasks in dynamic physical environments. While VLA models excel at mapping observations and instructions to actions, they typically lack the overarching "agentic layer" necessary to coordinate multiple sub-processes required for successful task completion.

The key challenges and motivations identified are:

- **Beyond Action Prediction:** Long-horizon tasks necessitate a broader set of capabilities than just predicting the next action. An agent must sequentially and adaptively perform perception (identifying task-relevant objects), planning (selecting subgoals), pre-execution assessment (checking executability), low-level action generation, post-execution verification (confirming task progress), and recovery (handling failures).
- **Implicit vs. Explicit Decisions:** End-to-end VLA policies, while providing a unified learning interface, often leave intermediate task decisions implicit. This opacity makes failure diagnosis difficult, as it is unclear whether a failure originates from object grounding, subgoal selection, or low-level execution.
- **Validity and Executability of Model Decisions:** LLM-based robotic agents can make explicit intermediate reasoning steps and tool calls. However, a proposed decision from a model may not be valid or executable in the current physical state. It could be incompatible with the current phase, rely on outdated observations, omit necessary arguments, or simply fail to achieve the intended physical outcome.
- **Robustness in Challenging Conditions:** These problems are particularly acute under partial observability, delayed feedback regarding task progress, and contact-rich interactions, where small errors can lead to cascading failures.
- **Need for a Unified Framework:** There is a need for a unified approach that can bridge the gap between high-level model decision-making and low-level physical execution. Such a framework should enforce execution constraints, verify physical outcomes, and use this evidence to inform subsequent decisions and learning.

To address these motivations, EmbodiedSkills sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Orchestrate Embodied Skills:** To provide a closed-loop framework that explicitly coordinates perception, planning, execution, verification, and recovery, treating each skill decision as an execution proposal.
2. **Ensure Execution Validity and Outcome Verification:** To implement a runtime that rigorously checks the prerequisites of a skill before execution and verifies its outcome afterward, preventing invalid operations and making failures traceable.
3. **Establish a Shared Executable-Skill Interface:** To define a consistent interface that connects high-level skill selection, bounded low-level VLA execution, and post-action verification, allowing components to interact effectively and be replaced or adapted independently.
4. **Support Component-Level Training and Adaption:** To record structured trajectories of planning, execution, verification, and recovery events, providing supervision for individual components and supporting optional online adaptation.
5. **Achieve Strong Performance on Complex Tasks:** To instantiate the framework with state-of-the-art VLA models and evaluate its effectiveness on challenging long-horizon and memory-dependent manipulation benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EmbodiedSkills is structured as a guarded finite-stage controller operating over executable embodied skills. This design separates the policy, which proposes structured operations, from a runtime environment that validates and executes these proposals, records their results, and maintains an updated state.

#### 3.1. Agent State and Skill Decision

At each loop step `t`, the agent's state `s_t` is defined by the current phase (`z_t`), a set of available task artifacts (`M_t`), and an ordered loop trace (`H_t`). Task artifacts can include observations, perception results, the world state, the task plan, active subgoal, execution reports, and recovery context.

The policy receives a compact, deployment-consistent context `C_t`, which summarizes the current task instruction, phase, relevant artifact summaries, recent errors, and a bounded history of decisions and skill results. The policy then selects a structured decision `d_t` from a state-dependent action set `A_t`. Decisions can be `RunSkill(k, q)` (execute a skill), `AdvanceStage` (progress to the next phase), or `FinishRun` (terminate).

#### 3.2. Executable Skill Contract

A central component is the executable skill contract `k`, defined as `(X_k, Y_k, pre_k, exec_k, post_k, fail_k)`.
- `X_k` and `Y_k` specify typed input and output schemas.
- `pre_k` defines prerequisites that must be met before execution.
- `exec_k` is the executable operation itself.
- `post_k` defines how the skill's successful execution updates the agent's state.
- `fail_k` maps potential failures to explicit status and evidence.

This contract ensures that a model-generated result is treated as a proposal until its schema and prerequisites are validated. Successful outputs become typed artifacts with provenance and freshness information, preventing the silent reuse of stale data. Failed operations generate explicit evidence for subsequent policy decisions. The contract acts as a composition interface, a runtime validity boundary, and a structured source for training traces.

#### 3.3. Phase-Structured Skill Space

The runtime organizes the agent's operation into six ordered semantic phases: `Observe`, `Plan`, `Preflight`, `Execute`, `Verify`, and `Recover`. These phases provide structure but do not impose a rigid sequential pipeline. The agent policy can revisit earlier phases (e.g., re-observe or replan) if new evidence invalidates the current plan. Object grounding is task and policy-conditioned, adapting to varied interaction requirements.

#### 3.4. Guarded Runtime Transition

The policy proposes decisions, but the runtime's guarded transition logic controls their execution. Separate guards (`G_skill`, `G_advance`, `G_finish`) are applied to different decision types. These guards check:
- Phase compatibility.
- Availability of required inputs.
- Freshness and validity of artifacts.
- Legality of state transitions.

If a decision is blocked by a guard, it is recorded as such, rather than being executed, ensuring execution safety and state consistency.

#### 3.5. Bounded Action Execution

In the `Execute` phase, a low-level VLA policy takes the active subgoal, current observation, robot state, and preflight evidence to generate a bounded action chunk (`a_t`). An action chunk is a command sequence with a specified type, horizon, and context tie. The runtime further validates this action chunk against criteria such as horizon limits, adherence to action schema, numerical validity, and consistency with the current subgoal and observation (`R_act`). A semantic subgoal may require multiple such bounded chunks, with fresh evidence collected and verified after each chunk's execution.

#### 3.6. Verification and Recovery

The `Verify` phase evaluates the active subgoal using fresh post-action evidence and predicts a semantic route (`Advance`, `Continue`, `Reobserve`, `Replan`, `Recover`, `Finish`). This route dictates whether the agent moves to the next subgoal, attempts the current subgoal again, refreshes observations, revises the plan, or initiates recovery. The `Recover` phase interprets failure evidence to produce a revised execution context, which is then re-checked.

#### 3.7. Modular Interfaces and Training

EmbodiedSkills mandates modular interfaces for all components (perception, planning, action generation, verification, recovery). These components can be independently trained or replaced as long as they adhere to the shared skill and state contracts.

Training primarily relies on **component-level supervised adaptation**. The planner is trained to generate executable subgoals, the low-level VLA policy is adapted with subtask-level demonstrations, and a high-level scheduler (e.g., Qwen3-VL) learns to make phase-appropriate skill decisions from deployment-consistent traces. This approach allows components to be improved independently. **Deployment-consistent training samples** ensure that training data accurately reflects the context available during deployment.

**Optional closed-loop adaptation** is also supported, where interactive trajectories containing model decisions, executed skills, and observed outcomes can be collected. This data can be used for online policy optimization, though the paper emphasizes component-level supervision as the primary training paradigm for the reported results.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of EmbodiedSkills was evaluated through an instantiation using Qwen3-VL-based agent components and an OpenPI/π0.5 low-level VLA policy. Evaluations were conducted on RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO, and memory-dependent RMBench tasks.

#### 4.1. RoboTwin 2.0 Evaluation

- **Performance:** Across 50 diverse RoboTwin 2.0 manipulation tasks, the task-adapted low-level VLA policies achieved an average success rate of **86.20%**.
- **Comparison:** This result represents a **3.46 percentage point improvement** over the 82.74% π0.5 reference reported by LingBot-VA.
- **Task-Specific Gains:** Significant improvements were observed in tasks requiring contact-sensitive and multi-stage operations, such as "Hanging Mug" (+20 points), "Blocks Ranking Size" (+15 points), "Open Microwave" (+15 points), "Move Can Pot" (+10 points), and "Move Stapler Pad" (+10 points).
- **Overall Consistency:** The task-adapted policies improved performance on 39 out of 50 tasks, matched the reference on one task, and showed minor regressions (typically 1-2 percentage points) on ten tasks that already had high baseline success rates.

#### 4.2. LIBERO Evaluation

- **Performance:** On the four LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long), the VLA policy instantiation achieved an average success rate of **97.40%**.
- **Comparison:** This is a **0.55 percentage point improvement** over the 96.85% official OpenPI reference.
- **Suite-Specific Gains:** Improvements were consistent across all suites, with the largest gain observed on LIBERO-Long, where the success rate increased from 92.4% to 93.6% (+1.2 points). This suggests an advantage in handling longer task sequences. The high baseline performance on Spatial, Object, and Goal suites limited the absolute margin for improvement.

#### 4.3. RMBench Memory-Dependent Tasks

- **Performance:** On four memory-dependent M(n) tasks from RMBench, the task-adapted π0.5 execution approach achieved a **12.5% average success rate**.
- **Context:** This evaluation provides an assessment of subtask-conditioned execution in scenarios where the correct action depends on prior interaction history, rather than a direct comparison against a specific baseline developed within the EmbodiedSkills framework.

#### 4.4. AgentLoop Ablations

A controlled ablation study on all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks evaluated the contributions of core EmbodiedSkills mechanisms: semantic subtask conditioning, intermediate verification, and adaptive action chunking.

- **Full Configuration:** The complete plan-execute-verify loop achieved **86.20%** average success.
- **"w/o Verify" (without intermediate verification):** Removing intermediate outcome checks, while retaining the subtask sequence and total action budget, resulted in a significant drop to **48.2%** success (a 38.0-point decrease). This highlights the importance of adapting execution based on observed progress.
- **"w/o Subtask" (without semantic subtask conditioning):** When the low-level policy received only the original whole-task instruction instead of an active subtask, success dropped further to **34.4%** (a 51.8-point decrease from Full). This indicates the critical role of decomposing tasks into manageable semantic subgoals for low-level policy guidance.
- **"1 chunk/subtask" (one fixed 32-step action chunk per subtask):** Retaining the subtask sequence but limiting each subtask to a single fixed-length action chunk without retries or adaptive continuation resulted in **19.5%** success. This demonstrates that semantic decomposition alone is insufficient; many subtasks require flexible, multi-chunk execution to succeed.

These ablation results collectively underscore that the combination of explicit subtasks, post-action verification, and adaptive continuation is crucial for the framework's performance on long-horizon tasks.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Execution Examples

Qualitative examples from RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrated successful executions across various task types, including object reorientation ("Adjust Bottle"), multi-object stacking ("Stack Three Blocks"), and articulated interaction ("Turn Switch"). In these examples, the high-level planner decomposed the task into visually grounded subgoals, and the adapted VLA policy successfully navigated the robot through the required intermediate states to achieve the final, environment-verified goal.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

EmbodiedSkills contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by offering a structured and principled framework for transforming VLA action models into robust, closed-loop embodied agents. Its significance and potential impact lie in several key areas:

- **Enhanced Reliability and Robustness:** By separating policy proposals from runtime-enforced execution, the framework explicitly checks prerequisites and verifies outcomes. This design reduces the likelihood of executing invalid actions, makes failures explicit and traceable, and allows for systematic recovery, thereby enhancing the overall reliability and robustness of VLA agents in complex, long-horizon tasks. This is a critical step towards deploying robots in unstructured, real-world environments.
- **Improved Diagnosability of Failures:** The structured execution traces, which record multimodal context, skill outcomes, runtime errors, and state transitions, provide a comprehensive record for diagnosing failures. This clarity helps identify whether issues originate from high-level planning, low-level execution, or verification, facilitating targeted improvements and debugging.
- **Modular and Adaptable Architecture:** The definition of independently trainable and replaceable interfaces for components like planning, verification, action generation, and environment interaction promotes modularity. This allows researchers and engineers to specialize or upgrade individual components without altering the entire agent loop, fostering faster development cycles and leveraging advancements in specific sub-fields (e.g., a new VLA policy or perception module).
- **Effective Coordination of VLA Policies:** The framework demonstrates how powerful but primarily action-predicting VLA models can be effectively orchestrated within an intelligent agent loop. By providing semantic subgoals, pre-execution checks, and post-action verification, EmbodiedSkills allows VLA policies to operate within a controlled context, maximizing their chances of success on complex tasks.
- **Foundational for Agentic Learning:** The generation of deployment-consistent structured trajectories provides a direct and effective mechanism for supervising the training of the agentic layer's components. Furthermore, the framework supports optional online adaptation with interactive feedback, offering pathways for continuous improvement through real-world experience. This lays a practical foundation for advanced agent-level reinforcement learning and adaptation.
- **Cross-Benchmark Applicability:** The consistent performance gains demonstrated across diverse benchmarks like RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO (including long-horizon tasks) suggests the generalizability of the EmbodiedSkills framework. Its ability to integrate different low-level VLA policies and operate effectively across varied task taxonomies indicates its potential as a broadly applicable standard for VLA agent development.

In summary, EmbodiedSkills represents a methodological advancement in bringing VLA models closer to practical, deployable embodied agents. By prioritizing execution safety, verifiable outcomes, and modularity, it provides a valuable architectural blueprint for designing and training more capable and dependable robotic systems.
