# Measuring Language Transfer in Robot Policies: Adding Greek to a Cosmos3 Vision-Language-Action Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> A study localizing an open Cosmos3 vision-language-action stack into Greek using only machine-rephrased instructions, no architecture change. Swapping to a multilingual text tower plus bilingual demonstration sampling yields Greek instruction-following: 48.6% on a ten-goal suite and a stable 6.9-point margin over its wrong-instruction floor on ninety tasks. The main contribution is the control protocol showing five standard instruments report success where there is none.

## Problem

- Robot foundation models are trained, benchmarked and pretrained almost entirely in English, and no robot demonstration corpus exists for most languages, leaving non-English speakers without any usable interface.
- Standard manipulation suites give each scene one trained goal, so success rates reflect scene affordance rather than language; prior work already shows policies largely ignore instructions.
- Perturbation-based language controls assume how much a paraphrase should matter, and single-run low-resource comparisons plus training loss give no reliable ranking of recipes.

## Method

- An LLM rephrasing pipeline produces Greek imperatives under a mandatory glossary with anti-calque rules, covering 1,273 BridgeData V2 captions and 53,207 DROID plus LIBERO instructions; pipe-separated instruction fields give free 50/50 bilingual sampling.
- A guaranteed-null ==cross-lingual control==: an identically trained policy provably unable to follow the probe language, plus three-way evaluation (correct English, correct Greek, wrong Greek instruction) on a discriminative multi-goal LIBERO suite.
- Recipe search over Cosmos3: ==multilingual 8B tower== swap, ==frozen-tower action-adapter-only training==, ==world-model warm start==, monolingual ablations, and a seven-phrasings-per-task diversity arm, scored with ==paired task-level bootstrap== and ==sign-flip permutation tests==.

## Results

- On a single-goal suite the policy scored **84.6%** under Greek and **82.6%** under deliberately wrong instructions; the multi-goal suite separated them to **48.6%** versus a **4.4%** floor.
- Across ninety tasks and three seeds, bilingual training cleared its wrong-instruction floor by **6.7-7.1 points** while Greek-only training reached at most **2.7**; English-only training left Greek at chance (**9.0%**).
- Switching to an independent translator cost **23.3** Greek points versus **3.9-4.7** in English; seven phrasings per task halved that to **~12**; warm-starting and tower-unfreezing dropped Greek to **13.6%** and **18.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Build the null before the metric: any instrument cheaper than the control that falsifies it, including training loss and colour histograms, will report success where none exists.
> - Low-resource metrics are seed-unstable where high-resource ones are not: validating a pipeline in the high-resource language wrongly convinces you single runs are informative.
> - Multilingual pretraining is necessary but transfers nothing without target-language demonstrations, and a policy trained on machine translations learns the translator's phrasing habits, not the language.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) models combine visual observations, natural-language instructions, and robot actions. Recent systems such as RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, and Cosmos-based policies have demonstrated that language can provide a flexible interface for robot manipulation. However, training and evaluation in this area remain centered on English. Robot demonstration datasets are generally annotated in English, and benchmarks usually issue English commands.

The paper addresses the underdeveloped problem of **cross-lingual transfer in embodied policies**, focusing on Greek. Unlike multilingual text-only systems, a robot policy must connect language to specific objects, spatial relations, and action sequences. A model may recognize Greek text in a general language setting without using it to select the correct physical behavior.

The work also responds to a broader methodological concern in multimodal machine learning: benchmarks can produce high scores even when a model ignores the modality being evaluated. In robot imitation learning, a policy can exploit visual scene cues when each scene has only one associated goal. In such circumstances, the robot may perform the correct behavior without interpreting the instruction. The authors therefore treat evaluation design as a primary research question rather than simply reporting Greek success rates.

The study builds on findings concerning causal confusion in imitation learning, language-insensitive VLA benchmarks, multilingual representation transfer, machine-translation artifacts, and degradation of pretrained representations during fine-tuning. Its contribution is not a new architecture, but an empirical analysis of which conditions allow a multilingual language tower to influence robot actions and which controls are needed to verify that transfer.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has four main objectives.

#### 2.1. Determine whether Greek can be added without collecting new human demonstrations

Because no Greek robot demonstration corpus was available, the authors translate English instructions and captions using language models. The practical question is whether machine-generated target-language annotations can provide a low-cost alternative to collecting Greek demonstrations through additional robot teleoperation.

The study deliberately contains no human-authored Greek. This enables the authors to isolate the effect of machine-produced language, although it also creates a potential confound because the policy may learn the phrasing habits of the translation system rather than Greek more generally.

#### 2.2. Identify the model requirements for language transfer

The authors compare an English-centric text tower with a multilingual tower. They ask whether supervised fine-tuning can teach Greek to a model whose text representation was effectively trained on English, or whether multilingual pretraining is a prerequisite.

They also examine whether language understanding in a video world model transfers automatically to an action policy that uses related model components. In particular, they test whether a Greek-adapted world-model checkpoint is a useful policy initializer.

#### 2.3. Measure whether the policy actually follows Greek instructions

A high task-success rate is not sufficient evidence of language understanding. The authors therefore compare correct Greek commands with deliberately incorrect Greek commands. They use multi-goal scenes in which the instruction is necessary to select among several possible actions. This creates a more informative test than single-goal scenes.

The paper also evaluates English-only and Greek-only training, tests the effect of keeping or unfreezing the text tower, and measures generalization to independently produced Greek phrasings.

#### 2.4. Establish practical evaluation recommendations

The authors document five preliminary conclusions that were later retracted after controls or replication. These include conclusions about English demonstrations scaffolding Greek, monotonic degradation with translation distance, and the benefit of independently generated paraphrases. The goal is to demonstrate how apparently positive conclusions can arise from insufficient controls, small evaluation suites, or random training seeds.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Model and training setup

The study uses the open **Cosmos3** robot-learning stack. The system includes a video world model and an action policy. The relevant language pathway is provided by either:

- an English-centric 2B text tower; or
- an 8B multilingual tower based on Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct.

The policy uses the language tower to condition action adapters, which predict sixteen-step action chunks at 20 frames per second. Training uses supervised fine-tuning with imitation-learning demonstrations. The text tower is frozen in the standard policy experiments, while action-related adapter modules are trained.

The main configuration uses 2,000 iterations, AdamW with a learning rate of \(5 \times 10^{-5}\), weight decay of 0.05, a cosine schedule, and eight B200 GPUs. The reported random seeds are 42, 43, and 44 when multiple runs are available.

#### 3.2. Machine-produced Greek data

An LLM pipeline rephrased English instructions into Greek imperatives using a mandatory glossary and anti-calque rules. The data included:

- 1,273 structured scene captions from a subset of BridgeData V2;
- 53,096 unique DROID instructions associated with 57,639 successful real-robot episodes; and
- 111 LIBERO instructions.

After filtering, 1,222 scene captions were used in the initial world-model experiments. Bilingual policy training appended Greek variants to English instruction fields, and the loader sampled the variants uniformly, producing an approximately 50/50 English-Greek mixture without changing the data loader.

The multilingual tokenizer fragmented Greek substantially more than English. Across ten evaluation commands, Greek required 274 tokens compared with 72 English tokens, an inflation of 3.81 times. The authors consider this a possible contributor to the performance gap, although they do not establish it as the cause.

#### 3.3. Study 1: Localizing the world model

The first study examined whether the video-generation pathway could produce scenes conditioned on Greek captions.

The English-centric model was trained under several conditions: a frozen text tower, reduced-rate tower training, full-rate tower training for three times as many iterations, and a Greek-only continuation. Greek-conditioned outputs remained structureless noise in all configurations.

The multilingual model was trained using the same basic recipe. A larger run used 6,836 clips with a 70/30 Greek-English mixture. Held-out generations were judged for coherence and content match. A language model judge assessed whether clips were coherent and whether they corresponded to the source scene caption.

#### 3.4. Study 2: Testing policy instruction following

Policies were evaluated on LIBERO tasks under three instruction conditions:

1. the correct English instruction;
2. the correct Greek instruction; and
3. a wrong instruction taken from another task.

The initial LIBERO-10 suite contains one trained goal per scene. The more discriminative LIBERO-Goal suite places ten possible goals in the same scene. The authors later used a ninety-task suite containing twenty scenes with two to seven goals per scene.

Each policy was evaluated in closed loop using simulator goal predicates. The main ten-goal evaluation used 50 trials per task, or 500 episodes per condition. The ninety-task evaluation used 20 trials per task, or 1,800 episodes per condition and seed.

Because success rates were highly clustered by task, the authors treated tasks rather than individual episodes as the sampling unit. They used paired task-level bootstrap intervals and exact paired sign-flip permutation tests. They emphasize that three training seeds per arm still yield a minimum attainable two-sided run-level p-value of 0.100, so cross-policy comparisons are reported as directional orderings rather than conventionally significant effects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. A multilingual tower is necessary but insufficient

The English-centric tower did not acquire usable Greek grounding through the tested caption fine-tuning procedures. By contrast, the multilingual tower produced coherent Greek-conditioned scenes after target-language fine-tuning.

However, coherence did not imply accurate grounding. On held-out scenes, English generations were coherent in all evaluated cases and achieved a good content match in 90% of cases. Greek generations were coherent in 64% of cases, but none achieved a good content match; 30% received a partial match. Thus, Greek conditioning transferred the general scene domain more reliably than the specific scene content.

#### 4.2. World-model adaptation harmed policy performance

A policy initialized from the Greek-adapted world model achieved 79.2% English success and 13.6% Greek success on the multi-goal suite. Training the policy from the original base checkpoint instead produced 96.4% English success and 48.6% Greek success.

The authors describe this as negative transfer. One possible explanation is that video-generation fine-tuning damaged action-relevant representations. Another is domain mismatch: the world model was adapted using BridgeData V2 data from a real WidowX robot, while the policy was evaluated on a simulated Franka environment. Since the study did not separate these factors, the causal explanation remains unresolved.

#### 4.3. Single-goal benchmarks substantially overestimate language use

On LIBERO-10, the bilingual policy achieved 84.6% with correct Greek instructions. It also achieved 82.6% with deliberately wrong Greek instructions. The small difference indicates that the policy could largely exploit scene affordances rather than interpret language.

The multi-goal suite provided a stronger test. With ten goals available in each scene, the from-base bilingual policy reached 48.6% Greek success and 96.4% English success, while wrong Greek instructions produced 4.4% success. This separation indicates genuine, although incomplete, Greek instruction use.

#### 4.4. Greek following was partial and non-compositional

On the ten-goal suite, Greek performance was highly uneven across goals. In one reference seed, five goals achieved between 72% and 98% success, while five achieved between 0% and 24%. The exact split changed across seeds, so it was not a stable property of particular commands.

The policy had difficulty distinguishing commands that shared a destination but named different objects. For example, “put the bowl on top of the cabinet” reached 94% Greek success, whereas “put the wine bottle on top of the cabinet” reached 0% in the reference run. Failure analysis showed that Greek failures usually resulted in successful completion of another task, rather than motor failure. In 82% of sampled Greek failures, the robot completed a different available goal, similar to the wrong-instruction condition. This suggests a language-comprehension problem combined with a tendency toward an over-practiced behavior.

#### 4.5. Bilingual training was more reliable on the larger suite

On LIBERO-90, the bilingual policy achieved 27.4% correct-Greek success against a 20.5% wrong-instruction floor. Its margin over the floor was 6.7–7.1 percentage points across three seeds.

The Greek-only policy achieved margins of −0.3, 0.7, and 2.7 points across its three seeds. Therefore, Greek-only training showed weak and inconsistent instruction use, whereas bilingual training produced a stable positive margin. The authors interpret this as evidence that target-language demonstrations are necessary but not sufficient, and that retaining English demonstrations may help provide a behavioral scaffold. They do not claim a fully resolved causal explanation because the number of training runs remains small.

#### 4.6. Machine-translation phrasing created a substantial generalization penalty

When evaluated on independently generated Greek commands, single-phrasing policies lost an average of 23.3 percentage points relative to their training phrasings. Comparable English rewordings caused average drops of 3.9 points for syntax changes and 4.7 points when object or surface nouns were also replaced.

Training on seven Greek phrasings per task reduced the within-policy independent-translator penalty to approximately 12 points, although it also reduced English performance by 3.6 points. The authors initially claimed that performance declined monotonically with increasing distance from the training translator and that their own paraphrases understated the independent-translator gap. Both claims were withdrawn after three-seed replication. The robust conclusion is narrower: unseen Greek phrasing reduces performance, and phrasing diversity reduces that penalty.

#### 4.7. Several intuitive interventions failed

Unfreezing the multilingual text tower reduced performance to 82.4% English and 18.0% Greek in the reported configuration. English-only training left Greek at approximately its wrong-instruction floor. Training loss was also uninformative: six policies had losses within a 1.4% range while Greek success varied from 9.2% to 66.6%.

The large DROID corpus could not be evaluated with closed-loop success because the authors did not have the required physical robot. An action-prediction proxy separated English correct from wrong instructions by only 2.28% but found Greek indistinguishable from the null at the tested training stage.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It shows that evaluating multilingual robot policies requires controls that distinguish language use from scene-driven behavior. Wrong-instruction tests, multi-goal scenes, independent phrasings, larger task suites, and multiple training seeds are all necessary to interpret results.

For practical localization, the study suggests checking multilingual support in the base text tower before training, adding target-language demonstrations while retaining English data, freezing the pretrained tower, and using several target-language phrasings per task. These recommendations are inexpensive relative to collecting new robot demonstrations.

The results also indicate that machine translation can produce measurable but limited transfer. On the larger suite, Greek instruction following remained substantially below English and was only a modest margin above the policy’s instruction-blind floor. The absence of human-authored Greek, reliance on one model family, simulation-based closed-loop evaluation, limited seed counts, and unresolved tokenizer-versus-representation effects restrict generalization. Native-speaker evaluation, additional architectures, in-domain world-model adaptation, real-robot testing, and broader task generalization are needed.

Overall, the paper argues that the central challenge in multilingual robot-policy research is not merely adding translated text, but demonstrating that the resulting policy uses that text to select and execute the intended action.
