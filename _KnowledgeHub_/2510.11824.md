# Empirical Study on Robustness and Resilience in Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Beihang University and collaborators empirically investigated cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) systems, formally distinguishing and evaluating their robustness and resilience under real-world uncertainties. Their extensive study across diverse environments revealed that hyperparameter optimization alone improved cooperation by 52.60%, robustness by 34.78%, and resilience by 60.34% on average, while demonstrating that trustworthiness does not generalize across different uncertainty types or agent scopes.

## Problem

- Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) systems, despite successes in simulations, struggle with reliable deployment in real-world environments due to unaddressed uncertainties like observation errors and action perturbations.
- The concepts of 'robustness' (withstanding uncertainties) and 'resilience' (recovering from disruptions) are often conflated in MARL literature, limiting a precise understanding of how systems perform under stress.
- The specific impact of hyperparameter choices, typically optimized for cooperation in ideal settings, on the robustness and resilience of MARL policies in uncertain real-world conditions remains largely unexplored.

## Method

- The study conducted a large-scale ==empirical investigation==, involving over 82,620 experiments across 18 real-world tasks and three MARL algorithms (MADDPG, MAPPO, HAPPO).
- It formally defined and quantitatively evaluated robustness as the ability to maintain performance under continuous uncertainties, and resilience as the capacity to recover from perturbed states.
- The research systematically applied thirteen diverse uncertainty types (observation, action, environment) and analyzed the impact of fifteen different hyperparameters on cooperation, robustness, and resilience.

## Results

- Hyperparameter optimization alone yielded average improvements of **52.60%** in cooperation, **34.78%** in robustness, and **60.34%** in resilience.
- Combining optimized hyperparameters with a robust MARL method (ERNIE) further improved cooperation by **89.43%**, robustness by **65.83%**, and resilience by **82.96%**.
- MADDPG exhibited greater resilience to action uncertainties due to its persistent exploratory noise, while MAPPO and HAPPO showed superior performance under observation uncertainties.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The relationship between cooperation and robustness/resilience significantly weakens with increased uncertainty severity, indicating that simply optimizing for cooperation is insufficient for severe real-world disruptions.
> - Robustness and resilience do not generalize across different uncertainty modalities (e.g., observation vs. action) or agent scopes (e.g., single agent vs. all agents), necessitating specific strategies for varied threats.
> - Hyperparameter choices often have a stronger influence on trustworthy MARL performance than algorithmic choice, with specific settings like higher critic learning rates and Leaky ReLU consistently improving metrics, while practices like GAE, PopArt, and parameter sharing can be detrimental in certain environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has achieved notable successes in various complex simulated environments, such as StarCraft, turbulent flow modeling, and network control. However, the direct deployment of MARL systems into real-world scenarios faces challenges due to uncertainties that are often not present or accounted for in idealized training environments. These uncertainties include observation errors, action perturbations, and environmental unpredictability, which can significantly degrade the performance of MARL policies.

Existing MARL research has explored robustness against uncertainties, primarily through the lens of robust RL and MARL, categorizing uncertainties into state, action, and environment types. While this body of work focuses on ensuring stability under worst-case adversaries within a defined uncertainty set, it often conflates the concepts of "robustness" and "resilience." In fields like control theory, ecology, and economics, these concepts are recognized as distinct but complementary: robustness refers to the ability to withstand uncertainties, while resilience refers to the capacity to recover from disruptions. This distinction has been largely overlooked or interchangeably used in the MARL literature.

Furthermore, the role of hyperparameters in MARL system performance, particularly concerning robustness and resilience, has been underexplored. While single-agent RL research has shown that hyperparameters can account for significant empirical differences between algorithms, and MARL benchmarks like MAPPO have highlighted the importance of hyperparameter optimization for cooperative performance, their specific impact on maintaining stability and recovery under real-world uncertainties remains largely unquantified. Policies tuned for optimal cooperation in ideal settings may not translate to trustworthy performance in uncertain environments.

This study addresses these gaps by conducting a large-scale empirical investigation to systematically evaluate the relationship between cooperation, robustness, and resilience in MARL. It formally distinguishes robustness and resilience, assesses the generalizability of robustness across different uncertainty modalities and agent scopes, and quantifies the critical role of hyperparameters in developing trustworthy MARL systems. By doing so, the work aims to provide empirical insights and practical recommendations for building MARL systems capable of operating reliably in unpredictable real-world conditions, moving beyond theoretical discussions and limited-scale experiments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were to conduct a comprehensive empirical study to understand and evaluate cooperation, robustness, and resilience in cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) systems. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations:

1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap and Trustworthy MARL:** While MARL has demonstrated success in simulations, deploying these systems in real-world environments introduces diverse uncertainties (observation errors, action perturbations, environmental unpredictability) not typically present during training. Policies optimized solely for cooperation in ideal settings often fail under these real-world conditions. The motivation is to build trustworthy MARL systems that can maintain stable functionality and recover from disruptions, which is crucial for practical deployment.

2. **Distinguishing Robustness and Resilience in MARL:** The concepts of robustness and resilience, while complementary, have been extensively studied and differentiated in fields like control theory, ecology, and economics. Robustness refers to the ability to withstand uncertainties and maintain stability, while resilience describes the capacity to recover from disruptions. In MARL literature, these terms are frequently conflated or used interchangeably, leading to an incomplete understanding of how MARL systems perform under various forms of stress. A key objective was to formally define and empirically differentiate these concepts within the MARL context to facilitate more precise evaluation and algorithmic development. The paper provides a motivating example using a metropolitan power grid to illustrate this distinction under varying uncertainty severities.

3. **Quantifying the Impact of Hyperparameters on Trustworthy MARL:** Hyperparameters are acknowledged as critical for MARL performance, with careful tuning often yielding more significant improvements than algorithmic innovations alone, as demonstrated by previous work in single-agent RL and cooperative MARL (e.g., MAPPO). However, the effect of hyperparameter tuning, typically performed to maximize cooperative rewards in ideal environments, on policy robustness and resilience under uncertainty remains largely unexplored. The research aims to investigate whether hyperparameter choices, which improve cooperation, similarly enhance robustness and resilience, or if they introduce vulnerabilities, as suggested by initial observations presented in the paper.

To achieve these objectives, the study undertook a large-scale empirical evaluation involving over 82,620 experiments across diverse environments, uncertainty types, and hyperparameter configurations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a large-scale empirical study to systematically evaluate cooperation, robustness, and resilience in MARL, utilizing a standardized experimental framework.

#### 3.1. Formal Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) defined by a tuple: $G = \langle N, S, O, O, A, P, R, \gamma \rangle$. This involves N agents, a global state space S, a joint observation space $O$, an observation emission function $O$, a joint action space $A$, state transition probability P, a shared reward function r, and a discount factor $\gamma$. Each agent $i$ has a policy $\pi_i(a_i|\tau_i)$ that maps its observation history $\tau_i$ to an action $a_i$. The objective of the joint policy $\pi$ is to maximize the expected discounted cumulative reward $J(\pi)$.

#### 3.2. Definitions of Robustness and Resilience

The study formally defines:
- **Robustness** ($J_{robust}(\pi)$): Measures a policy's ability to maintain performance under continuous uncertainties. It is defined as the expected discounted cumulative reward over episodes where uncertainties $u \in U$ are active throughout the episode.
- **Resilience** ($J_{resilience}(\pi)$): Measures a policy's ability to recover from disruptions. It is defined as the expected discounted cumulative reward starting from a perturbed state $s_u$ (at timestep $t_u$) that was induced by an uncertainty $u$, with no further uncertainties applied after $t_u$.

#### 3.3. Algorithms Evaluated

The study focused on three widely used MARL algorithms that support continuous control, suitable for the chosen real-world environments:
- **MADDPG**: An off-policy actor-critic method, known for its exploration noise.
- **MAPPO**: An on-policy PPO-based method with a centralized critic and parameter sharing for homogeneous agents.
- **HAPPO**: A variant of MAPPO designed for heterogeneous agents, representing methods that account for agent-specific roles.

#### 3.4. Real-World Environments

Four diverse real-world environments were used, totaling 18 tasks, to ensure broad applicability and capture varied challenges:
- **Dexterous Hand Manipulation (Dexhand)**: Six tasks involving precise bimanual robotic control (e.g., PushBlock, SwingCup, BlockStack), enabling transfer to physical robots.
- **Quadrotor Swarm Control (Quad)**: Six tasks focusing on multi-robot navigation and formation control (e.g., static_diff_goal, o_random, swarm_vs_swarm), also transferable to physical robots.
- **Intelligent Traffic Control (Traffic)**: Three tasks based on real-world traffic data, involving adaptive control (e.g., ATSC Grid, CACC Catch-up, CACC Slow-down).
- **Active Voltage Control (Voltage)**: Three tasks derived from real-world power grid data, requiring adaptive control with noisy dynamics (e.g., 33-Bus System, 141-Bus System, 322-Bus System).

#### 3.5. Types of Uncertainties

Thirteen distinct uncertainty types were applied across observation, action, and environment modalities:
- **Observation Uncertainties (6 types)**: Gaussian noise, greedy worst-case attacks (Maximal Action Difference), and learned optimal attacks (ATLA/PA-AD). Each was applied either to all agents (small perturbation budget $\epsilon=0.1$) or a single agent (large budget $\epsilon=0.2$).
- **Action Uncertainties (6 types)**: Random policies, greedy worst-case policies (minimizing Q-value), and learned optimal policies. Each was applied to all agents (small probability $\epsilon=0.1$) or a single agent (large probability $\epsilon=0.2$).
- **Environmental Uncertainties (1 type)**: Sampled from 50 rollouts over environment-specific hyperparameters (e.g., collision radius, obstacle density in Quads; PV scale, demand scale in Voltage) to represent worst-case scenarios.

#### 3.6. Hyperparameters Evaluated

Fifteen hyperparameters were selected, including both general and algorithm-specific choices, with default values established for initial training (see Table 1 in the paper). These include:
- **General**: Network Hidden Size, Discount Factor ($\gamma$), Activation Function, Initialization Method, Neural Network Type (MLP/RNN), Actor Learning Rate, Critic Learning Rate, Feature Normalization, Share Parameters, Early Stop.
- **MADDPG-specific**: TD Steps (N-Step), Exploratory Noise.
- **MAPPO/HAPPO-specific**: Entropy Coefficient, Use GAE, Use PopArt.
For each hyperparameter, multiple discrete choices were evaluated.

#### 3.7. Experimental Procedure

The study involved a total of 82,620 experiments (5 random seeds $\times$ 27 evaluation settings $\times$ 18 tasks $\times$ 34 hyperparameter variations).
1. **Baseline Training**: All algorithms were initially trained on all tasks using default hyperparameters.
2. **Hyperparameter Variation**: For each of the 15 hyperparameters, one setting was varied at a time from its default to create different cooperative models.
3. **Robustness Evaluation**: The performance of these trained cooperative models was measured under each of the 13 uncertainty types.
4. **Resilience Evaluation**: For each uncertainty type, a new episode was initialized from the perturbed state encountered by the cooperative models, and their performance from this recovery point was assessed.
5. **Compute Resources**: The entire study utilized approximately 230,000 GPU hours (measured in GTX 4090 equivalent).
6. **Codebase**: A modular codebase was developed to allow easy integration of custom algorithms and environments, facilitating reproducibility.

#### 3.8. Data Analysis

To account for varied reward scales, different normalization methods were applied:
- **Z-Score Normalization**: For aggregating rewards across tasks (e.g., correlation analysis).
- **Performance Normalization**: To quantify performance variance attributed to hyperparameters.
- **Relative Performance Change**: To assess the impact of individual hyperparameter choices relative to the default.
Statistical significance was assessed using Pearson correlation, ANOVA, and paired t-tests to identify consistent trends.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The large-scale empirical study yielded several key findings regarding the relationships between cooperation, robustness, resilience, and the impact of hyperparameters in MARL:

#### 4.1. Relationship between Cooperation, Robustness, and Resilience

1. **Dependence on Uncertainty Severity**: While optimizing cooperation can enhance robustness and resilience under *mild* uncertainties, this relationship weakens as perturbations intensify. A linear trend was observed where the Pearson correlation between cooperation and robustness (r = 0.85, p < .001) and cooperation and resilience (r = 0.76, p < .001) significantly decreases with increased attack severity. This suggests that simple cooperative optimization is insufficient for severe real-world uncertainties, where dedicated robustness and resilience strategies are required.
2. **Linearity Across Uncertainty Types and Strategies**: The correlation between cooperation, robustness, and resilience generally holds under random, greedy, and optimal attacks, and for both observation uncertainty and perturbations affecting single or all agents (r $\geq$ 0.6, p < .05). Environment uncertainty also shows strong correlations (r $\geq$ 0.85) but with less statistical significance.
3. **Algorithm Sensitivity to Uncertainty Types**: Overall robustness and resilience are similar across algorithms. However, MADDPG demonstrates greater resilience to action uncertainties, attributed to its persistent exploratory noise during training. MAPPO and HAPPO, utilizing a centralized critic, exhibit superior performance under observation uncertainties.

#### 4.2. Uncertainty Diversity and Generalization

1. **Uncorrelated Uncertainty Modalities**: Robustness and resilience do not generalize across different uncertainty modalities. Observation, environment, and action uncertainties exhibit significantly low correlations with each other. A policy robust to action noise, for instance, may remain vulnerable to observation or environmental shifts. This was confirmed by one-way ANOVA for robustness (F(2, 153) = 9.53, p < .001) and resilience (F(2, 153) = 14.51, p < .001).
2. **Uncorrelated Agent Scopes**: Robustness and resilience do not generalize between uncertainties applied to individual agents versus those applied to all agents. Policies trained to defend against group-level perturbations may fail under attacks targeting a single agent. This finding was supported by one-way ANOVA for robustness (F(1, 142) = 4.36, p < .05) and resilience (F(1, 142) = 7.00, p < .05).

#### 4.3. Impact of Hyperparameters

Hyperparameter tuning was found to play a critical role, with two-way ANOVA showing a stronger effect than algorithm choice in 9 out of 18 tasks (p < 0.001).
1. **Early Stopping**: Generally effective, improving all metrics. Saving models based on combined performance (cooperation, robustness, resilience) consistently outperforms saving the final training step model (t(161) = 6.16, p < .001).
2. **Higher Critic Learning Rate**: Using a critic learning rate higher than the actor learning rate consistently improves cooperation, robustness, and resilience (t(161) = 10.02, p < .001). This aligns with two-timescale actor-critic theory.
3. **Leaky ReLU Activation**: Consistently outperforms ReLU in all three metrics (t(161) = 6.31, p < .001), likely by preventing dead neurons and maintaining stable gradients under distribution shifts.
4. **GAE and PopArt**: Counter-intuitively, these common practices *reduce* cooperation, robustness, and resilience in the evaluated environments (GAE: t(107) = 7.44, p < .001; PopArt: t(107) = 4.84, p < .001). This effect is hypothesized to be task-dependent, with GAE performing poorly in environments with sparse and variable rewards, and PopArt's benefits being unnecessary with stable reward scales.
5. **Parameter Sharing**: This technique, often credited for MAPPO's success, generally *reduces* overall performance in the benchmark (t(161) = 7.01, p < .001). Its benefits appear limited to specific homogeneous agent tasks (like in SMAC, per additional experiments) and can hinder learning agent-specific features in heterogeneous environments, increasing vulnerability to attacks.
6. **Exploration**: Increased exploration (higher entropy coefficient) benefits MAPPO and HAPPO (t(107) = 6.64, p < .001), enhancing robustness and resilience, consistent with maximum entropy RL theories. For MADDPG, the impact is task-dependent due to its additive action noise.
7. **Task-Specific Tuning**: Learning rates, hidden state sizes, discount factors, RNN usage, and network initialization methods do not show universal preferences and should be tuned based on the specific task.

#### 4.4. Improving Robustness and Resilience Through Hyperparameter Optimization

Optimizing hyperparameters alone led to substantial improvements: an average of 52.60% in cooperation, 34.78% in robustness, and 60.34% in resilience. This effect also generalized to robust MARL methods, with ERNIE (a general-purpose robust MARL method) showing average improvements of 89.43% on cooperation, 65.83% on robustness, and 82.96% on resilience when combined with optimized hyperparameters. Post-hoc analysis highlighted that parameter sharing benefits homogeneous agents (p < .05), smaller discount factors are advantageous for short episodes (p < .001), higher critic learning rates are beneficial for policy gradient methods (p < .05), and early stopping is consistently helpful (p < .001).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This empirical study contributes to the field of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning by addressing fundamental challenges related to the deployment of MARL systems in real-world environments characterized by uncertainty. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Understanding of Trustworthy MARL**: The work provides a rigorous empirical foundation for understanding "trustworthiness" in MARL by formally distinguishing and evaluating robustness and resilience. This clarification moves beyond the common conflation of these terms in MARL literature, offering a more nuanced framework for analyzing system performance under various types of disturbances. This is crucial for designing systems that can not only withstand initial shocks but also recover effectively from sustained or severe disruptions.

2. **Practical Guidelines for MARL Practitioners**: The study offers concrete, empirically derived recommendations for MARL practitioners. The findings on the effectiveness (or ineffectiveness) of specific hyperparameters directly inform implementation choices. For instance, the recommendation to use early stopping, a higher critic learning rate, and Leaky ReLU, while re-evaluating the utility of GAE, PopArt, and parameter sharing in certain contexts, can guide developers in building more robust and resilient MARL systems. The observation that hyperparameter tuning alone can yield substantial improvements across all three metrics (cooperation, robustness, resilience) underscores the immediate practical value of these insights.

3. **Challenging Established Assumptions**: Several findings, particularly regarding the detrimental effects of parameter sharing, GAE, and PopArt in certain environments, challenge widely held assumptions in the MARL community. These counter-intuitive results highlight the task-dependent nature of MARL performance and suggest that practices beneficial in one set of benchmark environments (e.g., SMAC, Multi-Agent MuJoCo) may not universally translate to real-world or more complex simulation settings. This encourages a more critical and context-aware approach to algorithm design and hyperparameter selection.

4. **Guiding Future Research Directions**: By demonstrating that robustness does not generalize across uncertainty modalities or agent scopes, the study emphasizes the need for future MARL research to develop methods that are specifically designed for diverse uncertainty types and agent-level effects. This necessitates multi-faceted evaluation strategies for trustworthy MARL, moving beyond single-point assessments. The identified sensitivities of different algorithms to specific uncertainty types (e.g., MADDPG for action noise, MAPPO/HAPPO for observation noise) could also guide the development of hybrid or adaptive MARL architectures.

5. **Benchmarking and Reproducibility**: The provision of a large-scale empirical study (over 82,620 experiments) across real-world environments and the open-sourcing of the modular codebase contribute significantly to the reproducibility and standardization of research in trustworthy MARL. This benchmark allows other researchers to integrate custom algorithms and environments, validating and expanding upon these findings.

In conclusion, this research provides a comprehensive empirical analysis that redefines and quantifies critical aspects of trustworthy MARL. By offering actionable insights and challenging existing norms, it lays a foundation for the development and deployment of more reliable, stable, and recoverable multi-agent intelligent systems in complex and uncertain real-world applications.
