# Vision-Based Multi-Task Manipulation for Inexpensive Robots Using End-To-End Learning from Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> This research presents an end-to-end learning from demonstration framework for inexpensive robots, enabling multi-task vision-based manipulation. It integrates a VAE-GAN for visual feature regularization and an autoregressive multimodal action predictor, achieving success rates between 76% and 88% on five diverse manipulation tasks using only monocular RGB input.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic control for complex, open-world manipulation often relies on difficult-to-engineer hand-crafted controllers, which are not suitable for uncontrolled environments.
- Enabling inexpensive robots to perform diverse, vision-based manipulation tasks is challenging due to hardware limitations (e.g., lack of proprioception) and the complexity of real-world objects and scenarios.
- Existing Learning from Demonstration (LfD) methods frequently require extensive demonstrations and struggle with generalization to unseen situations or multimodal human solutions.

## Method

- A deep ==recurrent neural network== controller was developed to map raw monocular RGB images directly to robot joint commands, enabling end-to-end vision-based control.
- A **VAE-GAN** (Variational Autoencoder Generative Adversarial Network) was integrated as an auxiliary loss for image reconstruction, regularizing the visual feature extractor to learn more robust features.
- An ==autoregressive multimodal action prediction== strategy using a ==Mixture Density Network (MDN)== was employed to model diverse valid human solutions and ensure temporal coherence in predicted joint commands.

## Results

- The full proposed architecture achieved success rates ranging from **76%** to **88%** across five distinct and complex manipulation tasks on a low-cost Lynxmotion AL5D robot.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that both the VAE-GAN for regularization and the autoregressive multimodal action prediction were critical components, with their absence significantly reducing success rates (e.g., as low as **8%** for a single-task, no-autoregressive baseline).
- Qualitative observations showed that the full controller generated smooth trajectories and exhibited an ability to correct its own mistakes, such as re-positioning to adjust an incorrectly pushed box.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining multi-task learning with a shared deep neural network significantly improves performance and generalization across diverse manipulation skills, even on low-cost hardware.
> - Auxiliary tasks, such as VAE-GAN-based image reconstruction, effectively regularize visual feature learning, enhancing robustness for fine perception and challenging object properties.
> - Utilizing an autoregressive multimodal action predictor is crucial for handling the inherent ambiguity and multiple valid solutions present in human demonstrations, leading to more robust and coherent robot behaviors than unimodal prediction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of robotic learning, specifically addressing the challenge of enabling autonomous manipulation in open-world environments. Traditional approaches often rely on hand-engineered controllers, which are difficult to develop for complex, uncontrolled settings like a user's home. This research contributes to the growing body of work that utilizes machine learning to overcome these challenges.

Two primary paradigms for learning robot behaviors are Learning from Demonstration (LfD) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). LfD, which this paper employs, involves a human user demonstrating desired tasks to the robot, thereby teaching it a policy based on observed actions. This contrasts with RL, where a robot learns by trial and error through interaction with its environment, guided by a reward signal. While RL can discover optimal policies, it often requires extensive interaction and careful reward engineering, particularly for complex manipulation tasks. LfD, conversely, leverages human expertise to provide efficient initial learning, requiring fewer interactions than learning from scratch. The authors acknowledge the existing literature on LfD applications, including autonomous helicopter maneuvers, object manipulation, and various household tasks, while noting the persistent challenge of generalizing learned behaviors to unseen situations.

A significant recent advancement in robotic control is the development of end-to-end controllers, which directly map raw sensory inputs (such as camera images) to robot control signals using deep neural networks. This approach, heavily influenced by the success of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in computer vision, allows for the integration of perception and control into a single system. This paper aligns with this trend, utilizing a deep recurrent neural network to process visual input and generate joint commands.

Furthermore, this research contributes to the area of multi-task learning in robotics. Earlier work has explored training neural networks to perform multiple related tasks simultaneously, often by sharing network parameters or using task-specific modules. The concept that shared parameters and training on multiple related tasks can improve performance across all tasks has been previously demonstrated. This paper specifically investigates the benefits of training a single, shared-parameter network on diverse manipulation tasks.

A distinguishing feature of this work within the landscape is its focus on low-cost, imprecise hardware and readily available sensory inputs (monocular RGB images). This aims to increase the social impact and accessibility of robotic solutions for Activities of Daily Living (ADLs), which are everyday tasks crucial for assistive robotics. The research aims to advance the capabilities of such inexpensive platforms, which typically lack proprioception, relying solely on vision for environmental understanding and control. The proposed system distinguishes itself by combining multi-task learning, end-to-end vision-based control, and specific architectural choices (recurrent networks, VAE-GAN for regularization, autoregressive multimodal action prediction) to achieve complex manipulation skills through direct behavior cloning, without relying on additional refinement methods like dataset aggregation or reinforcement learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a method for autonomous, open-world execution of a diverse range of manipulation skills using inexpensive robotic hardware. This is primarily motivated by the need for assistive robots capable of performing Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) in uncontrolled home environments for disabled or elderly individuals.

The key motivations and specific objectives can be broken down as follows:

- **Social Impact and Accessibility:** A central motivation is to achieve maximum social impact by developing perception and control methods compatible with low-cost, imprecise robotic hardware and easily accessible sensory inputs, such as standard camera images. This aims to make assistive robotics affordable and deployable in typical user homes, allowing individuals to customize robot behaviors based on their specific needs and preferences.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Hand-Engineering:** Hand-engineering robust and resilient vision-based control strategies for open-world, varied tasks is exceptionally difficult. The authors are motivated to move beyond this approach to a learning-based paradigm.
- **Efficiency of Learning from Demonstration (LfD):** LfD is chosen over reinforcement learning due to its efficiency in requiring orders of magnitude fewer interactions. Human demonstrators can teach tasks without needing specialized knowledge of robot control or programming.
- **Reducing Demonstration Requirements:** While LfD is more efficient than RL, human demonstrations are still time-consuming. The authors are motivated to reduce the number of demonstrations required by combining data from multiple tasks and incorporating auxiliary losses to improve sample efficiency and generalization.
- **Multi-Task Learning for Generalization and Robustness:** The paper aims to train a single controller to accomplish multiple distinct manipulation tasks, including picking, placing, and non-prehensile manipulation. The motivation is that sharing network parameters across tasks and training on multiple tasks simultaneously will enhance generalization and robustness, potentially improving performance on individual tasks.
- **Addressing Challenges of Low-Cost Hardware:** Inexpensive robots often lack advanced proprioceptive capabilities. The objective is to develop a vision-based controller that can operate effectively using only raw image input, thereby compensating for the limitations of the hardware.
- **Robustness to Diverse Object Properties:** The tasks involve objects with varied physical properties (rigid boxes, jointed pliers, deformable towels). The system aims to handle this diversity within a single control framework.
- **Specific Architectural Contributions:**
 - **Image Reconstruction for Regularization:** A key objective is to integrate VAE-GAN-based image reconstruction as an auxiliary loss. This is motivated by the hypothesis that forcing the network to reconstruct input images will regularize the visual feature extractor, improving its ability to learn relevant features for control and enhancing generalization without additional unlabeled data.
 - **Autoregressive Multimodal Action Prediction:** The authors aim to use an autoregressive estimator to predict robot joint commands. This is motivated by the observation that many manipulation tasks have multiple valid solutions, and a unimodal predictor would average these solutions, leading to incorrect or impossible actions. An autoregressive multimodal predictor is expected to capture these distinct modes and provide more appropriate actions, as well as ensure coherence between joint predictions.
 - **Recurrent Neural Networks for Temporal Coherence:** The use of recurrent neural networks (LSTMs) is motivated by the need for memory in robot control. This allows the robot to maintain a consistent strategy over time, handle temporal dependencies (e.g., waiting before grasping), and manage temporary occlusions or insufficient current visual information.

In summary, the core objectives are to enable complex, multi-task robot manipulation on affordable platforms using vision-only, end-to-end learning from demonstration, leveraging novel architectural components for improved generalization, robustness, and sample efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology outlined in the paper consists of three primary stages: data collection through human demonstration, training a deep recurrent neural network, and autonomous task execution.

**A. Task Demonstration and Data Collection**
The initial phase involves collecting data from human demonstrations.
- **Robot Platform:** A low-cost, 6-axis Lynxmotion AL5D robotic arm equipped with a two-finger gripper was utilized.
- **Teleoperation System:** To facilitate intuitive demonstrations, a teleoperation system was employed where the robot's end-effector followed the user's hand. Two methods for tracking hand position and orientation were explored: a Leap Motion controller for "naked" hand tracking and a PlayStation Move controller. The entire setup, including the robot and demonstration system, costs approximately $500.
- **Data Recording:** During demonstrations, the system recorded 128x128 RGB images of the scene and the corresponding robot joint commands at a frequency of 33Hz.
- **Data Augmentation:** The recorded trajectories were down-sampled to 4Hz. This process creates redundant trajectories with different starting point offsets, effectively augmenting the dataset and aiding in network regularization. For example, a high-frequency trajectory `{t1, t2, ...}` down-sampled by a factor of 8 would yield 8 trajectories like `{t1, t9, t17, ...}`, `{t2, t10, t18, ...}`, etc.
- **Dataset Size:** Approximately 3 hours of demonstrations were collected for each of the five manipulation tasks, resulting in 398 to 909 distinct trajectories per task. 80% of this data was used for training and 20% for validation.

**B. Neural Network Architecture**
The proposed neural network architecture (illustrated in Figure 2 of the paper) integrates a VAE-GAN-based autoencoder with a recurrent controller network.
- **Visual Feature Extraction:** Input RGB images are first processed by convolutional layers, augmented with batch normalization, to extract low-dimensional visual features. This encoder component is shared between the autoencoder and the controller network.
- **VAE-GAN Autoencoder:** This component, based on the VAE-GAN approach, aims to encode input images into a latent representation (z) and reconstruct realistic images (`x`). It includes:
 - An **Encoder**: Maps input `x` to a latent distribution `q(z|x)`.
 - A **Generator (Decoder)**: Reconstructs an image `˜x` from a sampled latent `z` (`p(x|z)`).
 - A **Discriminator**: Differentiates between real input images `x` and reconstructed images `˜x`. Critically, the comparison for reconstruction loss (`E_rec`) occurs at the feature level (after the third convolutional layer of the discriminator), rather than pixel-wise, to reduce blurriness and uncertainty.
 - A **KL-divergence penalty** (`E_prior`) is applied to encourage the latent distribution `q(z|x)` to be close to a unit Gaussian, regularizing the latent space.
- **Controller Network:**
 - **Input:** The visual features extracted by the shared encoder are combined with a one-hot vector indicating the current task.
 - **Recurrent Processing:** These combined inputs are fed into three layers of layer-normalized Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cells. Each LSTM layer has 100 memory cells. LSTMs are used to introduce memory, allowing the network to maintain consistent strategies and handle temporal dependencies or temporary occlusions.
 - **Multimodal Autoregressive Action Prediction:** The output of the LSTM layers is used to predict robot joint commands using a Mixture Density Network (MDN). Instead of predicting all `j` joint configurations simultaneously, the system uses an autoregressive estimator, similar to Neural Autoregressive Distribution Estimator (NADE). This means each joint's prediction (e.g., base, shoulder, elbow, gripper) is conditioned on the predictions of the preceding joints in the current time-step. The MDN models the probability distribution of each joint as a linear combination of `m` Gaussian components (m=50 in this work), allowing for multimodal predictions. Skip connections from the input to all LSTM layers are also employed.
- **Regularization:** In addition to the VAE-GAN's regularization, noise from sampling the encoded latent features is introduced into the LSTM input. Dropout (with a probability of 0.5) is also applied to mitigate overfitting.

**C. Training the Network**
The network is trained using backpropagation with two distinct error signals, alternating between them at each iteration with a 0.5 probability.
- **Controller Loss (`E_controller`):** This is the negative logarithm likelihood of the Mixture Density Network's output, given by `E_controller = -ln{∑ α_i(x)g_i(y|x)}`, where `α_i(x)` are mixing coefficients and `g_i(y|x)` are Gaussian components.
- **Autoencoder Loss (`E_AE`):** This loss combines three components:
 - `E_prior`: KL-divergence between the latent distribution `q(z|x)` and a unit Gaussian prior `p(z)`.
 - `E_rec`: Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the extracted features (from the discriminator's third convolutional layer) of the real image `x` and the reconstructed image `˜x`.
 - `E_GAN`: Binary cross-entropy loss for the GAN discriminator, ensuring realistic reconstructions.
- **Implementation Details:**
 - Latent space size: 256.
 - Initialisation: VAE-GAN parameters are based on recommendations, while LSTM parameters are initialized uniformly between -0.08 and 0.08.
 - **Two-Step Training:**
 1. **Step 1 (Initial):** The full network is unrolled and trained using sequences of 5 time-steps, with a batch size of 100 examples, for 5 epochs. The Adam optimizer is used.
 2. **Step 2 (Fine-tuning LSTMs):** Features from the convolutional layers are dumped, and only the LSTM layers are trained. This step uses sequences of 50 time-steps, mini-batches of size 128, for 2000 epochs. The RMSProp optimizer is used with an initial learning rate of 0.005, a decay of 0.999, and gradients clipped in the range [-1, 1]. The learning rate is decreased by a factor of 2 every 100 epochs.

**D. Executing the Policy at Test Time**
During deployment, the trained controller operates in a continuous loop:
- The current environment image is captured.
- The neural network processes the image and task selector, and the LSTM predicts each joint configuration sequentially.
- Once all joint predictions are available, the robot executes the action.
- A new image is recorded, and the cycle repeats.
- **Bias for Sampling:** To mitigate the robot repeating human demonstration mistakes, a bias parameter `b` (ranging from 0 to 10) is introduced during sampling from the multimodal probability distribution. This bias `b` modifies the mixing coefficients and standard deviations (equations 10 and 11 in the paper) to favor higher probability areas, with `b=1` found to be effective.

**E. Justification of Architectural Choices**
The paper provides empirical justifications for the key architectural decisions:
- **Predicting entire probability distribution:** Addresses the issue of multimodal human solutions, where averaging distinct solutions (e.g., avoiding an obstacle left or right) would lead to an incorrect or impossible action (e.g., collision).
- **Recurrent Neural Networks (LSTMs):** Provide memory, allowing the robot to maintain a consistent strategy over time, handle sequential dependencies, and manage situations where the current visual input is insufficient (e.g., temporary occlusions or waiting phases).
- **Autoregressive Density Estimator:** Conditions the prediction of each joint on the predictions of preceding joints within the same time-step. This ensures coherent actions, such as closing the gripper only after the end-effector is in a correct grasping position.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the proposed multi-task learning method on a low-cost Lynxmotion AL5D robot across five distinct manipulation tasks (T1-T5), which are representative components of Activities of Daily Living (ADLs).

**A. Manipulation Tasks:**
The five tasks involved diverse objects and manipulation types:
- **T1: Pick up a small bubble wrap and put it into a small plate.** Challenging due to the thin, deformable nature of the bubble wrap and potential occlusion by the arm. Success was defined by placing the bubble wrap inside the plate.
- **T2: Push a round plate to a specified area.** Required accurate detection of the plate and precise pushing to achieve the target position. Success was defined by the plate being within a 2cm radius larger than its own radius of the target.
- **T3: Push a large box to a specific position and orientation.** Required adjusting both position (within 2cm wider than the box) and orientation (within 20° error) of the box. Demonstrated mistake correction by repositioning to push from a different side if the box moved unexpectedly.
- **T4: Close a set of open pliers and orient them parallel to the table borders.** Challenging for convolutional layers to detect thin handles. Success required completely closed pliers with a 10° orientation error tolerance.
- **T5: Pick up a towel and rub a small screwdriver box to clean it.** A longer, multi-step task involving picking, wiping the entire box at least once, and then replacing the towel.

**B. Autoencoder Validation:**
The autoencoder's ability to reconstruct input images was visually validated. Figure 3 in the paper illustrates original and reconstructed image pairs. The reconstructed images generally capture the objects and the robot arm with reasonable detail, suggesting that the latent features encoded by the VAE-GAN are sufficiently rich for the manipulation tasks.

**C. Performance Comparison of Network Architectures:**
The core results are presented as success rates for each task (Table I), comparing the full proposed architecture against several ablations. Each task was attempted 25 times, with time limits (45-75 seconds) for completion before being counted as a failure.

- **Single-task (no autoregressive):**
 - This baseline used the network architecture without the autoregressive estimator, and a separate network was trained for each task individually.
 - Success rates ranged from 8% (T5) to 44% (T3).
 - **Finding:** This approach proved difficult to train due to overfitting, indicating that large numbers of demonstrations are required for a single task and that its generalizations were limited without the full architecture.

- **Full network (no autoregressive):**
 - This configuration utilized weight sharing across tasks but still lacked the autoregressive estimator.
 - Success rates improved for T2-T5 (20-64%) compared to the single-task approach, but performance on T1 (16%) was lower.
 - **Finding:** Training a single network on multiple tasks (weight sharing) generally improved performance for most tasks, demonstrating the benefit of multi-task learning. However, the absence of autoregressive prediction remained a limiting factor, leading to lower success rates compared to the full model, especially for tasks requiring precise coordination.

- **Full network (no VAE-GAN):**
 - This version included the autoregressive estimator and multi-task training but excluded the VAE-GAN-based image reconstruction.
 - Success rates were notably lower, particularly for tasks involving smaller or more frequently occluded objects (T1: 12%, T5: 16%). T2 achieved a relatively high 72%.
 - **Finding:** The VAE-GAN component, which enforces image reconstruction, significantly contributed to the robustness of the visual feature extractor. Its absence resulted in poorer visual understanding, particularly for tasks requiring fine perception, indicating its role in regularization and effective feature learning.

- **Full network (Full architecture):**
 - This represents the complete proposed method, incorporating multi-task learning, recurrent neural networks, the VAE-GAN for regularization, and the autoregressive multimodal action prediction.
 - Achieved the highest success rates across all tasks: T1 (76%), T2 (80%), T3 (88%), T4 (76%), T5 (88%).
 - **Main Finding:** The combination of all proposed architectural choices (multi-task training, VAE-GAN reconstruction, and autoregressive action prediction) significantly outperforms the ablated models, demonstrating that each component contributes to overall performance, robustness, and generalization. The system achieved success rates between 76% and 88% for diverse and complex manipulation tasks on a low-cost robot.

**D. Qualitative Observations:**
Beyond numerical success rates, the full controller generated smooth trajectories and exhibited an ability to correct its own mistakes. An example shown in Figure 4 illustrates the robot over-pushing pliers but then moving to the other side to push them back into the desired orientation. The robot also learned to return to a default state after completing a task, reflecting a preference from the demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning the deployment of learning-based solutions on affordable hardware. The findings highlight several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Accessibility and Affordability in Assistive Robotics:** The demonstration that a low-cost robotic arm (total setup around $500) can perform complex manipulation tasks from raw monocular RGB images has substantial implications for assistive robotics. By removing the barrier of expensive hardware and complex programming, this approach paves the way for wider adoption of robots in homes to assist individuals with Activities of Daily Living (ADLs). This democratizes access to robotic assistance, moving it closer to a practical reality for a broader population.
- **Advancement in Vision-Based Control for Limited Hardware:** The method successfully enables control of an inexpensive robot lacking proprioceptive capabilities using only visual input. This demonstrates the robustness of the proposed deep neural network controller in extracting sufficient information from raw images to infer and execute precise manipulation, even with inherent hardware limitations.
- **Effectiveness of Multi-Task Learning:** The work provides empirical evidence that training a single neural network on multiple, diverse manipulation tasks significantly improves performance across all tasks compared to single-task learning. This suggests that the network learns generalizable visual features and motor skills that are transferable and beneficial across different contexts. This approach is more sample-efficient as common patterns across tasks can be learned from a larger, shared dataset.
- **Validation of Novel Architectural Components:** The research empirically validates the contribution of specific architectural choices:
 - **VAE-GAN for Regularization:** The integration of image reconstruction via VAE-GAN is shown to substantially improve generalization and robustness, particularly in tasks involving small or occluded objects. This highlights the value of auxiliary tasks in regularizing feature learning for downstream control.
 - **Autoregressive Multimodal Action Prediction:** The use of an autoregressive estimator for joint predictions and a Mixture Density Network for multimodal action distributions is crucial for handling situations where multiple correct solutions exist. This prevents the averaging of dissimilar motions that would lead to impossible or suboptimal actions, enhancing the robot's ability to execute coherent and robust behaviors.
 - **Recurrent Neural Networks:** The reliance on LSTMs demonstrates their importance in maintaining temporal consistency in robot actions, handling temporary occlusions, and managing sequential task components.
- **Path Towards Scalable Learning from Demonstration:** The findings suggest that multi-task learning offers a promising direction for scaling robotic capabilities. As more diverse task demonstration data is collected, the potential exists for a single multi-task network to require fewer individual demonstrations for each new task. This could significantly reduce the effort required to teach robots new behaviors in the future.
- **Contribution to Reproducible Research:** The open-sourcing of the system's code (https://github.com/rrahmati/roboinstruct-2) facilitates reproducibility and encourages further research and extension of the methodology by the broader scientific community.

In conclusion, this research provides a robust, accessible, and scalable framework for teaching complex manipulation skills to inexpensive robots using end-to-end learning from demonstration. Its potential impact lies in fostering the development of truly assistive robots capable of operating in the unstructured environments of human homes, thereby improving the quality of life for individuals needing support with daily activities.
