# PISTO: Proximal Inference for Stochastic Trajectory Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> The Georgia Institute of Technology researchers developed PISTO (Proximal Inference for Stochastic Trajectory Optimization), an algorithm that unifies STOMP with variational inference and proximal methods to enhance stability and efficiency in robotic motion planning and control. PISTO achieved an 88.57% success rate in motion planning benchmarks, outperforming STOMP (67.59%), and delivered 1.4x to 3.2x faster runtimes with up to 2.8x higher rewards in contact-rich MuJoCo tasks compared to baselines.

## Problem

- STOMP, a stochastic trajectory optimization method, lacked a rigorous theoretical underpinning for its optimization process, despite its ability to handle non-differentiable costs.
- Stochastic optimization methods can suffer from instabilities, leading to poor convergence or divergence in complex, non-convex cost landscapes.
- Existing optimization-based planners often require differentiable cost functions and can be susceptible to local minima.

## Method

- **PISTO** reinterprets STOMP as implicitly minimizing the forward KL divergence from a Boltzmann trajectory distribution, grounding it in a principled variational inference framework.
- The algorithm introduces a proximal penalty term to the variational inference objective, regularizing updates and providing a trust-region mechanism to stabilize the optimization process.
- It derives closed-form, derivative-free mean updates computable as expectations under a surrogate distribution, estimated efficiently via importance-weighted Monte Carlo sampling.

## Results

- PISTO achieved an **88.57%** success rate in 7-DOF robot arm motion planning across 300+ tasks, outperforming CHOMP (**63.05%**), STOMP (**67.59%**), and NGD (**75.76%**), with planning times averaging **0.237** seconds (twice as fast as STOMP and NGD).
- In contact-rich MuJoCo tasks (e.g., PushT, Hopper, HumanoidRun), PISTO consistently delivered higher accumulated rewards, showing up to a **2.8x** improvement over baselines like CEM and MPPI.
- The algorithm demonstrated faster runtimes, achieving speedups ranging from **1.4x** to **3.2x** compared to baselines on most MuJoCo tasks, while maintaining reliable convergence with tight standard deviations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - STOMP implicitly minimizes the forward KL divergence between a Gaussian proposal and a Boltzmann distribution representing the optimal path posterior.
> - Augmenting the forward KL objective with a proximal penalty is equivalent to minimizing the forward KL divergence to a surrogate distribution, which acts as a trust-region mechanism for stability.
> - Solving the reverse KL minimization problem with respect to this surrogate distribution allows for derivative-free mean updates using importance sampling, maintaining flexibility for non-differentiable costs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Motion planning is a foundational component in robotics, enabling autonomous systems to execute tasks by generating feasible trajectories in complex environments. The research on trajectory generation broadly falls into several categories, each with distinct advantages and limitations.

**Sampling-based methods,** such as Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM) and Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT*), are notable for their ability to explore high-dimensional configuration spaces and provide asymptotic completeness guarantees. However, the trajectories generated by these methods often lack smoothness and dynamic feasibility, necessitating substantial post-processing for practical application.

**Optimization-based planners** represent trajectories in continuous time and optimize for criteria such as smoothness, collision avoidance, and dynamic constraints. Approaches like CHOMP (Covariant Hamiltonian Optimization for Motion Planning), sequential convex programming, and Gaussian process methods (GPMP) offer impressive speed and scalability. Nevertheless, these methods typically require cost functions and constraints to be differentiable, and they can be susceptible to local minima in highly non-convex or cluttered environments.

**Stochastic trajectory optimization methods** aim to broaden the class of tractable objectives by incorporating sampling. Algorithms like STOMP (Stochastic Trajectory Optimization for Motion Planning) and path-integral methods (e.g., PI$^2$, MPPI) perturb candidate trajectories and update them through cost-weighted averaging. This allows them to effectively handle non-differentiable and discontinuous cost functions, a common characteristic of real-world scenarios such as collision detection or contact dynamics.

This paper positions itself within the "Planning as Inference" paradigm. This framework interprets optimal control as a probabilistic inference problem, where trajectory costs define a Boltzmann distribution whose high-probability regions correspond to desirable plans. Existing works under this paradigm include connections to linearly-solvable control, path-integral methods, and variational formulations in reinforcement learning. Variational inference approaches, such as Gaussian Variational Inference for Motion Planning (GVIMP), approximate this Boltzmann distribution with a structured Gaussian family, providing a principled KL-divergence objective. Other related methods include those leveraging Stein Variational approaches, Mixtures of Gaussian Processes for Trajectory Optimization (MGPTO), and P-GVIMP.

The current work builds upon this landscape by explicitly revealing the implicit variational inference structure of STOMP. Furthermore, it integrates concepts from **proximal methods**, which augment optimization objectives with penalties on the distance from the current solution, effectively creating a trust region to prevent overly aggressive updates. By combining the variational inference perspective with proximal regularization, the authors propose a novel algorithm designed to offer enhanced stability and efficiency in stochastic trajectory optimization, while retaining the benefits of handling non-differentiable costs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a fundamental question regarding STOMP: what objective does it implicitly optimize? While STOMP is recognized for its ability to handle non-differentiable and discontinuous costs through sampling-based updates, a rigorous theoretical underpinning for its optimization process was less explicit.

Based on this foundational inquiry, the key objectives of this work are:

- **Establish the Variational Inference (VI) Structure of STOMP:** A major objective is to demonstrate that STOMP implicitly minimizes the reverse KL divergence from a Boltzmann trajectory distribution. This connection aims to provide an elegant variational inference structure underlying STOMP's updates, thereby grounding it within a broader, principled inference-based planning framework. This theoretical revelation is intended to motivate subsequent algorithmic improvements.
- **Develop a Stabilized Stochastic Trajectory Optimization Algorithm:** The paper aims to propose an algorithm that addresses potential instabilities in stochastic optimization methods like STOMP. This objective is motivated by the observation that uncontrolled updates can lead to poor convergence or divergence in complex cost landscapes.
- **Introduce Proximal Regularization to the VI Objective:** To achieve stabilization, a core objective is to augment the variational inference objective with a proximal penalty. This penalty is designed to regularize the updates by controlling the distance between successive Gaussian proposals, thus admitting a trust-region interpretation. This regularization is intended to improve numerical stability and convergence properties.
- **Derive Closed-Form, Derivative-Free Mean Updates:** The research seeks to derive closed-form expressions for the mean updates within the proposed proximal inference framework. These updates are targeted to be computable as expectations under a surrogate distribution, which can then be efficiently estimated via importance-weighted Monte Carlo sampling. This approach aims to maintain the derivative-free nature of STOMP, allowing it to handle non-differentiable and discontinuous costs without modification, while offering principled stability.
- **Demonstrate Empirical Effectiveness Across Diverse Robotic Tasks:** A practical objective is to validate the performance of the proposed algorithm, PISTO (Proximal Inference for Stochastic Trajectory Optimization), across a range of robotics benchmarks. This includes robot arm motion planning tasks, comparing its success rate, planning time, path length, and clearance against existing gradient-based and stochastic methods. Additionally, the paper aims to demonstrate PISTO's effectiveness in contact-rich MuJoCo locomotion and manipulation tasks, evaluating its performance in terms of achieved reward and runtime against competitive baselines like CEM and MPPI.

In essence, the motivation is to leverage theoretical insights from variational inference and proximal methods to develop a more robust, efficient, and broadly applicable stochastic trajectory optimization algorithm for challenging robotics problems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this paper centers on a novel approach called Proximal Inference for Stochastic Trajectory Optimization (PISTO). It commences by re-interpreting STOMP within a variational inference framework and then builds upon this insight to introduce proximal regularization for stabilized updates.

**A. Variational Inference Interpretation of STOMP**

The paper first analyzes STOMP's objective function, which minimizes the expectation of a cost functional $S(\tilde{Y}) + \frac{1}{2}\tilde{Y}^\top R \tilde{Y}$ over a Gaussian proposal distribution $N(Y, \Sigma)$. Here, $S(\tilde{Y})$ represents state-dependent costs (e.g., obstacle avoidance), and $\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Y}^\top R \tilde{Y}$ is a control cost for smoothness, implicitly corresponding to a Gaussian smoothness prior $N(0, R^{-1})$.

The core theoretical contribution in this initial phase is **Theorem 1**, which proves that minimizing STOMP's objective is equivalent to solving a variational inference problem: $min_Y D_{KL}(N(Y, \Sigma) \parallel Y^\star)$, where $Y^\star \propto \exp(-S(\tilde{Y}))N(0, R^{-1})$ is a Boltzmann distribution representing the optimal path posterior. This establishes STOMP as implicitly performing a *forward* KL divergence minimization.

**B. PISTO Formulation: Proximal Inference**

PISTO introduces a novel framework for solving this inference problem, utilizing a proximal approach.

1. **Shift to Reverse KL Minimization:** While STOMP implicitly performs forward KL minimization, PISTO focuses on solving a *reverse* KL minimization problem for its updates: $min_Y D_{KL}(Y^\star \parallel N(Y, \Sigma))$. **Lemma 1** states that the optimal mean for this reverse KL problem is given by the expectation $Y^\star = E_{Y^\star}[\tilde{Y}]$. This moment-matching property is crucial. The paper highlights that, with a specific choice of covariance $\Sigma = R^{-1}$, the mean can be estimated via importance-weighted Monte Carlo sampling, similar to the STOMP update.

2. **Proximal Update Rule (Theorem 2):** PISTO augments the forward KL objective with a proximal penalty term between the current Gaussian proposal $N(Y, \Sigma)$ and the previous iterate's Gaussian approximation $N(Y^k, \Sigma)$. The proximal objective for iteration $k+1$ is defined as:
 $Y^{k+1} = \text{arg min}_Y D_{KL}(N(Y, \Sigma) \parallel Y^\star) + \frac{1}{\eta} D_{KL}(N(Y, \Sigma) \parallel N(Y^k, \Sigma))$.
 Here, $\eta > 0$ is a step size parameter. This proximal formulation serves as a trust-region mechanism, preventing aggressive steps and stabilizing the optimization. **Theorem 2** demonstrates that this augmented proximal objective is equivalent to minimizing the forward KL divergence to a *surrogate* distribution $Y_k^\star$:
 $Y^{k+1} = \text{arg min}_Y D_{KL}(N(Y, \Sigma) \parallel Y_k^\star)$.
 The surrogate distribution $Y_k^\star \propto (Y^\star)^{\frac{\eta}{\eta+1}} (N(Y^k, \Sigma))^{\frac{1}{\eta+1}}$ geometrically interpolates between the true target and the current approximation.

3. **Moment-Matching Solution to Proximal Inference (Theorem 3):** To compute the update $Y^{k+1}$, PISTO solves the *reverse* KL minimization problem with respect to the surrogate distribution: $Y^{k+1} = \text{arg min}_Y D_{KL}(Y_k^\star \parallel N(Y, \Sigma))$. **Theorem 3** establishes that the solution to this problem is the mean of the surrogate distribution: $Y^{k+1} = E_{Y_k^\star}[\tilde{Y}]$.

**C. Importance Sampling for Expectation Estimation**

To estimate the expectation $E_{Y_k^\star}[\tilde{Y}]$, PISTO employs importance sampling. The surrogate distribution $Y_k^\star$ is re-expressed in a tilted-Gaussian form. With the common choice $\Sigma = R^{-1}$, the importance weights simplify to $w^k(\tilde{Y}) \propto \exp(-\gamma(S(\tilde{Y}) + \tilde{Y}^\top R Y^k))$, where $\gamma = \frac{\eta}{\eta+1}$.

The final Monte Carlo estimator for the update is:
$Y^{k+1} \approx \sum_{m=1}^M \bar{w}^m (Y^k + \epsilon^m)$, where $\epsilon^m \sim N(0, R^{-1})$ are i.i.d. samples, and $\bar{w}^m$ are normalized weights. This forms the basis of **Algorithm 1**, a simple, derivative-free, and parallelizable procedure.

**D. Extension to Policy Optimization for Contact-Rich Tasks**

PISTO is extended to policy optimization in the action space for tasks with known dynamics models. The optimal control problem is formulated as minimizing a sum of stage costs over a control sequence $U$, with a quadratic regularization term $1/2 U^\top R U$. The state trajectory is determined by recursive application of dynamics. The problem is cast into the form suitable for PISTO, where the cost function $S(U; x_0)$ represents the rollout cost. Algorithm 1 applies directly, with samples now being control sequences evaluated by rolling out the dynamics. This allows PISTO to optimize directly in control space without requiring differentiability of the dynamics or cost.

**E. Covariance and Proximal Step Size Annealing**

To manage exploration and exploitation, PISTO implements annealing strategies:
- **Covariance Scheduling:** The smoothness matrix $R$ is scaled by $\sigma^k$, which follows a cosine annealing schedule from an initial to a final value.
- **Proximal Step Size Annealing:** The proximal parameter $\eta$ is annealed exponentially from small values (encouraging exploration) to larger values (exploiting high-reward samples).
- **Temperature Parameter ($\tau$):** An additional temperature parameter $\tau$ is introduced to scale the impact of the cost term in the importance weights, allowing independent control over reward sensitivity.

**F. Implementation Details (Appendix B)**

Further refinements include:
- **Elite Sample Selection:** Only samples in the lowest K-th percentile of cost (elite set) are used for computing importance weights, aiming to improve convergence and reduce variance.
- **Momentum-Accelerated Exponential Moving Average:** A momentum-based update scheme is employed, where the update direction is maintained via an exponential moving average. This temporal regularization damps oscillations and provides momentum for navigating non-smooth cost landscapes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical findings regarding STOMP's underlying structure and empirical results demonstrating PISTO's performance across various robotic tasks.

**A. Theoretical Findings**
The paper establishes that STOMP implicitly minimizes the KL divergence from a Boltzmann trajectory distribution, revealing its variational inference structure. This theoretical connection motivates the development of PISTO. PISTO itself is formulated as a proximal inference algorithm that augments the reverse-KL objective with a proximal penalty between successive Gaussian proposals. This formulation is shown to admit a trust-region interpretation, contributing to the stabilization of optimization dynamics. A key result is the derivation of closed-form mean updates for PISTO, which are computable as expectations under a surrogate distribution and amenable to importance-weighted Monte Carlo sampling.

**B. Experimental Results for Motion Planning (Robot Arms)**
Experiments were conducted on a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda robot across seven manipulation environments from the MotionBenchMaker dataset, totaling over 300 planning tasks. PISTO was benchmarked against CHOMP, STOMP, and a Natural Gradient Descent (NGD) method.
- **Success Rate:** PISTO achieved an 88.57% success rate, outperforming CHOMP (63.05%), STOMP (67.59%), and NGD (75.76%). This indicates a substantial improvement in the ability to find valid, collision-free paths.
- **Planning Time:** PISTO demonstrated significantly faster planning times, averaging 0.237 seconds. This is approximately twice as fast as the competing stochastic methods, STOMP (0.481s) and NGD (0.499s), and also faster than CHOMP (0.498s).
- **Path Length:** PISTO generated paths with an average length of 8.421, which is comparable to STOMP (8.424) and shorter than CHOMP (8.519) and NGD (8.838), suggesting efficiency in trajectory execution.
- **Path Clearance:** PISTO achieved an average path clearance of 0.124, which is competitive, surpassing CHOMP (0.108) but lower than NGD (0.161). This metric relates to the distance maintained from obstacles.

**C. Experimental Results for Policy Optimization (Contact-Rich MuJoCo Tasks)**
PISTO was further validated on five contact-rich tasks in MuJoCo, including PushT, Hopper, Walker2d, HumanoidRun, and HumanoidStandUp. The results were compared against CEM (Cross-Entropy Method) and MPPI (Model Predictive Path Integral).
- **Reward:** PISTO consistently outperformed both CEM and MPPI baselines in terms of accumulated reward per step across all five tasks. For example, PISTO achieved approximately a 1.5x improvement on Walker2d and a 2.8x improvement on HumanoidRun compared to the best baseline. Notably, PISTO converted a negative MPPI performance on PushT (-0.17) into a positive reward of 0.46, indicating a significant gain in task accomplishment.
- **Runtime:** PISTO generally exhibited faster runtimes, achieving speedups ranging from 1.4x to 3.2x compared to baselines on most tasks. A particularly significant speedup was observed on HumanoidRun (36.48s for PISTO versus approximately 115s for baselines).
- **Reliability:** The reported tight standard deviations across 50 independent runs suggest reliable convergence of PISTO despite the non-differentiable dynamics inherent in contact-rich environments. PISTO also successfully handled high-dimensional tasks, such as the 17-DOF humanoid running and standing-up tasks, within minutes.

Overall, the findings indicate that PISTO provides a robust, efficient, and higher-performing solution for both collision-free motion planning and complex, contact-rich trajectory optimization, surpassing several established baseline methods in success rate, planning speed, and accumulated reward.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PISTO algorithm represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic motion planning and control, offering both theoretical advancements and practical performance improvements.

**A. Theoretical Significance**
The work provides a fundamental theoretical insight by formally connecting STOMP's empirical success to a principled variational inference framework. By demonstrating that STOMP implicitly minimizes the KL divergence from a Boltzmann trajectory distribution, the authors provide a rigorous foundation for a class of stochastic optimization methods that were previously less explicitly understood from an inference perspective. This connection positions STOMP within a broader probabilistic framework, opening avenues for more principled algorithmic design. The introduction of proximal regularization within this variational inference context provides a novel mechanism for stabilizing updates, which is theoretically justified by its trust-region interpretation.

**B. Algorithmic and Performance Impact**
PISTO addresses key challenges in existing trajectory optimization methods:
- **Stability and Robustness:** The proximal formulation inherently stabilizes the optimization process, leading to significantly higher success rates (88.57% on motion planning benchmarks, outperforming CHOMP, STOMP, and NGD) and reliable convergence, even in tasks with discontinuous contact dynamics. This enhanced robustness makes PISTO suitable for deployment in complex, real-world robotic systems where instability can be critical.
- **Efficiency:** PISTO demonstrates considerable efficiency, achieving planning speeds twice as fast as competing stochastic methods in motion planning and 1.4x-3.2x faster runtimes in many contact-rich MuJoCo tasks. This is attributed to the closed-form moment-matching updates and the ability to leverage importance-weighted Monte Carlo sampling, which is inherently parallelizable.
- **Flexibility:** Inheriting STOMP's derivative-free nature, PISTO retains the crucial ability to handle non-differentiable and discontinuous cost functions, such as those arising from collision detection or complex physical interactions like contacts. This makes it broadly applicable to a wider range of robotics problems that challenge gradient-based optimizers.
- **Trajectory Quality:** PISTO not only achieves higher success rates but also produces shorter, smoother paths in motion planning, and significantly higher rewards in contact-rich tasks (e.g., 1.5x to 2.8x improvement over baselines in MuJoCo).

**C. Broader Impact on Robotics**
The implications for robotics are substantial. PISTO's ability to efficiently generate high-quality, collision-free, and dynamically feasible trajectories in complex, high-dimensional, and contact-rich environments can advance capabilities in:
- **Manipulation:** More reliable and faster planning for industrial robots and humanoid manipulators in cluttered workspaces.
- **Locomotion:** Robust control policy optimization for complex legged robots navigating varied terrains and performing dynamic maneuvers.
- **Autonomous Systems:** Improved planning components for self-driving cars, drones, and other autonomous agents operating in uncertain and dynamic environments.

The work also outlines clear directions for future research, including the incorporation of available gradients, joint optimization of mean and covariance (beyond fixed covariance), and extending the framework to receding-horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) settings. These next steps suggest that the PISTO framework could serve as a foundation for further advancements in intelligent and autonomous robotic systems.
