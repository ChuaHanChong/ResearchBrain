# Achieving Stable High-Speed Locomotion for Humanoid Robots with Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Science and Technology of China and the Ningbo Institute of Digital Twin developed Kinodynamic-regularized Stable Locomotion Control (KSLC), a deep reinforcement learning method enabling humanoid robots to achieve stable high-speed locomotion up to 3.5 m/s in simulation. The approach incorporates angular momentum regulation and velocity-adaptive rewards, effectively utilizing upper-body arm movements to enhance stability and velocity tracking.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots typically struggle with instability and are limited to low-velocity locomotion (around 1.0 m/s) when controlled by Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL).
- Existing DRL methods often simplify the problem by disabling upper-body joints, which restricts natural balancing mechanisms and can lead to erratic behaviors at higher speeds.
- Challenges in achieving precise velocity tracking and generalization across a wide range of speeds due to fixed reward functions in DRL, often resulting in conservative robot behaviors.

## Method

- Proposed **Kinodynamic-regularized Stable Locomotion Control (KSLC)**, a DRL framework using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== to integrate kinodynamic priors into the control policy.
- Designed a novel reward function that includes an ==angular momentum-based reward== to actively minimize total angular momentum (especially yaw) using coordinated arm movements, and velocity-related rewards for adaptive base height, foot clearance, and joint positions.
- Implemented ==curriculum learning== strategies (velocity and cycle time) to progressively train the robot for higher speeds and dynamically adjust gait parameters, alongside ==domain randomization== for sim-to-real transfer robustness.

## Results

- The KSLC-enabled humanoid robot (XBot-L) achieved stable locomotion at a target velocity of **3.5 m/s** in the Humanoid-Gym simulator, a substantial improvement over typical DRL-based methods.
- Demonstrated superior stability with reduced fluctuations in zero-commanded velocity directions compared to baseline methods, confirming effective angular momentum compensation.
- Successfully validated the learned policy with zero-shot sim-to-sim transfer in the high-fidelity MuJoCo simulator, where the robot tracked a commanded velocity of **4.0 m/s** without falling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively leveraging upper-body joints, specifically arm movements, can significantly improve a humanoid robot's stability at high speeds by counteracting destabilizing angular momentum.
> - Integrating physically informed kinodynamic priors into DRL reward functions leads to more robust and stable locomotion policies that mimic biomechanical principles.
> - Dynamically adjusting reward components based on commanded velocity and employing curriculum learning are crucial for enabling DRL agents to generalize locomotion patterns across a wide range of speeds.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous humanoid robots capable of navigating and interacting with human-centric environments is a grand challenge in robotics. While humanoid robots offer unparalleled versatility due to their human-like skeletal structure and ability to utilize existing infrastructure, achieving robust and stable locomotion, especially at high speeds, remains a significant hurdle.

Historically, classical control methods for humanoid locomotion demand extensive manual effort to account for specific situations and the robot's inherent complexity. The advent of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has revolutionized this landscape, offering a paradigm shift towards automated discovery of optimal control strategies through iterative trial-and-error in simulation. DRL's benefits, such as robustness, reduced manual design, and efficiency through parallel training, have made it a popular methodology in robot control.

However, existing DRL-based methods for humanoid locomotion have primarily been limited to low-velocity walking (around 1.0 m/s) and often simplify the problem by disabling upper-body joints to reduce the action space and improve stability. When upper-body joints are engaged, these robots often exhibit unpredictable, unnatural, or erratic twisting behaviors, leading to instability. Recent works, such as Radosavovic et al. and Zhang et al., have shown progress in real-world or whole-body humanoid locomotion using DRL, but high-speed, precise velocity tracking with full upper-body engagement remains largely unaddressed. Challenges persist due to the humanoid's high center of mass and numerous joints, which complicate DRL training.

This paper directly addresses these limitations by demonstrating that, contrary to conventional approaches, leveraging upper-body joints (specifically arm movements) can substantially improve stability and effectiveness for both walking and running at high speeds. The authors draw inspiration from biomechanical studies of human locomotion and classical robotics work by Raibert, which emphasized angular momentum principles for balance. While angular momentum-based control has been explored in conjunction with Model Predictive Control (MPC) (e.g., Kojio et al., Ding et al.), its integration with DRL, particularly for high-speed humanoid locomotion, has been largely unexplored.

Thus, this work positions itself at the intersection of advanced DRL for complex robotic systems and biomechanically inspired control principles. It seeks to bridge the gap between low-speed DRL-controlled humanoids and the ultimate goal of agile, human-like locomotion in real-world scenarios, thereby pushing the boundaries of what DRL can achieve for high-DoF, dynamically challenging robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to achieve precise and stable high-speed locomotion control for humanoid robots, effectively preventing the development of unstable gaits or falls. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and challenges:

1. **Overcoming Instability at High Speeds:** Humanoid robots are highly prone to instability at increased velocities, largely due to the imbalanced angular momentum generated, particularly by the swinging legs in the global yaw direction. The conventional approach of disabling upper-body joints to simplify control often restricts the robot's natural balancing mechanisms, leading to limitations in speed and agility.
2. **Enhancing Velocity Tracking and Generalization:** Existing DRL methods, often relying on fixed reward functions, tend to encourage a single motion pattern (e.g., a specific walking style) and struggle to accurately track a wide range of commanded velocities, especially at higher speeds. This leads to conservative behaviors or failures when confronted with commands outside their narrow training focus. The motivation is to enable the robot to adapt its gait and motion patterns dynamically across varying speeds.
3. **Leveraging Full-Body Kinodynamics:** The authors challenge the prevailing idea that upper-body movements are detrimental or unnecessary for DRL-based locomotion. Instead, they hypothesize that coordinated arm movements, similar to those in human locomotion, can actively counteract destabilizing forces and improve overall stability. The motivation is to explore and exploit the full kinematic potential of humanoid robots.
4. **Developing a Robust and Deployable Control Policy:** Beyond simulation, the ultimate goal is to develop a control policy that can transfer effectively to real-world hardware. This necessitates robustness to simulation discrepancies and a demonstration of performance in high-fidelity environments, motivating the use of domain randomization and sim-to-sim validation.

To address these objectives, the authors propose the **Kinodynamic-regularized Stable Locomotion Control (KSLC)** method. The core idea is to integrate DRL with prior kinodynamic knowledge to discover optimal control policies that inherently promote stability. Specifically, they aim to design a reward function that minimizes angular momentum, encouraging the robot to use its arms and elbow flexion to stabilize its body, and to introduce velocity-based rewards to improve tracking accuracy and generalization across a broad spectrum of commanded velocities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Kinodynamic-regularized Stable Locomotion Control (KSLC), a DRL-based approach that integrates kinodynamic priors to stabilize humanoid robots, particularly for high-speed locomotion.

**A. Problem Formulation and DRL Algorithm:**
The objective is formulated as finding an optimal control policy π\* that minimizes the difference between the robot's actual velocity (v_π) and the commanded velocity (v_cmd), expressed as `π* = argmin_π ||v_π - v_cmd||_1`. This is treated as a sequential decision-making problem. The humanoid robot's state (x_t), including proprioceptive sensor data, clock signals, and commanded velocities, is used to determine actions (a_t), which are target joint positions for a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller. The DRL algorithm employed is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, chosen for its efficiency and suitability for parallel computation, to train a deep neural network (DNN) that approximates the optimal policy. The robot model used is **XBot-L** within the **Humanoid-Gym** simulator.

**B. Kinodynamics Priors and Upper-Body Utilization:**
A central tenet of KSLC is the integration of kinodynamic priors. Recognizing that unrestrained joints can lead to instability, especially the significant angular momentum generated by swinging legs, the method proposes actively utilizing upper-body joints to counterbalance these forces. Specifically, the left and right shoulder pitch and elbow pitch joints of the XBot-L are allowed to generate angular momentum that counteracts the leg swing, thereby enhancing stability (as illustrated in Fig. 3). This directly challenges the conventional approach of disabling arm joints.

**C. Reward Function Design – The Core of KSLC:**
The total reward (`r_t`) is a weighted sum of three categories, with carefully tuned coefficients (`α_a`, `α_v`, `α_c`):
`r_t = α_a r_a_t + α_v r_v_t + α_c r_c_t`

1. **Angular Momentum-based Reward (`r_a_t`):**
 - This reward directly integrates the kinodynamic prior to minimize total angular momentum (`L_total`), especially in the yaw direction, which is critical for high-speed stability.
 - The reward function is defined as `r_a_t = clip(-exp (||A_a_t||^2) , c1, c2)`, where `||A_a_t||^2` is the L2 norm of the angular momentum. The exponential term harshly penalizes large angular momentum, while the clip function mitigates extreme values.

2. **Velocity-related Rewards (`r_v_t`):**
 - Designed to overcome the limitations of fixed reward functions, which hinder generalization and accurate tracking across varied velocities. This category comprises three sub-rewards:
 - **Base Height Reward (`r_b_t`):** Encourages dynamic adjustment of the robot's center of mass (CoM) height based on commanded velocity, similar to human locomotion. It's calculated as `r_b_t = exp(-β_b_t ||h_b_t - γ_b_t * ĥ_b_t * g(v_cmd_t, v_max_t)||^2)`, where `g` scales the target height based on the ratio of commanded to maximum velocity.
 - **Feet Clearance Reward (`r_f_t`):** Promotes appropriate foot clearance during the swing phase, adjusted by locomotion velocity to prevent tripping and enhance terrain navigation. It is given by `r_f_t = ξ(t) * (h_f_t - ĥ_f_t * g(v_cmd_t, v_max_t))`, with `ξ(t)` being a swing mask.
 - **Joint Position Reward (`r_p_t`):** Facilitates appropriate increases in joint angles to enable longer strides, crucial for high-velocity tracking. The reward `r_p_t = exp(β_p_t ||(ĥ_p_t - θ_p_t) exp(-θ̇_p_t)||^2) - γ_p_t clip(||(ĥ_p_t - θ_p_t) exp(-θ̇_p_t)||^2, c1, c2)` incorporates a velocity-related coefficient `exp(-θ̇_p_t)` to adjust the importance of joint position tracking error based on joint velocity.

**D. Curriculum Learning Strategies:**
To enable the robot to learn high-speed locomotion without falling into local optima or struggling with early high-velocity commands:
1. **Velocity Curriculum:** Training starts with low-velocity commands, and the range of commanded velocities is progressively expanded based on a reward-based update rule. If the linear velocity tracking reward (`r_trk_t`) exceeds a threshold (`λr_trk, max_t`), the velocity command interval `[v_min, v_max]` is updated.
2. **Cycle Time Curriculum:** The gait cycle time (stride frequency) is dynamically adjusted. As commanded velocity increases, the robot is encouraged to shorten leg movement cycle times. A reward-based update rule gradually reduces the cycle time, with a minimum period of 0.48 s.

**E. Domain Randomization:**
To minimize the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization is applied during training. This involves randomizing various physical parameters of the simulated environment (e.g., frictions, body mass, external forces) to train a more robust policy that can generalize better to real-world uncertainties and high-fidelity simulators.

**F. Sim-to-Sim Validation:**
The policy trained in the low-fidelity Humanoid-Gym is validated using zero-shot sim-to-sim experiments in a high-fidelity simulator, MuJoCo. This approach serves as a robust proxy for sim-to-real transfer, as agents performing well in sim-to-sim often show satisfactory real-world performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate the significant effectiveness of the Kinodynamic-regularized Stable Locomotion Control (KSLC) method in achieving stable high-speed locomotion for humanoid robots.

1. **Superior Velocity Tracking and Stability:**
 - The KSLC-enabled humanoid robot (XBot-L) successfully tracked a target velocity of **3.5 m/s** in the Humanoid-Gym simulator. This is a substantial improvement over the approximate 1.0 m/s typically reported for DRL-based humanoid locomotion methods.
 - Crucially, the KSLC method resulted in **reduced fluctuations** in zero-commanded velocity directions, indicating enhanced stability during locomotion compared to the baseline.
 - In contrast, the baseline method failed to track high commanded velocities, resorting to conservative, low-velocity locomotion, highlighting KSLC's ability to drive agile, high-performance gaits. This difference is clearly illustrated in the learning curves (Fig. 4) and performance metrics (Fig. 5).

2. **Effective Utilization of Upper-Body Kinodynamics:**
 - A key finding is that by allowing and encouraging coordinated arm movements (shoulder pitch and elbow pitch joints), KSLC enables the robot to effectively counteract the destabilizing angular momentum generated by the swinging legs, particularly in the global yaw direction. This validates the central hypothesis that leveraging, rather than disabling, upper-body joints is beneficial for stability at high speeds.
 - The visual evidence (Fig. 6) and qualitative observations confirmed that the robot generated appropriate arm movements for stabilization, mimicking human-like running mechanics.

3. **Improved Generalization through Velocity-Related Rewards and Curriculum Learning:**
 - The proposed velocity-related reward functions (base height, feet clearance, and joint position rewards) allowed the robot to adapt its motion patterns dynamically across a wide range of commanded velocities. This effectively addressed the limitation of fixed rewards, which often lead to conservative policies.
 - The curriculum learning strategies (velocity and cycle time curricula) proved instrumental in enabling the robot to learn complex high-speed gaits. By progressively increasing the challenge, the robot learned to increase stride frequency and adjust its gait cycle dynamically, essential for achieving higher velocities without falling.

4. **Robust Sim-to-Sim Validation:**
 - The policy trained with KSLC in the Humanoid-Gym demonstrated **consistent or even superior performance** during zero-shot sim-to-sim validation in the high-fidelity MuJoCo simulator.
 - The XBot-L successfully tracked a commanded velocity of **4.0 m/s** in MuJoCo without falling, further confirming the robustness and transferability of the learned policy.
 - The arm movement patterns observed in MuJoCo were consistent with those in the training environment, reinforcing the effectiveness of the angular momentum-based reward and showing the adaptability of the learned gaits across different simulation fidelities (Fig. 6). This strong sim-to-sim performance suggests a high potential for successful sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in achieving stable and agile locomotion. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Breakthrough in High-Speed Humanoid Locomotion:** By enabling stable locomotion at speeds up to 3.5 m/s (and even 4.0 m/s in high-fidelity simulation), this work pushes the boundaries far beyond the typical 1.0 m/s achieved by previous DRL methods. This is a critical step towards making humanoids practically useful in dynamic, real-world scenarios that demand speed and agility.
2. **Re-evaluation of Upper-Body Control Strategies:** The most impactful contribution is the demonstration that actively utilizing, rather than disabling, the upper-body joints (arms) is crucial for enhancing stability at high speeds. This challenges a common simplification in humanoid DRL research and establishes a new paradigm for designing reward functions that leverage full-body kinodynamics based on human-inspired priors (like angular momentum compensation).
3. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization for DRL:** The integration of physically informed kinodynamic priors into the DRL reward function makes the learned policies more grounded in physical reality and inherently more stable. Coupled with velocity-related rewards and curriculum learning, the method produces a control policy that generalizes well across a wide range of commanded velocities, rather than being confined to specific gaits or speeds. This adaptability is key for deploying robots in unpredictable environments.
4. **Strong Potential for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot sim-to-sim validation in a high-fidelity simulator like MuJoCo is a strong indicator of the policy's robustness and its high potential for direct transfer to physical humanoid robots. This reduces the time and cost associated with real-world experimentation and brings practical deployment closer to reality.
5. **Foundation for Advanced Humanoid Capabilities:** Stable and fast locomotion is a fundamental prerequisite for a wide array of advanced humanoid tasks, including dynamic manipulation, carrying loads, rapid navigation in complex environments (e.g., disaster response, logistics), and even human-robot collaboration in fast-paced settings. This work lays a crucial foundation for enabling these capabilities.
6. **Inspiration for Future Research:** The success of incorporating human-inspired kinodynamic priors opens new avenues for DRL research in robotics. Future work can explore integrating more complex biomechanical principles, such as balance control during perturbations, or adapting gaits to various terrains, further enhancing humanoid robot intelligence and physical prowess.

In conclusion, the KSLC method represents a substantial leap forward for humanoid robotics, transforming these complex machines from slow walkers into potentially agile runners. By intelligently combining the learning power of DRL with fundamental principles of physics and biomechanics, this research paves the way for a new generation of high-performing humanoid robots capable of operating effectively in dynamic, human-centric environments.
