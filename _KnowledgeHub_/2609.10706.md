# HuRo: Robotizing Human Videos for Scalable VLA Pretraining

> [!summary] Summary
> HuRo is a robotization pipeline that turns heterogeneous egocentric human videos into robot-aligned episodes. It removes human arms through segmentation and inpainting, overlays a rendered robot, and retargets hand motion into robot actions. The resulting dataset holds 630K episodes and 142M frames. Pretraining a GR00T-based VLA on it raises real-robot completion from 51.5% to 80.3%, and OOD completion from 34.9% to 72.2%.

## Problem

- Joint observation-action robotization of human videos has mostly been shown in task-matched settings, with human demos collected or converted for specific downstream robot tasks rather than as scalable pretraining data.
- Large-scale human-video action pretraining retargets recovered hand motion but keeps human-centric observations, which leaves the visual embodiment gap between human hands and robot bodies unaddressed.
- Prior visual robotization used robot-overlaid human videos only for visual-encoder or auxiliary prediction objectives, so it never tested end-to-end policy pretraining with aligned robot actions.

## Method

- ==Annotation stage==: estimates the missing cues for each clip, namely camera intrinsics, metric gravity-aligned ==SLAM== camera trajectories, MANO hand poses, and VLM-captioned language instructions filtered by a caption-frame verification pass.
- ==Action conversion==: PyRoKi two-stage ==retargeting== jointly optimizes a chunk-level base alignment (translation plus yaw) and robot joint configurations, then smooths the full trajectory. Actions are defined as the next retargeted state.
- ==Visual conversion== and **HuRo** dataset: SAM2 arm masks and ProPainter inpainting remove humans, and Isaac Sim renders the bimanual dexterous ALLEX robot from the aligned camera. This builds 630K episodes from five egocentric sources for GR00T-N1.6 flow-matching pretraining.

## Results

- Scaling HuRo pretraining from **0%** to **100%** raises in-distribution completion from **68.1%** to **88.4%** and OOD completion from **34.9%** to **72.2%** across four real ALLEX tasks. It also surpasses the pi0.5 and GR00T N1.6 references.
- Removing the robot overlay keeps ID performance (**89.4%** vs **88.4%**) but cuts OOD to **55.7%** versus **72.2%**. This falls below even the **10%** overlay subset (**59.5%**).
- End-to-end visual-plus-action pretraining reaches **61.1%** ID and **50.0%** OOD on Diverse Pick-and-Place and beats visual-only transfer. HuRo at **0.7M** frames also outperforms an I2V+IDM generated-video baseline at **7M** frames.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visually robotizing human videos matters mainly for robustness, not in-distribution accuracy. The observation gap shows up under spatial and visual shift, so robot overlays buy OOD generalization.
> - Retargeted action supervision transfers much more than visual pretraining alone. Robotized human data should pretrain the action head end to end, not just the visual encoder.
> - Real human motion, even kinematically imperfect and lacking contact physics, scales better than generated video with inverse-dynamics pseudo-actions. Grounded motion beats synthetic observations as a pretraining data source.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have become a major framework for robot manipulation because they combine visual perception, language conditioning, and action prediction in a single policy. Recent VLA research indicates that performance generally improves with more diverse pretraining data, but collecting large quantities of real-robot demonstrations is costly, slow, and constrained by the available robot platforms and laboratory environments.

Human videos provide a potentially scalable alternative. Egocentric datasets such as Ego4D, EPIC-Kitchens, EgoDex, EgoVerse, and Ego10K contain interactions with many objects, scenes, and manipulation strategies. However, human videos do not directly provide robot observations, robot states, or executable robot actions. The human hand, camera configuration, kinematics, and viewpoint differ from those of a robot. This creates an embodiment gap involving both perception and action.

Prior work has addressed this gap in several ways. One group of methods performs visual robotization by replacing human hands with rendered robot hands or by editing videos into robot-like observations. H2R and Masquerade, for example, use human videos primarily for visual representation learning or future end-effector prediction. A second group derives action supervision from reconstructed human hand motion while retaining human-domain observations, as in VITRA and EgoScale. A third group jointly converts human observations and actions into robot-aligned demonstrations, including Phantom, DexUMI, WARPED, RwoR, and MimicDreamer. These approaches have generally been evaluated in task-matched settings, where the source videos are closely related to the target robot task.

HuRo focuses on a different question: whether heterogeneous human videos can be converted into a common robot-aligned observation-action format and used as a scalable pretraining resource for VLA policies. The work therefore combines visual conversion and motion retargeting in one pipeline, constructs a large dataset, and evaluates how performance changes with the amount of robotized data.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to determine whether robotized human videos can provide useful supervision for downstream real-robot manipulation. The paper addresses three related questions.

First, the authors examine whether increasing the quantity of robotized human-video data produces predictable improvements in downstream performance. This tests whether human videos can function as a scalable pretraining source rather than merely as task-specific augmentation.

Second, the work investigates whether visual robotization is necessary when retargeted action labels are already available. Human-centric observations may contain useful object and motion information, but they can also cause a mismatch between pretraining and deployment domains. The authors compare robot-overlay observations with original human observations to measure the effect on in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization.

Third, the paper evaluates the importance of action supervision. Earlier robotized-video methods have often transferred visual representations without using retargeted actions in an end-to-end VLA objective. HuRo compares visual-only transfer with joint pretraining of the visual pathway and action head.

The broader motivation is to reduce dependence on expensive robot data while preserving robot-relevant supervision. The authors aim to transform existing human-video resources into data that more closely resembles the input and output domains of a robot policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Robotization Pipeline

HuRo converts egocentric human videos into episodes containing robotized RGB observations, robot states, robot action targets, and language instructions. The pipeline has three stages: human-video annotation, action conversion, and visual conversion.

##### 3.1.1. Human-video annotation

Input videos are standardized to 30 frames per second and rectified using estimated camera intrinsics. The pipeline uses DROIDCalib for camera self-calibration and AnyCalib as a fallback for nearly static clips. Hands are detected with 100DoH, and BOT-SORT maintains consistent left-right hand identities over time. HAWOR then estimates MANO-based 3D hand poses.

The method also estimates the camera trajectory. Masked DROID-SLAM is applied while excluding visible hands, reducing the influence of moving foreground regions. Because monocular SLAM does not provide metric scale directly, MoGe-2 is used for scale estimation, and GeoCalib aligns the recovered world frame with gravity.

Frames with valid hand annotations are grouped into manipulation segments and divided into bounded temporal chunks. For each chunk, projected wrist trajectories are drawn on sampled frames and supplied to Qwen3.5, which generates a natural-language instruction. A verification stage filters captions that are inconsistent with the video or do not describe meaningful hand-object manipulation.

##### 3.1.2. Action conversion

The recovered human hand motion is converted into target-robot motion. Fingertip positions and local hand-structure features are transformed from the source camera frame into a video world frame. Because this frame is not aligned with the robot base, the method estimates a chunk-level transformation consisting of a three-dimensional translation and a yaw rotation.

PyRoKi performs retargeting in two stages. First, the alignment transform and robot joint configurations are jointly optimized on sparsely sampled timesteps. The objective includes fingertip tracking, preservation of local hand structure, consistency between the robot’s camera and the estimated human egocentric camera, and kinematic regularization. Second, with the alignment fixed, the complete robot trajectory is optimized at every timestep, with an additional temporal-smoothness term.

The resulting joint configurations are converted into robot policy states. Since the original human videos do not contain robot actions, HuRo defines an action target from the subsequent state, effectively using the next robot state as the action label. Wrist actions are represented as relative translations and continuous six-dimensional rotations, while hand joints use absolute targets.

##### 3.1.3. Visual conversion

The visual stage removes the visible human embodiment and inserts the retargeted robot. SAM2 segments the human arms, with Detectron2 providing auxiliary person-region prompts. ProPainter inpaints the segmented regions to generate frames in which the human arms have been removed.

The target robot is rendered in Isaac Sim using the estimated camera intrinsics, the transformed egocentric camera trajectory, and the retargeted robot joint configurations. The rendered robot is composited onto the inpainted video. Each final episode therefore contains a robot-like observation sequence aligned with robot states and actions.

#### 3.2. Dataset and Policy

The main HuRo dataset targets ALLEX, a bimanual dexterous robot with two seven-degree-of-freedom arms, two 15-degree-of-freedom hands, a two-degree-of-freedom neck, and a two-degree-of-freedom waist. The source videos come from five datasets: EgoDex, EgoVerse, Ego4D, Ego10K, and EPIC-Kitchens.

The resulting collection contains approximately 630,000 episodes, 142 million processed frames, and 1,317 hours of video at 30 frames per second. EgoDex contributes approximately 55.5% of the frames, EgoVerse 26.5%, Ego4D 10.4%, Ego10K 6.0%, and EPIC-Kitchens 1.7%.

The VLA policy is based on GR00T-N1.6-3B. It receives a robotized image, a language instruction, and the current robot state. The action head predicts a 40-step action chunk using a flow-matching objective. The vision-language backbone is initialized from the released GR00T checkpoint, while the action head is initialized from scratch. Pretraining uses 80,000 optimization steps with a global batch size of 2,048. Downstream finetuning uses 30,000 steps and a batch size of 128.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Scaling With Robotized Data

The main result is a positive relationship between HuRo pretraining scale and downstream manipulation performance. Without HuRo pretraining, the average overall completion score is 51.5%. Using 10%, 50%, and 100% of the dataset raises the score to 68.2%, 74.0%, and 80.3%, respectively.

The improvement occurs under both evaluation regimes. In-distribution completion increases from 68.1% without HuRo pretraining to 88.4% with the full dataset. Out-of-distribution completion increases from 34.9% to 72.2%. The OOD conditions include changes in object position, scene layout, and visual appearance.

On the four-task ALLEX suite, the full HuRo model achieves 83.3% OOD completion for Apple Pick-and-Place, 79.2% for Cup Stacking, 86.1% for Cup-Noodle Handover, and 73.3% for Microwave Loading. These results are aggregate task scores based on binary or stage-wise completion criteria.

#### 4.2. Visual Robotization

The no-overlay variant uses the same retargeted action labels as HuRo but retains the original human-video observations. It achieves slightly higher ID performance than the full model, 89.4% versus 88.4%, but its OOD performance is lower, 55.7% versus 72.2%.

This comparison suggests that robot-like visual observations are particularly useful for generalization. The effect is not simply due to more data or action supervision, because both variants use the same pretraining data and retargeted actions. The robot overlay appears to reduce the visual mismatch between pretraining and deployment, especially under spatial and visual shifts.

#### 4.3. Retargeted Action Supervision

In a separate Diverse Pick-and-Place experiment, the authors compare no pretraining, visual-only pretraining, and joint visual-action pretraining. Visual-only transfer provides limited improvement, whereas end-to-end pretraining with retargeted actions reaches 61.1% ID completion and 50.0% OOD completion.

Qualitative examples show that action-supervised pretraining improves grasp selection. For instance, the full model more often grasps a scrub brush by its handle rather than from above. On unseen handled cups, it is also more likely to insert the fingers through the handle. These findings indicate that the action head learns manipulation-specific correspondences that are not captured by visual representation transfer alone.

#### 4.4. Comparisons With Other Data Sources

HuRo is compared with an image-to-video plus inverse-dynamics baseline, in which robot-like videos are generated and pseudo-actions are inferred. At matched pretraining budgets of 0.7, 3.5, and 7.0 million frames, HuRo performs better at each tested scale. HuRo using 0.7 million frames also exceeds the evaluated generated-video baseline using 7.0 million frames in both ID and OOD evaluation.

The paper also compares HuRo with human-domain action representations. Human-HRDT and Human-VITRA retain human observations and use reconstructed hand actions. On OOD Cup Stacking and Cup-Noodle Handover, the robotized observation-action model performs better than both human-domain variants. The human-domain models often reach the object but fail during stack alignment or bimanual handover.

Additional experiments show that HuRo pretraining can transfer to OpenArm, although the primary data were robotized for ALLEX. Joint pretraining on ALLEX and OpenArm robotizations improves Diverse Pick-and-Place completion from 86.1% to 91.7% in ID evaluation and from 63.9% to 77.8% in OOD evaluation.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

HuRo provides evidence that heterogeneous human videos can be transformed into robot-aligned VLA pretraining data at a scale beyond that of many prior robotized-video studies. Its contribution is not only the dataset size, but also the coupling of visual robotization, camera-motion-aware retargeting, language annotation, and action supervision within one format.

If the approach generalizes, it could reduce the amount of task-specific real-robot data needed for initial policy training and make pretraining less dependent on a single robot platform. The multi-embodiment experiments suggest that shared human-video annotations can be reused for different robot morphologies, with only robot-specific retargeting and rendering needing to be recomputed.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of the results. Robot overlays do not explicitly model occlusion with scene geometry, and inpainting or compositing artifacts may affect learning. The dataset contains kinematic and visual supervision but no force or tactile information, limiting its relevance to contact-rich tasks. Retargeting does not model self-collision or physical contact; only 55.2% of audited trajectories had no detected non-grasp self-contact. Consequently, the trajectories function primarily as pretraining labels rather than executable demonstrations. Finally, the real-world evaluation is concentrated on four tasks and primarily on the ALLEX embodiment. Further studies are needed to determine how robotization fidelity, source-video biases, contact modeling, and embodiment differences affect performance.
