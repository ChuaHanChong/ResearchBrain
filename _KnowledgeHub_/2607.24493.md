# KAI: A Kinematic-Aware Interface for Data-Efficient Articulated Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The Chinese University of Hong Kong and Shanghai AI Laboratory developed KAI, a Kinematic-Aware Interface that embeds explicit kinematic structure into robotic manipulation policies. This approach achieves higher success rates with less demonstration data and improves generalization to novel environments and objects, partly by leveraging human interaction videos for co-training.

## Problem

- End-to-end learning for articulated object manipulation requires extensive, costly robot demonstrations to implicitly infer kinematic structures.
- Existing methods exhibit brittle generalization to unseen backgrounds, visual distractors, or object variations due to reliance on superficial visual cues.
- Prior intermediate representations often describe where or what to interact, but lack explicit encoding of the fundamental kinematic constraints dictating how an articulated object moves.

## Method

- Introduced the ==Kinematic-Aware Articulation Interface (**KAI**)==, which predicts future locations and displacements of interaction keypoints on an object's moving part, unifying pose and motion.
- Applied a ==geometric loss== (LgeoL_{geo}Lgeo) consisting of ==structural and motion-specific terms== (for prismatic or revolute joints) to enforce physical kinematic consistency on predicted keypoint trajectories.
- Implemented a ==\"Perception–KAI–Action\" phased-reasoning transformer architecture==, where **KAI** tokens attend only to perceptual inputs, enabling ==action-agnostic supervision== and ==co-training== with human interaction videos lacking robot actions.

## Results

- KAI achieved an average success rate of **82.9%** across six simulation tasks, outperforming all baselines, and demonstrated robust generalization to unseen backgrounds, lighting, and object instances.
- Showcased enhanced data efficiency, matching or surpassing the best baseline's performance with half the training data and achieving a **22 percentage point** improvement in low-data regimes (**100** demonstrations).
- Improved real-world robustness to visual distractions and unseen object instances, with human video co-training providing up to **+13.4%** success rate gains in challenging cluttered environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly integrating kinematic structure via KAI provides a strong inductive bias, significantly reducing the data required for learning robust articulated object manipulation policies.
> - Decoupling kinematic understanding from action generation through a phased-reasoning architecture and action-agnostic KAI design enables effective transfer of knowledge from heterogeneous data sources like human videos.
> - Enforcing geometric consistency on predicted keypoint trajectories through structural and motion-specific losses is crucial for robust, physically plausible, and generalizable articulated object manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of articulated objects (e.g., doors, drawers, laptops) is a fundamental capability for robots operating in human environments. Such objects are characterized by specific kinematic constraints governing their motion. The broader research landscape in articulated object manipulation generally follows two main approaches: prediction-planning methods and end-to-end learning-based methods.

Prediction-planning methods explicitly estimate object properties, such as joint parameters, from sensory observations and then use these estimates to plan actions. These methods often require specialized object models or estimation algorithms, which can be robust but may struggle with generalization to novel objects or complex visual scenes without specific prior knowledge.

In contrast, end-to-end learning-based methods train policies to map raw observations directly to robot actions. While offering flexibility and demonstrating generalization potential in some cases, these approaches typically do not explicitly model the underlying kinematic structure of articulated objects. This implicit learning of kinematics from data alone places a substantial demand on the volume of training demonstrations, leading to high data collection costs. Furthermore, policies learned in this manner can rely on superficial visual cues, potentially resulting in brittle generalization when environmental conditions or object appearances vary from the training data.

To address the data efficiency challenges of end-to-end methods, prior research has introduced various structured intermediate representations. These representations aim to decompose the manipulation problem by predicting task-relevant properties from observations. Examples include affordance maps (indicating where to interact), point flow (predicting desired motion patterns), and grasp pose estimations (specifying contact points for grasping). While these representations provide some structure to the learning problem, they often describe *where* to interact or *what* motion should occur, rather than directly encoding the *kinematic structure* that dictates *how* the object moves.

The KAI work positions itself by proposing an intermediate representation that explicitly captures this kinematic structure. By providing an explicit geometric and kinematic prior, KAI aims to offer a stronger inductive bias for learning articulated object manipulation policies. This approach is intended to combine the generalization benefits of end-to-end learning with the data efficiency and robustness typically associated with methods that incorporate explicit structural understanding. The work specifically builds upon advancements in visual feature extraction (e.g., DINOv2, CLIP) and transformer-based policy architectures (e.g., GPT-2 inspired backbones) to integrate this kinematic-aware representation into a modern imitation learning framework. Furthermore, its action-agnostic design for the kinematic representation allows for leveraging heterogeneous data sources, such as human interaction videos, which is an emerging direction for improving real-world robot robustness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a data-efficient and robust method for articulated object manipulation by explicitly integrating kinematic understanding into policy learning. The authors identify several key motivations for this objective:

1. **Addressing Data Inefficiency in End-to-End Learning:** Traditional end-to-end imitation learning policies, which learn direct perception-to-action mappings, often require extensive and costly robot demonstrations to infer the kinematic structure of articulated objects implicitly. Without explicit kinematic priors, these models may struggle to generalize robustly under limited data, relying instead on superficial visual cues that do not transfer well across varying environments or object appearances. The motivation is to reduce this data burden.

2. **Improving Generalization to Unseen Conditions:** Existing methods often exhibit brittle generalization when exposed to novel backgrounds, visual distractors, or variations in object appearance that were not present in the training data. This limitation stems from policies learning correlations that are specific to the training environment rather than fundamental properties of the object's motion. The objective is to enhance generalization by embedding a more fundamental, structural understanding of articulated motion.

3. **Enhancing Inductive Bias:** The authors suggest that existing intermediate representations (e.g., affordance maps, point flow, grasp poses) primarily describe *what* or *where* to interact. They argue that a more effective inductive bias for articulated manipulation should explicitly capture the *kinematic constraints*—that is, *how* an articulated joint moves. The motivation is to design a representation that is inherently aligned with the physical mechanics of articulated objects.

4. **Facilitating Co-training with Heterogeneous Data:** Robot demonstration data is expensive and time-consuming to collect. Human interaction videos, while abundant, typically lack robot action labels, making direct co-training challenging for action-centric policies. A key motivation is to design an intermediate representation that is "action-agnostic," allowing supervision from diverse data sources, including human interaction videos, to further enhance robustness and leverage a broader spectrum of available data.

In summary, the research aims to overcome the limitations of data hunger and poor generalization in articulated object manipulation by introducing a Kinematic-Aware Articulation Interface (KAI). This interface is designed to provide a strong, interpretable inductive bias by explicitly encoding the geometric and kinematic structure of articulated objects, thereby improving sample efficiency, generalization, and the ability to learn from varied data sources.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The KAI methodology integrates a structured intermediate representation, termed the Kinematic-Aware Articulation Interface (KAI), into an end-to-end policy learning pipeline. This integration is designed to explicitly model the kinematic structure of articulated objects.

#### 3.1. Kinematic-Aware Articulation Interface (KAI) Design

KAI represents the kinematic structure of an articulated object by predicting the state and motion of *K* interaction keypoints located on the object's moving part. For each keypoint *i* at timestep *t*, KAI predicts a sequence of future locations ($l^i_{t+\delta+1}$) and corresponding displacements ($d^i_{t+\delta}$) over a horizon of *N* timesteps. The displacements are defined as the difference between consecutive keypoint locations ($d^i_{t+\delta} = l^i_{t+\delta+1} - l^i_{t+\delta}$). This approach couples the part's pose with its motion pattern into a unified representation.

To ensure that the predicted keypoints adhere to physical kinematic constraints, a geometric loss ($L_{geo}$) is applied:
$L_{geo} = \lambda_s L_{struct} + \lambda_m L_{motion}$

- **Structural Loss ($L_{struct}$):** This term enforces isometric constraints, penalizing changes in pairwise distances between keypoints over time. This prevents the object's shape from deforming non-physically during interaction, thereby preserving its rigidity.
 $L_{struct} = \sum_{\delta=0}^{N-1} \sum_{i,j \in K} \left\| \left\| l^i_{t+\delta+1} - l^j_{t+\delta+1} \right\|^2 - \left\| l^i_t - l^j_t \right\|^2 \right\|^2_2$

- **Motion Loss ($L_{motion}$):** This term is specialized based on the type of articulated joint (prismatic or revolute).
 - **Prismatic Joints ($L_{pris\_motion}$):** For translational motion, this loss minimizes the variance of displacement vectors across all keypoints. This ensures that all points on the moving part translate coherently along a single joint axis.
 $L_{pris\_motion} = \sum_{\delta=0}^{N-1} \sum_{i \in K} \left\| d^i_{t+\delta} - \bar{d}_{t+\delta} \right\|^2_2$, where $\bar{d}_{t+\delta} = \frac{1}{|K|} \sum_{j \in K} d^j_{t+\delta}$
 - **Revolute Joints ($L_{rev\_motion}$):** For rotational motion, a subset of keypoints ($K_a$) is designated as stationary anchors on the rotation axis, while others ($K_m$) are moving points. The loss penalizes any displacement of anchor points and enforces that the orthogonal projection of each moving point onto the rotation axis remains constant over time.
 $L_{rev\_motion} = \sum_{\delta=0}^{N-1} \left( \sum_{a \in K_a} \left\| d^a_{t+\delta} \right\|^2_2 + \sum_{i \in K_m} \left\| \text{proj}(l^i_{t+\delta+1}) - \text{proj}(l^i_{t+\delta}) \right\|^2_2 \right)$

#### 3.2. Model Architecture

The model uses a "Perception–KAI–Action" phased-reasoning architecture, built on a 24-layer GPT-2 transformer backbone.
- **Perception Module:** Takes RGB-D images, robot proprioceptive state, and natural language instructions as input.
 - **Vision:** Employs DINOv2 for 2D semantic features, lifted to 3D via point cloud projection. A sparse 3D encoder processes the point cloud for geometric features. These are fused by a spatial aligner into visual tokens.
 - **Language:** Uses a CLIP text encoder to process instructions into embeddings.
 - **Robot State:** Encodes joint positions (or end-effector pose) and gripper state via MLPs.
 - All perceptual inputs are integrated into a unified set of visual, language, and robot state tokens.
- **Phased-Reasoning Architecture:** This design enforces a specific information flow using unidirectional attention masks.
 - KAI tokens attend exclusively to the perceptual tokens (vision, language, robot state) to predict keypoint locations and motion displacements, without access to action-level information. This separation ensures that KAI focuses purely on structural understanding.
 - The action decoder then generates robot actions, conditioned on both the original perceptual tokens and the predicted KAI tokens. This structure decouples structural understanding from control, which is essential for co-training with action-agnostic data.
- **Decoder:** Separate branches for KAI and action prediction. The KAI decoder predicts future keypoint locations and displacements. The action decoder predicts arm actions (continuous motion, Smooth L1 loss) and gripper actions (discrete command, Binary Cross Entropy loss).

#### 3.3. Co-Training Strategy

KAI's action-agnostic design allows it to be supervised by human interaction videos (e.g., HOI4D dataset) that lack robot action labels.
- **KAI Supervision:** KAI predictions (keypoint locations and displacements) are supervised directly from bounding box annotations of articulated parts in human videos. The KAI loss ($L_{KAI}$) consists of three terms:
 - Location Loss ($L_{location}$): Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth keypoint locations.
 - Motion Loss ($L_{motion}$): MSE between predicted and ground-truth displacement sequences.
 - Geometric Loss ($L_{geo}$): The regularization term described above, ensuring kinematic consistency.
 - $L_{KAI} = \alpha_1 L_{location} + \alpha_2 L_{motion} + \alpha_3 L_{geo}$
- **Action Supervision:** For samples from the robot action dataset, an additional action loss ($L_{action}$) is applied, based on the predicted KAI representation and perceptual tokens.
- **Overall Training Objective:** Mini-batches mix samples from both human video and robot action datasets. The total loss is:
 $L = \lambda_1 L_{KAI} + \lambda_2 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{action} \cdot L_{action}$
 where $\mathbf{1}_{action}$ is an indicator function, enabling action loss only for robot action data.

This co-training strategy leverages readily available human interaction data to enhance the model's understanding of articulated object kinematics, thereby improving robustness and generalization without requiring additional robot demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, evaluating KAI's performance in terms of generalization, data efficiency, and the contribution of its core components.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted on six tasks (opening/closing a laptop, door, drawer) in Isaac Sim, using 800 expert demonstrations per task, with evaluation across 200 unique initial states, 8 unseen backgrounds, and lighting conditions. Baselines included ACT, Seer, DP3, RISE-2, and ArticuBot.

- **Generalization Performance:** KAI achieved an average success rate of 82.9% across the six tasks, surpassing all baselines. For example, in "Close Laptop," KAI achieved 100% success, while the next best (ACT) was 93.5%. The performance advantage was consistent, validating the effectiveness of KAI's structured intermediate representation.
- **Impact of Video Co-training in Simulation:** Co-training with human interaction videos (HOI4D dataset) yielded a modest improvement in simulation, increasing the average success rate from 82.9% to 84.6%. The authors attributed this limited gain to a perceptual domain gap between the HOI4D videos and the synthetic images, which was expected to be less pronounced in real-world scenarios.
- **Data Efficiency Analysis:** KAI demonstrated enhanced sample efficiency. When trained with only half the demonstration data (400 episodes), KAI (75.9% SR) matched or surpassed the performance of the strongest baseline (Seer, 73.4% SR) trained with the full 800 episodes. In low-data regimes (100 demonstrations), KAI achieved 42.4% SR, outperforming the best baseline (Seer, 20.2% SR) by 22 percentage points. This indicates that KAI's kinematic priors allow it to learn effective policies from substantially fewer examples.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Component Contributions:** Removing KAI entirely resulted in a significant drop to 44.1% average success rate, highlighting the critical role of the structured kinematic representation. Adding KAI tokens alone increased the success rate to 75.0%. Incorporating the geometric consistency loss ($L_{geo}$) further improved performance to 82.9%, demonstrating its regularization effect on keypoint predictions.
 - **Architectural Design Choices:** Replacing KAI with a future depth prediction task (Ours w/ Pred. Depth) resulted in a much lower 49.6% success rate, indicating that generic visual prediction is less effective than structured kinematic reasoning. Removing the causal attention mask, which allows bidirectional attention between KAI and action tokens (Ours w/o Causal Attn.), reduced the average success rate to 78.5%, supporting the phased-reasoning architecture where kinematic inference precedes action generation.

#### 4.2. Real-world Experiments

Real-world evaluations were conducted on a Franka Research 3 arm for three tasks (opening drawer, closing laptop, closing microwave), with 100 demonstrations per task in a single clean setting. Evaluation focused on generalization to unseen backgrounds, object distractors, and unseen object instances, measured by Success Rate (SR) and Localization Success Rate (Loc-SR).

- **Robustness to Visual Distractions:**
 - Without distractions, KAI (66.7% SR / 80.0% Loc-SR) substantially outperformed baselines (e.g., Seer: 37.8% SR / 64.4% Loc-SR).
 - Under background distractions, KAI maintained 62.2% SR / 77.8% Loc-SR, showing only a minor drop from the no-distraction setting. Baselines saw larger declines (e.g., Seer: 26.7% SR / 51.1% Loc-SR).
 - Under object distractors, KAI again performed robustly with 62.2% SR / 71.1% Loc-SR, while baselines experienced more significant performance degradation.
 - This stability indicates that KAI's kinematic priors reduce reliance on superficial visual features, leading to more robust behavior under distribution shifts.
- **Enhanced Impact of Video Co-training in Real World:** In the real world, video co-training provided more substantial gains than in simulation. Average SR increased by +6.6% (no distractions), +11.1% (background distractions), and +13.4% (object distractors). This suggests that the shared camera characteristics and scene statistics between human videos and robot observations in the real world enable more effective transfer of kinematic knowledge. The increasing gains with more severe distractions imply that diverse human interaction scenes assist in localizing contact points under challenging visual conditions.
- **Generalization to Unseen Object Instances:** KAI demonstrated superior zero-shot transfer to unseen laptop instances in simulation (38.0% SR vs. Seer's 18.2% SR) and real-world (66.7% SR / 73.3% Loc-SR vs. baselines at 46.7% SR / 53.3% Loc-SR). With video co-training, real-world unseen instance generalization further improved to 80.0% SR / 93.3% Loc-SR. This supports the notion that KAI learns transferable kinematic principles rather than object-specific geometries.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the Kinematic-Aware Articulation Interface (KAI) presents several significant advancements in robotic manipulation and holds potential for broader impact.

**Improved Data Efficiency:** KAI's primary contribution is its ability to achieve high performance in articulated object manipulation with significantly less demonstration data. By embedding explicit geometric and kinematic priors, the method provides a strong inductive bias that guides policy learning more effectively, particularly in low-data regimes. This is crucial for practical robotic applications where collecting large, high-quality demonstration datasets is often expensive and time-consuming. Reducing the data requirement lowers the barrier to deploying manipulation skills in new environments and for novel tasks.

**Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The explicit encoding of kinematic structure allows KAI to learn more fundamental and generalizable representations of articulated motion. This enables robust transfer from a single, clean training environment to diverse, unseen conditions, including varying backgrounds and visual distractors in both simulation and the real world. Policies developed with KAI exhibit less reliance on superficial visual cues, leading to more stable performance under distribution shifts. This robustness is vital for robots operating in dynamic and unstructured human environments.

**Leveraging Heterogeneous Data Sources:** KAI's action-agnostic design for representing object kinematics is significant because it allows for effective co-training with human interaction videos, which typically lack robot action labels. This ability to integrate abundant, low-cost human video data complements robot demonstrations, further enhancing real-world robustness, especially under challenging visual distractions. This approach opens avenues for leveraging large repositories of human interaction data, accelerating the development of capable robotic systems without exclusively relying on costly robot-specific data collection.

**Foundational for Future Robotics Research:** The work establishes a precedent for explicitly integrating structured physical knowledge into deep learning policies for robotics. This approach could be extended to other domains where physical constraints are critical, such as deformable object manipulation or complex assembly tasks. The concept of an interpretable, intermediate representation that decouples structural understanding from control offers a framework for more explainable and debuggable robotic learning systems.

**Potential for Practical Applications:** The demonstrated capability to manipulate common articulated objects like doors, drawers, and laptops robustly and efficiently suggests potential for direct application in service robotics, logistics, and manufacturing. Robots with this capability could perform a wider range of tasks in domestic settings, warehouses, or factories, leading to increased automation and efficiency.

**Future Directions (as indicated by limitations):** The authors note that the current evaluation focuses on short-horizon manipulation tasks and a single robot platform. Extending KAI to long-horizon, sequential tasks and validating its generality across different robot embodiments are natural next steps. Addressing these limitations could further broaden the impact and applicability of the kinematic-aware interface in more complex and diverse robotic systems.
