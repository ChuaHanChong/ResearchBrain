# Realizing Robotic Swimming with Unified Fluid-Robot Multiphysics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University and MIT developed a unified, differentiable framework for strongly coupled fluid-robot multiphysics simulation using variational mechanics and an integral-form immersed boundary method. This framework demonstrated significantly higher fidelity (e.g., ~75% error reduction for dynamic gaits) compared to particle-based baselines, successfully optimizing and transferring C-start maneuvers for an eel robot from simulation to hardware.

## Problem

- Existing multiphysics simulators for robotics struggle to accurately represent complex fluid dynamics, such as incompressibility and vortex interactions, at higher Reynolds numbers critical for dynamic swimming.
- Many fluid-structure interaction (FSI) methods in robotics are weakly coupled, leading to numerical instability, or are limited to single-body systems, making them unsuitable for articulated multibody robots.
- Traditional immersed boundary methods (IBM) face challenges like fluid penetration or numerical ill-conditioning when applied to articulated multibody robotic systems in close proximity.
- The lack of differentiability in most multiphysics simulators hinders efficient gradient-based optimization for designing and learning complex robotic control policies and gaits.

## Method

- A unified mathematical framework was developed, deriving coupled incompressible Navier-Stokes and manipulator equations from a single Lagrangian using the principle of least action, intrinsically linking fluid and robot dynamics.
- ==Discrete variational mechanics== was employed to discretize the unified action, leading to a stable, well-conditioned, implicit time-integration scheme for joint simulation of articulated bodies and fluid.
- An integral "weak-form" formulation of the ==immersed boundary method== was introduced, which robustly handles articulated multibody systems by enforcing no-slip conditions across entire solid-body boundaries.
- The simulator's differentiability was enabled using the ==implicit function theorem==, allowing for efficient gradient-based optimization of robot control parameters for tasks like gait design.

## Results

- The unified multiphysics method achieved significantly lower root-mean-square error (RMSE) in tracking the head link configuration for steady undulatory swimming, demonstrating approximately a **75% error reduction** at higher amplitudes compared to an SPH baseline.
- The differentiable simulator successfully optimized a highly dynamic C-start escape maneuver for a physical eel robot, enabling the transfer of optimized gait parameters from simulation to hardware to achieve a **90° turn**.
- The proposed method offered higher fidelity for dynamic swimming gaits and demonstrated computational efficiency advantages over an SPH baseline, particularly on CPU architectures for the presented tasks (e.g., **0.87 seconds/timestep** vs. **9.09 seconds/timestep** for SPH on CPU).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Deriving fluid-robot dynamics from a unified Lagrangian using the principle of least action ensures a physically consistent and stable strong coupling between the two domains.
> - Discrete variational integrators provide a robust and accurate method for discretizing complex multiphysics systems, maintaining physical properties and yielding stable implicit time-integration.
> - An integral weak-formulation of the immersed boundary method enhances its robustness and accuracy for articulated multibody systems by preventing fluid penetration and addressing singularities at overlapping nodes.
> - The differentiability of the tightly coupled fluid-robot system is crucial for enabling efficient gradient-based optimization and learning of complex bio-inspired gaits, facilitating sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges within the field of robotics, specifically concerning the simulation and control of robots operating in complex multiphysics environments. This area has seen increasing interest due to applications such as deformable-object manipulation, fluid transport, and locomotion. A significant hurdle in these fields is the difficulty of real-world data collection and training, which has consequently driven the development of multiphysics simulation platforms.

Within this broader context, the paper specifically focuses on bio-inspired locomotion for underwater vehicles. Researchers have long been interested in emulating the swimming efficiency and agility of biological organisms, attributing their performance to intricate vortex interactions between the body and the fluid. While various bio-inspired hardware designs have been developed, autonomous operation matching the capabilities of their biological counterparts remains largely unrealized. This gap is attributed to the inherent difficulty in accurately modeling and simulating systems where both whole-body robot dynamics and complex fluid-structure interaction (FSI) are critical.

Existing multiphysics simulators for robotics often employ particle-based computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methods (e.g., Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics - SPH), which are suitable for graphics and fluid transport tasks. However, these methods frequently struggle with representing complex boundary conditions, maintaining fluid incompressibility and volume conservation, and accurately modeling fluid as a continuum, particularly in higher-Reynolds-number regimes relevant to dynamic swimming. These limitations can lead to unstable simulations and significant discrepancies between simulated and real-world behavior (sim-to-real gap).

Conversely, traditional FSI methods in CFD are often tailored for single-body systems or rely on simplified fluid dynamics (e.g., potential or Stokes flow for low Reynolds numbers). Eulerian methods capable of handling articulated multibody systems commonly found in robotics are not fully developed. While commercial solvers like COMSOL offer strongly coupled, Eulerian-based FSI via the Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) method, they typically involve computationally expensive remeshing, which struggles with large displacements. Recent differentiable incompressible Navier-Stokes (INS) based simulators (e.g., Aquarium) for robotics have been developed but employ a "weakly" coupled approach, relying on separate dynamics solvers for the robot, which can lead to instability. Furthermore, classical immersed boundary methods (IBM) used in these approaches are often optimized for single-body systems and can face issues like singularities when multiple bodies move in close proximity.

This research aims to bridge these gaps by introducing a unified, differentiable framework for strongly coupled fluid-robot multiphysics. It seeks to provide a more accurate and stable simulation environment for articulated multibody robots in dynamic fluid environments, thereby facilitating the development of autonomous and high-performance underwater robotic systems through gradient-based optimization and learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the capability of realizing robotic swimming that can approach the efficiency and agility observed in fish. This overarching goal is motivated by the persistent challenge of accurately simulating the complex, coupled dynamics between a robot's body and the surrounding fluid, which involves intricate vortex interactions. These dynamics are governed by a combination of ordinary and partial differential equations, making their simulation substantially more complex than that of classical rigid robotic systems.

The core motivation for this work stems from identified limitations in existing multiphysics simulation platforms, particularly in the context of bio-inspired underwater locomotion:
- **Inadequate Fluid Dynamics Representation:** Current robotics simulators often struggle to accurately capture complex fluid phenomena such as incompressibility, volume conservation, and the formation and shedding of vortices, especially in dynamic, higher-Reynolds-number flows critical for efficient swimming. Particle-based methods frequently introduce artificial damping and parameter sensitivity, contributing to unstable simulations and poor sim-to-real transfer.
- **Weak Coupling and Instability:** Many existing approaches to fluid-structure interaction in robotics are "weakly" coupled, where fluid and robot dynamics are solved separately and interact through explicit force terms. This can lead to numerical instability and inaccuracies, particularly in scenarios with strong reciprocal interactions between the fluid and the robot.
- **Challenges with Multibody Articulated Systems:** Traditional FSI methods and immersed boundary methods are often designed for single, rigid bodies. Adapting them to articulated multibody robotic systems introduces complexities such as fluid penetration at coarse discretizations or numerical ill-conditioning and singularities when robot segments move close together.
- **Lack of Differentiability for Optimization:** For designing and optimizing complex robot gaits and control policies, differentiable simulators are essential as they enable efficient gradient-based learning. Many existing multiphysics simulators lack this capability for tightly coupled fluid-robot systems.

To address these motivations, the research sets forth several specific objectives:
1. **Unified Multiphysics Formulation:** To develop a unified mathematical framework for strongly coupled fluid-robot dynamics by deriving the coupled incompressible Navier-Stokes and manipulator equations from a single Lagrangian using the principle of least action. This approach aims to intrinsically link the physics as a single optimization problem.
2. **Stable and Accurate Simulation Scheme:** To employ discrete variational mechanics to discretize the unified action directly, thereby developing a stable, well-conditioned, and physically accurate implicit time-integration scheme for joint simulation of articulated bodies and fluid.
3. **Robust Immersed Boundary Method:** To extend the immersed boundary method to a new integral "weak-form" formulation that robustly handles articulated multibody robotic systems, preventing fluid penetration and addressing singularities that can arise from overlapping boundary nodes.
4. **Differentiable Dynamics:** To enable the computation of derivatives of the fully coupled dynamics using the implicit function theorem, thereby supporting gradient-based optimization and learning tasks for robotic control.
5. **Hardware Validation and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To validate the proposed simulator and its gradients by optimizing and realizing bio-inspired swimming gaits (e.g., undulatory swimming and C-start escape maneuvers) on a physical eel robot and demonstrating successful transfer of these optimized gaits from simulation to real-world hardware.
6. **Benchmarking against Baselines:** To benchmark the performance and fidelity of the unified multiphysics approach against state-of-the-art particle-based robotics simulators to quantitatively demonstrate its advantages.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper centers on a unified, differentiable framework for simulating strongly coupled fluid-robot multiphysics, leveraging variational mechanics and an enhanced immersed boundary method.

**A. Fluid-Robot Multiphysics as Optimization:**
The foundation of the approach is the formulation of the combined fluid-robot system as a single continuous-time optimization problem, based on the principle of least action.
- **Unified Lagrangian:** The system's dynamics are derived from a single Lagrangian, which combines the kinetic and potential energies of both the fluid and the rigid multibody robot.
- **Constraints:** The formulation includes several constraints:
 - Kinematics for fluid velocity field ($\dot{q}_f = v_f$).
 - Conservation of mass for incompressible fluid ($\nabla \cdot v_f = 0$).
 - Boundary conditions for the fluid domain ($v_f = v_{bc}$).
 - Kinematics for robot bodies ($\dot{q}_r = v_r$).
 - Internal robot constraints (e.g., joint limits).
 - Crucially, the no-slip boundary condition at the fluid-robot interface ($v_f = {}^b v_r$) is treated as a coupling constraint.
- **Euler-Lagrange Equations:** Applying variational calculus to this unified action principle yields the coupled incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for the fluid and manipulator equations for the robot. The coupling forces, including fluidic pressure and forces arising from the no-slip condition, naturally emerge as dual variables associated with their respective constraints. Viscous damping is incorporated as an external force term in the action.

**B. Coupled Discretization of the Fluid-Robot Multiphysics:**
To simulate the system, the continuous-time action integral is discretized using discrete variational mechanics, leading to an implicit time-integration scheme.
- **Spatial Discretization:** The fluid domain is spatially discretized using a second-order finite-volume method to obtain discrete operators for mass matrix, gradient, convective term, and Laplacian.
- **Temporal Discretization:** A discrete action is formulated, which incorporates discrete Lagrangians for both fluid and robot, as well as external forces. This formulation addresses the temporal inconsistency typically found in variational integrators between configurations (q_k) and velocities (v_k) by defining constraints at their respective time steps.
- **Discrete Euler-Lagrange Equations:** The first-order necessary conditions of this discretized action yield a system of coupled equations that represent the discrete dynamics for both the robot and the fluid, along with their associated constraints (incompressibility, boundary conditions, no-slip). This system can be solved iteratively using Newton's method to find the next state (configurations, velocities, and dual variables) given the current state. The authors note that this integration scheme is equivalent to a second-order implicit leap-frog method.

**C. An Integral-Form Immersed Boundary Method (IBM):**
The classical immersed boundary method (IBM) is extended to provide a more robust and physically accurate coupling between the Eulerian fluid grid and Lagrangian robot geometry, especially for multibody systems.
- **Weak Formulation:** Instead of enforcing the no-slip condition at discrete boundary nodes (which can lead to fluid penetration or singularities at overlapping nodes), the constraint is formulated as an integral over the solid-body boundary. This ensures that the no-slip condition is satisfied across the entire boundary, not just at specific points.
- **Integrated Convolution Matrix:** Discretization of this integral-form IBM results in a new integrated convolution matrix ($\bar{E}$), which maps fluid velocities to those at the robot boundary. This formulation improves robustness and compatibility with multibody representations.
- **Implicit Forcing:** Unlike classical IBMs that explicitly define an inverse mapping for fluid forcing, here the corresponding forces arise implicitly from differentiating through this integral constraint within the variational framework.

**D. Optimization With Fluid-Robot-Multiphysics Gradients:**
The differentiability of the unified fluid-robot multiphysics simulator is leveraged for gradient-based optimization.
- **Objective Function:** A general objective function J(x_1:N) is defined, dependent on the full fluid-robot state over a trajectory.
- **Implicit Dynamics:** The simulation dynamics are treated as implicit constraints f(x_k+1, x_k; β) = 0, where β are the optimization parameters (e.g., gait parameters).
- **Gradient Computation:** The objective gradient ∇_β J is computed via the chain rule, where the sensitivities $\frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \beta}$ and $\frac{\partial x_{k+1}}{\partial x_k}$ are obtained by applying the implicit function theorem to the entire multiphysics problem. This ensures that the gradients account for the intricate sensitivity between robot and fluid states.

**E. Experimental Setup and Validation:**
- **Hardware:** A bio-inspired eel robot, composed of 6 rigid, thin-plate segments connected by revolute joints driven by servo motors, was used. The robot is untethered with an onboard microcontroller and battery. It operates in a 122 x 122 cm walled water pool, mimicking the simulation environment. A top-down camera tracks the head link's configuration using AprilTags.
- **Simulation Environment:** The fluid domain is discretized into 1 cm fluid cells. A smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) baseline implemented in Genesis, a state-of-the-art robotics multiphysics simulator, was used for comparison, maintaining consistent fidelity.
- **Validation Tasks:**
 - **Steady Undulatory Swimming:** The robot's joints were actuated with a sinusoidal profile (1 Hz, wavelength equal to robot body length) at varying amplitudes (10°, 20°, 30°, 40°). Performance was evaluated by the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the head link configuration over 4-second trajectories across 20 trials.
 - **C-start Escape Maneuver Optimization:** A highly dynamic C-start maneuver, involving an initial bending phase followed by a propulsion phase, was optimized to achieve a 90° turn from rest. The objective function was a quadratic cost on the robot's final center-of-mass configuration. Optimization parameters included joint bending angles during the bend phase, the initial phase, and the wavelength of the undulating wave. The L-BFGS algorithm was used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's results are presented through validation studies comparing the proposed unified fluid-robot multiphysics framework with a smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) baseline and real-world hardware experiments.

**A. Steady Undulatory Swimming:**
- **Qualitative Observation:** For an undulatory gait with a 40° joint-angle amplitude, the unified multiphysics approach demonstrated forward-swimming motion that qualitatively aligned with the hardware observations. In contrast, the SPH baseline achieved minimal forward progress.
- **Quantitative Performance (RMSE):** The unified multiphysics method consistently achieved substantially lower root-mean-square error (RMSE) in tracking the head link configuration compared to the SPH baseline across all tested joint-angle amplitudes (10°, 20°, 30°, 40°).
 - The improvement in accuracy became more pronounced at higher amplitudes. For the 40° amplitude case, the unified method achieved an error reduction of approximately 75% compared to the baseline, particularly in the forward-swimming direction (y-axis).
 - While the SPH baseline's RMSE increased with amplitude, the unified method maintained a more consistent, slightly decreasing trend.
- **Conclusion:** These results indicate that the unified fluid-robot multiphysics framework offers significantly higher fidelity for robotic swimming simulations, especially for more dynamic gaits, compared to the particle-based SPH baseline. The authors attribute the SPH baseline's limitations to its inability to adequately satisfy physical constraints such as incompressibility and volume conservation.

**B. Optimizing a Highly Dynamic C-Start Maneuver:**
- **Optimization Success:** The differentiable nature of the unified multiphysics framework enabled the successful optimization of a highly dynamic C-start escape maneuver. The optimization process, using the L-BFGS algorithm, yielded specific gait parameters (joint bending angles, initial phase, and wavelength) designed to achieve a 90° turn.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The gait parameters obtained from simulation optimization were successfully executed on the physical eel robot hardware, realizing the desired 90° turn.
- **Sim-to-Real Discrepancies:** While the qualitative turning behavior transferred well, the simulation consistently undershot the actual travel distance observed in the real world. This discrepancy is attributed to potential factors such as unmodeled free-surface dynamics, servo compliance, approximations in thin-plate geometry, and uncertainties in physical parameters like robot mass properties and fluid viscosity.
- **Baseline Failure:** The SPH baseline simulation, when rolled out with the same optimized control trajectory, failed to realize both the appropriate turning and the desired propulsion for the C-start maneuver, further demonstrating its unsuitability for highly dynamic robot-swimming tasks.

**C. Computational Cost:**
- A 3-second C-start simulation at a 100 Hz sample rate within the unified multiphysics framework required approximately 261 seconds (0.87 seconds per timestep) on a CPU (M3-Ultra Mac Studio).
- In comparison, the SPH baseline implemented in Genesis required significantly more time: approximately 2727 seconds (9.09 seconds per timestep) on the same M3-Ultra CPU, and 300 seconds (1 second per timestep) on an NVIDIA RTX 3070-Ti GPU.
- These figures indicate that while the proposed method involves implicit time integration and is computationally intensive, it can offer computational efficiency advantages over certain particle-based methods, particularly on CPU architectures for the presented tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper offers several significant contributions and has potential impacts across multiple domains within robotics and simulation.

**A. Advancing Fluid-Robot Multiphysics Simulation:**
The development of a unified framework for deriving and simulating strongly coupled fluid-robot multiphysics, based on the principle of least action and discrete variational mechanics, represents a notable advancement. This approach inherently ensures a consistent and physically accurate coupling between the fluid and robot dynamics, addressing a long-standing challenge in the field. The introduction of an integral-form immersed boundary method also improves the robustness and accuracy of handling articulated multibody systems, which is crucial for complex robotic designs.

**B. Enabling Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
By demonstrating significantly higher fidelity compared to weakly coupled and particle-based baselines, particularly for dynamic swimming gaits, the proposed simulator has the potential to dramatically improve sim-to-real transfer. This reduction in the gap between simulated and physical performance is critical for the development and deployment of autonomous underwater vehicles and other robots operating in fluid environments, allowing control policies to be designed and refined in simulation with greater confidence in their real-world applicability.

**C. Facilitating Gradient-Based Optimization and Learning:**
The differentiability of the unified multiphysics simulator is a key enabling factor. It allows for the efficient application of gradient-based optimization algorithms to design and learn complex, bio-inspired control policies and gaits. This capability can accelerate the research and development cycle for robotic systems, moving beyond trial-and-error or heuristic approaches to discover optimal or near-optimal behaviors for agility, efficiency, and robustness in fluidic environments. The successful optimization of a highly dynamic C-start maneuver on an eel robot provides empirical evidence of this impact.

**D. Foundation for Bio-inspired Robotics:**
This framework provides a robust and accurate tool for researchers working on bio-inspired underwater robotics. The ability to precisely model and optimize intricate fluid-structure interactions, including vortex shedding and highly transient maneuvers, can lead to breakthroughs in designing robots that replicate or even surpass the performance of biological swimmers in terms of propulsion efficiency, maneuverability, and stealth. This could have implications for marine exploration, environmental monitoring, and underwater inspection tasks.

**E. Broader Applicability and Future Research:**
While focused on robotic swimming, the underlying principles of unified Lagrangian derivation, discrete variational integrators, and enhanced immersed boundary methods are generalizable. The framework could potentially be extended to address other challenging multiphysics problems in robotics, such as rigid-soft body interactions, manipulation of deformable objects in fluids, or even aerial robotics where aerodynamic forces are dominant. The authors themselves indicate future work on computational scalability for 3D and large-scale problems, connections to other CFD methods, and extensions to other multiphysics challenges, underscoring the foundational nature of this research.
