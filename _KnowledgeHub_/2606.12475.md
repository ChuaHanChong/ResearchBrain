# Learning to Assist: Collaborative VLAs for Implicit Human-Robot Collaboration

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates the application of action-chunking Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies for implicit human-robot collaboration, identifying 'demonstration action leakage' as a critical failure mode causing premature robot assistance. An inference-time steering method is proposed that effectively mitigates these false starts, reducing total task time by 14% and critical failures by 55% in a user study, while enabling smoother robot motions with longer execution horizons.

## Problem

- Traditional Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) systems rely on hand-engineered pipelines, which limit scalability and flexibility for diverse tasks and implicit human coordination.
- End-to-end, data-driven VLA models, while capable in autonomous manipulation, face unique challenges in implicit HRC, particularly concerning timely assistance and interpreting subtle human intent.
- Action-chunking VLA policies can prematurely initiate robot actions ('false starts') in collaborative settings due to 'demonstration action leakage' where training data action chunks span latent task transitions.

## Method

- Applied ==action-chunking Vision-Language-Action (VLA)== policies (**Diffusion Transformer** and **π0.5**) trained via ==imitation learning== to collaborative assembly tasks involving human-robot interaction and object handovers.
- Identified ==demonstration action leakage== as a failure mode and developed an inference-time ==policy steering== approach that leverages automatically generated ==basin points== (waiting positions) from demonstrations via ==MeanShift clustering==.
- The steering mechanism, combining ==FK (Feynman-Kac) Steering== and ==Basin Pull Steering==, biases policy sampling towards conservative, waiting actions when appropriate, preventing premature movements without requiring model retraining.

## Results

- The fine-tuned π0.5 VLA model demonstrated **100% progress** and significantly faster task and robot times compared to DiT on simpler collaborative tasks.
- In a **16-participant** user study, the proposed steering with a longer execution horizon (**12 samples**) reduced total task completion time by **14%** (**350.1s to 302.0s**) and critical task failures by **55%** (**1.1 to 0.5**) on a complex assembly task.
- The longer execution horizon with steering resulted in lower motion jerkiness and was preferred by **11 out of 16 participants** for its smoothness and speed, despite a slight increase in recoverable mistakes and minor handover collisions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - End-to-end VLA models are capable of learning complex collaborative behaviors from demonstrations, though acquiring reliable handover skills requires a larger dataset than manipulation subtasks.
> - Action-chunking in VLA policies introduces 'demonstration action leakage' in HRC, a specific failure mode causing robots to commit to actions before humans are ready, indicating a need for context-aware timing.
> - Inference-time policy steering provides an effective, non-invasive method to mitigate premature robot actions, enabling the use of longer execution horizons for smoother and more efficient collaboration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) is a field focused on combining the complementary strengths of humans and robots to enhance task efficiency. Traditional HRC systems often rely on hand-engineered pipelines, which, while effective for specific scenarios, present limitations in scalability and flexibility when applied to new tasks or interactions. These systems typically incorporate specialized modules for intent inference and human-aware motion planning, with machine learning often utilized in a limited, modular capacity rather than end-to-end.

Concurrently, the field of robot learning has advanced significantly, particularly with large-scale imitation learning approaches. These methods have shifted robot capabilities from expert-designed systems to data-driven ones, directly learning from expert demonstrations. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which adapt pre-trained vision-language models for robot control, have demonstrated proficiency across various autonomous manipulation tasks. However, the application of these end-to-end, data-driven VLA models to the complexities of HRC, especially implicit collaboration, remains an area requiring further investigation.

Collaborative settings introduce challenges beyond those in autonomous manipulation, primarily concerning coordination with humans and timely assistance. This necessitates the robot's ability to interpret human intent, often from subtle or implicit signals, rather than explicit commands. While some prior work has explored VLAs in HRC, these efforts frequently concentrate on language-based interactions or generative co-pilots for teleoperation. The specific challenges of collaborative timing and robust implicit intent recognition using VLAs, particularly from raw robot observations like images, have been less studied.

This paper addresses this gap by investigating the application of action-chunking VLA policies to implicit HRC. It identifies a specific failure mode ("demonstration action leakage") inherent to action-chunking policies in collaborative contexts and proposes an inference-time mitigation strategy. By doing so, the work aims to extend the capabilities of data-driven, end-to-end robot learning to flexible and implicit human-robot collaboration, moving beyond traditional hand-engineered HRC pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the efficacy and challenges of applying end-to-end, imitation learning-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to support collaborative manipulation in Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) settings. The overarching motivation is to transition HRC system design from reliance on hand-engineered pipelines, which often lack scalability and flexibility for diverse tasks, to data-driven approaches that can learn collaborative behaviors directly from expert demonstrations.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

- **Assess VLA Applicability to Collaborative Tasks:** To determine whether VLA policies, which have shown capabilities in autonomous manipulation, can effectively learn and execute collaborative tasks that involve interaction with a human. This includes tasks requiring sequential steps and object handovers.
- **Identify Challenges in Collaborative VLA Policies:** To characterize the specific difficulties that arise when action-chunking VLA policies are trained on and deployed in collaborative tasks. The authors hypothesize that collaborative settings, particularly those requiring implicit coordination, introduce unique timing and intent recognition challenges not prevalent in purely autonomous scenarios.
- **Analyze the Impact of Action Chunking on HRC:** To investigate how the inherent mechanism of action chunking in VLA policies, where actions are predicted over a future horizon, might lead to suboptimal or erroneous collaborative behavior, especially concerning the timing of assistive actions. This is motivated by qualitative observations of premature robot actions.
- **Propose and Evaluate Mitigation Strategies for Premature Assistance:** To develop and test methods that can mitigate undesirable behaviors such as "false starts" or premature commitments to actions by the robot. The motivation is to enhance the robot's ability to wait for appropriate human signals without sacrificing overall task performance or requiring complex architectural changes or retraining. This is aimed at improving the robot's timing in implicit collaboration.
- **Characterize Factors Affecting Real-World Collaborative VLA Performance:** Beyond basic task success, the research aims to understand how factors like model choice, execution speed, and policy horizon length influence the efficiency, robustness, and user experience in actual human-robot collaborative scenarios.
- **Validate Proposed Methods through User Studies:** To conduct a user study with human participants on a complex, long-horizon collaborative task to evaluate the proposed steering method's impact on objective metrics (e.g., task completion time, failures) and subjective perceptions (e.g., workload, usability, fluency). This is crucial for understanding the practical implications of the developed techniques.

In essence, the research is motivated by the potential of data-driven VLAs to create more flexible and generalizable collaborative robots, while also proactively addressing the specific challenges and failure modes that emerge when these models interact with humans in implicitly coordinated tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted methodology to investigate collaborative VLAs, including comparative model evaluation, identification of a specific failure mode, development of a mitigation strategy, and a user study.

**Problem Setting:**
The study focuses on implicit Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) in collaborative assembly tasks. The robot's role is to provide timely assistance by inferring human intent ($z_h$) from observable context ($o$) and a language command ($l$), generating a distribution over robot actions ($a$). The policy $\pi_\theta(a|o,l,z_h)$ effectively marginalizes over latent intent $\pi_\theta(a|o,l) = \int \pi_\theta(a|o,l,z_h)p(z_h|o,l)dz_h$. The policies utilized are action-chunking, predicting partial trajectories over a prediction horizon ($T_p$), and executing actions via receding-horizon control over an execution horizon ($T_a$). Policies are trained from collaborative demonstrations ($D_\tau$), where observations indicating a "waiting state" may precede action targets spanning a requested assistive motion, leading to "demonstration action leakage."

**Tasks and Apparatus:**
Three tabletop collaborative assembly tasks were designed with increasing complexity:
1. **Abstract Assembly:** Sequential collaboration with 3D-printed blocks, focusing on color matching. No physical human-robot interaction.
2. **Mini-Chair:** Incorporates human-to-robot (H2R) and robot-to-human (R2H) handovers of bolts and screwdrivers, requiring intent recognition (e.g., assembling seat vs. back).
3. **Radio:** A longer, more complex task combining sequential and handover steps (2 tool handovers, 8 object handovers, 2 sequential manipulations), used in the user study to stress-test policies.

The experimental setup included a UFactory X-Arm 6 robot, two Intel RealSense D435i cameras (scene and wrist-mounted), providing RGB images, end-effector (EE) pose, and gripper position as observations. The action space comprised EE pose and gripper position. Demonstrations were collected by a second person teleoperating the robot with a SpaceMouse while a seated collaborator performed the human portion of the task. Policy inference was conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 5090.

**VLA Models and Training:**
Two state-of-the-art VLA models were evaluated:
1. **Diffusion Transformer (DiT):** Based on the Large Behavior Model (LBM) architecture.
2. **$\pi_{0.5}$:** A fine-tuned base model from the OpenPi family, pretrained on general manipulation.
Both models were trained on 100 collaborative demonstrations at 10 Hz, with a chunk prediction horizon ($T_p$) of 16 samples and an execution horizon ($T_a$) of 8 samples. Fine-tuning details, including hyperparameters and action space specifics, are provided in Appendix A. An additional experiment (Appendix B) investigated the impact of demonstration budget on $\pi_{0.5}$ performance for the Radio task, revealing that while manipulation skills adapted quickly, handover behaviors required more data (up to 60 demonstrations for reliability).

**Speeding Up Robot Execution:**
To improve efficiency, a simple alignment approach was implemented to enable faster-than-demonstration execution. This method truncates new action chunks to minimize the distance from the last commanded position (Equation 3), aiming to reduce tracking error, distribution shift, and inter-chunk jitter without requiring training-time modifications.

**Identification of Action Leakage:**
Qualitative analysis of initial rollouts revealed a failure mode termed "demonstration action leakage." This occurs when action chunks from training data span latent task transitions (e.g., from waiting to a handover initiation) despite the observation indicating the robot should still be waiting. This leads to premature robot movements, false starts, and potential false commitments (e.g., handing over an object before the human is ready).

**Mitigating False Starts through Policy Steering:**
An inference-time steering approach was developed to bias policy sampling towards conservative actions, thus preventing premature commitments. This method leverages automatically generated "basin points" — waiting positions inferred from demonstrations using MeanShift clustering. The approach consists of two mechanisms:
1. **FK Steering (Feynman-Kac Steering):** The policy sampler is augmented to make conservative chunks more likely. It adapts Feynman-Kac steering by sampling multiple candidate action chunks (particles) and re-weighting them based on a reward function $r(a_t)$. This reward function is designed to favor trajectories that remain close to or move towards the active basin point, penalizing drift away. A sequential Monte Carlo procedure with resampling biases the particle distribution towards conservative chunks.
 - The reward $r(a_t)$ has three terms: $r_{curr}$ (for current EE position within basin), $r_{traj}$ (for trajectory end-point near basin), and $r_{drift}$ (penalizes drift from current position relative to basin).
2. **Basin Pull Steering:** To counteract slow drift outside the basin, a corrective force is applied. For the selected action chunk, the commanded end-effector position is interpolated towards the active basin, only activating when the robot is within the basin radius and conservative chunks are sampled.

Hyperparameters for steering were kept consistent across tasks (Appendix C). An experiment tested the efficacy of steering by having the sped-up $\pi_{0.5}$ wait in a pre-handover state for five minutes with an idle collaborator. Drift and false commitments were measured across varying execution horizons ($T_a$) and steering ablations.

**User Evaluation:**
A user study with 16 participants was conducted on the complex Radio task to evaluate two collaborative VLA configurations:
1. **Shorter Execution Horizon (4 samples):** A baseline aiming to minimize false starts and drift through frequent replanning.
2. **Longer Execution Horizon (12 samples) with Steering:** This condition used the proposed steering approach to enable a longer horizon while mitigating false starts.
The order of conditions was counterbalanced. Objective measures included total task time, robot time, human time, mistakes (recoverable inaccuracies), failures (requiring reset), and handover collisions. Subjective measures collected after each condition included NASA Task Load Index (TLX), System Usability Scale (SUS), and Human-Robot Fluency questionnaires, along with user preferences and semi-structured interviews. Jerk analysis was also performed (Appendix D.3).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the capabilities, limitations, and improvements of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in implicit Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC).

**VLA Capability in Collaborative Tasks:**
- **Model Performance:** The fine-tuned $\pi_{0.5}$ model consistently demonstrated stronger performance (lower task completion time, higher success rate) compared to the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) policy. $\pi_{0.5}$ achieved 100% progress and significantly faster task and robot times on both Abstract Assembly and Mini-Chair tasks, with minimal mistakes and failures (Table 1). This suggests that VLA pretraining on general manipulation tasks and recovery data is beneficial for collaborative tasks.
- **Speedup Benefits:** Applying a simple alignment approach to speed up robot execution generally reduced total task time without significant degradation in accuracy. For Abstract Assembly, speeding up $\pi_{0.5}$ reduced total time from 71s to 63s. The benefits were less pronounced in Mini-Chair where proactive fetching minimized human waiting time.
- **Data Requirements:** An analysis of training data budget (Appendix B) showed that $\pi_{0.5}$ rapidly adapted to manipulation-centric subtasks (achieving zero manipulation errors with 20 demonstrations). However, learning reliable handover behaviors required a larger dataset (approximately 60 demonstrations), indicating a specific data need for interactive HRC components.

**Identification and Mitigation of Action Leakage:**
- **Action Leakage as a Failure Mode:** Experiments confirmed that action leakage in action-chunking policies leads to "premature robot assistance" or "false starts." In pre-handover waiting states, increasing the execution horizon ($T_a$) generally led to a higher number of false commitments (premature handovers) and increased model drift (cumulative end-effector path length) (Figure 5, Table 6). This occurs because action chunks can cross latent task transition boundaries, causing the robot to initiate movement before a clear human signal.
- **Effectiveness of Steering:** The proposed inference-time steering approach, combining FK Steering and Basin Pull Steering, significantly mitigated false commitments and reduced drift across various execution horizons (Figure 5, Table 6). It successfully biased policy sampling towards conservative, waiting actions without requiring model retraining or architectural changes. FK steering alone was less robust if the robot drifted outside the basin radius, highlighting the importance of Basin Pull.

**User Evaluation Results (Radio Task):**
A 16-participant user study compared a shorter execution horizon policy ($T_a=4$) against a longer execution horizon policy ($T_a=12$) with the proposed steering mechanism. Both policies used the sped-up fine-tuned $\pi_{0.5}$ model.
- **User Preference:** 11 out of 16 participants preferred the longer-horizon (12+Steer) condition, citing noticeable improvements in smoothness and speed. The remaining five participants preferred the shorter-horizon policy, perceiving it as more accurate.
- **Objective Performance:**
 - **Time-on-Task:** The 12+Steer condition resulted in significantly shorter total task completion times (mean 302.0s vs. 350.1s; p < .001). This was primarily driven by significantly reduced robot execution time (mean 179.0s vs. 231.3s; p < .001) (Figure 6, Figure 12).
 - **Failures:** The 12+Steer condition exhibited significantly fewer failures requiring a reset (mean 0.5 vs. 1.1; p < .05) (Figure 6, Figure 14).
 - **Mistakes:** The 12+Steer condition had a significantly higher number of recoverable mistakes (mean 2.8 vs. 0.6; p < .01), particularly in handover and screwdriver manipulation, suggesting reduced reactivity but quick recovery (Figure 6, Figure 13).
 - **Handover Collisions:** The 12+Steer condition also recorded a significantly higher number of minor handover collisions (mean 0.5 vs. 0.06; p < .05) (Figure 15).
- **Subjective Measures:** No statistically significant differences were found in participants' ratings across NASA-TLX, System Usability Scale (SUS), or Human-Robot Fluency questionnaires (Figure 6, Figure 16-19). SUS scores for both conditions were generally below the bottom 50 percent of ratings.
- **Jerk Analysis:** Larger execution horizons ($T_a$) were associated with noticeably lower motion jerkiness (Figure 10, Figure 11), contributing to the perceived smoothness of the longer-horizon policy.

In summary, the research established VLAs as capable of collaborative tasks, identified action leakage as a critical HRC-specific failure mode, and demonstrated that inference-time steering can effectively mitigate this issue, enabling more efficient and smoother collaborative behavior with longer execution horizons.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) by addressing key challenges in enabling flexible and data-driven robotic assistance. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Advancement in Data-Driven HRC:** The study demonstrates that Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, trained end-to-end with imitation learning, can effectively learn and execute complex collaborative tasks. This represents a step towards moving HRC system design from hand-engineered pipelines to more adaptable, data-driven systems, which can simplify the development of robots for novel collaborative scenarios.
- **Identification of a Novel VLA Failure Mode in HRC:** The paper identifies "demonstration action leakage" as a specific and significant failure mode for action-chunking VLA policies in implicit HRC. This phenomenon, where action chunks prematurely cross latent task transitions, leading to erroneous assistive actions, is a new insight that is particularly relevant to the application of VLAs in interactive, timing-sensitive environments. Recognizing this issue is crucial for the robust deployment of VLA models in human-centric tasks.
- **Development of a Practical Mitigation Strategy:** The proposed inference-time steering method offers a practical and non-invasive solution to mitigate premature assistance caused by action leakage. By leveraging automatically generated basin points and biasing policy sampling, the method effectively reduces false starts and drift without requiring architectural changes or retraining of the VLA model. This enables the use of longer execution horizons, which can enhance temporal consistency and motion smoothness.
- **Improved HRC Efficiency and Performance:** The user study provides empirical evidence that the steering approach, when combined with a longer execution horizon, leads to significantly faster collaboration and fewer critical task failures compared to a shorter-horizon baseline. While subjective measures did not show significant differences, the objective improvements in efficiency and robustness are crucial for the practical adoption of collaborative robots. The reduction in motion jerkiness with longer horizons also contributes to a more predictable and potentially safer interaction.
- **Guidance for Future VLA Design:** The findings highlight that while VLAs may adapt quickly to general manipulation in collaborative settings, interactive components like handovers require more specific training data. This provides valuable guidance for developing future VLA models that are specifically pre-trained or fine-tuned for diverse HRC interactions, potentially reducing the data requirements for task-specific adaptation.
- **Implications for Robot Autonomy and Trust:** By enabling robots to wait for implicit human signals and avoid premature actions, the steering mechanism can contribute to more human-aware and trustworthy robotic behavior. Better timing and fewer unexpected movements can enhance human comfort and collaboration fluency, even if subjective measures in this study did not capture this fully.
- **Foundation for Further Research:** The limitations identified, such as the need for more robust intent tracking, alternative mitigation strategies (e.g., training-time changes, adaptive horizons), and continued investigation into human-robot collisions, pave the way for future research directions in developing even more capable and safe collaborative VLA systems. The exploration of mixed implicit/explicit collaboration is also a notable area for future work.
