# DIDO: Distilling Interaction-Centric Dynamics into One-Step Denoising for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> DIDO is a World Action Model that compresses a four-step robot-domain video generator into a single denoising step using distribution matching distillation, then grounds the distilled prediction with interaction-centric tokens supervised by gripper and object bounding-box trajectories and by frozen DINOv3 object features. A coupled action expert reads these latents directly, reaching 99.0% on LIBERO, 92.0% on RoboTwin, and 76.6% on LIBERO-Plus.

## Problem

- World Action Models predict future frames with iterative denoising, so the foresight that helps manipulation costs latency that closed-loop robot control cannot absorb.
- Prior efficiency work truncates denoising to one or a few early steps, but early steps resolve only static scene structure while gripper and object regions stay blurry.
- Existing object-aware world models and generic one-step distillation keep robot-object interaction implicit, so no method recovers the late-emerging interaction dynamics inside a single step.

## Method

- **One-Step World Dynamics Distillation** applies ==distribution matching distillation== with frozen real and online fake score networks, training a single-step generator to match a robot-adapted four-step ==Wan2.2-TI2V-5B== teacher.
- **Interaction-Centric Visual Reasoning** embeds 64 tokens in object, interaction, gripper and alignment groups, supervised by 32-step future boxes with ==smooth-L1== plus ==GIoU== and by multi-layer ==DINOv3== crop features.
- **Interaction-Aware Action Prediction** couples a ==flow-matching== action expert through ==mixture-of-transformers== shared attention, with **Dynamics-Based Token Refinement** keeping the top-3 of 20 regions at full resolution and pooling the rest.

## Results

- **DIDO** reaches **99.0%** average on LIBERO, beating the strongest prior methods by **0.5** points and the single-step distilled Flash-WAM by **2.9** points, and **92.0%** on RoboTwin without embodied pretraining.
- On LIBERO-Plus it averages **76.6%** versus **75.0%** for the closest baseline, and cumulative ablation moves **97.7/71.4** to **98.3/72.3** to **98.9/75.9** to **99.0/76.6** across the three components.
- Inference takes **384 ms** versus **562 ms** for the four-step teacher, a **32%** cut, and real-robot scores reach **80.8** and **66.0** against **74.5** and **53.3** for the strongest baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion coarse-to-fine ordering means truncation systematically discards the content that matters most for control; distilling to the converged distribution is preferable to stopping early.
> - A handful of explicitly supervised entity tokens carried 33.9% of action attribution from 5.74% of the cache, showing dense future-video tokens are largely redundant for policy learning.
> - Structural grounding buys robustness more than in-distribution accuracy, since ablation gains were far larger under perturbation than on the clean benchmark; camera-shift scores nonetheless remained weakest.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

DIDO addresses an efficiency problem in robotic manipulation at the intersection of vision-language-action models (VLAs), video generation, diffusion or flow-based modeling, and predictive world models.

VLAs typically map current visual observations and language instructions directly to robot actions. These models can perform manipulation without explicitly representing how the scene will evolve. World Action Models (WAMs) take a different approach by predicting future visual dynamics and using those predictions to support action generation. Future representations can encode anticipated object motion, gripper movement, and environmental changes, potentially improving control in tasks requiring temporal reasoning.

The primary cost of many WAMs is that their video generators use iterative denoising. Multiple denoising steps increase inference latency, which is consequential for closed-loop control, where a robot must repeatedly observe its environment, predict an action, execute it, and replan. Previous efficiency strategies have either removed future imagination, used representations from only the first few denoising steps, or distilled multi-step models into one-step predictors.

DIDO focuses on a specific limitation of naive truncation. According to the paper’s analysis, early denoising steps form static scene structure relatively quickly, but the gripper, manipulated object, and their interaction remain underdeveloped until later steps. Therefore, simply stopping after one step preserves broad scene information while losing the manipulation-relevant dynamics that are most useful for control. DIDO extends one-step distillation with explicit interaction-centric supervision, placing it within a broader movement toward object-centric, structured, and semantically grounded representations for robot policies.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has three main objectives.

First, it seeks to characterize what information is available at different points in a video model’s denoising trajectory. The authors compare decoded frames and latent representations after one, two, and four denoising steps. They observe that static backgrounds and coarse scene layout become visible early, whereas motion in the interaction region develops progressively. This motivates treating denoising not as a uniform refinement process, but as one in which different spatial regions and semantic entities converge at different rates.

Second, the work aims to transfer the useful dynamics of a multi-step video model into a single denoising step. The desired one-step model should not merely imitate the partially denoised output produced after the first teacher step. Instead, it should approximate the converged output distribution of the complete multi-step teacher.

Third, DIDO aims to make the one-step representation explicitly sensitive to the entities involved in manipulation. The gripper and target object are supervised through future bounding-box trajectories, while dedicated tokens represent the target object, the gripper, their interaction, and object-specific visual features. This design is intended to recover spatial and temporal information that generic distribution matching might not preserve reliably.

The broader motivation is to combine predictive foresight with control-speed requirements. The authors seek a model that retains the robustness benefits of future modeling while approaching the latency of policies that do not perform test-time future prediction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. One-Step World-Model Distillation

DIDO is built on Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, initialized from a publicly released four-step distilled checkpoint. The authors first fine-tune this model on robot manipulation demonstrations to create a robot-domain four-step teacher.

The teacher is then compressed into a one-step generator using distribution matching distillation (DMD). Rather than matching the teacher’s intermediate denoising trajectory, DMD matches perturbed distributions associated with the single-step student and the converged teacher. A frozen teacher score network estimates the target distribution, while an online fake-score network tracks the evolving student distribution. The student is updated using the difference between these score estimates.

At inference, the score networks are discarded. The student generates the future video latent in a single forward pass. The video decoder is used for visualization and training-related processing, but future frames do not need to be decoded and re-encoded for action prediction.

#### 3.2. Interaction-Centric Visual Reasoning

DIDO introduces 64 additional tokens divided into four groups of 16:

1. Object tokens represent the manipulated target object.
2. Interaction tokens represent the coupled gripper-object relationship.
3. Gripper tokens represent the robot end-effector.
4. Alignment tokens preserve object-centric visual appearance.

These tokens are inserted into the video transformer and evolve jointly with video representations.

The object and gripper branches predict 32 future bounding boxes, corresponding to the action horizon. The object branch uses object and interaction tokens, while the gripper branch uses gripper and interaction tokens. Sharing the interaction tokens between the two branches encourages them to encode relative spatial and temporal evolution rather than treating the two trajectories independently. The box loss combines smooth-L1 and generalized intersection-over-union terms.

The alignment tokens are trained against features from a frozen DINOv3 encoder. For each frame, the target-object bounding box is used to crop and resize the object region. DINOv3 produces object-centric visual tokens, and alignment-token representations from selected layers of the video transformer are projected into the same feature space and trained with an L1 alignment loss. This provides appearance-level supervision in addition to trajectory-level supervision.

The required annotations are automatically generated. Target objects are parsed from language, localized with an open-vocabulary detector, and tracked through the sequence. Simulator state supplies gripper positions in LIBERO and RoboTwin, while Grounding DINO and SAM 3 are used for real-world gripper tracking.

#### 3.3. Interaction-Aware Action Prediction

In the second training stage, DIDO couples the one-step world model to an action expert using a mixture-of-transformers architecture. The action expert directly accesses internal video-model representations rather than decoded future frames.

The action expert generates chunks of 32 actions with flow matching. It receives image-conditioning features, interaction-centric tokens, alignment tokens, current visual representations, and future-video representations. To reduce redundant visual context, DIDO applies dynamics-based token refinement. The spatial feature map is divided into 20 regions, and local changes between current and future value features are calculated. The three regions with the largest predicted changes retain fine-grained tokens; the remaining 17 regions are average-pooled. This allocates more representational capacity to areas likely to contain manipulation activity.

#### 3.4. Two-Stage Training

Stage I optimizes the DMD loss, interaction trajectory loss, and DINOv3 alignment loss. Stage II jointly trains the video model, interaction heads, alignment projections, action expert, and proprioception encoder. It retains a video-generation objective alongside action prediction and interaction supervision. The language tokenizer, VAE, and DINOv3 encoder remain frozen.

The experiments use only the robot demonstrations associated with each evaluation setting. No additional robot video or cross-embodiment data is used. Action inference uses ten Euler integration steps for the flow-matching action expert, while the world model itself performs one denoising step.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Benchmark Performance

On LIBERO, DIDO reports an average success rate of 99.0% across the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. Its suite-specific results are 99.8%, 99.6%, 98.6%, and 98.0%, respectively. This exceeds the reported averages of LingBot-VA and Faster-WAM by 0.5 percentage points and Fast-WAM by 1.4 points. Compared with Flash-WAM, another one-step distilled WAM, DIDO is higher by 2.9 points.

On RoboTwin, DIDO obtains a 92.0% average success rate, compared with 92.2% for LingBot-VA. The DIDO result is higher than Fast-WAM’s 91.8% and Flash-WAM’s 85.5%. The result indicates that the method transfers beyond single-arm LIBERO tasks to contact-rich bimanual manipulation.

On LIBERO-Plus, which introduces systematic shifts in camera, robot initialization, language, lighting, background, noise, and layout, DIDO reaches a 76.6% average success rate. It exceeds Faster-WAM by 1.6 points and performs particularly well under language, lighting, robot-initialization, and layout changes. However, its camera-shift result is 45.3%, lower than several baseline categories, showing that robustness is not uniform across perturbation types.

#### 4.2. Real-World Evaluation

The real-world study uses four Galbot G1 tasks, including object placement, sequential bowl-and-pepper manipulation, wiping and storing a cloth, and opening a drawer before placing a toy inside. Performance is measured with staged progress scores rather than binary success.

Under position generalization, DIDO achieves an average score of 80.8, compared with 74.5 for π0.5 and 53.8 for Fast-WAM. When visual clutter is added, DIDO reaches 66.0, compared with 53.3 and 33.0. Its average degradation between the two protocols is 14.8 points, smaller than the degradation reported for both comparison policies.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Efficiency

The denoising analysis shows that action performance improves when later teacher representations are used: LIBERO success increases from 97.7% after one step to 98.4% after four steps, while LIBERO-Plus increases from 71.4% to 72.6%. This supports the claim that later denoising steps contain additional control-relevant information.

The ablation study starts with one-step truncation. On LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, the baseline scores 97.7% and 71.4%. Adding one-step distillation raises these to 98.3% and 72.3%. Adding interaction-centric reasoning produces 98.9% and 75.9%, and dynamics-based token refinement produces 99.0% and 76.6%.

DIDO reduces end-to-end latency from 562 milliseconds for the four-step teacher to 384 milliseconds on an NVIDIA H100, a reported reduction of approximately 32%. It uses 191 conditioning tokens after refinement instead of 323. Compared with Fast-WAM’s 356 milliseconds, DIDO adds 28 milliseconds while retaining explicit future modeling.

Attribution analysis indicates that interaction-centric tokens account for 33.9% of action attribution while comprising only 5.74% of cached tokens. Object, interaction, gripper, and alignment tokens each show higher per-token attribution than current and future visual tokens, suggesting that the compact structured representations are actively used by the action expert.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

DIDO contributes an empirical account of the uneven emergence of information during video denoising and a method for transferring late-forming interaction dynamics into a one-step model. Its results suggest that predictive world models need not choose between multi-step foresight and low-latency control if the information most relevant to manipulation is explicitly supervised and selectively represented.

Potentially, this approach could support faster closed-loop robotic control, especially in settings where action replanning must occur frequently. The interaction-centric token design may also provide a general framework for integrating geometric trajectories and visual features into generative policies.

The paper’s limitations qualify its scope. Bounding boxes provide coarse supervision and may be inadequate for multi-object interactions, deformable objects, occlusions, or fine contact geometry. Automatic tracking can introduce noise, particularly when object references are ambiguous. The training pipeline requires teacher preparation, distillation, and joint policy learning, making it more complex than direct policy training. Finally, the one-step model remains dependent on the quality and coverage of the robot-adapted four-step teacher. Further work is needed to test richer interaction representations, more embodiments, broader environments, and simpler training procedures.
