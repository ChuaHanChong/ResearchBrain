# How Should a Simulation-to-Reality Transfer Budget Be Spent?

> [!summary] Summary
> A controlled sim-to-sim pendulum study that treats real-robot measurement time as a budget split between system identification and domain randomization. It sweeps identification rollouts n against randomization width w, training SAC policies and scoring zero-shot return on a hidden target. Measurement dominates: ~10 rollouts close most of the transfer gap, and training at the point estimate beats any widened band.

## Problem

- System identification and domain randomization consume the same scarce real-robot interaction budget, yet prior work studies each method separately without measuring how to divide that budget.
- Prior randomization-adaptation methods assume broader training distributions pay for themselves, but the value of extra measurement fidelity is asserted rather than evaluated against randomization breadth directly.
- Conflicting empirical claims about breadth versus exact dynamics matching are confounded by structural model mismatch, leaving the well-specified identifiable regime untested as a baseline.

## Method

- A ==sim-to-sim budget-allocation protocol==: a hidden-parameter reality simulator stands in for hardware, with unknown bob mass and rod length far from the engineer's nominal prior.
- ==Identification by grid-search== fitting of one-step angular-velocity prediction error over physically plausible bounds, using ==random-torque excitation rollouts== corrupted by Gaussian sensor noise.
- A ==two-axis sweep== of budget n against relative randomization width w, training ==soft actor-critic (SAC)== per cell and scoring zero-shot return, with ==paired-bootstrap significance== over seeds.

## Results

- The n=0 row sits between **-717** and **-771** regardless of width, while budgets from **10** rollouts upward reach about **-344**; the zero-to-ten jump is **+427** with confidence interval [**+407**, **+448**].
- At every positive budget the widest band significantly underperforms training at the estimate, with paired differences of roughly **165-198**, and even a narrow **0.2** band loses by **18-35**.
- A truth-bracketing prior-range baseline returns **-749**, statistically indistinguishable from estimate-centered breadth and **413** below pure fidelity, so coverage of the truth does not replace measurement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Measure the parameters you can identify first, then reserve randomization only for the residual uncertainty that parameter fitting cannot remove.
> - Distributional coverage of the true system is not sufficient: one policy trained across a wide plausible range is mediocre on every specific system in it.
> - The measurement plateau tracks sensor noise and excitation quality, so any reported rollout knee is calibration-specific, and structural mismatch may still favor breadth.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a powerful paradigm for training control policies through iterative interaction. However, applying RL directly to physical robots is often impractical due to the high cost, slow speed, and safety concerns associated with real-world interactions. Simulation provides an alternative by allowing policies to be trained at scale. This approach, known as sim-to-real transfer, introduces a significant challenge: the "reality gap." This gap arises because simulators inherently approximate real-world dynamics, and policies trained in simulation may exploit these approximations, leading to performance degradation or failure when deployed on a physical robot. Reducing this reality gap is a central and ongoing challenge in robot learning.

Two primary strategies have been developed to address the reality gap:
1. **System Identification (SI)**: This method involves using measurements from the real robot to estimate its physical parameters. These estimated parameters are then used to tune the simulator, making its dynamics more closely match the target physical system.
2. **Domain Randomization (DR)**: Instead of precisely matching the simulator to the real system, DR trains policies across a distribution of randomized simulators. The goal is to encourage the policy to learn robust behaviors that generalize well to a range of dynamics, including the true system, even if the exact parameters are unknown.

Both system identification and domain randomization are widely used and have demonstrated efficacy in various sim-to-real contexts. However, a critical aspect that remains underexplored is how to optimally allocate a limited "measurement budget"—the scarce resource of real-robot interaction time—between these two competing strategies. Existing research often studies SI and DR in isolation, leaving the question of their relative value and optimal allocation under a fixed budget largely unanswered. This work positions itself to directly investigate this budget allocation problem, aiming to provide empirical guidance on whether to prioritize precise measurement or broad randomization when real-world data is constrained. The authors note that their study complements prior work, such as that by Truong et al., which suggests that higher-fidelity simulation is not always cost-effective, thus reinforcing the need to directly evaluate the value of measurement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the practical constraint of limited real-robot interaction time in sim-to-real transfer pipelines. When faced with a finite budget for collecting data from a physical robot, practitioners must decide whether to invest this data in accurately identifying the robot's physical parameters to reduce simulator mismatch, or to use it to justify a broader distribution of parameters for domain randomization, thereby aiming for a more robust policy. The current understanding of this trade-off is limited, prompting the core question of this study.

The key objectives of the paper are:
1. **Formulate a Budget-Allocation Problem**: To establish a framework that quantifies the trade-off between system identification and domain randomization, treating them as competing demands for a single, fixed real-robot measurement budget. This involves mapping the full "return surface," which represents policy performance across varying levels of identification budget and randomization width.
2. **Evaluate Relative Efficacy in an Identifiable Regime**: To empirically investigate, in a controlled sim-to-sim setting where the system dynamics are identifiable and well-specified, how the allocation of the real-data budget impacts zero-shot transfer performance.
3. **Compare Training Strategies**: To directly compare the performance of policies trained at the estimated parameters (minimal randomization) against those trained over a wider randomization band centered around these estimates.
4. **Assess Prior-Range Randomization**: To test a common alternative: training over a wide, "truth-bracketing" prior-range randomization distribution, to determine if such broad randomization can substitute for direct measurement.
5. **Assess Robustness**: To confirm the observed trends and optimal allocation across different reality gaps and sensor noise levels, evaluating the generality of the findings within the specified regime.

The authors explicitly state that they do not propose a new algorithm but rather aim to make the identification-versus-randomization trade-off measurable under a fixed budget, providing empirical insights into how this budget can be most effectively spent.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Experimental Setup

The study employs a controlled sim-to-sim experimental design using two pendulum simulators:
- **Reality Simulator (R)**: This acts as a proxy for the physical robot. It has fixed but hidden physical parameters: bob mass (*m*) and rod length (*ℓ*), collectively denoted as *θ*. The true, hidden parameters, *θ*<sup>⋆</sup>, were set to (2.0, 1.5). This value was chosen to be significantly distant from a nominal prior, *θ*<sub>0</sub> = (1.0, 1.0), to ensure a substantial "reality gap" that required bridging.
- **Training Simulator (T)**: This simulator exposes *m* and *ℓ* as adjustable parameters. It is used for policy training.
Both simulators utilize the same underlying Pendulum dynamics equations; the only unknown to the learner is the specific value of *θ*<sup>⋆</sup>. Other parameters such as gravity, timestep, and torque/speed limits are fixed and shared.

#### 3.2. Budget and Allocation Definition

The core of the methodology lies in systematically varying two independent quantities:
- **Identification Budget (*n*)**: This represents the number of rollouts performed on the reality simulator (R) and dedicated to system identification. The study sweeps *n* through a range of values: {0, 5, 10, 25, 50}. A value of *n* = 0 signifies no identification, with training occurring around the nominal prior *θ*<sub>0</sub>.
- **Randomization Width (*w*)**: This parameter controls the spread of the randomization distribution around the current parameter estimate during policy training. It is expressed as a fraction of the point estimate, with *w* values swept across {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5}. A *w* = 0 indicates training solely at the point estimate without randomization.

**System Identification Procedure**:
1. For each *n* > 0, *n* rollouts are collected on the reality simulator (R).
2. These rollouts are generated using a random-torque excitation policy, where torques are drawn independently and identically distributed from a uniform distribution U(−2, 2) N m (the full torque limit).
3. A grid search is performed over physically plausible bounds for *m* and *ℓ* ([0.3, 3.0]²), with a fine resolution of 61×61 to minimize quantization error.
4. The objective of the grid search is to minimize the one-step angular-velocity prediction error, yielding a point estimate *θ*̂ = (*m*̂, *ℓ*̂).
5. Gaussian sensor noise with a standard deviation *σ* = 1.0 is added to the measured angular velocity during identification, which causes the identification error to decrease roughly as 1/√*n*.
**Domain Randomization Procedure**:
1. For each *w* > 0, during policy training, the parameters (*m*, *ℓ*) are resampled component-wise and uniformly from the range [*θ*̂(1 − *w*), *θ*̂(1 + *w*)] at the start of each episode. These values are clipped to the physical bounds [0.3, 3.0].
2. Randomization itself is performed entirely in simulation and incurs no real-robot cost.

#### 3.3. Training and Evaluation Procedure

- **Policy Training**: For each unique (*n*, *w*) combination, a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) policy is trained in the randomized training simulator (T) for 75,000 steps. SAC was chosen for its sample efficiency and its ability to reliably solve the pendulum task. Standard hyperparameters were used (learning rate 10<sup>−3</sup>, batch size 256, discount 0.99, replay buffer 10<sup>5</sup>, target smoothing *τ* = 0.005).
- **Evaluation**: The trained policies are evaluated for zero-shot transfer performance on the hidden reality simulator (R). Evaluation consists of 20 deterministic episodes, each with a 200-step horizon. The performance metric is the pendulum quadratic cost summed over the episode; therefore, returns are negative, and larger (less negative) values indicate better performance. Each experimental "seed" uses its own fixed set of evaluation initial conditions.
- **Experimental Scope and Statistics**: The study involved 250 total runs, with ten independent seeds for each of the 25 unique (*n*, *w*) grid cells. For statistical analysis, paired contrasts across the ten matched seeds were used, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived from 2×10<sup>4</sup> resamples via a paired bootstrap method. An interval excluding zero was defined as statistically significant.

#### 3.4. Robustness Checks

To further validate the findings, two sets of follow-up experiments were conducted:
- **Prior-Range Baseline**: An additional baseline was tested at *n* = 0, where randomization was performed uniformly over the full physically plausible bounds [0.3, 3.0]² for (*m*, *ℓ*). This range deliberately brackets the true parameters *θ*<sup>⋆</sup>, allowing for comparison with the estimate-centered randomization.
- **Varying Reality Gaps and Noise Levels**: A reduced sweep of parameters was conducted at two additional reality gaps (smaller gap: *θ*<sup>⋆</sup> = (1.5, 1.2); larger gap: *θ*<sup>⋆</sup> = (2.5, 2.0)) and two additional sensor noise levels (*σ* = 0.5 and *σ* = 2.0) to assess the robustness of the observed trends.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical insights into the allocation of real-robot measurement budget between system identification and domain randomization, particularly within a well-specified and identifiable regime.

#### 4.1. Impact of Identification Budget and Randomization Width

The primary results, visualized as a grid of mean zero-shot returns, indicate a strong dependence on the identification budget (*n*) and a comparatively weaker, often detrimental, dependence on randomization width (*w*).
- **Dominance of Identification**: The performance surface was observed to be "almost entirely vertical," meaning that changes in the identification budget (*n*) resulted in substantial shifts in performance, while changes in randomization width (*w*) had a less pronounced, or negative, effect.
 - The baseline *n* = 0 row, where no identification occurred, yielded mean returns between −717 and −771, irrespective of randomization width.
 - In contrast, cells with *n* ≥ 10 consistently showed mean returns near −340, demonstrating a significant improvement.
 - The first few identification rollouts had the most pronounced effect. Even *n* = 5 rollouts dramatically improved the *w* = 0 column to approximately −420. By *n* = 10, the return reached −344. The most substantial performance gain observed in the study was the jump from *n* = 0 to *n* = 10 at *w* = 0, which registered an improvement of +427 (95% CI [+407, +448]).
 - The identification process was confirmed to be effective; mean identification error decreased from 1.12 (prior-to-truth distance) to 0.64 at *n* = 50. The remaining error was attributed to additive sensor noise and the finite resolution of the identification grid.
- **Limited Benefit, Often Detriment, of Randomization Width**: Across any given row (fixed *n*), widening the randomization band around the current parameter estimate did not improve return.
 - For any positive identification budget (*n* ≥ 5), the widest band (*w* = 0.5) consistently resulted in significantly worse performance compared to no randomization (*w* = 0). The paired differences ranged from approximately 165 to 198, with all 95% CIs excluding zero.
 - At *n* = 0 (no identification), the difference between *w* = 0.5 and *w* = 0 was statistically indistinguishable (Δ = −7, 95% CI [−34, +19]), indicating that randomization offered no significant benefit when no real data was used for identification.

#### 4.2. Optimal Budget Allocation

Analysis of the best-performing width (*w*<sup>⋆</sup>) at each budget level further clarified the optimal allocation strategy.
- The performance curve, plotting the best mean zero-shot return against the identification budget, showed a rapid increase in return with the initial budget, then plateauing. By *n* = 10, the performance had converged to that of the "pure-fidelity" reference (the best cell at *n* = 50, *w* = 0), reaching −344.
- The optimal width *w*<sup>⋆</sup> was found to be 0.2 only at *n* = 0, but this marginal gain was not statistically significant (Δ = +54 vs. *w* = 0, 95% CI [−4, +114]).
- Crucially, for all budgets from *n* = 5 onwards, the optimal width *w*<sup>⋆</sup> was 0. This implies that once any real-world data was available for identification, training the agent solely at the point estimate (*w* = 0) significantly outperformed training with any randomization band, even a narrow one (e.g., *w* = 0.2), with paired differences ranging from 18 to 35 and all 95% CIs excluding zero.

#### 4.3. Prior-Range Baseline Performance

To address the potential artifact of estimate-centered randomization (especially at *n*=0, where the band might not cover the true parameters), a baseline with a wide, truth-bracketing prior-range randomization was tested at *n*=0. This approach randomized (*m*, *ℓ*) uniformly over the full bounds [0.3, 3.0]², which explicitly included the true parameters *θ*<sup>⋆</sup>.
- This truth-bracketing baseline yielded a mean return of −749 ± 18.
- This performance was statistically indistinguishable from both the estimate-centered breadth (*n* = 0, *w* = 0.5; return −764) and the no-data point (*n* = 0, *w* = 0; return −771).
- It remained significantly poorer than the pure-fidelity setting (*n* = 50, *w* = 0; return −337), with a difference of 413 (95% CI [−442, −385]).
- This finding suggests that simply broadening the randomization distribution to cover the true parameters does not compensate for a lack of specific parameter measurement; a single policy attempting to be robust across a wide, unknown range may perform poorly on any specific system.

#### 4.4. Robustness Across Operating Points

The study evaluated the robustness of these findings across different reality gaps and sensor noise levels.
- The qualitative ordering consistently held: a positive identification budget significantly improved return, and the widest randomization band significantly lowered return at the full budget across all tested operating points.
- **Impact of Noise**: The rate at which the identification budget plateaued depended on the measurement noise level. At lower noise (*σ* = 0.5), identification performance saturated around five rollouts, while at higher noise (*σ* = 2.0), it required closer to fifty rollouts to reach its floor.
- **Impact of Reality Gap**: The small, non-significant benefit of randomization at *n* = 0 was observed to scale with the reality gap. It was insignificant at the main gap, became significantly unhelpful at a smaller gap (where the prior was already closer to truth), and was significantly helpful at a larger gap (where a wider band might, by chance, encompass parts of the true system).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Primary Conclusions

The research provides empirical evidence to inform the allocation of real-robot measurement budgets in sim-to-real transfer, specifically within a regime where the simulator's model class is well-specified and parameters are identifiable. The key conclusion is that **measurement of identifiable parameters consistently outweighs broad domain randomization**.
- A relatively small investment in system identification (e.g., 5-10 rollouts in the tested pendulum setting) can close a substantial portion of the reality gap, leading to significant performance improvements.
- Once real-world data is available for identification, policies achieve optimal zero-shot transfer performance when trained precisely at the estimated parameters, rather than over a widened randomization band around them.
- Broad randomization, even when it encompasses the true system parameters, does not compensate for a lack of direct measurement.
- These findings suggest a prioritized approach for sim-to-real pipelines: **first, measure the parameters that are identifiable within the simulator's model, and only then reserve randomization for any remaining uncertainties that cannot be captured through parameter fitting.**

#### 5.2. Limitations

The authors acknowledge several limitations that contextualize the applicability of their findings:
- **Well-Specified Identification Model**: The most critical limitation is that the reality simulator uses the *exact same* dynamics equations as the training simulator. This implies that the estimator's model class perfectly contains the true system, and the only discrepancy is zero-mean sensor noise. Consequently, system identification is theoretically guaranteed to converge, and there is no "structural model mismatch" (e.g., unmodeled friction, backlash, latency, contact forces) for domain randomization to absorb. The study's conclusion that fidelity beats breadth is therefore applicable to this specific, identifiable regime and does not necessarily generalize to scenarios dominated by structural model mismatch, where randomization might indeed prove more valuable.
- **Estimate-Centered Randomization**: While the study did include a "prior-range baseline" that randomized over truth-bracketing bounds and still performed poorly, the primary randomization strategy centered the distribution around the current parameter estimate. At *n* = 0, this meant the randomization band might not cover the true parameters. This aspect, however, was mitigated by the prior-range baseline results. Future work could explore methods that adapt the randomization band from data.
- **Budget Axis Dependence**: The specific numerical "knee" (e.g., 10 rollouts) where identification benefits plateaued is dependent on the type of excitation policy used (random torque) and the experimental calibration (reality gap, noise level). More informative or Fisher-optimal excitation strategies could potentially achieve similar gains with fewer rollouts, making the reported budget axis a conservative estimate.
- **Other Caveats**: The study focused on a single task (pendulum) and a two-parameter dynamics family. Extremely wide randomization bands could lead to pathological dynamics or require more training steps than allotted. The statistical significance statements relied on paired bootstrap intervals over seed means rather than per-episode tests. Additionally, the best achieved return was noted as being short of an optimally controlled pendulum.
