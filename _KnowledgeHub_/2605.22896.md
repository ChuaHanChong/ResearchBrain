# Agentic-VLA: Efficient Online Adaptation for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The Agentic-VLA framework introduces an agentic training approach to enhance vision-language-action (VLA) models, enabling efficient online adaptation by integrating adaptive reward synthesis, language-guided exploration, and experience memory. This system from Scinetics achieves state-of-the-art performance with a 97.8% average success rate on the LIBERO benchmark, demonstrating a 2.4x faster convergence and a 2.4x reduction in sample complexity compared to prior methods.

## Problem

- Existing VLA models trained via supervised fine-tuning exhibit poor generalization, often memorizing trajectories and failing when environmental conditions deviate from training data.
- Current VLA training paradigms suffer from low efficiency, requiring hundreds of expert demonstrations per task which leads to high data collection costs and limits scalability.
- Prior online adaptation methods for VLAs often rely on noisy reward signals, inefficient random exploration strategies, and lack explicit mechanisms for transferring knowledge across related tasks.

## Method

- An agentic training framework is proposed that dynamically manages reward design, exploration, and knowledge transfer based on the VLA's current learning state.
- Adaptive Reward Synthesis (ARS) generates capability-aware, progress-based dense rewards by decomposing tasks into sub-goals and adjusting reward weights based on the VLA's mastery of each sub-goal.
- Language-Guided Exploration (LGE) replaces random exploration with structured, semantically-informed suggestions from a pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM), improving sample efficiency.
- Experience Memory (EM) enables cross-task knowledge transfer by storing and retrieving task-relevant policy parameters for warm-starting new tasks.

## Results

- Achieved an average 97.8% success rate on the LIBERO benchmark, an 8.6% improvement over the SFT baseline and 2.0% over the previous state-of-the-art RL method (EVOLVE-VLA).
- Demonstrated 2.4x faster convergence, reaching 90% success rate on LIBERO-Long in 700 iterations, and a 2.4x reduction in sample complexity compared to EVOLVE-VLA.
- Showed strong cross-task generalization, achieving 31.2% success in zero-shot transfer (vs. 0% for SFT) and outperforming baselines on the dual-arm RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, particularly under domain randomization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adjusting reward weights based on the VLA's competence in sub-goals creates an effective curriculum, significantly boosting performance on complex, long-horizon tasks.
> - Leveraging a VLM to provide natural language exploration suggestions offers a more efficient and targeted exploration strategy than random sampling for high-dimensional VLA action spaces.
> - Storing and interpolating policy parameters from previously adapted tasks facilitates robust warm-starting, enabling efficient cross-task knowledge transfer and adaptation to new, related tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robots capable of diverse manipulation tasks is a prominent area within robotics and artificial intelligence. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an advancement in this domain, leveraging pre-trained vision-language representations to interpret instructions and generate actions for robotic control. Existing VLA models, such as RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2022), OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024), and $\pi^0$ (Black et al., 2024), are primarily trained through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on expert demonstrations. While these models have demonstrated capabilities, they face two primary limitations that hinder real-world deployment.

First, these models exhibit poor generalization. They tend to memorize specific trajectories, leading to failures when environmental conditions deviate from the training distribution. A single error during execution can cascade into complete task failure due to a lack of intrinsic error recovery or adaptive replanning mechanisms.

Second, current VLA training paradigms suffer from low efficiency. They typically require hundreds of demonstrations per task, resulting in high data collection costs that scale linearly with the number of tasks. This dependency limits the practical scalability of VLAs to the broad range of tasks necessary for general-purpose robotics.

Recent research has begun to explore online adaptation for VLAs, often incorporating reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning to improve performance beyond imitation. Examples include VLA-RL (Lu et al., 2025), SimpleVLA-RL (Li et al., 2025), $\pi_{RL}$ (Chen et al., 2025), and EVOLVE-VLA (Bai et al., 2025). However, these approaches often depend on oracle reward signals or noisy learned rewards, use inefficient random exploration strategies, and lack explicit mechanisms for transferring knowledge across related tasks.

This work addresses these identified limitations by introducing an "agentic training framework." The authors propose that the learning process itself should be adaptive, with learned agents dynamically managing reward design, exploration, and knowledge transfer based on the current learning state. Agentic-VLA aims to enhance the generalization capabilities, training efficiency, and cross-task transfer of VLA models, positioning it as an advancement in online adaptation for robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to adapt efficiently and continuously in online settings, thereby addressing fundamental limitations of current VLA training paradigms. The paper's key motivations and specific objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Addressing the Generalization Challenge**: Current VLA models, primarily trained via supervised fine-tuning (SFT), demonstrate brittle generalization. They tend to memorize specific demonstrations rather than learning robust, underlying task semantics. This leads to failures when deployment conditions differ even marginally from the training data, and they lack mechanisms for error recovery or adaptive replanning.
 - **Objective**: To develop an adaptive framework that allows VLAs to generalize to novel environments, recover from errors during execution, and exhibit more robust behavior by learning from online interactions rather than solely from static demonstrations.

2. **Overcoming the Efficiency Challenge**: The training of existing VLA models typically demands extensive expert demonstrations (hundreds per task). This requirement creates a bottleneck for scaling VLAs to a diverse range of tasks in real-world environments due to the high costs and time associated with data collection.
 - **Objective**: To significantly improve the training efficiency and sample complexity of VLA models, reducing their reliance on large datasets of task-specific demonstrations and facilitating scalability for general-purpose robotics.

3. **Advancing Online Adaptation Methods**: While prior work has explored online adaptation through reinforcement learning (RL) for VLAs, these approaches often encounter specific challenges:
 - **Reliance on noisy reward signals**: Existing RL methods frequently depend on hand-engineered or imperfect learned reward functions that can mislead the learning process.
 - **Objective**: To develop an adaptive reward mechanism that dynamically generates and adjusts reward signals based on the VLA's evolving capabilities and task complexity, providing more stable and informative feedback.
 - **Inefficient exploration**: Traditional RL exploration, based on random sampling, is often inefficient for high-dimensional VLA action spaces, leading to slow learning.
 - **Objective**: To introduce a structured and systematic exploration strategy that guides the VLA toward promising behaviors more efficiently than random trial-and-error, leveraging semantic guidance.
 - **Limited knowledge transfer across tasks**: Many online adaptation methods learn each task in isolation, failing to leverage previously acquired knowledge from related tasks.
 - **Objective**: To implement a mechanism for storing and retrieving task-relevant knowledge, enabling effective cross-task transfer and warm-starting adaptation for similar or new tasks.

By addressing these specific challenges through an agentic training framework, the authors aim to move VLA systems closer to capabilities such as continuous learning and deployment in dynamic, open-ended real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates robotic manipulation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), where the state comprises visual observations, proprioceptive data, and a language-based task instruction. A VLA policy maps states to action distributions, with continuous actions discretized into tokens. The central methodological challenge addressed is the absence of oracle reward signals during deployment, necessitating effective online adaptation through self-generated feedback.

Agentic-VLA introduces three synergistic components: Adaptive Reward Synthesis (ARS), Language-Guided Exploration (LGE), and Experience Memory (EM). The training procedure involves an iterative loop:
1. **Warm-Starting**: For a new task, Experience Memory retrieves relevant prior policy weights for initialization.
2. **Interaction and Exploration**: The VLA interacts with the environment, guided by Language-Guided Exploration, which provides structured suggestions.
3. **Reward Generation**: Adaptive Reward Synthesis evaluates trajectories and generates appropriate rewards.
4. **Policy Update**: The VLA policy is updated using GRPO (Grouped Regularized Policy Optimization).
5. **Memory Update**: Successful adaptations are stored back in Experience Memory.

Each component is detailed below:

#### 3.1. Adaptive Reward Synthesis (ARS)

ARS dynamically generates and adjusts reward functions tailored to the VLA's current capabilities, addressing the challenge of noisy or unavailable oracle rewards.

- **Task Decomposition**: For complex, long-horizon tasks, ARS automatically decomposes the task instruction (l_task) into a sequence of sub-goals (G = {g1, g2..., gK}) using a language model (LM_decompose). For example, "turn on the stove and put the moka pot on it" might be decomposed into locating the stove, turning it on, grasping the pot, and placing it.
- **Capability-Aware Reward Adjustment**: ARS maintains a capability estimate (ˆc_k ∈) for each sub-goal g_k, which is an exponential moving average of recent success rates for that sub-goal. The reward weight (w_k) for each sub-goal is then computed as (1 - ˆc_k). This mechanism creates a curriculum, prioritizing sub-goals where the VLA is struggling (low ˆc_k) and diminishing weights for mastered sub-goals.
- **Progress-Based Dense Rewards**: For each sub-goal, a learned critic model, VLAC (Zhai et al., 2025), provides dense progress estimates (∆_k(τ)) for a given trajectory τ. The total reward R(τ) is calculated as the sum of capability-adjusted weights (w_k) multiplied by their respective progress estimates (∆_k(τ)), ensuring informative feedback focused on the current learning frontier.

#### 3.2. Language-Guided Exploration (LGE)

LGE replaces random exploration with structured, semantically-informed guidance, enhancing sample efficiency.

- **VLM-Based Exploration Critic**: A pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025), is utilized as a zero-shot critic. It analyzes the current visual observation, task instruction, and recent trajectory context. A structured prompt instructs the VLM to act as a manipulation expert, generating natural language suggestions (s_explore) to identify issues and propose improvements (e.g., "try approaching the object from the left side").
- **Suggestion-Conditioned Action Generation**: The generated exploration suggestions (s_explore) are concatenated with the original task instruction (l_task) to augment the prompt for the VLA policy's action generation: a_t ∼ π_θ(a|s_t, l_task ⊕ s_explore). This biases the action distribution toward suggested behaviors while retaining stochasticity. Suggestions are only provided during exploration phases.
- **Adaptive Suggestion Frequency**: The frequency of exploration suggestions (p_suggest(t)) is dynamically adjusted. It decreases exponentially as the VLA's smoothed recent reward (¯R(t)) improves, allowing the policy to develop autonomous competence over time.

#### 3.3. Experience Memory (EM)

EM enables cross-task knowledge transfer by storing and retrieving task-relevant policy parameters.

- **Memory Structure**: The memory bank M stores tuples (e_i, θ_i, m_i), where e_i is a task embedding (computed by a frozen language encoder from l_task), θ_i are the adapted policy parameters, and m_i contains metadata (e.g., success rate, training iterations).
- **Warm-Start Retrieval**: When a new task with instruction l_new is encountered, its embedding e_new is computed. The k most similar stored tasks are retrieved from M based on cosine similarity. The initial policy parameters (θ_init) for the new task are then computed as a weighted combination of the retrieved policy parameters, with weights determined by their similarity to e_new. The authors state that this weight-space interpolation is robust because all policies share the same architecture, originate from a common pre-trained VLA initialization (OpenVLA-OFT), and undergo relatively local adaptations. The retrieval is sharply peaked toward the most similar tasks, and the interpolated parameters serve as an initialization that can be refined by subsequent online adaptation.
- **Memory Update**: After successful adaptation to a new task, its task embedding, adapted policy parameters, and metadata are added to the memory bank. A capacity management strategy prioritizes diverse, high-quality entries.

The policy optimization is performed using GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), starting from an OpenVLA-OFT (Kim et al., 2025) base model. The authors conduct experiments using Llama-3-8B for task decomposition and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct for the exploration critic.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Agentic-VLA was conducted on the LIBERO benchmark (Liu et al., 2023), encompassing Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long task suites, and extended to the dual-arm RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark for assessing generalization. Key metrics reported include Success Rate (SR) and Progress (normalized task completion). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation over 5 independent seeds for LIBERO experiments.

#### 4.1. Main Results on LIBERO Benchmark

- **Overall Performance**: Agentic-VLA achieved an average success rate of 97.8% across the LIBERO benchmark, establishing a state-of-the-art performance. This represents an 8.6% improvement over the SFT baseline (OpenVLA-OFT) and a 2.0% improvement over the previous state-of-the-art RL method, EVOLVE-VLA.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks**: The most significant gains were observed on LIBERO-Long tasks, where Agentic-VLA demonstrated a +12.3% improvement over the SFT baseline and +3.7% over EVOLVE-VLA. This indicates the effectiveness of the adaptive reward synthesis and curriculum learning for complex, multi-step tasks.
- **Consistency**: Improvements were consistent across all task suites: +5.9% on Spatial, +8.5% on Object, and +7.6% on Goal, relative to the SFT baseline. The low standard deviations (e.g., ±0.4 on average SR) suggest stability across multiple runs.

#### 4.2. Low-Data Regime (One-Shot Learning)

- In the challenging one-shot learning setting (a single demonstration per task for pre-training), Agentic-VLA achieved an average success rate of 70.5%. This is a +26.9% improvement over the SFT baseline and +9.2% over EVOLVE-VLA.
- The largest improvements were on LIBERO-Object (+38.3%) and LIBERO-Long (+36.1%), suggesting the framework's ability to leverage online learning and knowledge transfer when supervised data is scarce.

#### 4.3. Cross-Task Generalization

- Agentic-VLA demonstrated the capability for cross-task transfer without task-specific demonstrations. When trained on LIBERO-Long and evaluated on LIBERO-Object without specific demonstrations, it achieved a 31.2% success rate, whereas direct SFT transfer yielded 0%.
- This represents a +10.4% improvement in success rate and +14.5% in task progress compared to EVOLVE-VLA in the same zero-shot transfer scenario.

#### 4.4. Training Efficiency

- Agentic-VLA exhibited faster convergence, reaching 90% success rate on LIBERO-Long in 700 training iterations, which is 2.4× faster than EVOLVE-VLA (1,680 iterations).
- It also showed a 2.4× reduction in sample complexity, requiring only 22.4k rollouts compared to 53.8k for EVOLVE-VLA.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies and Controlled Comparisons

- Ablation studies confirmed that each of the three core components (Adaptive Reward Synthesis, Language-Guided Exploration, Experience Memory) contributes to both success rate and convergence speed. Removing any component led to a degradation in performance.
- Controlled comparisons against alternative curricula (e.g., uniform, fixed-schedule, learning-progress sampling), exploration bonuses (RND, ICM), and retrieval methods (random retrieval) indicated that Agentic-VLA's specific design choices for ARS, LGE, and EM were more effective. For instance, capability-aware ARS outperformed other curriculum strategies, and language-guided exploration proved more effective than reward-agnostic novelty for VLA action spaces.

#### 4.6. Analysis of Adaptive Reward Synthesis

- Visualization demonstrated that reward weights dynamically adjusted during training, shifting focus from mastered sub-goals to those where the VLA struggled, effectively implementing a curriculum.
- LM-generated task decompositions achieved 0.91 coverage of human oracle sub-goals (compared to 0.94 for oracle), resulting in a success rate (98.1%) comparable to using oracle decompositions (98.4%).

#### 4.7. Generalization to RoboTwin 2.0

- On the dual-arm RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, Agentic-VLA consistently outperformed strong baselines (RDT and $\pi^0$) in both Easy and Hard settings. The performance margin was particularly pronounced under the Hard setting, which includes domain randomization (e.g., clutter, lighting, texture, table height), where Agentic-VLA achieved an average subset success rate of 34.7% compared to 16.3% for $\pi^0$ and 13.7% for RDT. This suggests the adaptation mechanisms are robust to different task structures, embodiments, and visual variations.

#### 4.8. Qualitative Analysis

- The framework enabled emergent capabilities: error recovery (e.g., autonomously re-adjusting a grasp after a slip), adaptive object handling (e.g., adjusting trajectory for a displaced knob), novel strategy discovery (e.g., side-grasp from LGE suggestion), and structured exploration (e.g., LGE suggesting correct sub-task order).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Agentic-VLA represents an advancement in the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by addressing key limitations related to generalization, training efficiency, and cross-task knowledge transfer. Its design, integrating adaptive reward synthesis, language-guided exploration, and experience memory, moves VLA systems closer to being capable of continuous learning and adaptation in diverse and dynamic environments.

The demonstrated improvements in success rates across various task complexities, particularly for long-horizon and low-data scenarios, indicate its potential to reduce the reliance on extensive expert demonstrations. The 2.4× faster convergence and reduced sample complexity are practical benefits, lowering the computational resources and real-world interaction time required for training and fine-tuning robotic policies. The ability to achieve non-trivial success rates in cross-task transfer, without task-specific demonstrations, suggests an enhanced capacity for autonomous skill acquisition and repurposing. Furthermore, its performance on the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark under domain randomization indicates robustness beyond a single benchmark environment.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The paper acknowledges several limitations and areas for future research:
- **Reward Hacking**: Despite the adaptive nature of reward synthesis, a misalignment can occur between the semantic understanding of the progress estimator and the strict environment success criteria, potentially leading to policies that maximize estimated progress without fully completing the task. Future work could focus on improved calibration between these signals.
- **Memory Scalability**: The current experience memory stores full policy weights. As the number of tasks grows, this approach may become resource-intensive. Exploring more compact policy representations, such as storing only adapter weights or task-specific residuals, is a direction for scalability.
- **Real-World Deployment Challenges**: While the work shows promise in simulation, deploying a component-rich system like Agentic-VLA on physical robots introduces complexities. These include reliably integrating perception, VLM-based language feedback, online policy updates, progress estimation, and memory retrieval under real-world noise and safety constraints. Error propagation among modules is a concern. The authors suggest that the framework's sample efficiency and warm-start capabilities could mitigate some real-world costs. Staged real-world validation, including sim-to-real transfer of individual components like the progress estimator and VLM exploration critic, is considered an important next step.
- **Safety of Exploration**: Autonomous learning through exploration, even language-guided, raises safety considerations in physical environments. Ensuring safe behavior during early training stages remains an open challenge.

Overall, Agentic-VLA contributes a framework that could facilitate the development of more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems, with the potential to broaden the applicability of VLA models in complex, real-world manipulation tasks, subject to continued research on its identified limitations.
