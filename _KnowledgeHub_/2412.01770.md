# Robot Learning with Super-Linear Scaling

> [!summary] Summary
> The CASHER pipeline enables super-linear scaling of robot learning data with sub-linear human effort by using crowdsourced digital twins and an iterative training process for generalist policies. This approach resulted in a 62% zero-shot success rate for a manipulation task in unseen real-world kitchens and improved performance by an average of 55% through unsupervised fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Robot learning is constrained by the scarcity and high cost of obtaining diverse, high-quality training data.
- Existing data collection methods, such as teleoperation, scale linearly with human effort, limiting the acquisition of large datasets needed for generalist robot capabilities.
- Generating realistic and diverse data in simulation requires significant human effort from designers for scene, task, and reward creation, hindering scalability.

## Method

- **CASHER** (==Crowdsourcing and Amortizing Human Effort for Real-to-Sim-to-Real==) is introduced, a pipeline for continual data collection and training of generalist robotic policies.
- Leverages ==crowdsourced 3D reconstructions== of real-world scenes to create diverse ==digital twins== in a physics simulator, reducing the need for expert-designed content.
- Implements an ==amortized data collection== strategy where a continually improving generalist policy generates demonstrations for new simulated environments, progressively reducing the reliance on human input.

## Results

- Achieved a **62%** zero-shot success rate for the 'put object into sink' task on **8** unseen real-world kitchens after training on **56** environments, demonstrating strong sim-to-real correlation (**R²=0.92**).
- Showcased significant amortization of human effort, with the number of human demonstrations needed per new environment decreasing over time due to the generalist policy's improved performance.
- Unsupervised scanned deployment fine-tuning boosted performance by an average of **55%** on target scenes where the generalist policy initially had **≤20%** success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Super-linear scaling of robot learning data is achievable with sub-linear human effort by combining crowdsourced digital twins and a self-improving generalist policy.
> - Model-generated demonstrations, being consistent with learned behaviors, contribute to more robust policy learning and can gradually replace the need for human-provided data.
> - Unsupervised fine-tuning, using only a video scan of a new environment, allows pre-trained generalist policies to significantly adapt and improve performance without additional human demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot learning aims to enable robots to learn behaviors from data, facilitating their deployment in unstructured environments with improved generalization and robustness. A central challenge in this field, analogous to other machine learning domains, is acquiring a sufficient quantity and quality of diverse training data. Unlike vision and language domains, where vast datasets are passively available, robot learning data is expensive to collect.

Current approaches to data collection for robotics include:
- **Teleoperation:** This method involves human operators controlling robots to generate demonstrations. While effective for obtaining high-quality data, it scales linearly with human effort and is therefore limited in its ability to generate the large-scale datasets required for emergent capabilities seen in other AI fields. Efforts to scale teleoperation have increased data volume, but it remains significantly smaller than vision and language datasets.
- **Autonomous Learning (Reinforcement Learning - RL):** Real-world RL can reduce human intervention but faces challenges such as the need for frequent resets, high sample complexity for complex tasks, and the difficulty of learning in high-dimensional real-world settings. Simulation offers advantages for RL by providing manageable resets and abundant data, but it incurs significant costs in scene, task, and reward creation.
- **Procedural and Synthetic Data Generation:** This involves generating environments and behaviors computationally. While it can produce large amounts of data, randomly generated environments may not accurately reflect the distribution of real-world scenarios, and generating effective behaviors procedurally requires prior assumptions.
- **Real-to-Sim-to-Real Transfer:** This paradigm aims to leverage the benefits of simulation while mitigating the "sim-to-real gap." Policies are trained in simulation and then transferred to the real world. However, existing real-to-sim-to-real methods often struggle with generalization to new environments, requiring significant human effort for adaptation to each new scenario.

This work addresses the data scarcity problem by proposing a pipeline that aims to scale data collection for robot learning "superlinearly" with "sublinear" human effort. It positions itself by leveraging the advantages of simulation (abundant data, manageable resets) and real-world realism (via digital twins), while aiming to overcome the traditional scaling limitations of both. The key innovation lies in amortizing human effort through crowdsourced digital twins and model generalization, contrasting with methods that scale linearly with human involvement or rely on potentially unrealistic synthetic data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data collection and learning pipeline for robotics that scales more efficiently than current methods, specifically achieving "super-linear" scaling of data with "sub-linear" human effort. This addresses the fundamental bottleneck of data scarcity in robot learning, which hinders the development of generalist policies capable of operating in diverse, unstructured environments.

The motivation stems from several critical observations in the field:
- **Limited Data Availability:** Unlike other machine learning domains, data for robot learning is not passively available. Robots are not yet ubiquitous enough to generate vast quantities of incidental interaction data.
- **High Cost of Real-World Data Collection:** Collecting data via teleoperation in the real world is inherently limited by human effort, requiring expertise, physical infrastructure, and incurring costs that scale linearly with the amount of data collected. This prevents the acquisition of data scales comparable to those used in vision and language models.
- **Challenges in Simulation Data Generation:** While simulation offers the potential for "free" data, creating realistic scenes, defining tasks, and designing rewards in diverse environments still demands significant human effort from designers and engineers. Procedural generation alone often fails to capture the complexity and distribution of "natural environments."
- **Lack of Generalization in Existing Real-to-Sim-to-Real:** While real-to-sim-to-real techniques can be effective for specific scenarios, they often lack the ability to generalize across different environments without substantial re-training or additional human input.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose **Crowdsourcing and Amortizing Human Effort for Real-to-Sim-to-Real (CASHER)**. The core motivations and objectives are:
1. **Scaling Content Generation:** To address the cost of scene creation in simulation, the objective is to shift the burden from expert designers to non-expert users and cheap data collection methods. This is achieved by leveraging 3D reconstruction techniques to crowdsource digital twins of real-world scenes, which are then brought into simulation.
2. **Amortizing Behavior Generation:** To address the cost of generating optimal behaviors in diverse simulated environments, the motivation is to use model generalization to reduce the required human data over time. The objective is to create a "data flywheel" where an improving generalist policy can progressively replace human effort in generating demonstrations for new environments.
3. **Enabling Generalist Policies:** The ultimate goal is to train generalist visuomotor policies that exhibit non-trivial zero-shot generalization capabilities across a wide range of environments and tasks.
4. **Efficient Fine-tuning:** To bridge the gap between generalist performance and optimal task execution in specific target environments, an objective is to develop efficient fine-tuning methods that require minimal or no additional human demonstrations, relying instead on environment scans.

The paper outlines four specific contributions:
1. A novel continual data collection system based on real-to-sim-to-real for training generalist policies.
2. A novel scanned deployment fine-tuning technique for improving generalist policy accuracy on a target environment without additional human demonstrations.
3. A detailed analysis of scaling laws for zero-shot performance of generalist policies.
4. Evaluation of few-shot performance for these generalist policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CASHER is a real-to-sim-to-real pipeline designed for large-scale, continual data collection to train generalist robotic manipulation policies. It comprises three main elements: fast digital twin generation, multi-environment model learning with amortized data collection, and efficient fine-tuning in new environments.

#### 3.1. A. Real-to-Sim Scene Synthesis

The pipeline begins with creating digital twins of real-world scenes in simulation. This process offers advantages such as decentralized, asynchronous data collection without physical robot setups and more accurate representation of real-world complexities than procedurally generated simulations.
- **Crowdsourced 3D Reconstruction:** Non-expert users employ mobile software (e.g., Polycam, ARCode) to perform 3D reconstruction from real-world videos or image sequences. These methods generate high-fidelity 3D meshes using photogrammetry techniques like Gaussian splatting or Neural Radiance Fields. This enables crowdsourcing of diverse environments with varied layouts, colors, and lighting conditions.
- **Simulation Integration and Curation:** The reconstructed real-world scans are transferred into a physics simulator (e.g., Isaac Sim). A user-friendly Graphical User Interface (GUI) is utilized for scene articulation and curation, allowing for the addition of objects of interest (e.g., bowls, mugs, boxes) and marking task-relevant sites (e.g., sinks, cabinets). This process accommodates static and articulated environments.

#### 3.2. B. Amortized Data Collection

Given the diverse simulated scenes, CASHER aims to efficiently generate visuomotor trajectories demonstrating optimal behavior for learning generalizable decision-making policies.
- **Hybrid Approach for Trajectory Generation:** The approach balances human effort with robust state coverage. It starts with sparse-reward reinforcement learning (RL) bootstrapped by human demonstrations. This addresses exploration and reward design challenges inherent in pure RL while overcoming the scalability limitations of purely human-provided data.
- **Iterative Learning Process:** The total number of environments is divided into batches of size *K*.
 1. **Initial Batch (K environments):** For the first batch, a multi-environment visuomotor policy ($\pi_G$) is initialized. Optimal visuomotor trajectories are generated using RL fine-tuning, bootstrapped by human-provided demonstrations for each environment. These trajectories (containing visual observations, actions, and low-dimensional privileged Lagrangian states) are then distilled into a single, perception-based generalist multi-environment policy ($\pi_G$) using visuomotor policy distillation. The RL fine-tuning is performed using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a Behavioral Cloning (BC) loss on privileged state-based policies ($\pi_s$).
 2. **Subsequent Batches:** For subsequent batches of *K* environments, the key insight is to leverage the generalization capabilities of the current $\pi_G$.
 - **Model-Generated Demonstrations:** $\pi_G$ is deployed in the new simulated environments to autonomously collect simulated demonstrations (T). These demonstrations include paired visual observations, actions, and privileged state information.
 - **State-Based Policy Training:** The collected privileged state-based trajectories (T) are used to train a robust state-based policy ($\pi_s^1$) for the new environments via demonstration-bootstrapped RL.
 - **Human Fallback:** Environments where $\pi_s^1$ performs below a specified success rate are identified as a "failed set" (F). For these environments, human demonstrators are queried to provide high-quality demonstrations, which are then used to train a second state-based policy ($\pi_s^2$) for the failed set.
 - **Data Aggregation and Generalist Policy Update:** Trajectories from $\pi_s^1$ (for non-failed environments) and $\pi_s^2$ (for failed environments) are added to the growing dataset (D). The visuomotor generalist policy ($\pi_G$) is then updated by fitting D across all accumulated environments using supervised learning (teacher-student distillation). This involves training a policy from high-dimensional perceptual observations (e.g., colored point clouds) by distilling the knowledge from the state-based policies. The process retains data from previous batches to mitigate catastrophic forgetting.
- **Scaling Law and Efficiency:** As $\pi_G$ trains across more environments, its generalization improves, progressively reducing the need for human demonstrations in new environments. This results in the "superlinear scaling" of data collection with "sublinear" human effort, creating a data flywheel effect.

#### 3.3. C. Fine-tuning of Generalist Policies on Deployment

Generalist policies may not achieve optimal performance in specific test environments. CASHER proposes two fine-tuning methods:
- **Unsupervised Scanned Deployment Fine-tuning:** For a new target environment ($E_{test}$), a digital twin is created via scanning. The pre-trained $\pi_G$ is executed in $E_{test}$ to autonomously collect a dataset of successful trajectories ($T_{test}$). This model-generated data (without human intervention) is then used to train a state-based policy ($\pi_s$) using demonstration-bootstrapped RL. Finally, $\pi_s$ is distilled into a fine-tuned visuomotor policy ($\pi_{Gf}$), which can be deployed in the real world from visual observations. This leverages the generalist policy's initial capabilities to generate self-improvement data.
- **Few-shot Supervised Fine-tuning:** This alternative involves collecting a limited set of human-provided real-world demonstrations ($D_h$) for the target environment. The generalist policy ($\pi_G$) is then fine-tuned using supervised learning (maximum likelihood) on $D_h$. Architecturally, this typically involves freezing early visual processing layers and fine-tuning only the final fully-connected layers.

#### 3.4. D. Implementation Details

State-based policies use Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). Point cloud policies utilize a volumetric 3D point cloud encoder (based on Convolutional Occupancy Networks) to convert point cloud observations into embeddings, which are then fed into an MLP along with robot state information. To handle the large datasets (tens of thousands of trajectories, millions of state-action pairs), data is stored on disk, and batches are queried dynamically to manage memory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of CASHER focused on understanding its scaling laws, amortization capabilities, fine-tuning efficacy, cross-task applicability, and extrapolation to multi-object scenarios. Experiments were conducted using a Franka Research 3 arm on two primary tasks: placing objects (bowls, mugs, cups) into sinks and placing boxes onto shelves/opening cabinets. Environments were crowdsourced, totaling 56 scenes for the sink task and 36 for the shelf task, with real-world evaluations conducted across two institutions on unseen scenes.

#### 4.1. A. Zero-Shot Scaling Laws Analysis

- **Performance Scaling:** As the number of training environments increased, the zero-shot success rate of generalist policies in real-world evaluations consistently improved. For the "put object into sink" task, training on 56 environments resulted in a 62% success rate, a significant increase from policies trained on fewer environments (e.g., 16% for 9 environments). This demonstrates superlinear scaling of performance with training data diversity.
- **Sim-to-Real Correlation:** A strong linear correlation ($R^2 = 0.92$) was observed between performance in simulation and performance in the real world. This indicates that improvements in simulated training directly translate to real-world capabilities, validating the real-to-sim-to-real pipeline.
- **Robustness and Extrapolation:**
 - **Diverse Environments:** Policies showed improved robustness, with success rates increasing from 16% to 60% when evaluated across eight additional real-world kitchens not included in the training set, confirming the generalization across diverse settings.
 - **Environmental Disturbances:** Policies maintained success rates above 30% even under challenging conditions such as extreme lighting changes, environmental clutter, and physical disturbances, although performance decreased.
 - **Multi-Object Extrapolation:** When trained on single-object scenes, the policies demonstrated an ability to extrapolate to multi-object scenarios, successfully placing two objects 80% of the time and three objects 10% of the time sequentially in a scene with multiple items.

#### 4.2. B. Amortized Human Data Needed Through Continual Data Collection

- **Reduced Human Effort:** CASHER, with its continual data collection mechanism, demonstrated a significant reduction in the quantity of human demonstrations required as training progressed across multiple batches of environments. The number of human demonstrations needed decreased over time, confirming the amortization hypothesis.
- **Improved Performance:** The continually learned policy (CASHER amortized) achieved higher overall performance compared to a baseline where human demonstrations were provided for each environment individually (CASHER non-amortized). This suggests that model-generated data, being consistent with learned behaviors, can lead to more robust policy learning than the variability introduced by human demonstrations.
- **Compute Efficiency:** While CASHER shifts the burden from human effort to computational resources, the compute time required also tended to decrease as the system scaled. This is attributed to the generalist policy's improved success rate, which reduces the number of trials needed to collect successful autonomous demonstrations.

#### 4.3. C. Fine-Tuning of Generalist Policies

- **Unsupervised Scanned Deployment Fine-tuning:** This method enabled a substantial improvement in performance without any additional human demonstrations. On target scenes where the generalist policy initially performed poorly ($\le$ 20% success), unsupervised fine-tuning using only a video scan of the environment resulted in an average performance increase of 55%.
- **Few-shot Supervised Fine-tuning:** Utilizing a small number of human demonstrations (10 demos) for fine-tuning led to an average performance increase of 54% in scenarios where the base policy initially had low success rates ($\le$ 20%).

#### 4.4. D. Analysis of CASHER on More Tasks

- **Task Generalization:** CASHER was successfully applied to two additional tasks: "putting a box on a cabinet" (requiring more precise grasping) and "opening a cabinet" (involving articulated objects).
- **Performance on New Tasks:** For both tasks, performance increased with the number of training environments, indicating that the generalization capabilities are not saturated. Few-shot fine-tuning on 10 demonstrations significantly improved performance by 36% and 30% respectively compared to an imitation learning baseline (which had 0% success).

#### 4.5. E. Baseline Comparisons with Large Scale Real-World Data Collection

- **Comparison with Open X-Embodiment Models:** Evaluation against state-of-the-art robot models trained on large-scale real-world datasets (OpenVLA and Octo) revealed that these models yielded 0% success rates on the target tasks in zero-shot settings. Even after fine-tuning Octo with 10 demonstrations, success rates remained at 0%. This highlights the current challenges in zero-shot and few-shot generalization for complex manipulation tasks using solely real-world data and underscores the potential benefits of CASHER's simulation-centric approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents CASHER, a system designed to address the data scarcity bottleneck in robot learning by enabling super-linear scaling of data collection with sub-linear human effort. The work demonstrates a functional pipeline for training generalist policies through a real-to-sim-to-real approach, leveraging crowdsourced digital twins and model generalization.

The significance and potential impact of CASHER are multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Large-Scale Data for Robot Learning:** CASHER offers a scalable methodology for generating vast, diverse datasets by moving data collection into simulation using crowdsourced real-world scans. This could potentially bridge the gap between the data volumes currently available for robot learning and those that have driven advancements in other AI fields like vision and language.
- **Reduced Human Reliance:** By continuously reducing the human effort required for data collection as generalist policies improve, CASHER paves the way for more autonomous and cost-effective robot learning. This amortization of human labor is critical for scaling robot capabilities beyond laboratory settings.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** The demonstrated improvements in zero-shot success rates with increasing training environments, coupled with robustness to environmental disturbances and extrapolation to multi-object scenarios, indicate that CASHER can produce more generalized and reliable robot policies.
- **Efficient Deployment and Adaptation:** The unsupervised scanned deployment fine-tuning technique allows pre-trained generalist policies to adapt to new, unseen real-world environments with a simple video scan and no additional human demonstrations. This capability significantly lowers the barrier to deploying robots in novel situations.
- **Foundation for Robotic Foundation Models:** By facilitating the creation of larger, more diverse datasets and the training of generalist policies, CASHER contributes to the ongoing development of "robotic foundation models." These models aim to provide broad capabilities that can be fine-tuned for specific tasks, similar to large language models.
- **Democratization of Data Collection:** The reliance on easily accessible mobile software for 3D reconstruction enables non-experts worldwide to contribute to the creation of diverse simulated environments, potentially democratizing robot data collection.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations. The current approach shifts the data collection burden from human effort to computational resources. While an observed reduction in compute time with scaling is reported, the overall computational demand still exceeds that of collecting real-world demonstrations. Furthermore, current simulators still face challenges in accurately modeling complex real-world phenomena like liquids or deformable objects, limiting the types of tasks CASHER can currently address. However, the authors posit that as compute resources advance and simulator research progresses, systems like CASHER are well-positioned to benefit from these improvements, further enhancing their scalability and applicability.

In conclusion, CASHER provides a framework for scaling robot learning through an iterative, self-improving data collection process in simulation, moving towards a future where robots can acquire robust and generalizable skills with significantly less human intervention.
