# Towards Metric-Agnostic Trajectory Forecasting

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a paradigm for trajectory forecasting by training models to output a well-calibrated, metric-agnostic predictive distribution rather than optimizing for specific evaluation metrics. The framework employs metric-specific post-processing policies to adapt the learned distribution, enabling a single model to achieve state-of-the-art performance on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset across both distance-based and window-based metrics.

## Problem

- Trajectory forecasting models are often tailored to specific evaluation metrics, leading to inconsistent performance across different benchmarks and obscuring genuine advancements.
- The tight coupling between training and benchmark metrics makes it difficult to assess whether improvements stem from better forecasting capabilities or better metric-specific tuning.
- Benchmark metrics typically evaluate only a fixed set of K predicted trajectories, providing an incomplete assessment of the underlying probabilistic distribution's quality or calibration.

## Method

- A paradigm shift is proposed, advocating for training models with ==metric-agnostic probabilistic objectives== (e.g., ==negative log-likelihood==) to learn well-calibrated predictive distributions.
- ==Trajectory Distribution Evaluation (TraDiE)== policies are introduced as ==sampling-based, metric-specific post-processing== techniques to map a learned predictive distribution to K trajectories and confidences for standard evaluation metrics.
- The framework is instantiated with **DONUT-NLL**, an adapted ==decoder-only transformer==, and systematically compares various ==position distribution families== (Laplace, ==Generalized Gaussian==, Gaussian Scale Mixture) for learning predictive distributions.

## Results

- TraDiE policies consistently improved performance across distance-based (minFDE) and window-based (soft mAP, mAP, miss rate) metrics when applied to NLL-trained models.
- DONUT-NLL, using Step-NLL and a generalized Gaussian position distribution, achieved state-of-the-art performance on the Waymo test set across various metrics, outperforming existing methods (including ensembles) with approximately **5x fewer parameters**.
- The policies successfully applied to open-source baselines (MTR, QCNet), demonstrating their model-agnostic nature and revealing insights into the quality and potential miscalibrations of their predictive distributions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing the predictive distribution directly via negative log-likelihood (NLL), especially with time-dependent mixture weights (Step-NLL), is more beneficial than optimizing a metric-specific loss.
> - TraDiE policies serve as a diagnostic tool, revealing miscalibration in models trained with winner-takes-all objectives or those with misaligned position distributions.
> - The Generalized Gaussian distribution offers superior flexibility for learning well-calibrated predictive distributions compared to Laplace or Gaussian scale mixtures, particularly when combined with NLL training and policy optimization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate trajectory forecasting is a critical capability for autonomous driving systems, enabling proactive anticipation of other traffic participants' behaviors and facilitating safe maneuver planning. Human driving behavior is inherently non-deterministic and multimodal, necessitating models that can predict multiple plausible future trajectories and quantify associated uncertainties for downstream applications.

Historically, trajectory forecasting models have evolved from early approaches utilizing bird's-eye-view rasterizations and convolutional neural networks to more modern techniques that employ sparse input representations for agents and map elements, often leveraging graph neural networks and attention mechanisms. Current state-of-the-art models frequently represent uncertainty through a fixed number of possible futures, typically as modes of Gaussian or Laplacian mixtures. Training these models often involves a winner-takes-all (WTA) loss, where the mode closest to the ground truth is optimized using negative log-likelihood or L1/L2 losses. Post-processing steps, such as k-means clustering or non-maximum suppression (NMS), are also common to refine outputs for specific metrics.

A key observation in the current landscape is that many leading trajectory forecasting models are specifically tailored to the evaluation metrics of popular benchmarks, such as Argoverse 2 (which emphasizes distance-based metrics like Brier-minFDE) and the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (which uses window-based metrics like soft mAP). This metric-specific optimization often leads to models that perform well on one set of metrics but poorly on others, due to the inherently conflicting objectives these metrics encourage (e.g., precise positioning for distance-based metrics versus broad coverage for window-based metrics). Consequently, assessing whether model improvements stem from genuinely better forecasting capabilities or merely better metric-specific tuning becomes challenging. Moreover, these benchmark metrics do not directly evaluate the quality or calibration of the underlying predictive distribution generated by the model.

This work proposes a departure from this trend, advocating for a paradigm shift where the primary focus is on learning a well-calibrated, metric-agnostic predictive distribution. The authors argue that optimizing for evaluation metrics should be treated as a downstream task, executed through specific post-processing policies applied to the learned distribution. This approach aims to foster the development of more general and robust forecasting models, allowing researchers to concentrate on the fundamental challenges of representing future trajectories and their uncertainties. The research draws inspiration from prior work that explored decoupling distribution estimation from reporting, such as HOME, but aims to establish a more general, model-agnostic, and theoretically grounded framework directly derived from metric definitions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of current trajectory forecasting practices, where models are often designed and trained with a strong bias towards specific evaluation metrics. This tight coupling between model training and benchmark metrics leads to several problems:
1. **Conflicting Goals**: Different benchmark metrics encode distinct, sometimes opposing, objectives. For instance, distance-based metrics like minFDE reward models for predicting trajectories that are extremely close to the ground truth, potentially by clustering predictions tightly. Conversely, window-based metrics like soft mAP prioritize broad coverage of the future state space, penalizing redundant predictions within the same window. Models optimized for one type of metric tend to perform suboptimally on the other.
2. **Obscured Progress**: When models are heavily tuned to specific metrics, it becomes difficult to ascertain whether reported improvements reflect genuine advancements in forecasting capability or simply better alignment with the nuances of a particular metric. This can hinder scientific progress by diverting focus from fundamental improvements in predictive modeling.
3. **Incomplete Distribution Assessment**: Benchmark metrics typically evaluate only a finite set of K predicted trajectories and their confidences, providing a partial view of a model's overall predictive quality. They do not directly assess the fidelity or calibration of the underlying probabilistic distribution that the model implicitly or explicitly represents. A poorly calibrated distribution, even if it yields good benchmark scores through aggressive tuning, may not be suitable for diverse downstream applications.

In response to these issues, the authors propose a paradigm change with the following key objectives:
1. **Advocate a Paradigm Shift**: To promote the training of trajectory forecasting models using metric-agnostic probabilistic objectives. The aim is to learn well-calibrated predictive distributions that provide a faithful representation of uncertainty, independent of the specific metrics used for evaluation. Metric optimization is then treated as a separate, downstream task.
2. **Develop Metric-Specific Policies**: To introduce Trajectory Distribution Evaluation (TraDiE) policies. These are sampling-based, metric-specific post-processing techniques designed to map a predictive distribution to the set of K trajectories and confidences required by standard trajectory forecasting metrics (e.g., minFDE, (soft) mAP, and miss rate). Crucially, these policies operate on the output of a single, underlying model without requiring retraining.
3. **Instantiate and Validate the Framework**: To apply this new paradigm to a state-of-the-art model, DONUT, by developing DONUT-NLL. This variant adapts DONUT's training objective to directly optimize the predictive distribution using negative log-likelihood (NLL) and uses it as a testbed to demonstrate the effectiveness of distribution-centric training combined with the proposed TraDiE policies.
4. **Systematically Compare Position Distributions**: To investigate which position distribution families (Laplace, Generalized Gaussian, Gaussian Scale Mixture) are best suited for learning well-calibrated predictive distributions within this framework.
5. **Demonstrate Model Agnosticism**: To apply the TraDiE policies to other strong open-source baseline models (MTR and QCNet) to confirm that the policies are generally applicable and can reveal insights into the quality of their predictive distributions, including potential miscalibrations.

The overarching motivation is to refocus the trajectory forecasting community on developing models that produce high-quality, calibrated predictive distributions, which can then serve as a flexible and general interface for various downstream tasks, including optimizing for diverse evaluation metrics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on decoupling the training objective from the evaluation metrics by introducing metric-specific post-processing policies applied to a learned predictive distribution.

**Predictive Distribution Learning**:
The core idea is that models should be trained to output a calibrated probabilistic distribution $p_n(X_n)$ over an agent's future trajectories $X_n$. This distribution is intended to be a general representation of uncertainty. To achieve this, the authors adapt the training of a trajectory forecasting model to directly optimize the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the ground-truth trajectories under the predicted distribution.

**Trajectory Distribution Evaluation (TraDiE) Policies**:
For evaluation, existing benchmarks require a fixed set of $K$ predicted trajectories and associated confidences, $\{( \hat{X}_{nk}, \hat{\pi}_{nk} )\}_{k=1}^K$. TraDiE policies are defined as mappings $P: p_n(X_n) \rightarrow \{( \hat{X}_{nk}, \hat{\pi}_{nk} )\}_{k=1}^K$, designed to generate these outputs optimally for a specific metric given the model's predictive distribution. For efficiency and broad applicability, these policies primarily operate on the marginal predictive endpoint distribution $p_{nT}(x_{nT})$, which is the distribution of the agent's position at the final timestep $T$.

- **Policies for Distance-Based Metrics (e.g., minFDE)**:
 For minFDE, the objective is to minimize the expected Euclidean distance between the ground-truth endpoint and the closest of the $K$ predicted endpoints, under the model's predictive distribution. This is formulated as $E_{x_{nT} \sim p_{nT}} [ \min_k \| \hat{x}_{nkT}^{pos} - x_{nT}^{pos} \|_2 ]$. This expectation is approximated using Monte Carlo sampling, drawing $M=3000$ samples from $p_{nT}(x_{nT})$. The $K$ predicted endpoints $\{\hat{x}_{nkT}^{pos}\}$ are then treated as free variables and optimized through gradient descent (specifically, 300 steps of Adam with a learning rate of 0.2), repeated 10 times with different random initializations to find the best solution.

- **Policies for Window-Based Metrics (e.g., (Soft) mAP, Miss Rate)**:
 Optimizing for average precision globally is complex due to its dependency on a global ranking of predictions. The authors propose a per-agent greedy heuristic. First, a set of candidate endpoints is sampled from the predictive distribution. For (soft) mAP, a prediction is a true positive (TP) if its endpoint is the highest-confidence endpoint within a specific evaluation window $W(y_{nT})$ around the ground-truth endpoint $y_{nT}$. Since $y_{nT}$ is unknown at inference, the policy uses samples $\tilde{x}_{nT}$ from $p_{nT}$ to estimate the probability that a candidate endpoint $\hat{x}_{nkT}^{pos}$ becomes a true positive: $TP_{nk}(\hat{x}_{nkT}^{pos}) = E_{\tilde{x}_{nT} \sim p_{nT}}[TP_{nk}(\hat{x}_{nkT}^{pos}, \tilde{x}_{nT})]$. The policy iteratively selects the endpoint that maximizes this probability, assigning this probability as its confidence score $\hat{\pi}_{nk}$. This greedy approach naturally encourages coverage and avoids placing multiple predictions within the same window, aligning with the objectives of (soft) mAP and miss rate. For miss rate, which focuses on whether any prediction hits the window, the same heuristic is applicable as it maximizes the total number of covered windows.

**DONUT-NLL Implementation**:
The proposed paradigm is instantiated using DONUT, a decoder-only transformer. The original DONUT used 1D Laplace distributions for positions (aligned to local longitudinal/lateral directions) and von Mises distributions for heading, along with mixture weights for $K$ trajectories. Its loss function included a winner-takes-all (WTA) regression loss (optimizing the log-likelihood of the best-matching mode) and a classification loss for final timestep mixture weights.

The authors introduce two NLL-based variants for DONUT-NLL:
1. **Traj-NLL**: Directly minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the full ground-truth trajectory under the predicted mixture distribution. In this variant, mixture weights $\pi_{nk}$ are shared across all timesteps $t$, implying each component $k$ represents a complete trajectory hypothesis.
2. **Step-NLL**: Increases temporal flexibility by defining an independent mixture for each timestep. This means mixture weights $\pi_{nkt}$ can vary across time, allowing the importance of different modes to evolve throughout the prediction horizon.

**Position Distribution Families**:
To systematically evaluate the impact of different uncertainty representations, the authors experiment with various position distribution families as drop-in replacements for DONUT's original Laplace distribution:
- **Laplace Distribution**: Provides sharper peaks and heavier tails.
- **Generalized Gaussian Distribution**: A flexible family that includes both Laplace ($\beta=1$) and Gaussian ($\beta=2$) as special cases, controlled by a shape parameter $\beta$.
- **Gaussian Scale Mixtures**: Composed of $J=5$ Gaussian components sharing a single mean but having different scales, offering flexibility in capturing multi-scale uncertainty.

**Experimental Setup**:
Experiments were conducted on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, requiring predictions of $K=6$ trajectories over an 8-second horizon. The policies were also applied to open-source baselines MTR (a Gaussian mixture model using NMS) and QCNet (a Laplacian mode predictor using WTA) to assess model agnosticism. Waymo's multi-horizon evaluation (3s, 5s, 8s) was handled by applying policies separately for each horizon and combining endpoints by rank, using the 8s confidence for the full trajectory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to address several key questions regarding the effectiveness of TraDiE policies, the impact of training objectives, the choice of position distributions, and the model-agnostic nature of the approach.

**Effectiveness of TraDiE Policies and Exposure of Miscalibration**:
The application of TraDiE policies to models trained with probabilistic NLL objectives (Traj-NLL and Step-NLL) consistently resulted in substantial performance improvements across both distance-based (minFDE) and window-based (soft mAP, mAP, miss rate) metrics (Table 1). Specifically, the distance policy reduced minFDE, and the window policy significantly improved soft mAP, mAP, and miss rate. This demonstrates that a single underlying predictive distribution can be effectively adapted to optimize for heterogeneous metrics without retraining the model.

Conversely, applying the distance policy to models trained with the winner-takes-all (WTA) objective, which directly optimizes a distance-based loss, generally *degraded* minFDE performance compared to naive WTA evaluation. This suggests that WTA training, while achieving good scores on its target metric, often leads to poorly calibrated predictive distributions. The policies thus serve as a diagnostic tool, revealing miscalibration rather than merely improving scores through post-processing. A similar observation was made with QCNet, where its minFDE performance degraded significantly under the distance policy (Table 3), indicating a distribution mismatch, likely due to its Laplace parameterization struggling with curved trajectories.

**Impact of Training Objectives**:
Comparing training objectives, Step-NLL consistently outperformed both WTA and Traj-NLL once the TraDiE policies were applied (Table 1). This finding supports the central hypothesis that optimizing the predictive distribution directly via NLL (especially with the added temporal flexibility of time-dependent mixture weights in Step-NLL) is more beneficial than optimizing a surrogate metric-specific loss, particularly when metric-specific policies are employed at evaluation time. Furthermore, naive evaluation without policies would suggest different optimal design choices, such as WTA for minFDE, indicating a shift in preferred approaches under the proposed paradigm.

**Comparison of Position Distribution Families**:
A systematic comparison of position distributions (Table 2) revealed that the **Generalized Gaussian distribution**, when combined with Step-NLL and policy optimization, consistently outperformed Laplace and Gaussian scale mixtures across all metrics. This suggests that the generalized Gaussian's additional shape flexibility is advantageous for learning a well-calibrated predictive distribution that can be effectively exploited by the downstream policies. As observed with training objectives, naive evaluation again favored different distributions (Laplace for minFDE, Scale Mixture for soft mAP and mAP), reinforcing the notion that focusing on distribution quality alters optimal model design.

**Model Agnostic Validation**:
The TraDiE policies were successfully applied to two additional baselines: MTR and QCNet (Table 3). MTR, which uses a 64-component Gaussian mixture, showed consistent improvements across metrics under the policies, even outperforming its original non-maximum suppression (NMS) post-processing. This confirmed the model-agnostic nature and transferability of the distribution-aware policies to different architectures. QCNet, however, showed a significant degradation in minFDE under the distance policy, which the authors attributed to its position distribution parameterization being misaligned with future motion on curved trajectories, causing an artificial spread in optimal endpoints. This further highlighted the diagnostic capability of the policies in exposing distribution quality issues.

**State-of-the-Art Performance**:
Crucially, DONUT-NLL, utilizing the Step-NLL loss and the generalized Gaussian position distribution, achieved state-of-the-art results on the Waymo test set (Table 4) when combined with the metric-specific policies. A single model, adapted post-hoc by different policies, outperformed existing methods (including ensemble approaches) on both distance-based (minFDE) and window-based (soft mAP, mAP, miss rate) metrics. Notably, it achieved this with approximately 5x fewer parameters than some competing models like IMPACT (e2e).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research proposes and validates a significant paradigm shift in trajectory forecasting, moving away from metric-specific model training towards learning well-calibrated, metric-agnostic predictive distributions. This new framework has several implications for the field:

1. **Fostering General Purpose Models**: By emphasizing the learning of an accurate and calibrated predictive distribution, the work encourages the development of more robust and versatile forecasting models. Such models provide a faithful representation of uncertainty that is valuable not just for benchmarks, but also for diverse downstream applications in autonomous driving, such as motion planning, risk assessment, and decision-making, which may have their own unique cost functions or requirements.

2. **Enhanced Model Evaluation and Diagnostics**: The introduction of TraDiE policies provides a novel and more insightful method for evaluating trajectory forecasting models. Unlike traditional benchmark reporting that can be influenced by metric-specific tuning, these policies assess the underlying quality and calibration of the predictive distribution. The ability of the policies to expose miscalibrated distributions (as demonstrated with WTA training and QCNet) offers a powerful diagnostic tool, guiding researchers toward fundamental improvements in modeling uncertainty rather than superficial metric optimization.

3. **Increased Flexibility and Adaptability**: A single, distribution-trained model can be flexibly adapted to optimize for different evaluation metrics or downstream objectives without requiring computationally expensive retraining. This capability streamlines the development process and allows the same core forecasting intelligence to serve various components of an autonomous system, each with potentially distinct performance criteria.

4. **Advancement in Predictive Performance**: The empirical results, particularly achieving state-of-the-art performance on the Waymo benchmark across both distance-based and window-based metrics with a single model (DONUT-NLL), demonstrate the practical efficacy of the proposed paradigm. This suggests that focusing on distribution quality can lead to superior overall forecasting capabilities compared to existing metric-tuned approaches.

5. **Re-evaluation of Design Choices**: The research highlights that the optimal design choices for trajectory forecasting models (e.g., training objectives, position distribution families) may differ significantly when the goal shifts from direct metric optimization to learning a well-calibrated predictive distribution. This opens new avenues for fundamental research into probabilistic modeling in this domain.

The main limitations identified include the computational cost of policies at inference time, which currently restricts their primary use to evaluation rather than real-time deployment, and the potential for overfitting with Step-NLL on smaller datasets like Argoverse 2. However, the foundational shift proposed by this work holds promise for guiding future research toward more principled and generalizable solutions in autonomous driving motion forecasting.
