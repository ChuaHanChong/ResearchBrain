# Thinking in Blender: Staged Executable Inverse Graphics with Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> SEIG (Staged Executable Inverse Graphics) rebuilds an editable Blender program from a single image using only a pretrained VLM. It replaces one monolithic generation with sequential stages for geometry, materials, composition and lighting, each closed by a scoped generator-verifier loop. It beats the VIGA baselines on most metrics without specialist 2D or 3D models, and supports relighting, editing and physics.

## Problem

- Inverse graphics is highly underconstrained. Neural representations like NeRF and Gaussian splatting entangle geometry, materials and lighting in latent form, so the result is not an editable, executable scene program.
- Prior VLM agents for executable inverse graphics, such as VIGA and BlenderGym, generate and refine the whole scene at once. Coupled factors let errors in one hide corrections to another.
- Strong prior pipelines depend on specialist 2D and 3D foundation models such as SAM and SAM-3D. This obscures what VLMs know, and single-view lifting adds artifacts such as duplicated Janus faces.

## Method

- ==Staged scene construction== follows an artist workflow. A ==hierarchical scene graph== seeds a ==coarse primitive scaffold==, with ==rollout sampling== of four candidates and a selector. Then geometry, material, composition and lighting stages run in order, each restricted to its own factor.
- Intra-stage ==generator-verifier loop==: the generator writes Blender code with tools to inspect, render and revert. A ==stage-scoped verifier== returns an explicit ==approval checklist==, with per-stage round budgets of 5, 3, 3 and 2.
- ==Stage-scoped tool restrictions==, such as a material-only executor, stop later stages from changing earlier results. Every stage commits a coherent, editable Blender program, giving named objects and separate lights for downstream use.

## Results

- On NeRF synthetic scenes SEIG scores PSNR **13.58**, DreamSim **0.3021**, DINO **0.7188** and CLIP **0.8830**. This beats VIGA VLM-only (**12.33**, **0.3693**, **0.6221**, **0.8451**) and VIGA full (**11.18**, **0.3624**, **0.5545**, **0.7986**).
- On Edit3D scenes SEIG again wins **five of six** metrics: PSNR **12.65**, LPIPS **0.3823**, DreamSim **0.3433** and DINO **0.6293**. VIGA full reaches only **0.4441** DreamSim and **0.4832** DINO. SSIM is roughly tied.
- SEIG beats VIGA full without SAM or SAM-3D, so gains come from harness design rather than tool access. It also beats VIGA VLM-only, confirming the value of stage decomposition. The outputs support relighting, object edits and physics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing a coupled, underconstrained task into ordered, verifiable stages that mirror expert workflow can matter more than a richer external toolkit. This holds even with a fixed, untuned VLM.
> - Scoped verifiers that return concrete approval checklists, plus per-stage tool restrictions and round budgets, give generators stable targets. They limit context degradation and stop later stages from undoing earlier work.
> - Greedy staging brings a risk: early errors propagate and cannot be fixed later, and many inference calls raise cost. Global refinement passes could help, but at higher compute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses the longstanding problem of inverse graphics, which involves reconstructing editable 3D scenes from 2D images. Historically, inverse graphics has evolved from early attempts at scene understanding like Roberts’ "Blocks World" to more sophisticated inverse rendering techniques focused on recovering geometry, illumination, and reflectance through analysis-by-synthesis formulations, intrinsic decomposition, and shape-from-shading. More recent approaches have leveraged neural networks for geometry and reflectance recovery from sparse views or single images, and have explored structured scene decomposition using differentiable rendering and primitive-based representations.

In recent years, the field has seen a significant shift towards neural scene representations such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting. While these methods are effective for high-fidelity scene reconstruction and novel view synthesis, they typically encode scene factors (geometry, materials, lighting) in latent neural representations that are not directly editable as structured graphics programs. Efforts have been made towards partial disentanglement within this paradigm, factoring shape and reflectance or separating geometry, BRDFs, and illumination, but these generally do not yield directly executable scene programs.

Simultaneously, the advancements in vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in visual reasoning, instruction following, and code generation. These models have shown promise in coarse 3D grounding, spatial reasoning, and geometric estimation from 2D observations. However, current VLMs often exhibit stronger performance in semantic reasoning than in precise geometric prediction or metric 3D understanding. This characteristic has prompted investigations into their ability to generate and manipulate 3D content through executable scene representations, with prior works exploring the generation of Blender programs from text instructions (e.g., SceneCraft, LL3M), code-based mesh generation (MeshCoder), or compositional 3D generation (BrickGPT). Other research focuses on generating articulated assets (Articulate-Anything) or simulation-ready environments (VDAWorld) from multimodal inputs. Benchmarking efforts like BlenderGym and IR3D-Bench have highlighted geometric precision and spatial consistency as limitations of VLM-driven 3D systems.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by specifically investigating whether *pretrained VLMs alone* can perform *executable inverse graphics* directly from a *single image*, reconstructing a scene as an *editable Blender program*. It distinguishes itself from existing VLM-based inverse graphics agents, such as VIGA, by proposing a staged reconstruction approach. VIGA formulates inverse graphics as an iterative write-render-compare-revise loop but treats reconstruction as a monolithic optimization problem, often struggling with joint reasoning of geometry, materials, composition, and lighting. This work contrasts by explicitly decomposing the reconstruction into sequential generator-verifier stages that independently recover individual scene factors, and by operating solely with a pretrained VLM without relying on specialized 2D or 3D foundation models (like SAM or SAM-3D) for preprocessing. This design choice aims to directly assess the inherent geometric, spatial, and compositional priors encoded within general-purpose VLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

Creating realistic and editable 3D scenes typically involves a labor-intensive process requiring specialized expertise in graphics software like Blender. Professional artists follow an iterative workflow, progressively defining geometry, assigning materials, arranging composition, configuring lighting, and adjusting camera parameters, constantly refining elements based on visual feedback. The primary motivation for this research is to automate this complex process, specifically investigating whether pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can replicate this staged workflow to perform executable inverse graphics directly from a single image.

The paper outlines several key objectives:

1. **Reconstruct editable Blender scenes from a single image:** The central goal is to output a fully editable Blender program, rather than a non-editable neural representation, by recovering underlying scene factors such as geometry, materials, composition, and lighting.
2. **Operate without specialized 2D/3D foundation models or differentiable rendering:** The research seeks to determine the capabilities of general-purpose VLMs for inverse graphics without relying on domain-specific tools, large-scale multi-view supervision, or differentiable rendering pipelines that simplify the optimization of scene parameters. This objective aims to isolate the inherent reasoning abilities of VLMs for 3D structure and appearance.
3. **Decompose the inverse graphics problem into sequential, verifiable stages:** The authors observe that while VLMs struggle with simultaneously inferring all scene factors, their performance can be enhanced by breaking down the complex problem into sequential, semantically meaningful stages. This mirrors the iterative workflow of human artists, reducing the overall complexity and allowing for focused refinement.
4. **Develop an agentic framework for progressive scene refinement:** The objective is to create a framework where a VLM acts as an agent, progressively refining the scene representation in executable Blender code space. Each refinement stage is paired with a verifier module to evaluate the current scene state and guide subsequent adjustments, ensuring continuous improvement towards the target image.
5. **Produce physically grounded scene representations:** The framework aims to maintain a physically grounded scene representation throughout the reconstruction process, enabling downstream graphics tasks such as novel-view synthesis, relighting, object editing, and physics simulation without further training or post-processing. This objective highlights the practical utility of the structured Blender output.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire to leverage the emerging capabilities of VLMs for automated 3D content creation, specifically focusing on the generation of structured, editable scene programs. The core hypothesis is that task decomposition through a staged generator-verifier loop can unlock robust executable inverse graphics capabilities in general-purpose VLMs, overcoming the limitations associated with attempting to solve the highly underconstrained problem monolithically.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Staged Executable Inverse Graphics (SEIG), is an agentic framework designed to reconstruct a 3D scene from a single reference image by producing executable Blender code. The approach is built on a multi-stage, additive pipeline where the scene is progressively constructed and refined. A pretrained Vision-Language Model (VLM) drives the entire process, interpreting the input image, reasoning about scene structure, and interacting with Blender via API calls.

**Core Principles:**
The method addresses the inherent complexity of inverse graphics by decomposing it into independently scoped and verifiable subproblems. Each stage is designed to depend only on the outputs of preceding stages, allowing the VLM to commit to each subproblem before advancing. Within each stage, a generator-verifier loop facilitates iterative refinement.

**VLM Integration:**
The framework utilizes Claude Opus 4.7 as the base VLM for both the generator and verifier across all stages. The model is used off-the-shelf without any fine-tuning, prompt-tuning, or task-specific supervision, ensuring that performance differences are attributable to the framework's design rather than model training.

**Staged Scene Construction:**
The pipeline follows a workflow analogous to human 3D artists, consisting of sequential stages:

1. **Scene Decomposition:** The VLM analyzes the reference image to generate a hierarchical scene graph. This graph includes a global scene root and object nodes, each with attributes such as visual description, approximate geometry, material appearance, spatial relations, and a Blender reconstruction strategy. The VLM recursively refines the graph until leaf nodes represent atomic components approximable by Blender primitives.
2. **Scene Initialization:** Based on the scene graph, this stage instantiates a coarse Blender scaffold. The VLM formulates an execution plan and generates Blender code to create each leaf node with initial geometry and material approximations. It also initializes coarse lighting and camera parameters to ensure scene visibility. To mitigate errors from this crucial initial step, a rollout sampling strategy is employed, generating multiple scene graphs and scaffolds, then selecting the candidate with the most complete object coverage and plausible structure.
3. **Geometry Stage:** With the coarse scaffold established, this stage refines the shape and physical structure of individual objects. The VLM performs local shape edits (meshes, curves), geometric transforms (scaling, rotation, alignment), and structural edits (adding missing parts, organizing hierarchies). The VLM is provided with interaction tools to render from alternative viewpoints, isolate objects, and revert unsuccessful edits based on visual feedback.
4. **Material Stage:** Following geometry refinement, this stage focuses on matching each object's material and surface appearance to the reference image. The VLM replaces coarse textures with detailed Blender PBR materials, initializing material slots and UV maps as needed. It edits surface properties like base color, roughness, specular, metallic, alpha, and bump/normal maps. A material-only tool restricts edits to material-related parameters, preventing alterations to previously refined geometry.
5. **Composition Stage:** After object-level geometry and material are finalized, this stage arranges the objects within the scene to match the reference image's layout. The VLM adjusts object transforms (scale, position, rotation, contact) and overall spatial organization. The agent may also adjust the target-view camera or use temporary arbitrary-view renders to assess layout, but cannot modify object geometry or materials.
6. **Lighting Stage:** As the final scene-level adjustment, this stage optimizes lighting parameters to match the rendered appearance with the reference image, while keeping object shape, appearance, layout, and camera fixed. The VLM infers lighting cues (direction, height, shadow properties, color temperature, exposure, contrast) and adjusts physical lighting controls (type, position, energy, color, size, softness, ambient) and render settings. Edits are instructed to be conservative due to sensitivity.

**Intra-Stage Generator-Verifier Refinement:**
Each of the above stages involves an iterative generator-verifier loop:
- **Generator:** The VLM, acting as the generator, inspects the current Blender scene using tools, writes stage-specific Blender code, executes the edit, and renders the updated result.
- **Verifier:** After each generator attempt, the VLM (as verifier) compares the rendered image against the reference. It inspects the scene through its own tools to identify stage-specific mismatches. The verifier returns an explicit, actionable approval checklist of visual discrepancies to the generator.
- **Loop Termination:** The generator refines until the verifier's checklist conditions are met. Each stage has a maximum round budget (e.g., five for geometry, three for material/composition, two for lighting). If the budget is exhausted, the verifier selects the best attempt to proceed to the next stage.

This structured, iterative approach aims to reduce the search space for the VLM, enabling more grounded reasoning and progressive scene construction through disentangled steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to assess the capabilities of the SEIG framework in reconstructing structured Blender scenes, to quantify the benefits of the staged approach, and to demonstrate the utility of the resulting editable scene representations.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

The SEIG framework was quantitatively evaluated against a monolithic agentic baseline, VIGA, in two configurations: VIGA$_{full}$ (using specialized 2D/3D foundation models like SAM and SAM-3D) and VIGA$_{VLM-only}$ (disabling these specialist tools to rely solely on the VLM agent, similar to SEIG). Evaluations were performed on curated sets of images from the NeRF synthetic dataset and the VoxHammer (Edit3D) dataset. Six metrics were used:
- **Pixel-level:** PSNR and SSIM.
- **Learned perceptual scores:** LPIPS and DreamSim.
- **Semantic similarities:** DINO and CLIP.

Across both the NeRF synthetic and Edit3D datasets, SEIG achieved the best scores on five out of six metrics. Specifically, on NeRF synthetic scenes (Table 1), SEIG scored 13.58 PSNR, 0.6881 SSIM, 0.3493 LPIPS, 0.3021 DreamSim, 0.7188 DINO, and 0.8830 CLIP, outperforming both VIGA variants in most categories. Similarly, on Edit3D scenes (Table 2), SEIG showed comparable or superior performance, with 12.65 PSNR, 0.6737 SSIM, 0.3823 LPIPS, 0.3433 DreamSim, 0.6293 DINO, and 0.8446 CLIP.

These results indicate that SEIG, despite not utilizing specialized 2D or 3D foundation models, generally produced more accurate reconstructions than VIGA$_{full}$. This suggests that the performance gains primarily stem from the proposed harness design (staged reconstruction) rather than access to external specialist tools. The superior performance over VIGA$_{VLM-only}$ further verifies the contribution of the per-stage decomposition, aligning with prior research that identifies visual precision, rather than tool orchestration, as a dominant bottleneck in current agentic 3D pipelines.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results

Qualitative evaluations supported the quantitative findings, demonstrating SEIG's ability to produce structured Blender outputs that align with reference images in geometry, surface appearance, and composition (Figure 8). The reconstructions suggest that careful harness design, without task-specific training, can enable high-quality inverse graphics from off-the-shelf VLMs.

Comparison with VIGA variants (Figure 3) revealed several observations:
- SEIG consistently produced more accurate reconstructions across geometry, material, and composition.
- VIGA$_{VLM-only}$ tended to lose smaller scene elements and surface details.
- VIGA$_{full}$ often generated fragmented, mis-colored meshes that failed to form a coherent scene, as the VLM agent could overwrite textures of 3D objects generated by SAM-3D.
- SEIG and VIGA$_{VLM-only}$ generally avoided common single-view lifting artifacts, such as the Janus failure mode, by reconstructing compositionally from primitives rather than lifting a single-view mesh prior.
- In cases of inherent ambiguity in single-view reconstruction (e.g., an occluded bread basket), SEIG produced perceptually coherent interpretations, even if not matching the exact ground truth, demonstrating compositional discipline. In contrast, VIGA variants struggled to recover coherent structures in such scenarios.

Starting from a coarse primitive-based scaffold, the geometry, material, composition, and lighting stages incrementally refine the scene, demonstrating the effectiveness of the staged approach in building a coherent, editable Blender program at each step.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for executable inverse graphics that reconstructs editable Blender scenes from single images using only a pretrained vision-language model. This approach offers several significant implications and potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Core Contributions and Advantages

- **Unlocking VLM Capabilities:** The work demonstrates that by decomposing the complex inverse graphics problem into sequential, individually verifiable subproblems, general-purpose VLMs can achieve robust executable inverse graphics without specialized 2D/3D foundation models, differentiable rendering, or task-specific training. This highlights the latent 3D, spatial, and compositional priors encoded within these models.
- **Structured and Editable Output:** A key advantage is the generation of a structured Blender file. Unlike neural scene representations (e.g., NeRFs or Gaussian Splatting) which are often entangled and latent, the Blender output provides a physically grounded representation where geometry, materials, and lighting are explicitly defined and separate. This direct editability streamlines downstream graphics operations.
- **Task Decomposition Effectiveness:** The staged generator-verifier loop is shown to be crucial for improving reconstruction fidelity. By allowing the VLM to focus on disentangled factors sequentially, it mitigates the "entangled-output bottleneck" that can limit monolithic reconstruction pipelines, leading to more accurate and coherent scene reconstructions.

#### 5.2. Downstream Applications and Practical Impact

The directly editable Blender scene output enables immediate application in various graphics tasks without requiring further training, model conversion, or post-processing:

- **Relighting:** Since geometry, materials, and light sources are distinct components within the Blender scene graph, light sources can be easily added, removed, or reconfigured. This allows for arbitrary new illumination to be applied, producing realistic changes in dominant color, direction, and intensity while preserving the recovered scene properties.
- **Object Editing:** The object-decomposed nature of the reconstruction allows for straightforward per-object manipulation. Individual scene graph nodes can be selected, moved, duplicated, retextured, or replaced directly in Blender, enabling creative control over the scene. Examples include duplicating aircraft parts, editing textures, manipulating object shapes, and altering object composition.
- **Physics Simulation:** The reconstructed scene, being a collection of addressable meshes in Blender, serves as a valid input for Blender's built-in physics engine. This enables simulations like rigid-body dynamics (e.g., mugs and saucers sliding on a shaking table) and soft-body deformation (e.g., a ball dropping onto a cushion) directly on the output. This capability is significant as it avoids the need for complex remeshing, watertighting, or geometry repair often required when converting implicit neural representations into discrete physical entities.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

Despite its advancements, the framework has identified limitations:
- **Error Propagation:** Errors introduced in early stages (e.g., inaccurate geometry) can propagate and constrain subsequent material, lighting, or compositional reasoning, leading to local minima that later stages find difficult to recover from. Future work could explore global refinement passes to re-optimize earlier scene factors, although this would increase computational cost.
- **Computational Expense:** The iterative nature of repeated inference calls across multiple generator-verifier stages results in substantially higher runtime and API costs compared to single-pass generation pipelines.

Future research could extend these ideas to more complex scenarios, including multi-image scene reconstruction, dynamic environments, physically grounded simulation, and long-horizon interactive editing. The findings suggest that staged executable scene representations represent a promising avenue for evolving general-purpose VLMs into controllable and versatile 3D content creation systems, bridging the gap between perception and actionable 3D content.
