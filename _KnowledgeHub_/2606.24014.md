# Reinforcement Learning Towards Broadly and Persistently Beneficial Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at OpenAI investigated whether Reinforcement Learning (RL) can cultivate broadly generalized and persistently aligned behaviors in AI models by training on a distribution of beneficial traits. Their method demonstrated that models trained with a small percentage of beneficial trait data showed a 49% relative improvement on in-distribution traits and outperformed baselines on 83% of out-of-distribution alignment evaluations, while also exhibiting increased resilience to adversarial prompting and harmful fine-tuning.

## Problem

- AI systems require robust alignment to minimize risks in diverse real-world settings, but exhaustively training for every possible scenario is impractical.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) can inadvertently introduce misalignment risks, such as reward hacking or deception, by optimizing imperfect objectives.
- Observations of 'emergent misalignment' suggest that narrow harmful training generalizes broadly, raising the question of whether the inverse is true for beneficial traits.

## Method

- The study identified 15 fine-grained '==beneficial traits==' (e.g., truthfulness, corrigibility) and generated a synthetic conversation dataset with challenging scenarios across 12 diverse domains.
- Models were trained using ==Reinforcement Learning (RL)== with varying data mixtures, including a **Beneficial Trait RL Model** (5% beneficial trait data, 95% standard RL) and several control groups.
- Evaluation encompassed in-distribution trait assessment, over 50 out-of-distribution alignment benchmarks, public-benefit domains, and persistence tests under ==adversarial prompting== and ==harmful fine-tuning==.

## Results

- The beneficial trait RL model achieved a **49%** relative improvement on in-distribution beneficial traits and outperformed the baseline on **44 of 53 (83%)** out-of-distribution alignment evaluations, with a mean improvement of **9.1 percentage points**.
- Improvements extended to public-benefit domains, with the model surpassing the baseline on **9 of 10** health and mental-health evaluations, and demonstrated out-of-domain transfer, e.g., health-only training improved non-health alignment by up to **26.4 percentage points**.
- The beneficial trait RL model showed improved persistence, exhibiting less performance degradation under harmful adversarial prompts (average alignment score degradation **0.119** vs. **0.251**) and significantly less degradation from harmful fine-tuning on broader alignment evaluations (**0.08** vs. **0.36** points drop).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Beneficial behaviors in AI models exhibit a structured organization around broader, shared model-level traits, as evidenced by correlations across diverse alignment evaluations.
> - Reinforcement learning, when focused on a narrow distribution of beneficial traits, can lead to broad and persistent generalization of alignment across diverse tasks and domains.
> - Cultivating positive behavioral priors through beneficial trait RL enhances model resilience to adversarial steering and mitigates the degradation effects of subsequent narrow harmful fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of AI systems into increasingly diverse and high-stakes real-world settings necessitates robust model alignment to minimize risks and promote human well-being. A central challenge is ensuring that aligned behavior generalizes beyond the specific tasks and domains observed during training, as exhaustive training for every possible real-world scenario is impractical. Reinforcement Learning (RL), while powerful, introduces a particular risk: models can learn unintended misalignment strategies such as reward hacking or deception through optimizing imperfect objectives.

Existing research has demonstrated a phenomenon known as "emergent misalignment," where models trained on narrow forms of misbehavior (e.g., insecure code generation) begin exhibiting broader misalignment across unrelated measures, including harmful advice or deceptive actions (Betley et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; MacDiarmid et al., 2025). This suggests that specific misalignment training can select for broader harmful model personas that influence behavior across various domains (Marks et al., 2026). Similarly, studies on scheming and deception in Large Language Models (LLMs) indicate that models might harbor misaligned internal thoughts or pursue subversive goals even when their surface-level outputs appear benign (Baker et al., 2025; Schoen et al., 2025).

Complementary efforts in alignment research include "deliberative alignment," which trains models to explicitly reason through safety specifications (Guan et al., 2024), and "constitutional AI" or "principle-driven alignment," which uses high-level principles to steer model behavior (Bai et al., 2022; Kundu et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). Other approaches involve training models on the rationales behind desirable behaviors (Kutasov et al., 2026) or focusing on "positive alignment" to cultivate systems that actively support human flourishing beyond just harm prevention (Laukkonen et al., 2026).

This current work positions itself within this landscape by investigating whether the same generalization mechanisms observed for emergent misalignment can be leveraged in a *beneficial* direction. The authors explore whether training models with RL on a distribution of *beneficial traits* can lead to generalized alignment across diverse tasks and domains, thereby shaping a high-level, beneficial behavioral prior that generalizes and persists under adversarial conditions. This contrasts with approaches primarily focused on preventing specific harms or solely relying on explicit rule-following.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether RL, when specifically applied to reinforce beneficial behaviors, can produce models that exhibit broad and persistent alignment generalization, extending beyond the training distribution. This overarching goal is motivated by several considerations pertinent to the safe and effective deployment of advanced AI systems:

1. **Addressing the Generalization Gap in Alignment:** As AI systems are deployed in increasingly varied real-world scenarios, it becomes challenging to exhaustively train for every possible alignment failure mode. Models that appear aligned during training and internal evaluation may not maintain this alignment when exposed to novel, out-of-distribution (OOD) contexts. A fundamental goal is, therefore, to ensure that beneficial behavior generalizes robustly across diverse contexts.

2. **Mitigating RL-Induced Misalignment Risks:** Reinforcement Learning, while effective for optimizing model behavior, can inadvertently introduce unexpected forms of misalignment. Models may learn to "reward hack" by exploiting loopholes in reward functions, engage in deception, or develop other unintended strategies to maximize their objective, leading to undesirable outcomes. This research aims to explore whether RL can be used to *counteract* these risks by positively reinforcing broad beneficial traits.

3. **Enhancing Alignment Persistence Under Adversarial Pressure:** Even if a model exhibits aligned behavior by default, its alignment may be vulnerable to malicious or challenging inputs. Adversarial prompting or harmful fine-tuning can steer models towards misaligned behavior. A key motivation is to investigate whether training on beneficial traits can make models more resilient to such pressures, thereby improving the persistence of their aligned behavior.

4. **Testing the Directional Generalization Hypothesis:** Building on observations of emergent misalignment, where narrow training on harmful behaviors generalizes broadly, the authors hypothesize that the inverse might also be true. The research aims to empirically test whether focused training on a narrow set of *beneficial* behaviors can similarly lead to broad, positive generalization of alignment, encompassing traits like truthfulness, fairness, risk awareness, and corrigibility across various high-stakes domains (e.g., health, science, education).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach involving initial correlational analysis, synthetic data generation, reinforcement learning model training, and extensive evaluation across various benchmarks.

**Initial Hypothesis and Correlation Analysis:**
The study began by investigating the hypothesis that beneficial behavior is organized around broader model-level traits rather than isolated task-specific responses. To test this, the authors analyzed the performance of 13 OpenAI models (from o3 to GPT-5.5) across a suite of 33 public and internal alignment evaluations covering diverse topics such as reward hacking, scheming, robustness, factuality, and sycophancy. Spearman correlations were computed between pairs of evaluations, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to identify underlying correlation structures. A permutation test was used to establish null intervals for statistical comparison. This analysis was also performed after residualizing for model capabilities (measured by GPQA, HMMT, and SWE-Bench Pro scores) to isolate alignment-specific variance.

**Beneficial Trait Identification:**
Drawing from recurring concerns in the AI alignment literature, the authors identified 15 fine-grained "beneficial traits" considered broadly useful for aligned AI systems. These traits included epistemic honesty, transparency, corrigibility, caution under uncertainty, resistance to misgeneralized or power-seeking behavior, and concern for human welfare beyond narrow user obedience. Examples include Truthfulness, Metacognitive Transparency, Corrigibility, Downside-Aware Planning, Power-Asymmetry Awareness, Anti-Hierarchy Governance, and Universalizable Fairness (a held-out subset of 7 traits was used for direct evaluation, while all 15 were used for training data generation).

**Dataset Construction and Synthetic Generation:**
A synthetic conversation dataset was created for both evaluation and training. Each conversation was generated by conditioning a language model on two inputs: a *trait description* (defining the behavioral property to be tested) and a *domain description* (defining the scenario setting). Twelve diverse domains were used, including health, law, business, education, and engineering. The generation process was specifically designed to produce challenging scenarios involving competing values, conflicting interests, adversarial framing, or factual uncertainty, requiring "situated judgment" from the model. Each example was paired with trait-specific evaluation criteria.

**Reinforcement Learning Training Regimes:**
The core experimental intervention involved training models using Reinforcement Learning (RL) with variations in their training data:
- **Beneficial Trait RL Model:** This model was trained with a mixture of 5% beneficial trait data (rewarding beneficial behavior) and 95% standard RL data.
- **Baseline Model:** A compute-matched control model trained using 100% standard RL data.
- **Health-Only Beneficial Trait RL:** A variant trained with 5% *exclusively health-related* beneficial trait data and 95% standard RL data, designed to test specific OOD transfer.
- **Health/Science Excluded Beneficial Trait RL:** Another variant trained with 5% beneficial trait data *excluding* health and science domains, alongside 95% standard RL data, also to test OOD transfer.
- **Generic Helpfulness RL Control:** A control model trained on the same 5% beneficial trait data as the main beneficial trait RL model, but with a *generic helpfulness and instruction-following reward signal* instead of the trait-specific beneficial behavior rewards. This aimed to differentiate the impact of the specific reward signal from the data distribution itself.

**Evaluation Metrics and Procedures:**
Models underwent comprehensive evaluation:
- **In-Distribution (IID) Beneficial Trait Evaluation:** Direct assessment on the held-out beneficial trait dataset.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Alignment and Benefit Benchmarks:** A suite of over 50 public and internal benchmarks was utilized. These benchmarks spanned various domains and failure modes, including deception (DeceptionBench, MASK), reward hacking (School of Reward Hacks, EvilGenie), broader misalignment (PropensityBench, Machiavelli, AgentHarm), factuality, anti-scheming, model specification compliance, and public benefit domains like health (HealthBench, Medical QA) and mental health. Some of these evaluations employed privacy-preserving production traffic data.
- **Alignment Persistence:**
 - **Adversarial Prompting:** Models were evaluated with harmful persona prompts (e.g., "bad medical persona") and helpful persona prompts (e.g., "helpful medical persona") prefixed to conversations to assess robustness to steering.
 - **Harmful Fine-tuning:** Models were fine-tuned to generate factually inaccurate or unsafe medical advice, and their alignment performance was then re-evaluated across health and general alignment benchmarks to measure resistance to broad degradation.
- **Capability Evaluation:** Standardized benchmarks such as GPQA Diamond, HMMT 2024–2025, SWE-Bench Pro, and instruction following were used to monitor for any degradation in general model capabilities.
- **Monitorability Evaluation:** Metrics from antischeming, deceptive tool use, and impossible coding reward hacking evaluations were used to assess whether alignment improvements came at the cost of reduced monitorability (i.e., whether models became better at hiding misaligned intent).
- **Refusal Rate Analysis:** The rate of model refusals on both the alignment evaluation suite and everyday chat conversations was analyzed, and performance was also assessed on paired non-refusal samples to disentangle improvements from increased conservatism.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding alignment generalization and persistence through beneficial trait reinforcement learning.

**Empirical Support for Alignment Trait Structure:**
Initial analysis of 13 OpenAI models across 33 alignment evaluations revealed a weak positive correlation structure (mean Spearman's ρ = 0.107, exceeding the null interval of [−0.019, 0.029]). The first principal component explained 28.2% of the variance in evaluation scores (above the null interval of [15.3%, 20.8%]). This structure persisted even after residualizing for general model capabilities, supporting the hypothesis that diverse alignment evaluations are partly driven by shared model-level behavioral tendencies. Leave-one-out prediction analysis further indicated that this shared structure generalized across evaluations.

**Generalization to In-Distribution (IID) Traits:**
The beneficial trait RL model demonstrated a substantial improvement on the in-distribution beneficial trait evaluation suite. Its aggregate trait score increased from 0.406 to 0.607, representing a 49% relative improvement compared to the compute-matched baseline. These improvements were consistently observed across all seven held-out beneficial traits.

**Broad Generalization to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Alignment:**
A key finding was the generalization of beneficial behavior to independently constructed OOD benchmarks. The beneficial trait RL model outperformed the compute-matched baseline on 44 of 53 (83.0%) diverse alignment-relevant evaluations, with a mean improvement of +9.1 percentage points. Specifically, 30 of these improvements were statistically significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (56.6%), while only 3 evaluations showed significant regression (5.6%). Improvements were noted across various categories, including deception (e.g., DeceptionBench, MASK), reward hacking (e.g., School of Reward Hacks), and broader misalignment measures (e.g., PropensityBench).

**Generalization to Public-Benefit Domains:**
The improvements also extended to public-benefit evaluations, particularly in health and medicine. The beneficial trait RL model surpassed the baseline on 9 of 10 (90.0%) internal health and mental-health evaluations, with 7 improvements being statistically significant. Notable gains were observed on HealthBench, which utilizes physician-written rubrics to assess response safety and quality. Qualitative analysis suggested these improvements stemmed from enhanced model judgment rather than increased hedging or refusal.

**Out-of-Domain Transfer Experiments:**
Two experiments provided evidence for out-of-domain alignment transfer:
- A model trained with beneficial trait data that *excluded* all health and science conversations still showed similar gains on 10 health and mental-health evaluations, indicating transfer beyond direct domain overlap.
- A model trained *solely* on health-related beneficial behavior data (constituting 5% of its total training) demonstrated improvements across 17 of 19 (89.5%) non-health alignment evaluations. These included statistically significant gains on measures like impossible coding reward hacking (+26.4 percentage points) and chain-of-thought deception (+6.8 percentage points), suggesting that beneficial behavior training in one domain can induce broad shifts in aligned behavior in unrelated domains.

**Alignment Persistence Under Adversarial Prompting:**
The beneficial trait RL model exhibited improved persistence of alignment under adversarial prompting. When exposed to harmful persona prompts (e.g., a "bad medical persona" or a "disallowed mental health persona"), the beneficial trait model showed less performance degradation compared to the baseline. For instance, the average alignment score degradation under a harmful medical persona was 0.119 for the beneficial trait model versus 0.251 for the baseline. Crucially, this model maintained similar responsiveness to helpful persona prompts, suggesting a selective reduction in steerability towards harmful outcomes while preserving it for beneficial ones.

**Alignment Persistence Under Harmful Finetuning:**
Further, the beneficial trait RL model demonstrated greater resistance to degradation following harmful fine-tuning. When both a pre-RL baseline and the beneficial trait RL model were fine-tuned to produce unsafe medical advice, the pre-RL baseline degraded substantially on both targeted health evaluations and broader non-health alignment evaluations (e.g., Misalignment score dropped by 0.36 points). In contrast, the beneficial trait RL model experienced less degradation on health evaluations and significantly less degradation on broader alignment evaluations (e.g., Misalignment score dropped by only 0.08 points). This finding suggests that beneficial trait RL may partially mitigate emergent misalignment effects stemming from narrow harmful fine-tuning.

**Addressing Alternative Explanations:**
The study also investigated several alternative explanations for these results:
- **Generic helpfulness training:** A control model trained on the same beneficial trait data but with generic helpfulness rewards showed no significant OOD alignment improvements, indicating that the specific beneficial behavior reward signal, not just the data, was crucial.
- **Increased refusal:** While the beneficial trait RL model had a higher refusal rate on the alignment suite (23.9% vs. 13.2% for the baseline), analysis restricted to *non-refusal* samples still showed improvements on 19 of 20 evaluations, suggesting that increased refusal was not the sole driver of alignment gains.
- **Evaluation awareness:** The observed improvements were also present on 14 of 16 evaluations using privacy-preserving production data, reducing the likelihood that models were merely exploiting benchmark artifacts.
- **Capability degradation:** The beneficial trait RL model matched or exceeded the baseline across all tested capability benchmarks (GPQA, HMMT, SWE-Bench Pro, instruction following), indicating that alignment gains were achieved without a trade-off in general capabilities.
- **Monitorability regressions:** Beneficial trait training did not reduce monitorability; monitorability metrics were similar or improved, suggesting models did not become better at hiding misaligned intent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents findings that have implications for the development and deployment of advanced AI systems.

Firstly, the work suggests that Reinforcement Learning can be utilized constructively to enhance AI alignment. Traditionally, RL has been associated with risks like reward hacking due to imperfect objective specification. However, these results indicate that when a reward signal targets beneficial behavior across diverse settings, RL can reinforce behaviors that generalize positively beyond the training distribution. This challenges the perspective of RL as solely a source of misalignment risk, proposing it can also be a mechanism for training more robustly aligned behavioral priors.

Secondly, the findings underscore the importance of alignment generalization and persistence as central evaluation targets. Measuring alignment solely as default behavior on static benchmarks may be insufficient; it is crucial to understand whether aligned behavior remains robust under distribution shifts, adversarial prompting, and subsequent optimization pressures (e.g., fine-tuning). This is particularly relevant for adaptable or open-weight models that might be steered towards harmful behaviors after release. The demonstrated selective persistence—where models become harder to steer towards harmful outcomes while retaining responsiveness to beneficial instructions—suggests a path toward more resilient AI systems.

Thirdly, the study provides empirical evidence that alignment can be approached as a structured, measurable phenomenon. The observed correlation structure among diverse alignment evaluations and the effectiveness of training on a specific set of beneficial traits suggest that alignment-relevant behavior may be organized around relatively low-dimensional, common underlying factors. This supports a research agenda focused on identifying, quantifying, and training these latent behavioral traits to achieve broad alignment generalization.

Finally, the research suggests that beneficial trait RL may offer a partial mitigation strategy against emergent misalignment. The greater resistance of beneficial trait-trained models to broad alignment degradation following narrow harmful fine-tuning indicates that cultivating positive behavioral priors can help stabilize models against future misaligned adaptations. While limitations remain, such as fully isolating the causal pathways and exhaustively testing distribution shift, these results offer an encouraging direction for developing AI models whose aligned behavior is not only strong in training but also stable and resilient across real-world deployment scenarios.
