# Manipulation of Deformable Linear Objects Using Model Predictive Path Integral Control with Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This work manipulates deformable linear objects such as cables into target shapes by pairing a learned dynamics model with sampling-based optimal control. A three-layer bidirectional LSTM, trained on MuJoCo-generated cable trajectories, predicts capsule velocities from relative positions plus end-effector state, and serves as the rollout model inside a Model Predictive Path Integral controller. On a Franka Emika Panda, the framework reaches 93 percent success in simulation and 85 percent for real 2D U-shape control.

## Problem

- Physics-based cable models like FEM and mass-spring systems need accurate material parameters and heavy computation, making them unsuitable for real-time robotic manipulation.
- Jacobian-based and point-based dynamics methods run in real time but only capture local deformation or sacrifice physical accuracy under large shape changes.
- Existing learned deformable-object models demand large datasets, and whether simulation-trained models transfer to real cables of differing stiffness and diameter stays unverified.

## Method

- **biLSTM** dynamics model: three bidirectional layers, 512 neurons each, predicting next-step capsule velocities from the joint cable plus end-effector state, chosen by random then grid hyperparameter search.
- ==Translation-invariant== 2D state representation: 10 subsampled capsules encoded as positions relative to the end-effector pose, with velocities as first differences, so deformation binds directly to actuator motion.
- **MPPI** controller: ==pink-noise== perturbations around a warm-started nominal sequence, biLSTM rollouts over the horizon, cost-exponential weighting of shape and control terms to update end-effector target positions.

## Results

- Multi-step rollouts over 150 timesteps give **3.3 cm** average shape error and **61.59 percent** velocity error, with structure, length and curvature preserved across long horizons.
- In simulation, U-shape control succeeded in **93 of 100** trials averaging **7.26 s**, while random target shapes succeeded in only **20.5 percent** of 1,000 trials averaging **13.3 s**.
- Real robot trials: **85 percent** success for 2D control of a 6 mm cable at **15.7 s** average, dropping to **50 percent** in untrained 3D and **60 percent** on an unseen 1.5 mm marker-free wire.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Encoding object state relative to the end effector buys translational invariance, letting one simulation-trained dynamics model transfer across cables of different diameter and stiffness.
> - Large gap between single-shape and random-shape success shows sampling-based controllers tuned for one target configuration do not generalize without re-tuning the cost and noise parameters.
> - Learned rollout models bottleneck sampling-based control: sample count trades against inference cost, and pre-bent physical objects break symmetry assumptions the simulator never modeled.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

When a robot moves a cable, it changes both where the cable is and what shape it has. Consider a robot holding one end of a cable while the other end is fixed. Moving the gripped end also changes the cable's shape, so the robot must anticipate deformation rather than treat the cable like a rigid object. A controller therefore needs a fast enough prediction of that changing shape to choose its next move. The authors pair a neural model trained on simulated cable motion with a controller that tests candidate robot motions against that model. On a real robot, a marked cable was successfully shaped into the desired U-shape. This shows the approach can work beyond simulation, but that successful sequence is an illustration, not a measure of reliability.

A successful 2D experiment with a 50 cm cable, 6 mm in diameter, with 9 markers. The sequence shows an example outcome, not trial statistics.

### 2. How the controller anticipates a bend

The system represents cable shape and motion using points along the cable relative to the robot end effector, and includes the end-effector state. A biLSTM (bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory network) predicts point velocities for the next step.

The controller is MPPI, or Model Predictive Path Integral control, a sampling-based form of model-predictive control. It samples candidate control sequences and rolls them forward using the biLSTM's predictions. It scores those sequences for distance from the target shape and for control effort, then updates the sequence toward lower-cost candidates. It applies the first action, receives the new state, and repeats. In other words, it plans several steps ahead, executes the first, then plans again from the updated state.

The prediction–selection–execution cycle. Target cable points feed the MPPI controller, and candidate sequences $U_{1}, U_{2}, \ldots, U_{k}$ feed the biLSTM model. Predicted points return to the controller, and the robot end-effector/current state closes the loop.

MPPI was already used for rope manipulation in Yan, Zhu, Jin, and Bohg's [*Self-Supervised Learning of State Estimation for Manipulating Deformable Linear Objects*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1911.06283). The present authors' distinguishing emphasis is using a biLSTM model trained on synthetic MuJoCo data inside MPPI to shape the cable. They then test that system with different physical objects and in a 3D setting.

### 3. What the tests establish

A trial counted as successful when the cable's maximum deviation from its target points was no more than 2 cm. In the real 2D experiment, the 50 cm, 6 mm cable with nine markers met that criterion in 85% of 20 trials. Mean elapsed time among successful trials was 15.7 seconds.

Results were weaker in the tested 3D and thinner-wire settings. The authors report 50% success across 20 trials for a 3D task with the same cable raised on a 7 cm platform. This scenario was not trained, and failures were trials not completed within 90 seconds. A 1.5 mm wire without physical markers, tracked using FastDLO with nine virtual points, had 60% success across 20 trials. Controller parameters were adjusted during real-world validation, and the paper says retuning may be needed for dissimilar scenarios.

Simulation shows the same target dependence. A simulated U-shape succeeded in 93% of 100 trials, with a 2 cm maximum-deviation criterion and a 30-second deadline. By contrast, only 20.5% of 1,000 simulated random-target trials succeeded. The authors say performance is better for U-like targets and can fail when a cable deforms initially in the wrong direction. Strong results on the tuned U-shape task do not imply equally reliable arbitrary-shape manipulation.

### 4. A loop, not a general guarantee

Prediction lets the controller reason about the shape change caused by a robot movement. Feedback lets it revise the next move after seeing the actual result. The experiments show that this loop can form a target U in simulation and on a robot, including tested cases beyond the training setup.

The measurements establish outcomes for particular conditions. They do not show superiority over an alternative controller or prove that the learned model alone caused success, and the paper reports no test separating the biLSTM's contribution from MPPI's. The low random-shape success rate marks the boundary between shaping a familiar goal and reliably controlling arbitrary deformations. The authors provide training data and model weights in the [DaRUS replication dataset](https://doi.org/10.18419/DARUS-5050).
