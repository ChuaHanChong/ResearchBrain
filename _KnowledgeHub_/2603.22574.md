# GIFT: Generalizing Intent for Flexible Test-Time Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> GIFT (Generalizing Intent for Flexible Test-Time Rewards) is a framework that allows robot reward functions, learned from human demonstrations, to generalize to novel environments by grounding state similarity in high-level human intent. It leverages large language models to infer intent from contrasting behaviors and subsequently align novel test states to intent-equivalent training states, achieving up to 20% higher win rates in complex tasks compared to baselines relying on surface-level features.

## Problem

- Learned robot reward functions often fail to generalize to novel scenarios, capturing spurious correlations from training data rather than the underlying abstract human intent.
- Existing methods for reward generalization primarily rely on surface-level visual or lexical similarities, which frequently diverge from the functional equivalence that humans consider relevant for a task.
- The notion of 'state similarity' is not static but dynamically determined by the human's high-level task intent, a context often missing in current reward learning paradigms.

## Method

- The GIFT framework utilizes Large Language Models (LMs) to infer the high-level human intent from contrastive pairs of human-preferred and non-preferred robot trajectories.
- It introduces an intent-conditioned kernel, implemented by LMs, to quantify the similarity between states based on the inferred intent, enabling functional alignment across distribution shifts.
- At test time, novel trajectories are aligned to intent-equivalent training states using an intent-conditioned alignment operator before applying a fixed, pre-trained reward function, allowing generalization without retraining.

## Results

- LM-inferred intents consistently showed higher cosine similarity with ground-truth intents, indicating the accuracy of the intent inference component.
- GIFT achieved higher pairwise win rates on unseen trajectory pairs across four simulated tasks, with up to a 20% improvement in complex tasks like 'Pack Backpack' compared to vision and language-based baselines.
- The framework demonstrated superior F1 scores for state alignment and maintained low false positive/negative rates, accurately mapping objects based on intent-relevance and effectively generalizing to real-world tasks with a Franka robot and held-out physical objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Meaningful state similarity for robust reward generalization is fundamentally driven by high-level human intent, moving beyond low-level visual or linguistic features.
> - Large Language Models can effectively deduce abstract human intentions by contrasting preferred and non-preferred robot behaviors, serving as powerful intent estimators.
> - LMs can act as implicit, intent-conditioned similarity functions, enabling the mapping of novel test-time semantic components to their functionally equivalent training-time counterparts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics extensively utilizes human input, such as demonstrations or preferences, to enable robots to learn desired reward functions and consequently, appropriate behaviors. This approach addresses the difficulty of hand-engineering explicit reward functions for complex tasks. Methods like Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) and preference learning aim to infer the underlying human objective from observed data.

However, a persistent challenge in this landscape is the generalization of learned reward functions. Reward functions trained on specific datasets often exhibit "reward misspecification" and "out-of-distribution misgeneralization." This occurs because models tend to latch onto spurious correlations present in the training data rather than capturing the abstract, high-level human intent. For instance, a robot taught to "pack a paintbrush" might fail when encountering "molding clay" for an "art supplies" task, even if both items serve the same functional purpose under that higher intent.

Prior research has attempted to enhance robustness and generalization through various mechanisms. These include data augmentation techniques, leveraging visual similarity (e.g., using pre-trained vision models like DINO) or language similarity (e.g., using BERT embeddings over object descriptions) to map novel observations to familiar ones. Other approaches involve using large pre-trained models to establish textual or visual correspondences between test-time and training-time observations, or projecting test images onto a known training manifold. While these methods improve generalization to some extent, they often rely on surface-level cues—visual appearance or lexical descriptions—which may not accurately reflect what humans actually consider relevant for a task. For example, a visually similar object might be functionally irrelevant, or a lexically similar word might refer to a functionally distinct object. These surface-level similarities can diverge from the true, underlying human intent.

The work presented in "GIFT" distinguishes itself by proposing a framework that moves beyond these surface-level correspondences. It argues that meaningful state similarity, crucial for reward generalization, is fundamentally driven by high-level human intent. By explicitly inferring and conditioning on this intent, GIFT aims to facilitate reward generalization that is robust to significant distribution shifts, such as new objects or layouts, which commonly lead to failures in existing methods. This positions GIFT as an advancement that seeks to bridge the gap between low-level data features and the abstract cognitive goals that define human preferences in robot tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robot reward functions, learned from human demonstrations, to generalize effectively to novel environments and scenarios that present significant distribution shifts from the training data. This generalization is sought without requiring retraining of the reward model at test time.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current reward learning paradigms:
1. **Failure to Generalize**: Reward functions learned from human demonstrations often fail to generalize when deployed in new scenarios. Instead of capturing the human's underlying, high-level intent (e.g., "pack art supplies"), they tend to "latch onto spurious correlations" present in the limited training data (e.g., learning to specifically "pack a paintbrush"). This overfitting leads to catastrophic failures where the robot either ignores crucial task aspects or fixates on irrelevant ones when encountering new objects or layouts.
2. **Limitations of Surface-Level Cues**: Existing methods that attempt to improve robustness often rely on visual or semantic similarity. However, the authors argue that these "surface-level cues often diverge from what humans actually care about" when defining task success. For instance, a vision model might mistakenly group a "scrubber" with a "paintbrush" due to visual resemblance, while a language model might group a "toothbrush" with a "paintbrush" due to lexical similarity. Neither of these captures the functional equivalence of a "paintbrush" and "molding clay" under the intent of "packing art supplies."
3. **Intent-Dependent Similarity**: A core insight motivating GIFT is that the notion of "state similarity" is not static but dynamically driven by the human's high-level task intent. Different intents induce different measures of similarity over the same set of states. For example, if the intent is "pack art supplies," a paintbrush and molding clay are similar. However, if the intent changes to "set aside painting supplies," then a paintbrush, watercolor, and easel become similar, while molding clay might become dissimilar. Without conditioning on intent, a robot lacks the contextual understanding to determine the appropriate notion of similarity, risking incorrect generalization.

To address these issues, the key objective is to introduce a framework that "grounds reward generalization in human intent rather than surface cues." This involves leveraging the commonsense reasoning capabilities of large language models (LMs) to:
- Infer the high-level intent from contrasting pairs of preferred and non-preferred behaviors observed during training.
- Use this inferred intent at deployment to align novel test states with behaviorally equivalent training states. This alignment allows the robot to reuse its pre-trained reward function without additional data collection or model fine-tuning.

In summary, the motivation is to overcome the generalization failures of learned reward functions by introducing an intent-conditioned state similarity mechanism, thereby enabling robots to understand and act upon the true human objective even in novel and diverse environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GIFT (Generalizing Intent for Flexible Test-Time Rewards) is a two-stage framework designed to enable learned reward functions to generalize across distribution shifts by grounding them in human high-level intent. The framework is agnostic to the specific reward learning algorithm, though the authors concretize their exposition using Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL).

**Problem Formulation Context:**
The problem is framed within the context of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), where a robot aims to find a policy that maximizes cumulative rewards. The reward function $R_{\theta}(\xi)$ is not known a priori and is learned from human input (e.g., demonstrations, preferences). The challenge arises when applying this learned reward function to novel test states ($S_{test}$) that differ from the training states ($S_{train}$), leading to two issues:
1. **Reward Misspecification:** The learned reward $R_{\theta}$ captures spurious correlations from limited training data rather than the true high-level intent ($R_{\theta}^*$).
2. **Out-of-Distribution Misgeneralization:** Even if the intent is captured in the training domain, $R_{\theta}$ may assign arbitrary or unreliable values to novel test states not encountered during training.

GIFT aims to address these issues by enabling the reward function to adapt to these shifts without retraining, through intent-conditioned state alignment.

**The GIFT Framework:**

1. **Inferring High-Level Intent ($\hat{\omega}$):**
 - GIFT leverages Large Language Models (LMs) to infer the human's underlying high-level intent.
 - The LM is provided with **contrastive trajectory pairs**:
 - Human-preferred demonstrations ($D_H$)
 - Reference (non-preferred) trajectories ($D_{\neg H}$), such as rollouts from a nominal controller in the same scenes.
 - By contrasting these behaviors, the LM generates a natural language summary of the high-level intent ($\hat{\omega}$) that explains the difference between $D_H$ and $D_{\neg H}$. This process is denoted as $\hat{\omega} \triangleq J_{LM}(D_H, D_{\neg H})$.
 - Trajectories are represented using human-interpretable feature values (e.g., end-effector position, object distances, gripper orientation) along with natural language descriptions of these features, enabling the LM to reason abstractly.

2. **Intent-Conditioned State Similarity ($K(s, s' | \omega)$):**
 - The core insight is that state similarity should be defined by the high-level intent, not just surface-level features.
 - GIFT introduces an **intent-conditioned kernel** $K(s, s' | \omega)$ that quantifies similarity between two states ($s, s'$) given an intent ($\omega$). Higher values indicate greater similarity.
 - LMs are utilized as implicit similarity functions. Given the inferred intent ($\hat{\omega}$) and descriptions of two states, the LM leverages its commonsense priors to determine their similarity.
 - This kernel aims to capture cross-modal semantic relationships and functional goals, ensuring that objects serving the same purpose under a given intent are considered similar (e.g., molding clay and a paintbrush for "pack art supplies").

3. **Aligning Test States ($f_{\hat{\omega}}(s')$):**
 - Once $\hat{\omega}$ is inferred, it is used to align novel test states to their nearest intent-equivalent training states.
 - An **intent-conditioned alignment operator** $f_{\hat{\omega}}$ maps a novel test state $s' \in S_{test}$ to a training state $s \in S_{train}$ by maximizing $K(s, s' | \hat{\omega})$.
 - For trajectories, $f_{\hat{\omega}}$ is applied state-wise: $f_{\hat{\omega}}(\xi') \triangleq (f_{\hat{\omega}}(s'_0), \dots, f_{\hat{\omega}}(s'_T))$.
 - This operator projects the semantic components of each test state into the training domain along intent-relevant dimensions, while preserving continuous dimensions (e.g., robot pose).

4. **Test-Time Reward Generalization:**
 - The reward function $R_{\theta}$ is learned *once* on training data and remains fixed.
 - At test time, unseen trajectories ($\xi'$) are evaluated by first aligning them into the training domain using $f_{\hat{\omega}}$ and then applying the fixed reward function: $e_{R_{\theta}}(\xi' | \hat{\omega}) \triangleq R_{\theta}(f_{\hat{\omega}}(\xi'))$.
 - Planning or policy selection is then performed using this re-evaluated reward: $\pi^* = \arg \max_{\pi} E_{\xi' \sim \pi}[e_{R_{\theta}}(\xi' | \hat{\omega})]$.

**Implementation Details:**

- **Intent Estimator Parameterization:** The authors use GPT-4o for all LM calls. The LM is prompted to identify the higher-level intent distinguishing preferred from non-preferred trajectories, yielding abstract summaries (e.g., "avoid liquids near water-sensitive objects").
- **Intent-Guided Alignment Implementation:** The alignment operator $f_{\hat{\omega}}$ is implemented via another GPT-4o call. It receives the inferred intent $\hat{\omega}$ as context and is prompted to map semantic components of the test state (e.g., object identity) to their intent-equivalent elements in the training set. This involves a binary relevance judgment: if an element is relevant under $\hat{\omega}$, it's mapped to a corresponding training element; otherwise, it's mapped to a 'distractor' class.
- **Reward Function ($R_{\theta}$) Implementation:** The experiments adopt Maximum Entropy IRL. The reward is parameterized as a linear function over time-aggregated trajectory features: $R_{\theta}(\xi) = \theta \cdot \phi(\xi)$. This linear parameterization is chosen for efficient learning, but the framework is compatible with more complex approximators. The crucial aspect is that $R_{\theta}$ and $\phi$ are fixed at deployment, with all generalization handled by $f_{\hat{\omega}}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates GIFT across four tabletop manipulation tasks using 7-DoF robot arms, both in simulation (Jaco robot in PyBullet) and real-world settings (Franka Research Robot), involving over 50 unseen objects.

**Sanity Check: Intent Inference Accuracy**
Before evaluating generalization, a sanity check assessed the quality of LM-inferred intents. The LM was tasked with deducing human intent from demonstration pairs. The results (Figure 3) showed that LM-inferred intents consistently achieved higher cosine similarity with ground-truth intents compared to a generic intent baseline across all simulated and real-world tasks. This finding indicates that the intent inference component of GIFT produces reasonable and task-relevant high-level intentions.

**RQ1: Effectiveness Compared to Baselines (Test-Time Pairwise Win Rate)**
To determine if high-level intent similarity leads to better reward performance, GIFT was compared against three baseline similarity methods:
- **Vision-based:** Using DINO embeddings for visual similarity.
- **Language-based:** Using BERT embeddings for textual description similarity.
- **LM_No Intent:** An ablation of GIFT, using an LM for direct state mapping without explicit intent inference or conditioning.
Each method used the same align-and-score procedure for reward evaluation.
The results (Figure 4) demonstrate that GIFT consistently achieved a higher pairwise win rate on unseen trajectory pairs across all four simulated tasks. The performance gains were particularly notable in more complex tasks like "Pack Backpack" and "Store Into Drawer," where GIFT showed a 20% improvement over the next best baseline. In simpler tasks like "Place Mug" and "Sweep Spill," improvements of 7% were observed. This supports the hypothesis that intent-conditioned similarity leads to superior generalization in novel test-time scenarios compared to low-level visual or language similarity.

**RQ2: Failure Modes for Low-level Features (Test-Time State Alignment F1 Score, False Positives/Negatives)**
This research question investigated why low-level features might fail. The ability of each similarity method to correctly map a test-time object to a ground-truth training-time object (or flag it as a distractor) was evaluated using F1 score, false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). Two variants for vision and language baselines were tested: an oracle (threshold maximized for F1 on test set) and a fixed-threshold non-oracle.
- **F1 Score:** GIFT achieved a superior F1 score for state alignment across all tasks (Figure 5), indicating more accurate object mapping based on intent.
- **Confounding States:** Analysis on confounding subsets (language confounds $S_{lang}$, visual confounds $S_{vis}$, and unknown-intent confounds $S_{unk}$) for complex tasks revealed significant differences (Figure 6). GIFT maintained low FP and FN rates across these categories. In contrast, oracle baselines, despite tuning thresholds, often traded errors across different types of confounds (e.g., reducing visual FPs but increasing language FNs), demonstrating their inability to correct a fundamentally misaligned similarity signal. LM_No Intent, lacking intent-relevance, exhibited a high FN rate.
- **Misalignments by Low-Level Features:** Detailed analysis illustrated the shortcomings of low-level features. For "Pack Backpack with Art Supplies," non-art supplies like a "toothbrush" and "broomstick" showed high language/vision similarity to a "paintbrush," while relevant art supplies like "molding clay" and "tablet with stylus" showed low similarity (Figure 8). Similarly, for "Store Valuables," an "unvaluable paper ring" was highly similar to a "valuable diamond ring" based on low-level features, while other valuable items like "MacBook Pro" were dissimilar (Figure 9). These findings underscore how superficial visual or language similarities lead to false positives and negatives, confirming the hypothesis that GIFT performs better by using intent-relevance.

**RQ3: Real-World Experiments (Franka Panda Robot Behavior)**
To assess transferability to physical settings, two tasks were recreated on a 7-DoF Franka robot using held-out physical objects. GIFT used intents inferred directly from real-world Franka demonstrations.
The results (Figure 7) showed that GIFT successfully translated to the physical domain. Planning with high-level intents produced behavior more aligned with human preferences on held-out objects compared to baselines. For example, GIFT correctly planned for packing "molding clay" (an art supply) and storing an "iPhone Pro" (a valuable), while correctly treating confounding objects as distractors. This demonstrates that LMs can infer robust intents from real-world, potentially suboptimal, human demonstrations, enabling flexible and accurate planning on physical robots.

In summary, the main findings consistently support that grounding reward generalization in high-level human intent, as implemented by GIFT, leads to more robust, accurate, and generalizable robot behavior compared to methods relying on surface-level visual or language cues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in GIFT addresses a fundamental limitation in robot learning from human input: the challenge of generalizing learned reward functions to novel, unseen environments. By grounding reward generalization in human intent rather than surface-level cues, GIFT offers a significant advancement with several potential impacts.

**Core Contribution to Robot Learning:**
The primary significance lies in reframing test-time reward reuse as an intent-conditioned alignment problem. This represents a conceptual shift from comparing states based on readily available visual or linguistic features to aligning states based on deeper, preference-driven semantic equivalences. This approach is more robust to "reward misspecification" and "out-of-distribution misgeneralization" by capturing the abstract functional roles of objects and actions as defined by human intent.

**Enhanced Generalization and Adaptability:**
GIFT enables robots to effectively generalize their learned behaviors to new objects, layouts, and contexts without requiring tedious re-training or extensive new demonstrations. This is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic, real-world environments where variations are common. The ability to correctly identify functionally equivalent items (e.g., "molding clay" as an "art supply" akin to a "paintbrush") despite disparate visual or lexical features means robots can maintain task performance even when encountering entirely novel instances.

**Reduced User Burden:**
By facilitating test-time generalization without retraining, GIFT significantly reduces the burden on human users. Instead of having to teach the robot new demonstrations for every slight variation in the environment or new object encountered, users can rely on the robot's ability to infer intent and adapt. This makes robot learning more practical and scalable for non-expert users.

**Effective Integration of Large Language Models (LLMs):**
The research highlights a practical and effective application of large language models for high-level commonsense reasoning in robotics. LMs are leveraged not just for generating text, but for inferring abstract human intent from contrasting behaviors and for conditioning state similarity based on that intent. This demonstrates the potential of LLMs to imbue robots with a richer understanding of human objectives, extending their utility beyond basic perception or action.

**Towards More Human-Aligned Robots:**
By focusing on human intent, GIFT moves towards creating robots that understand and act in ways that are more deeply aligned with human preferences and goals. This contributes to the broader goal of intuitive and effective Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), where robots can interpret nuanced human objectives rather than just mimicking surface-level actions.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The identified limitations also point to important areas for future research. The dependence on the quality and consistency of LM-inferred intent suggests the need for uncertainty-aware intent kernels and mechanisms for calibrating confidence or abstaining when the LM is uncertain. Addressing potential noise in symbolic object descriptors and mitigating LLM biases (e.g., hallucination, amplification of training-set biases) are critical for robust real-world deployment. Future work could explore refining intent estimates through additional user queries or developing guardrails to ensure safe and reliable operation.

In conclusion, GIFT provides a principled framework for robust reward generalization in robotics, shifting the paradigm from surface-level feature matching to intent-driven state alignment. This advancement has the potential to make robot learning more efficient, generalizable, and aligned with complex human objectives, thereby broadening the applicability of robots in diverse and unpredictable real-world settings.
