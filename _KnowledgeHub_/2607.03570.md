# Cross-Embodiment Robot Manipulation via a Unified Hand Action Space

> [!summary] Summary
> UHAS represents dexterous hand actions as deformations of a canonical unit sphere fitted to any hand URDF, with a Cascade Inverse Kinematics solver mapping deformations to embodiment-specific joints at up to 150 Hz. PPO policies trained in this space perform in-hand cube reorientation across Allegro, LEAP, Shadow and MANO hands, reaching ~99% success and zero-shot transfer to unseen hands.

## Problem

- Large-scale robot learning datasets and policies are dominated by parallel-jaw grippers, leaving dexterous multi-finger hands comparatively underexplored despite their far richer manipulation capability.
- Existing dexterous systems define actions in embodiment-specific joint spaces, so policies, datasets and retraining pipelines must be rebuilt for every hand with different kinematics.
- Prior cross-embodiment work uses separate action heads, latent actions or canonical joint representations; geometric sphere correspondences were confined to grasp synthesis, not action spaces.

## Method

- Automatic ==sphere construction== from a hand URDF: radius 2l/pi from palm-to-fingertip length, centered above the palm, axes canonicalized and distances normalized to a shared unit sphere.
- ==Sphere deformation action space== (**UHAS**): driving planes aligned with fingers control lateral angular displacement, per-plane driving vectors control radial displacement, interpolated into a full field (15 dimensions typical).
- ==Cascade Inverse Kinematics== splits joints into lateral (precomputed lookup table) and encompassing (root-to-fingertip 1D subproblems), running at 150 Hz; ==PPO== trains policies on shared sphere-frame observations.

## Results

- In simulation UHAS reaches **99.1-99.8%** success across Allegro, LEAP, Shadow and MANO, beating the joint-control baseline (**98.0-99.6%**), and one multi-hand policy matches per-hand policies.
- Zero-shot transfer to a held-out hand gives **95.3-98.1%** success except Shadow at **85.7%**; **500** finetuning iterations, versus ~**4,500** from scratch, lift Shadow to **95.8%**.
- Real LEAP-hand trials average **2.0** consecutive reorientations for the LEAP-trained policy versus **0.6** for joint control; ablation picks **2** driving vectors per finger (**98.7%**, **4.5h** training).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A shared geometric abstraction of the workspace, rather than joint space, can serve as an action interface, letting one policy control hands with different finger counts.
> - Cross-embodiment gains are asymmetric: transfer degrades most between 4-finger and 5-finger morphologies, and multi-hand training yields conservative policies that lose real-world performance to specialists.
> - Near-saturated simulation success hides a large sim-to-real gap; system identification and domain randomization did not prevent drops to ~2 reorientations on hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robot manipulation, driven by imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and vision-language-action (VLA) models, have enabled impressive capabilities in tasks such as grasping, pick-and-place, and long-horizon execution. However, much of the progress in large-scale robot learning systems and datasets has predominantly focused on simpler end-effectors, such as parallel-jaw grippers. Dexterous robotic hands, despite their potential for more intricate manipulation, remain comparatively underexplored. This is primarily due to their diverse kinematic structures, varied action parameterizations, and complex control interfaces. Consequently, existing dexterous manipulation systems often rely on embodiment-specific action spaces, requiring separate datasets and retraining procedures for each hand, which significantly impedes the transfer of learned behaviors across different robotic platforms.

This work addresses the challenge of cross-embodiment transfer for dexterous manipulation by proposing a unified representation. The broader landscape includes increasing efforts to develop generalist robot policies and foundation models that can operate across multiple robotic embodiments. Prior works have explored unified action representations through various mechanisms, such as different action heads, latent action spaces, or canonical joint-space representations. Additionally, geometric representations have emerged as a method for abstracting robot-object interactions for tasks like grasping, establishing correspondences between gripper surfaces and canonical shapes like spheres. This paper contributes to this evolving landscape by extending the concept of geometric representation, specifically sphere correspondence, beyond static grasp representation to define a dynamic, unified action space for dexterous manipulation across heterogeneous robotic hands. This approach aims to facilitate scalable policy learning and data sharing in a manner that is less dependent on specific hardware configurations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to overcome the limitations imposed by embodiment-specific robot manipulation policies, which currently restrict the transfer of learned behaviors across robotic hands with different kinematic structures. The core motivation stems from the observation that the diversity in mechanical designs, joint configurations, and control interfaces of dexterous hands makes it challenging to develop generalizable manipulation policies. Current systems typically require retraining or significant adaptation for each new hand embodiment, which is resource-intensive and hinders the development of scalable robot learning.

To address this, the authors propose the Unified Hand Action Space (UHAS), an embodiment-agnostic representation for dexterous manipulation actions. The central idea is to model manipulation actions not as movements in specific joint spaces, but as geometric deformations of a canonical sphere. This spherical representation is chosen because it offers several advantages:
- **Continuity and Topology-Agnosticism:** A sphere provides a continuous, closed surface that can be consistently parameterized, irrespective of the varying kinematic structures and number of fingers across different robotic hands.
- **Semantic Abstraction:** Many dexterous grasping and manipulation behaviors can be interpreted as coordinated radial and angular interactions around an object-centered workspace. Representing actions as sphere deformations allows for a higher-level, more semantic understanding of hand behaviors, abstracting away the low-level mechanical details.
- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** By defining actions in a shared geometric space, the representation intrinsically supports generalization. This enables policies to be trained on one or multiple hands and then potentially transferred, even zero-shot, to previously unseen robotic hands.

The specific objectives derived from this motivation include:
1. **Developing a Sphere-Based Action Representation:** To define robotic hand actions as geometric deformations of a canonical sphere.
2. **Creating a Mapping Algorithm:** To devise a mechanism that can translate these generalized sphere deformations into specific, executable joint configurations for various robotic hands. This algorithm needs to be computationally efficient for real-time control.
3. **Demonstrating Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** To empirically evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed unified action space in enabling stable dexterous control, multi-hand policy learning, and meaningful transfer (including zero-shot and rapid finetuning) across a range of robotic hands with different kinematic structures in both simulation and real-world environments.
4. **Facilitating Scalable Learning:** To lay groundwork for future large-scale robot learning systems and foundation models by enabling data sharing and policy generalization across heterogeneous robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development and validation of the Unified Hand Action Space (UHAS) and an associated Cascade Inverse Kinematics (CIK) algorithm.

#### 3.1. Unified Hand Action Space (UHAS)

UHAS is a sphere-based geometric action representation where robotic hand actions are modeled as deformations of a canonical sphere.
- **Automatic Sphere Creation:** Given a robotic hand's URDF model, a canonical sphere is automatically constructed. The process involves:
 - Identifying palm and fingertip coordinate frames in an open-hand configuration.
 - Calculating the palm center by averaging finger root positions and determining the average fingertip distance `l`.
 - Setting the sphere radius `r = 2l/π` and positioning the sphere center along the outward palm normal at distance `r` from the palm center.
 - Establishing a canonical orientation: z-axis aligns with the outward palm normal, x-axis with the middle finger direction.
 - Normalizing all distances by `r` to create a scale-invariant unit sphere, enabling a shared representation across hands.

- **Unified Hand Surface Correspondence:** Dense geometric correspondences are established between the canonical sphere surface and the robotic hand.
 - Points are uniformly sampled on the sphere surface, and their spherical coordinates (`θ`, `ϕ`) and surface normals are computed.
 - These sampled sphere points are projected onto nearby points on the interior hand surface (palm and fingers), creating correspondences.
 - While the 3D locations of hand surface points change with hand configuration, their associated spherical coordinates remain invariant, allowing hand actions to be expressed as sphere deformations.

- **Sphere Deformation Action Space:** To obtain a compact action representation, sphere deformations are modeled using only lateral angular displacements (`∆θ`) and radial expansions/contractions (`∆r`), with the north pole of the sphere rigidly anchored to the palm frame.
 - The deformation is parameterized using a sparse set of control primitives: a finite number of "driving planes" (passing through the sphere center at fixed azimuthal angles `θ_plane`) control `∆θ`.
 - Within each driving plane, "driving vectors" (control points at fixed polar angles `ϕ`) parameterize `∆r`.
 - The complete deformation field is reconstructed via interpolation of `∆θ` from neighboring driving planes and 2D interpolation of `∆r` from driving-vector displacements. For example, five driving planes with two driving vectors per plane result in a 15-dimensional action space. Driving planes are aligned with hand fingers.

#### 3.2. Cascade Inverse Kinematics (CIK)

The CIK algorithm maps a deformed sphere surface to embodiment-specific hand joint configurations.
- **Joint Classification:** Each hand joint is classified (once per hand, offline) into two categories based on its dominant effect on fingertip position in the sphere coordinate frame:
 - **Lateral joints:** Primarily affect the azimuthal angle `θ`, producing side-to-side finger motion.
 - **Encompassing joints:** Primarily affect the radial distance `r` and polar angle `ϕ`, allowing fingers to conform to the sphere surface.
 - This decomposition enables efficient cascaded solving.

- **Lateral Joint Mapping:** A lookup table is precomputed for each lateral joint.
 - The joint is swept across its range, and for each value, encompassing joints are solved on the undeformed sphere.
 - The resulting fingertip azimuthal angle `θ_fingertip` is recorded, mapping `q_lateral` to `θ_fingertip`.
 - During inference, the target `∆θ` for a driving plane (aligned with a finger) is used to find `θ_fingertip_target = θ_initial + ∆θ`. The lookup table then yields the corresponding `q_lateral`.

- **Encompassing Joint Cascade:** After lateral joints are set, encompassing joints are solved sequentially from finger root to fingertip along each kinematic chain.
 - For each encompassing joint, a 1D inverse kinematics subproblem is solved to place downstream hand surface points onto their corresponding deformed sphere locations.
 - This non-iterative, forward-pass procedure progressively conforms the finger geometry to the target sphere deformation while maintaining kinematic feasibility.

- **Sphere Controller:** The combination of sphere deformation parameterization and CIK forms the Sphere Controller. The learned policy outputs sphere deformation parameters, which CIK converts into executable joint configurations at high frequency (e.g., 150 Hz). This decoupling allows real-time control across diverse hands.

#### 3.3. In-Hand Manipulation Experiments

- **Tasks:** In-hand cube reorientation in both simulation and the real world.
 - Simulation: NVIDIA Isaac Lab Repose Cube environment, adapted for Leap Hand, Allegro Hand (4 fingers), Shadow Hand, and a simulated MANO human hand (5 fingers). Each episode involves 10 sequential target orientations.
 - Real World: Experiments with a physical LEAP Hand and Allegro Hand. Policies run until the cube falls.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Average Consecutive Reorientations (max 10 in sim) and Success Rate (percentage of successful reorientation attempts).
- **Baselines:** A policy trained to directly predict hand joint positions, using joint positions and velocities as input.
- **Manipulation Policies:** Trained using RSL-RL PPO with a lightweight actor-critic network.
 - Action Space: Sphere deformations. One driving plane per finger with two driving vectors. For 4-finger hands, an extra driving plane is added at the ring-finger position to maintain a unified 5-plane action space with 5-finger hands.
 - Observation Space: Homogeneous observations derived from 2 points per finger (midpoint and fingertip) on the kinematic chain, expressed in the canonical sphere coordinate frame and normalized by the hand-specific sphere radius. Ring-finger observations are duplicated for 4-finger hands.
 - Reward Function: Adapted from Isaac Lab, augmented with regularization terms penalizing deviations of lateral and encompassing joint positions from a reference configuration.
 - Domain Randomization: Applied extensively during training (object properties, robot properties, hand pose, action space parameters) to improve cross-embodiment generalization and sim-to-real transfer.
- **Real-World Deployment:**
 - Cube pose estimation uses AprilTags.
 - System identification performed on the LEAP Hand's servo control law to match simulated dynamics to physical hardware.
 - Increased domain randomization for real-world trained models, including velocity limits.
 - Asymmetric actor-critic architecture (actor uses positional info, critic full state) to enhance robustness to real-world sensing noise.
 - Software-level communication management and physical modifications (reinforced base, thumb attachment for LEAP Hand) implemented to address hardware limitations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides evidence for the effectiveness of the UHAS and CIK approach in enabling cross-embodiment dexterous manipulation.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Main Results (Table 1):** The UHAS representation consistently demonstrated strong performance across all four tested robotic hands.
 - UHAS policies (Single-Hand, Multi-Hand, Zero-shot) generally outperformed the joint-space baseline in both task success rate and long-horizon stability (Average Consecutive Reorientations).
 - A single policy trained across all four hands (Multi-Hand) achieved performance comparable to policies trained specifically for individual hands (Single-Hand), indicating effective multi-hand policy learning.
 - Meaningful zero-shot transfer was observed, with policies trained on a subset of hands performing effectively on previously unseen robotic hands, despite significant morphological differences. For instance, the Zero-shot policy for MANO hand achieved 98.1% success and 8.9 consecutive reorientations.

- **Cross-Morphology Generalization (Table 2):** Policies showed transfer capabilities across hands with different numbers of fingers (e.g., 4-finger vs. 5-finger).
 - Policies trained on 5-finger hands (Shadow + MANO) showed substantial success on 4-finger hands (Allegro: 66.2% / 1.9; LEAP: 80.8% / 3.7), and vice-versa (Shadow: 83.2% / 4.0; MANO: 95.0% / 7.6). While a performance gap existed compared to in-distribution training, the results indicated that UHAS generalizes across different finger counts and kinematic structures.

- **Finetuning on Unseen Hands (Table 3):** Rapid adaptation capabilities were demonstrated.
 - Finetuning a policy (initially trained on MANO hand) for only 500 iterations on a new target hand significantly improved performance over zero-shot transfer, recovering strong manipulation capabilities. For example, Shadow hand improved from 85.7% success (4.4 reorientations) to 95.8% success (7.8 reorientations). This suggests that UHAS provides a robust foundation for efficient transfer learning.

- **Ablation Studies (Tables 4, 9, 10, 11, Appendix E):**
 - **Driving Vectors (Table 4):** Using two driving vectors per plane offered the optimal balance between performance and training efficiency for the cube reorientation task, outperforming one, three, or four vectors.
 - **Observation Points (Table 9):** Two observation points per finger (midpoint and fingertip) provided sufficient information, with marginal gains from additional points, making it a computationally efficient choice.
 - **Driving Planes (Table 10):** The difference between 4 and 5 driving planes was minimal for the specific reorientation task, suggesting some redundancy or task-specific insensitivity to finer control of the pinky finger.
 - **Domain Randomization (Table 11):** Domain randomization during training consistently improved zero-shot transfer performance, particularly for hands with larger morphological differences from the training set, by increasing policy robustness.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results with Sim-to-Real Transfer (Tables 5, 13)

- **Performance Gap:** A notable performance gap was observed between simulation and real-world deployment for both LEAP and Allegro hands, despite extensive system identification and domain randomization.
- **UHAS vs. Baseline:** All UHAS variants consistently outperformed the joint-control baseline in real-world experiments. Even the zero-shot UHAS model achieved higher average consecutive reorientations than the baseline.
- **Single-Hand vs. Multi-Hand (LEAP Hand - Table 5):** The model trained exclusively on the LEAP Hand achieved the best real-world performance (mean 2.0 consecutive reorientations). The multi-hand trained model underperformed the single-hand LEAP model. This was attributed to the multi-hand policy potentially learning more conservative behaviors to be safe and feasible across diverse kinematics, while single-hand training allowed for exploitation of specific hand workspace and kinematics.
- **Single-Hand vs. Multi-Hand (Allegro Hand - Table 13):** The Allegro-trained model achieved the highest real-world performance (mean 2.1), tied with the multi-hand trained model (mean 2.1). The zero-shot policy for Allegro performed worse (mean 0.8), similar to the LEAP hand.
- **Variability:** High variance across real-world trials was noted for both hands, with some runs performing well and others failing quickly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a significant step towards developing more generalized and scalable robot manipulation policies, particularly for dexterous robotic hands.
- **Addressing a Core Challenge:** The research directly tackles the fundamental challenge of embodiment-specific policies in dexterous manipulation, which has historically limited the widespread adoption and research of multi-finger robot hands. By proposing UHAS, the authors offer a solution that abstracts away low-level hardware differences.
- **Unified and Continuous Action Space:** The sphere-based geometric action representation (UHAS) provides a continuous, topology-agnostic interface that captures the semantic behaviors of diverse hands. This is a novel approach for dynamic action representation, extending prior work on geometric representations from grasping to active manipulation.
- **Efficient Kinematic Mapping:** The Cascade Inverse Kinematics (CIK) algorithm is a computationally lightweight and effective method for translating generalized sphere deformations into specific joint commands in real-time. This efficiency is crucial for practical robot control.
- **Enabling Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The demonstration of effective multi-hand policy learning, meaningful zero-shot transfer to unseen hands, and rapid finetuning across diverse kinematic structures (including different finger counts) signifies a substantial advancement. This capability can drastically reduce the need for extensive retraining and data collection for each new robot hand.
- **Facilitating Scalable Robot Learning:** The ability to train a single policy that can operate across multiple, heterogeneous platforms enables data sharing and accelerates the development of generalist robot policies, potentially contributing to the foundation models for robotics. This could lead to a broader application of dexterous manipulation skills across the robotics community.
- **Practical Implications:** The successful deployment in real-world environments, even with the acknowledged sim-to-real challenges and necessary hardware adaptations, validates the practical viability of the proposed method. This indicates that UHAS is not merely a theoretical construct but a functional control framework.

**Limitations** acknowledged by the authors include the sensitivity of dexterous manipulation performance to low-level PD controller parameters and reward design, necessitating extensive domain randomization. Moreover, while UHAS enables transfer, performance degradation can occur when transferring between substantially different hand morphologies (e.g., 4-finger to 5-finger hands). These limitations suggest avenues for future research to further enhance robustness and generalization capabilities.
