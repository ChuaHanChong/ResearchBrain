# ForceBand: Learning Forceful Manipulation with sEMG

> [!summary] Summary
> The ForceBand system introduces a low-cost, wrist-worn sEMG device to capture per-finger contact forces from human demonstrations, enabling robots to learn nuanced, force-aware manipulation policies. It achieved an 87% success rate in pick-squeeze-place tasks on a UR-5 robot, demonstrating superior force estimation and control compared to vision-based methods.

## Problem

- Robot learning from human demonstrations primarily captures kinematic and visual data, omitting essential contact force information needed for dexterous manipulation.
- Current force acquisition methods are limited: vision-based inference is imprecise due to occlusions, while instrumented tactile gloves are intrusive and impractical for scalable data collection.
- Reliably translating sEMG signals into fine-grained fingertip forces for robot learning is challenging due to signal noise and variability across users.

## Method

- A low-cost, wrist-worn sEMG and IMU system, **ForceBand**, with anatomically guided electrode placement, captures muscle activity without direct fingertip instrumentation.
- A comprehensive multimodal dataset is compiled to train EMG2Force, a model predicting per-finger forces from sEMG and IMU signals, adapted via user-specific calibration.
- Force-augmented human demonstrations are retargeted to a robot, training a ==flow matching== transformer policy to predict end-effector pose, gripper aperture, and desired grip force.

## Results

- The muscle-aware 8-channel sEMG placement achieved an MAE of **0.77** N and RMSE of **1.33** N for force prediction, showing an **18%** error reduction over an evenly spaced 8-channel layout.
- EMG2Force consistently outperformed vision-based methods in both hand-level and finger-level force and contact estimation, approximately halving error metrics.
- Robot policies trained with ForceBand-derived supervision attained an **87%** overall success rate on pick-squeeze-place tasks, demonstrating successful object-specific force control on a physical robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Anatomically guided sEMG electrode placement on the forearm provides richer information for fine-grained force prediction, reducing error by 18% compared to evenly spaced layouts.
> - sEMG-based force estimation surpasses vision-only methods, especially for occluded fingers, enabling more reliable per-finger contact and force inference.
> - Force-augmented demonstrations, when incorporated into policy learning, allow robots to acquire object-specific and time-varying force control for dexterous tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning from human demonstrations commonly utilizes data sources such as motion-capture trajectories and internet videos to teach robots new manipulation skills. However, these data streams predominantly capture the kinematic and visual aspects of human motion, frequently omitting critical information about contact forces. This omission is a significant limitation because contact forces are essential for performing dexterous and force-sensitive manipulation tasks, such as handling fragile objects, applying precise pressure, or manipulating deformable materials.

Existing methods for acquiring contact force information exhibit specific limitations. Vision-based approaches attempt to infer hand-object contact and forces directly from video. While these methods can sometimes detect the presence of contact, inferring precise force magnitudes from visual appearance alone remains challenging, often compounded by factors such as visual occlusion and motion blur. Another approach involves direct force sensing through instrumented tactile gloves. These devices offer more reliable force supervision but introduce practical drawbacks: they can occlude visual cues, potentially alter the natural dynamics of human hand-object interaction, and are often cumbersome, thereby hindering their scalability for large-scale data collection in diverse, everyday manipulation scenarios.

This research addresses these limitations by proposing surface electromyography (sEMG) as an alternative, less intrusive sensing modality. sEMG measures the electrical activity of muscles, providing an indirect proxy for the muscle activations that generate finger forces. While sEMG has been previously explored for applications like gesture recognition and grasp detection, its reliable application to fine-grained fingertip force estimation in robot learning contexts presents challenges due to the inherent noise in sEMG signals and their variability across individual users and different electrode placements.

ForceBand positions itself within this landscape by developing a system that can accurately translate sEMG signals into per-finger force traces. By integrating this force information with visual data from human demonstrations, the work seeks to provide a scalable and unobtrusive method for enriching robot training data with the critical force signals necessary for forceful manipulation tasks. This approach aims to advance robot capabilities by offering a method that balances accuracy, usability, and scalability, distinguishing itself from purely vision-based and directly instrumented tactile methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the ForceBand research is to enable robots to learn dexterous and forceful manipulation policies directly from human demonstrations. This overarching goal is motivated by the understanding that current human demonstration datasets, while rich in motion and appearance data, critically lack the contact force information essential for robust and sensitive robotic interactions.

The specific objectives of this work include:

1. **Develop a low-cost and reproducible wearable force-sensing system:** The initial objective was to design and construct a wrist-worn sEMG system that is both economically viable and easily replicable. This system was required to effectively capture muscle activity pertinent to fine-grained finger control without requiring direct instrumentation of the human fingertips during the actual task demonstrations. This aimed to provide an unobtrusive alternative to traditional tactile sensors, thereby facilitating more natural human performance.
2. **Collect a comprehensive multimodal dataset for force estimation:** To establish a reliable mapping from sEMG signals to fingertip forces, a significant objective was to curate a synchronized multimodal dataset. This dataset would incorporate egocentric video, sEMG, IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) data, and ground-truth per-finger fingertip force measurements across a diverse spectrum of actions, objects, and gesture types. This extensive data collection was critical for the robust training and validation of a force prediction model.
3. **Train an accurate and adaptable EMG2Force prediction model:** A core objective was to develop a machine learning model, designated EMG2Force, capable of predicting per-finger force traces from the collected sEMG and IMU signals. This model needed to demonstrate robustness against the intrinsic noise in sEMG signals and be adaptable to individual user physiological variations, necessitating a user-specific calibration procedure. The aspiration was for this model to yield force estimations that outperform existing vision-based methods.
4. **Integrate force-enriched demonstrations into robot policy learning:** The final objective was to establish a comprehensive pipeline capable of leveraging these force-augmented human demonstrations to train force-aware robot manipulation policies. This involved retargeting human hand motions and the estimated force profiles to a robot embodiment, followed by the development of a visuomotor policy designed to predict both action (motion) and force trajectories for downstream forceful manipulation tasks.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives is rooted in several recognized limitations within contemporary robot learning paradigms:

- **Deficiency in existing human demonstration data:** Current human demonstration datasets, while scalable, primarily capture kinematic data, thereby omitting crucial contact force information. This restricts robots to learning mere motions rather than the nuanced forces required for tasks involving deformable objects or those demanding precise grip force.
- **Limitations of current force acquisition methods:** Vision-based force estimation methods are inherently ambiguous, particularly in the presence of occlusions, which renders them unreliable for accurate force inference. Conversely, direct tactile sensing gloves, despite their accuracy, are intrusive, can alter natural human interactions, and are impractical for large-scale, everyday data collection, thus impeding the generation of diverse datasets.
- **Potential of sEMG as an unobtrusive proxy:** sEMG signals provide a non-invasive window into muscle activations, which directly correlate with the forces generated. This modality offers a promising pathway to acquire force-relevant information without directly instrumenting the fingertips during the actual task demonstrations, thereby preserving the naturalness of human actions. However, overcoming challenges such as signal noise, cross-user variability, and electrode placement sensitivity was a primary motivator for the technical design considerations within ForceBand.

By addressing these identified challenges, ForceBand aims to facilitate new opportunities for robots to learn and execute complex, force-sensitive manipulation behaviors directly from human examples.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ForceBand system is structured to transform human muscle activity into force-enriched demonstrations for the purpose of learning robot manipulation policies. The methodology integrates hardware design, multimodal data collection, a force prediction model, and a policy learning pipeline.

#### 3.1. Wearable Force Sensing Design

The foundational hardware component is a wrist-worn sEMG system that includes an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU).

- **Accessibility:** The design emphasizes the use of commodity components and standard prototyping techniques, with the total cost estimated to be as low as $300. The mechanical structure is designed for 3D printing, and the electronic components are modular.
- **Performance:**
 - **Low-noise biopotential acquisition:** The system incorporates an OpenBCI Cyton board, which is based on the ADS-1299 chip, known for its low-noise sEMG signal acquisition capabilities (0.14μV rms and a Signal-to-Noise Ratio of 119.5).
 - **Bipolar differential sensing:** At each electrode site, a differential pair measures local potential differences. This configuration assists in suppressing common-mode noise and motion artifacts, thereby maintaining signal fidelity during dynamic movements.
 - **Muscle-aware electrode placement:** Electrodes are positioned anatomically over forearm muscles deemed particularly informative for controlling finger movements (specifically thumb, index, and middle finger activations), rather than being uniformly distributed. This non-uniform placement strategy aims to maximize the information content per channel and reduce the overall number of sensors required for accurate fine-grained control. The system utilizes eight sEMG channels: seven are dedicated to controlling finger movements (extensor pollicis brevis, extensor digitorum, extensor pollicis longus, extensor indicis, flexor digitorum profundus, flexor digitorum superficialis, flexor pollicis longus), and one is placed for wrist flexion (flexor carpi radialis). A common ground reference electrode is positioned over the ulnar styloid process.

#### 3.2. Texture-Preserving Ground-Truth Fingertip Force Sensor

To provide ground-truth force data for training and calibrating the EMG2Force model, thin-film flexible force-sensitive resistors are employed. These sensors are integrated inside transparent gel finger cots. This design choice enables the camera to observe the natural appearance of the hand, in contrast to the visual obstruction caused by opaque gloves. The sensor wires are routed along the palm to minimize visual occlusion. These fingertip sensors are exclusively used during the initial dataset collection and the user-specific calibration phase, and are subsequently removed for target-task demonstrations.

#### 3.3. EMG2Force: Dataset and Force Prediction Model

- **Multimodal Force-Sensing Dataset:** A 10-hour dataset was compiled, featuring synchronized egocentric video, sEMG, IMU, and per-finger fingertip force measurements. This dataset encompasses a variety of manipulation scenarios, including four action categories (pinch/grasp, pick-and-place, open/close, pour) and four gesture types (2-finger, 3-finger, 5-finger atomic actions, and in-the-wild interactions). The dataset includes objects with diverse shapes, sizes, and weights to support robust force prediction beyond isolated or canonical grasps.
- **Fingertip Force Prediction Model (EMG2Force):** This model is developed to predict per-finger force traces.
 - **Inputs:** sEMG time series (8 channels) and IMU time series (10 dimensions, comprising 3 acceleration, 3 gyroscope, and 4 orientation components). These inputs are processed over a 5-second temporal window, sampled at 250 Hz.
 - **Feature Extraction:** The sEMG and IMU signals are concatenated along the channel dimension. A Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is then applied to this combined signal to generate a time-frequency spectrogram representation. This approach is intended to capture frequency-domain features of muscle activity.
 - **Architecture:** The model incorporates a dual-branch encoder. A one-dimensional convolutional encoder processes the raw time series input, while a pretrained DINOv3 encoder processes the spectrogram representation. The features extracted from both encoders are subsequently concatenated and fed into a transformer decoder.
 - **Output:** The decoder outputs predicted per-finger force traces for all five fingers over the corresponding 5-second temporal window.
 - **Calibration:** Following initial training on the comprehensive multimodal dataset, a brief user-specific calibration (approximately 15 minutes) is conducted using the ground-truth fingertip force sensors. This step adapts the model to the individual user’s specific muscle activation patterns and sensor placement.

#### 3.4. Force-Aware Policy Learning from Human Demonstrations

The force-enriched human demonstrations are then converted into robot-executable policies through a dedicated pipeline.

- **From Video to Embodied Observations:**
 - Per-frame 3D human hand keypoints (obtained from Aria MPS) are retargeted to an SE(3) end-effector pose and a 1-Degree-of-Freedom (DoF) gripper aperture, compatible with a parallel-jaw robot.
 - Objects are tracked using a combination of vision models: Grounding DINO for detection, SAM2 for segmentation, CoTracker for tracking across frames, and Orient-Anything for 6-DoF pose estimation.
 - Visual observations undergo transformation: the human arm is segmented and inpainted (using SAM2 and LaMa, respectively), and a virtual gripper along with tracked object keypoints are overlaid onto the inpainted image. This process generates an embodiment-agnostic RGB observation that preserves scene layout while abstracting human appearance.
 - Scene encoding is achieved using a set of per-entity tokens, one for each hand and each tracked object. Each token concatenates an entity-type indicator, the entity’s 6-DoF pose in a shared scene frame, the hand pose expressed in the entity’s local frame, and a per-entity scalar (grasp state for hands, a sentinel for objects). A 1-D grip force, calculated as the average of the thumb and index forces, is attached to the hand token and time-aligned with the visual frame.
- **Flow Matching with Force-Augmented Action:**
 - The policy employs a flow matching transformer, which takes the visual frame and a set of spatial-relation tokens as input.
 - This transformer predicts an action chunk over a K=50-step horizon. The action representation is augmented to include force: `a_t = [p; r_6D; g; f]`, where `p` denotes the 3D end-effector position, `r_6D` is a continuous 6D rotation representation, `g` is the grasp aperture, and `f` represents the desired grip force.
 - The transformer decoder is trained using conditional flow matching. This method involves regressing a velocity field that transports a Gaussian prior to the target action, with specific per-component weights assigned for pose, grasp, and force.
 - By incorporating force as both an input (via the hand token) and a prediction target, the network is designed to learn a closed-loop relationship between the current contact state and subsequent force commands.

#### 3.5. Three-Step Deployment and Robot Control

The practical deployment of the ForceBand system follows a three-stage procedure:

1. **Calibration:** A user performs a short, approximately 15-minute calibration session. During this, the user wears the ForceBand and collects paired sEMG, IMU, and ground-truth fingertip force data. This process adapts the EMG2Force model to the individual user’s unique muscle activation patterns and sensor placement.
2. **Data Collection:** After successful calibration, the fingertip force sensors are no longer required. The user then collects target-task demonstrations using only the ForceBand and video. The calibrated EMG2Force model subsequently labels these raw signals with per-finger force traces, producing force-augmented human demonstrations. These demonstrations are then retargeted to the robot embodiment for subsequent policy training.
3. **Robot Deployment:** During robot deployment, the learned policy predicts both motion and force trajectories for a UR-5 robot equipped with a Robotiq parallel-jaw gripper. To provide accurate force feedback, four Paxini force sensors are attached to the gripper fingertips. A pre-grasp adjustment phase is implemented to ensure stable initial contact: when the policy predicts a close command, execution is temporarily paused, and the gripper regulates to a fixed 5 N pre-grasp force. Once stable contact is established, the policy resumes execution, and a PD force controller tracks the predicted force trajectory during the subsequent squeeze and place stages.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The ForceBand system was evaluated across its hardware design, force estimation accuracy, and its utility in force-aware robot policy learning.

#### 4.1. Ablation on Electrode Placement

Experiments were conducted to assess the influence of electrode placement strategy on the accuracy of force prediction.

- **Comparison:** The muscle-aware 8-channel placement employed by ForceBand was compared against configurations with fewer sEMG channels (1, 2, and 4 channels) and an alternative evenly distributed 8-channel wristband layout.
- **Results:** The results indicated a consistent reduction in force prediction error with an increasing number of sEMG channels. Under a standardized 30-minute data collection protocol, the muscle-aware 8-channel placement achieved a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.77 N and a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 1.33 N for force prediction. This performance represented an 18% reduction in error when compared to an evenly spaced 8-channel layout, which yielded an MAE of 0.94 N and an RMSE of 1.77 N under the same protocol.
- **Conclusion:** These findings suggest that anatomically guided electrode placement significantly enhances the informational content of sEMG signals for force prediction, relative to a uniform wrist coverage.

#### 4.2. Contact and Force Estimation

The performance of the EMG2Force model in predicting per-finger contact and force was evaluated against several vision-based baseline methods.

- **Baselines:** The comparison included two vision-based estimators: VISOR-HOS (evaluated in both its raw configuration and a version fine-tuned on the ForceBand dataset) and FEEL (a 2-layer MLP trained on frozen DINOv3 features for binary contact detection and force regression).
- **Results:**
 - **Hand-level metrics:** ForceBand consistently surpassed all vision baselines in both hand-level binary contact detection (BCD) and hand-level force regression, approximately halving the error metrics compared to the best performing vision baseline.
 - **Finger-level metrics:** The performance advantage of ForceBand was more pronounced at the individual finger level, particularly for fingers prone to visual occlusion, such as the ring and pinky fingers. For these digits, ForceBand’s Precision-Recall Area Under Curve (PR AUC) values were over two times higher than those achieved by FEEL (e.g., 0.763 vs. 0.398 for the ring finger, and 0.590 vs. 0.314 for the pinky finger), and more than six times higher than a prevalence-matched random baseline.
- **Conclusion:** This demonstrates that while vision-based methods can provide some contact cues, they struggle to infer precise force magnitudes, especially when occlusions are present. The wrist-worn sEMG system, by directly capturing muscle activity, enables more reliable per-finger force estimation suitable for subsequent robot policy learning.

#### 4.3. Force-aware Policy Learning

The efficacy of utilizing ForceBand-derived force supervision for training robot policies was evaluated on a physical UR-5 robot operating in a real-world tabletop manipulation environment.

- **Task:** The robot was required to perform a pick-squeeze-place task, which necessitated object-specific and time-varying force control. Success criteria were defined at two levels: pick and place success (stable grasping, transport, and placement) and squeeze success (applying a purposeful, object-specific squeeze force distinct from picking/placing forces).
- **Object Set:** The evaluation utilized nine everyday objects of diverse shapes, sizes, weights, and compliance (ranging from 43g to 650g in weight, and 1mm to 72mm in grasp width), including both in-distribution and out-of-distribution (OOD) objects.
- **Baselines:** Two gripper-control baselines were employed for comparison: a Binary Gripper (which predicted discrete open or close commands) and a Continuous Gripper (which predicted a continuous gripper aperture). Both baselines used the same visual observations and retargeted motion representations as ForceBand.
- **Results:**
 - **ForceBand Performance:** ForceBand achieved an 87% overall success rate on the pick, squeeze, and place tasks. It successfully executed both pick-and-place and squeeze actions for the majority of tested objects (e.g., 10/10 pick-place and 10/10 squeeze for mustard, 10/10 pick-place and 9/9 squeeze for toothpaste). The policy generated distinct, object-specific peak forces, spanning a range from 3.2 N to 19.3 N, adapted to the specific object and task stage.
 - **Baseline Performance:** The Binary Gripper baseline could often achieve pick-and-place success but consistently failed to produce the required squeeze behavior. The Continuous Gripper baseline demonstrated inconsistent performance; it occasionally succeeded in squeezing soft objects where deformation correlates with aperture changes, but it failed on rigid objects and generally proved unreliable. This limitation was partly attributed to noisy gripper position estimates derived from human video.
 - **Generalization:** The learned ForceBand policy demonstrated the ability to produce meaningful force trajectories for Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) objects not encountered during policy training, suggesting a transferable interaction representation. Further generalization tests (Appendix H) indicated robustness in the overall task structure under visual distribution shifts (novel backgrounds, novel objects, extreme lighting, and visual distractors), although precise force magnitudes could be influenced by changes in visual texture.
- **Conclusion:** These results demonstrate that wrist sEMG provides effective force supervision for robot learning, enabling forceful manipulation behaviors that exceed the capabilities of motion-only or gripper-position-based policies. The direct prediction of force from sEMG facilitates more reliable object-specific force control.

#### 4.4. EMG2Force Model Ablation (Appendix D)

An ablation study on the EMG2Force model architecture was conducted to confirm the contributions of its multimodal components.

- **Results:** The full model, which combines time-domain sEMG, spectrogram features, and IMU signals, achieved the lowest MAE of 0.92 N. Removing the spectrogram branch from the model increased the error to 1.14 N, while removing the IMU input increased the error to 1.02 N.
- **Conclusion:** This supports the design choice of employing a multimodal time-frequency representation, indicating that both frequency-domain patterns of muscle activity and motion cues from the IMU provide complementary and valuable information for accurate fingertip force estimation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ForceBand research introduces a novel methodology for enriching human demonstrations with critical contact force information, addressing a long-standing challenge in robot learning from demonstrations. This work carries several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enabling Force-Aware Robot Manipulation:** By providing a scalable and unobtrusive means to capture per-finger contact forces, ForceBand allows robots to learn and execute tasks that necessitate precise, object-specific force control. This expands robot capabilities beyond purely kinematic or visual imitation, facilitating more dexterous and sensitive interactions with the environment, such such as handling fragile items, performing assembly operations requiring specific torque, or interacting with deformable objects.
- **Bridging the Human-to-Robot Dexterity Gap:** Humans naturally employ intricate force profiles during manipulation, a nuance that robots have historically struggled to replicate when learning solely from visual or kinematic data. ForceBand's approach enables the transfer of this nuanced human force control to robots, thereby contributing to the reduction of the dexterity gap between human and robotic manipulation.
- **Scalable Data Collection for Robot Learning:** The low-cost and wrist-worn design of the ForceBand system, combined with its ability to augment video demonstrations with force data after an initial calibration, offers a more scalable solution for collecting diverse and force-enriched human demonstration datasets. This addresses the limitations of more intrusive tactile gloves, which are impractical for large-scale data acquisition in everyday environments.
- **Accessible Research and Development:** The open-source provision of the bill of materials and the utilization of commodity components in the ForceBand hardware design render the system accessible and reproducible for researchers and developers. This accessibility has the potential to accelerate further research in force-aware robot learning, human-robot interaction, and prosthetic control.
- **Advancement in sEMG-based Sensing:** The research contributes to the field of sEMG interpretation by demonstrating that anatomically guided electrode placement, when integrated with a multimodal time-frequency model, can significantly enhance the accuracy of fine-grained fingertip force estimation, even for fingers subject to visual occlusion. This extends beyond traditional sEMG applications primarily focused on binary gesture recognition.
- **Potential for Real-World Applications:** The learned capability for forceful manipulation could have broad implications across various real-world applications, including assistive robotics (e.g., aid in household tasks requiring delicate handling), industrial automation (e.g., precise assembly of components), and rehabilitation robotics (e.g., inferring human intent for prosthetic control).

**Limitations and Future Directions:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations, which also serve to highlight avenues for future research:

- **Absolute Force Accuracy:** While ForceBand significantly improves upon vision-based methods, its absolute force prediction accuracy does not yet match that of direct force sensing. Future investigations could explore approaches such as integrating limited robot interaction data or employing reinforcement learning with the robot’s own force feedback to further refine accuracy.
- **Cross-User Generalization and Calibration:** The current system necessitates a user-specific calibration due to inherent variations in muscle signals across individuals. Scaling data collection to larger, more diverse user populations might reduce or potentially eliminate the need for per-user calibration, as suggested by prior work in sEMG.
- **Calibration Protocol:** The initial calibration phase currently relies on ground-truth fingertip force sensors. Developing a sensor-free calibration protocol, possibly based on standardized grasp interactions with calibrated objects or fixtures, could enhance convenience.
- **Visual Sensitivity:** Although the policy demonstrated robustness in task structure when tested on OOD objects, the precise magnitudes of applied forces showed some sensitivity to visual background and texture changes. This indicates that future work focused on making force prediction more robust to visual domain shifts is warranted.

In conclusion, ForceBand presents a contribution towards enabling robots to comprehend and replicate the crucial force component of human manipulation, employing an innovative and accessible sEMG-based sensing approach. This work provides a foundation for developing more capable and adaptable robotic systems.
