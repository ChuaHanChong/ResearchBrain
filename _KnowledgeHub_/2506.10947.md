# Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR

> [!summary] Summary
> This study demonstrates that Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) can significantly improve mathematical reasoning in Qwen models even when using "spurious" reward signals, challenging the necessity of high-quality, task-specific rewards. It further reveals that these gains are highly dependent on the base model's pretraining and do not generalize to other LLM architectures.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for Large Language Models typically relies on high-quality, task-specific reward signals, which are costly to obtain.
- The precise mechanisms by which RLVR enhances LLM capabilities are not fully understood, specifically whether it teaches new skills or primarily surfaces existing ones.
- Conclusions about RLVR's effectiveness and generalizability might be skewed if research relies heavily on models with unique pretraining characteristics, leading to potentially misleading results.

## Method

- Conducted systematic experiments using ==GRPO== with a range of binary reward functions, from high-quality ground truth to increasingly ==spurious signals== like format-based, random, and deliberately incorrect rewards.
- Evaluated the impact of these reward functions across diverse LLM architectures, including Qwen2.5-Math, general-purpose Qwen2.5, Llama3, and OLMo2 variants, on standard mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
- Analyzed changes in model reasoning strategies, particularly ==code reasoning frequency==, and performed intervention experiments to understand their causal impact; also investigated algorithmic mechanisms like ==GRPO's clipping bias==.

## Results

- Qwen2.5-Math models achieved substantial performance gains on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., up to **+24.1%** absolute accuracy on MATH-500) using various spurious reward signals, comparable to gains from ground truth rewards.
- These gains were highly model-dependent; Llama3 and OLMo2 variants showed minimal improvement or even performance degradation when trained with spurious rewards, highlighting a lack of generalization.
- For Qwen2.5-Math, spurious rewards amplified its pre-existing code reasoning strategy, increasing its frequency from **65%** to over **90%**, which accounted for a majority of performance improvements.
- The clipping mechanism within the GRPO algorithm was identified as a key driver for performance improvements from random rewards, creating a non-zero gradient bias that amplified existing high-probability behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RLVR, particularly with weak signals, primarily acts as an elicitation mechanism for pre-existing latent capabilities within the base model, rather than teaching new reasoning skills.
> - The success of RLVR, even with spurious rewards, is highly dependent on the model's pretraining distribution and inherent reasoning priors, such as a robust code reasoning ability in Qwen models.
> - Algorithmic artifacts, specifically the clipping mechanism in GRPO, can inadvertently provide a training signal that amplifies existing high-probability behaviors, explaining performance gains with seemingly random rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has been identified as an effective method for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Prior research (e.g., Lambert et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025) has demonstrated its utility, particularly in domains such as mathematical problem-solving. However, the precise mechanisms through which RLVR achieves these improvements have not been thoroughly elucidated.

Existing literature has explored reducing reliance on high-quality supervision signals in RL, including unsupervised methods like Self-Consistency Preference Optimization (ScPO) (Prasad et al., 2024) and Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) (Zuo et al., 2025), which use model consistency or majority voting to generate pseudo-rewards. These approaches suggest that internal model consistency might serve as a proxy for correctness. Concurrently, studies (Wang et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025; AI et al., 2025; Choshen et al., 2020) have begun to suggest that RL, particularly at the scales used in open-source post-training pipelines, may primarily elicit or amplify latent capabilities already present in the base model rather than teach entirely new reasoning skills.

This work contributes to this emerging perspective by systematically investigating the performance of RLVR under reward signals that provide minimal, no, or even incorrect task-relevant information. By demonstrating significant performance gains in certain models even with "spurious rewards," the paper challenges the assumption that RLVR necessarily relies on informative supervision to improve reasoning. It also introduces an examination of model-dependent outcomes, suggesting that pretraining priors play a crucial role in determining RLVR efficacy, thereby fitting into the broader discussion on the interpretability and generalizability of RL-based LLM fine-tuning methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to critically re-evaluate the role and effectiveness of reward signals in RLVR for improving language model reasoning. The motivation stems from a counterintuitive observation: RLVR appears to induce substantial performance gains in certain language models (specifically Qwen2.5-Math models) even when the reward signals are "spurious," meaning they have little, no, or outright negative correlation with the correct answer. This phenomenon suggests that the underlying mechanisms of RLVR might be more complex and model-dependent than commonly assumed.

More specifically, the key objectives are:
1. **Quantify the impact of spurious rewards:** To systematically measure the performance improvements achieved by RLVR using various weak and spurious reward functions (e.g., majority vote, format, random, and incorrect rewards) on mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
2. **Investigate model dependency:** To determine whether the effectiveness of spurious rewards is consistent across different language model families (e.g., Qwen, Llama3, OLMo2), or if it is highly dependent on specific model architectures or pretraining distributions.
3. **Unpack the mechanisms of "spurious" learning:** To analyze the GRPO training objective, particularly the clipping term, to hypothesize how non-informative rewards might still induce meaningful training signals.
4. **Identify and analyze amplified behaviors:** To identify specific pre-existing reasoning patterns within models that are preferentially amplified by RLVR, even under spurious rewards, and to investigate their correlation with performance improvements. The concept of "code reasoning" in Qwen2.5-Math models serves as a case study.
5. **Evaluate implications for RLVR research:** To highlight the practical implications of these findings, particularly concerning the validation and robustness of RLVR methods, and to propose recommendations for future research practices.

Ultimately, the research aims to foster a more nuanced understanding of how RLVR operates, moving beyond a simplistic view of reward-driven learning, and emphasizing the interaction between RL algorithms and the inherent capabilities or biases of pretrained language models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a systematic experimental approach, primarily utilizing the Group-wise Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm for fine-tuning language models on mathematical reasoning tasks.

#### 3.1. RLVR Framework and Base Models

The research adopted GRPO (Guo et al., 2025) as the reinforcement learning algorithm. The primary model investigated was Qwen2.5-Math-7B, a 7-billion parameter LLM specialized in mathematics, due to its prominence in RLVR literature and computational efficiency. The study also extended its analysis to a diverse set of models, including general-purpose Qwen2.5 (1.5B and 7B parameters), Llama3.1 (8B) and Llama3.2 (3B) (both base and instruction-tuned variants), and OLMo2 (7B base and SFT variants) (OLMo et al., 2024).

#### 3.2. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics

Experiments were conducted on three mathematical reasoning benchmarks:
- **MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021):** A subset of the MATH dataset for advanced mathematical reasoning. Performance was measured by pass@1 accuracy.
- **AMC (Li et al., 2024):** The American Mathematics Competition dataset. Performance was measured by average@8 accuracy.
- **AIME (Li et al., 2024):** The American Invitational Mathematics Examination dataset, featuring challenging multi-step reasoning problems. Both AIME24 and AIME25 (released after model knowledge cutoffs) were used, with average@8 accuracy reported.

#### 3.3. Reward Functions

A range of binary (0-1) reward functions were designed, replacing the standard ground-truth reward, to progressively weaken the task-relevant signal:
- **Ground Truth Rewards:** Standard RLVR, rewarding verifiably correct answers, serving as a baseline.
- **Majority Vote Rewards:** Rewards based on the majority answer from 64 sampled responses by the pre-RL model, serving as pseudo-labels.
- **Format Rewards:** Rewards given solely for including a non-empty `\boxed{}` expression in the response, incentivizing prompt following rather than correctness.
- **Random Rewards:** Rewards assigned randomly with a fixed probability (e.g., γ = 0.5), providing no task-relevant information.
- **(Majority-voted) Incorrect Rewards:** Rewards assigned only to responses matching specific, incorrect answers determined by majority voting, actively providing misleading supervision.

#### 3.4. Training and Decoding Configuration

Models were trained on 8 GPUs with a constant learning rate of 5e-7, a mini-batch size of 128, and a rollout batch size of 64. Sixteen rollouts per prompt were used for advantage computation. A sampling temperature of τ=1 was used during training. The KL regularization term in GRPO was set to λ=0 to isolate the effects of reward signals. Evaluation decoding used a temperature of 0.0 for pass@1 and 0.6 for pass@k.

#### 3.5. Analysis of GRPO Clipping Bias

To understand the efficacy of random rewards, the study analyzed the GRPO objective, focusing on the clipping term. The clipping function was hypothesized to introduce a bias, amplifying tokens with high prior probabilities. This hypothesis was investigated through:
- **Theoretical derivation:** Mathematical analysis of the expected gradient of the GRPO objective under random rewards, showing how the clipping term can introduce a non-zero, directional bias.
- **Ablation studies:** Experiments where the clipping term was removed from GRPO (via implementation alteration, matching mini-batch to rollout size, or reducing rollout size) to observe its impact on performance with random rewards.
- **Empirical validation:** Tracking average token probability and code reasoning frequency with and without clipping.

#### 3.6. Case Study: Code Reasoning

The study identified "code reasoning" (generation of Python code in reasoning steps, without execution) as a salient pre-existing behavior in Qwen2.5-Math models. The approach involved:
- **Qualitative and quantitative analysis:** Examining reasoning traces for code presence and its correlation with correctness in different models before RLVR.
- **Tracking behavior shifts:** Monitoring the frequency of code reasoning and overall accuracy during RLVR training with various reward functions.
- **Intervention experiments:**
 - **Prompting:** Explicitly inducing code reasoning by modifying prompts.
 - **RL-based elicitation:** Designing a "Python reward" that positively rewards responses containing the string "python" to explicitly encourage code reasoning.
- **Compound rewards:** Creating rewards that penalize code reasoning to assess its contribution to performance gains.

#### 3.7. Prompt Sensitivity Analysis

An additional analysis explored the sensitivity of Qwen2.5-Math-7B to different prompt templates, including commonly used prompts from other works and a "spurious prompt" (random LaTeX placeholder text), to gauge their impact on initial performance and RLVR training trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness of spurious rewards and the underlying mechanisms of RLVR.

#### 4.1. Spurious Rewards Yield Significant RLVR Gains on Qwen2.5-Math Models

- **Unexpected Performance Boost:** Qwen2.5-Math models demonstrated substantial performance improvements even with weak and spurious reward signals. For Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500, random rewards led to a 21.4% absolute accuracy gain, closely approaching the 29.1% gain achieved with ground truth rewards. Incorrect label rewards yielded a 24.1% gain.
- **Consistency Across Benchmarks:** Similar trends were observed on AMC and AIME24, where spurious rewards resulted in gains comparable to, or within a few percentage points of, ground truth rewards. AIME25, featuring questions post-dating model knowledge cutoffs, showed reduced but still positive gains from most non-ground-truth rewards, except for some cases of slight degradation or minimal change.
- **Robustness to Randomness:** Random rewards with varying probabilities (γ ∈ {0.001, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7}) consistently produced significant performance improvements, with γ=0 being the only exception, where no learning occurred.

#### 4.2. Lack of Generalization to Other Model Families

- **Model-Dependent Effects:** The effectiveness of spurious rewards was largely confined to Qwen models. For other model families, including general-purpose Qwen2.5 (non-Math), Llama3.1/3.2 (base and instruct), and OLMo2 (base and SFT), spurious rewards yielded minimal improvement or, in some cases, led to performance degradation.
- **Family-Level Consistency:** Models within the same family (e.g., Qwen2.5 variants) tended to behave similarly, suggesting shared pretraining distributions influence RL outcomes. Smaller models generally benefited less from spurious rewards.
- **Implications for Existing Research:** This finding suggests that many RLVR methods evaluated primarily on Qwen models may not generalize to other architectures, as demonstrated by re-evaluations of TTRL and One-Shot RL.

#### 4.3. Understanding Training Signals from Random Rewards in GRPO

- **Role of Clipping Bias:** Analysis of the GRPO objective revealed that the clipping term introduces a bias that can induce meaningful training signals even from random, uninformative rewards. When clipping was removed, random rewards failed to yield consistent performance improvements.
- **Asymmetric Gradient Updates:** The clipping mechanism was shown to asymmetrically reinforce high-probability tokens while suppressing low-probability ones. For high-probability tokens (e.g., π_old(y_t) = 0.85), the upper clipping threshold (e.g., 1.02) is rarely reached, leading to a consistent positive gradient bias. For low-probability tokens (e.g., π_old(y_t) = 0.02), small increases can easily trigger negative gradient bias (e.g., above 0.024), effectively concentrating probability mass on existing high-probability behaviors.
- **Exploitation of Priors:** This clipping bias causes the model to exploit and amplify its pre-existing high-probability behaviors, regardless of the reward's actual informativeness.

#### 4.4. Case Study: Code Reasoning as a Representative Pre-Existing Behavior

- **Code Reasoning in Qwen2.5-Math:** Qwen2.5-Math-7B exhibited a high frequency of code reasoning (65.0% of responses) prior to RLVR, despite lacking code execution. This behavior was strongly correlated with correctness, with code-based responses achieving 60.9% accuracy compared to 35.0% for natural language responses. Other model families either did not generate code or generated "bad code" that correlated with lower accuracy.
- **RLVR Amplifies Code Reasoning:** Under weak and spurious rewards, RLVR training on Qwen2.5-Math-7B rapidly increased code reasoning frequency (to over 90%), and this increase closely tracked the observed accuracy gains. With ground truth rewards, code frequency also increased initially but later declined as natural language reasoning improved, suggesting a different learning mechanism.
- **Intervening on Code Reasoning:**
 - **Prompting:** Explicitly prompting Qwen2.5-Math models to use code reasoning (e.g., starting with "LET’S SOLVE THIS USING PYTHON.") significantly improved their MATH-500 accuracy (e.g., +15.0% for Qwen2.5-Math-7B), while degrading performance for models that lacked effective code reasoning.
 - **"Python Reward":** An RLVR reward that exclusively rewarded responses containing "python" rapidly increased code usage to over 99% in Qwen2.5-Math-7B within 20 steps, yielding performance gains.
 - **Inhibiting Code Reasoning:** Compound rewards that penalized code reasoning reduced or eliminated gains from spurious rewards in Qwen2.5-Math, confirming its role. Conversely, for models where code reasoning was suboptimal (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B, OLMo2-7B-SFT), inhibiting code reasoning sometimes led to performance improvements.
- **Reasoning Strategy Switches:** For Qwen2.5-Math models, a significant portion of the performance gain (58.3% for 7B, 78.7% for 1.5B) originated from problems where the model switched from natural language to code reasoning.

#### 4.5. Sensitivity to Prompts

- Qwen2.5-Math-7B exhibited high sensitivity to prompts. Different existing prompts led to varied initial performance levels, sometimes substantially. Notably, a "spurious prompt" (random LaTeX placeholder text) resulted in the highest initial MATH-500 accuracy (68.8%) among tested prompts, indicating that task-irrelevant contextual perturbations can unexpectedly influence performance.

#### 4.6. Behavior of RL-Post-Trained Models

- Qwen Instruct models (already RL-post-trained) showed minimal further improvement from RLVR, even with ground-truth rewards, suggesting they might have reached a saturation point. Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B, also RL-post-trained, exhibited some marginal gains with ground-truth rewards, aligning more with its base model's behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications for the understanding and practice of reinforcement learning in the context of large language models:

#### 5.1. Re-evaluation of Reward Signals in RLVR

The most prominent finding is that highly informative or "correct" reward signals may not be strictly necessary for RLVR to improve reasoning capabilities in certain models. The observed efficacy of spurious rewards challenges the conventional understanding of how RLVR operates, suggesting that the training process can be significantly influenced by factors beyond explicit, accurate feedback.

#### 5.2. Importance of Pretraining Priors and Model Architecture

The work highlights that the base model's pretraining distribution and architectural properties heavily influence RLVR outcomes. Performance gains with spurious rewards are model-dependent, primarily observed in models like Qwen2.5-Math which possess specific "amplifiable" behaviors (e.g., code reasoning) that correlate with correctness. This implies that RLVR might often act as an "elicitation" mechanism, surfacing and reinforcing latent capabilities, rather than a "teaching" mechanism for novel skills.

#### 5.3. Call for Rigorous Validation in RLVR Research

The findings necessitate increased rigor in evaluating RL methods. The study strongly recommends:
- **Diverse Model Testing:** Future RLVR research should validate methods across a diverse range of language model families, not relying solely on a single "de facto" choice (like Qwen models, which were shown to be susceptible to spurious gains).
- **Spurious Baselines:** Incorporating spurious rewards (e.g., format or random rewards) as dummy baselines can help differentiate genuine capability improvements from the amplification of pre-existing, potentially superficial, behaviors.
- **Contextual Sensitivity:** Awareness of prompt sensitivity, even to "spurious prompts," is crucial for accurate evaluation.

#### 5.4. Implications for Interpretability and Generalizability

The research contributes to a deeper understanding of LLM interpretability by identifying specific reasoning patterns (like code reasoning) that are amplified during RLVR. It questions the generalizability of RLVR performance gains, particularly if they stem from the reinforcement of model-specific priors rather than universal reasoning improvements.

#### 5.5. Practical Warning and Ethical Considerations

The authors explicitly state that spurious rewards are proposed for analytical purposes only and should not be adopted as a strategy for developing true model capabilities. Misuse could lead to models exhibiting unreliable or undesirable behaviors. The anticipated impact is to improve the robustness and scientific rigor of RL research for LLMs.

In summary, this paper shifts the perspective on RLVR, emphasizing the interplay between the optimization algorithm's mechanics (like clipping bias) and the inherent characteristics of pretrained models. It underscores the need for careful experimental design and broad validation to ensure that observed performance gains reflect genuine advancements in reasoning rather than the amplification of pre-existing, model-specific patterns.
