# VLS: Steering Pretrained Robot Policies via Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The VLS framework enables pretrained robot policies to robustly adapt to out-of-distribution observation-language inputs at inference time without requiring retraining. It leverages Vision-Language Models to synthesize trajectory-differentiable reward functions, improving success rates on OOD tasks by up to 9.6x compared to base policies in simulation and demonstrating real-world efficacy.

## Problem

- Pretrained robot policies exhibit brittleness and severe performance drops when faced with even minor out-of-distribution (OOD) environmental changes or task instructions.
- Retraining or fine-tuning policies for every new OOD scenario is computationally expensive, data-intensive, and inefficient.
- Existing imitation learning approaches tightly couple action generation with specific training configurations, hindering generalization and OOD adaptation.

## Method

- VLS employs Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to ground OOD inputs and programmatically generate differentiable reward functions for task-relevant spatial requirements.
- It steers the action denoising process of frozen generative robot policies by injecting VLM-generated reward gradients, enhanced with diverse proposal initialization and Feynman–Kac resampling.
- A closed-loop execution control system uses adaptive guidance strength and a Schmitt-Trigger-based stage switching mechanism for robust multi-stage task execution in dynamic environments.

## Results

- VLS, applied to a frozen π0.5 policy, achieved average success rates of 94% on movable object tasks (7.4x improvement) and 87% on articulated part tasks (9.6x improvement) on the CALVIN benchmark, significantly outperforming prior steering methods.
- Leading pretrained VLA models (e.g., OpenVLA, π0) showed 0% success rates on OOD conditions in LIBERO-PRO, highlighting the necessity of VLS's adaptation.
- In real-world experiments with a Franka Emika robot, VLS significantly improved success rates under challenging OOD conditions, such as appearance and position shifts, achieving a 40% success rate even when replacing known objects with novel ones, where baselines failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inference-time steering is critical for adapting pretrained robot policies to out-of-distribution observation and language shifts, as even advanced Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models fail without explicit adaptation.
> - Vision-Language Models can effectively synthesize differentiable programmatic reward functions, providing robust and flexible guidance signals for low-level robot control.
> - Robust inference-time adaptation benefits from combining gradient-free global exploration (e.g., repulsive forces, Feynman–Kac resampling) with gradient-based local refinement for efficient and stable action generation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has seen significant advancements, particularly with the advent of large-scale imitation learning and generative models like diffusion and flow-matching policies. These "pretrained policies" (e.g., RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, π0.5) have demonstrated impressive in-distribution performance and the ability to learn complex motor skills from vast datasets. However, a persistent and critical challenge remains: the brittleness of these policies when faced with "out-of-distribution" (OOD) scenarios. Even minor changes in the environment (e.g., object placement, scene clutter, support surface shifts) or task instructions can lead to catastrophic failures. This limitation stems from imitation learning's tendency to tightly couple action generation with the specific spatial configurations and task specifications encountered during training, leading to an "OOD dilemma" where policies overfit to the training manifold.

Traditional approaches to address these failures, such as retraining or fine-tuning, are costly, data-intensive, and conceptually misaligned. They attempt to re-learn behaviors that often already exist within the policy's repertoire but cannot be selectively adapted. The current work, VLS, positions itself within a growing trend of "inference-time adaptation" or "steering" methods that aim to modify policy behavior at test time without altering the underlying model parameters. This paradigm draws inspiration from similar successes in large language models and image generation models, where external guidance reshapes outputs without retraining.

VLS differentiates itself from existing steering techniques in several ways:
- **VLM-based Scene Understanding with Re-optimization:** While some methods use VLMs to generate scene representations for online planning or iterative refinement (e.g., Voxposer, Rekep), these often involve computationally heavy rollouts or optimization loops, making them unsuitable for real-time control. VLS aims for lightweight, differentiable steering.
- **Value/Critic-guided Steering:** Approaches like V-GPS or VGD inject gradients from learned value or Q-models. VLS argues this is undesirable as it reshapes the policy towards an auxiliary learned objective, rather than maintaining the base policy as an invariant and using test-time constraints for modulation.
- **Dynamics/World-model Guided Steering:** Methods like DynaGuide or Latent Policy Barrier use external dynamics models, which can introduce sensitivity to model errors and increase computational burden. VLS avoids this reliance on learned dynamics.
- **Human/VLM-in-the-loop Steering and Verification:** Methods such as ITPS, FOREWARN, or Do What You Say use sparse, discrete feedback (e.g., human interaction, VLM-based filtering) to select among candidate actions. VLS, in contrast, provides *continuous, differentiable steering* throughout the generation process, which is more sample-efficient for fine-grained constraint satisfaction. VLS is related to VLA-Pilot but distinguishes itself by focusing on OOD scenarios, gradient-guided denoising with dynamic stage transitions, and extensive evaluation.
- **Online Improvement without Finetuning:** Policy Decorator, USR, and DSRL offer online refinement or latent steering, but still involve some form of online learning or interaction. VLS focuses on *pure inference-time adaptation* with no additional training at deployment.

In summary, VLS represents a significant contribution to the evolving landscape of generalizable robot learning by offering a training-free, inference-time adaptation framework. It uniquely leverages the open-world understanding of Vision-Language Models to synthesize *trajectory-differentiable reward functions*, which then guide the sampling process of frozen generative robot policies, addressing a critical bottleneck in deploying robust robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable pretrained, frozen robot policies to adapt robustly and efficiently to **out-of-distribution (OOD) observation–language inputs** at inference time, without requiring any retraining or fine-tuning of the base policy parameters.

The primary motivations for this objective are:
- **Addressing Brittleness under Train–Test Shifts:** State-of-the-art robot policies, particularly those based on diffusion or flow-matching, excel in-distribution but frequently fail when the environment or instruction deviates even slightly from the training data. These failures are not due to a lack of motor skill but rather an inability to adapt existing skills to new contexts.
- **Cost and Inefficiency of Retraining/Fine-tuning:** Re-training or fine-tuning for every new OOD scenario is prohibitively expensive, data-intensive, and conceptually inefficient. The required behaviors often already reside within the policy's learned capabilities; the challenge is how to *control* their execution under novel constraints.
- **Decoupling Skill Execution from OOD Task Specification:** The authors argue for a clear separation: the base policy should provide a robust set of reusable motor primitives, while a separate mechanism should control how these primitives are instantiated and composed under OOD inputs. This allows for more flexible and generalizable robotic systems.
- **Leveraging Open-World Understanding of VLMs:** The increasing power of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to understand open-world concepts and interpret complex instructions presents an opportunity to bridge the gap between human intent and robot action, even in novel scenarios. VLS aims to harness this capability to generate useful guidance signals.
- **Enabling Practical Deployment:** Ultimately, the goal is to make robot policies more reliable and deployable in dynamic, unpredictable real-world environments where OOD conditions are inevitable.

By formulating adaptation as an inference-time control problem, VLS seeks to unlock the full generalization potential of pretrained robot policies, making them capable of operating successfully in diverse and unforeseen scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VLS proposes a **training-free framework** that steers the sampling process of frozen generative robot policies (diffusion or flow-matching models) at inference time. Its core idea is to leverage Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to dynamically generate differentiable reward functions that guide the policy's action generation towards satisfying test-time spatial and task requirements. The VLS pipeline is structured around three main components:

**A. OOD Input Grounding and Reward Generation**
This component translates the high-dimensional OOD observation–language input (o, l) into a usable, differentiable guidance signal.
1. **OOD Input Grounding:**
 - Given an OOD observation (o) and language instruction (l), VLS first uses a VLM to identify task-relevant objects and regions.
 - For identified objects, the Segment Anything Model (SAM) extracts object masks.
 - DINOv2 extracts dense visual features, which are then filtered by the object masks.
 - Masked pixels are reprojected into a 3D point cloud using depth information.
 - These object-centric point clouds are clustered to obtain a compact set of task-relevant **3D keypoints (P)**. This geometric scaffold explicitly represents the spatial variables for reward evaluation.
2. **Programmatic Reward Generation:**
 - A VLM is queried to decompose the task implied by (o, l) into a sequence of **S stages**.
 - For each stage *s*, the VLM synthesizes a **differentiable programmatic reward function R_s**. This function takes an action proposal (a_k_t:t+T) and the grounded keypoints (P) as input and returns a scalar score.
 - Crucially, these reward functions are designed to be implemented as **PyTorch functions** composed of differentiable tensor operations (e.g., distances, dot products, soft constraints), ensuring that their gradients can be backpropagated through the action proposal.
 - The gradient of this reward function, **g_s = ∇_a_k_t:t+T R_s**, serves as the guidance signal, approximating the log-likelihood gradient required for steering.

**B. Action Denoising Process Guidance**
This component injects the VLM-generated reward gradients into the policy's denoising process to steer action generation.
1. **Diverse Proposal Initialization with Repulsive Forces:**
 - At the start of each denoising process, a batch of B action proposals are sampled from a Gaussian distribution.
 - To prevent early collapse to a single mode and ensure diversity, a repulsive force (based on RBF kernel distances between proposals) is introduced during initial denoising steps. This encourages proposals to remain spatially separated.
2. **Gradient-Based Refinement:**
 - The stage-specific reward gradient **g_s** is injected into the policy's denoising updates (either by modifying noise prediction in diffusion or velocity field in flow-matching models).
 - This steers action proposals toward regions of higher reward.
 - Stochastic refinement with multiple inner updates per denoising step (analogous to MCMC-based guidance) is used to improve stability and smooth exploration.
3. **Gradient-Free Resampling via Feynman–Kac Steering:**
 - Periodically, proposals within the batch are re-sampled using a Feynman–Kac (FK) steering mechanism.
 - Each particle (action proposal) is assigned a potential based on its reward value (exp(R_s)).
 - Normalized weights are computed, and multinomial resampling is applied, allowing high-reward particles to replicate while pruning low-reward ones. This balances continuous gradient guidance with discrete, reward-weighted exploration.

**C. Closed-Loop Execution Control and Stage Switching**
To handle dynamic real-world uncertainties and multi-stage tasks, VLS incorporates feedback into its execution control.
1. **Adaptive Guidance Strength:**
 - Within a task stage, the guidance strength (λ) for each action chunk is adaptively adjusted.
 - λ is increased when the current action chunk's reward is low relative to the initial reward for that stage, providing stronger correction.
 - As execution improves and rewards increase, λ is reduced, allowing the frozen base policy to dominate for fine-grained manipulation.
2. **Schmitt-Trigger-Based Stage Switching:**
 - To ensure robust transitions between task stages and prevent oscillatory behavior, a hysteresis-based switching mechanism (like a Schmitt trigger) is employed.
 - Two reward thresholds (R_high and R_low) are defined for the current stage.
 - The system advances to the next stage if R_t exceeds R_high, reinforces the current stage if R_t falls below R_low, and maintains the current stage otherwise.
 - A VLM is then queried to interpret the execution outcome and select the appropriate next-stage reward function or update the current one, providing robust coordination for long-horizon tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors systematically evaluated VLS in both simulation (CALVIN, LIBERO-PRO) and real-world settings (Franka Emika robot) under various observation and language shifts. The experiments aimed to answer four key questions:

- **Q1. Is inference-time steering necessary to handle observation and language shifts at test time?**
 - **Finding:** Yes, it is. On the LIBERO-PRO benchmark, leading pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (OpenVLA, π0, π0.5, π0.5 LeRobot) exhibit a severe drop in success rates under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions (position and task perturbations), often failing entirely. For instance, OpenVLA and π0 achieve 0% success. VLS, when applied to a frozen π0.5 policy, significantly improves its performance, demonstrating that even advanced VLAs struggle with OOD generalization without explicit adaptation mechanisms. This highlights that post-training on robot data can sometimes degrade the VLM's generalization when the execution environment deviates from the training manifold.

- **Q2. Does VLS provide stronger adaptation than existing inference-time steering approaches?**
 - **Finding:** Yes, VLS consistently outperforms prior steering methods. On the CALVIN benchmark, VLS achieved an average success rate of **94% on movable object tasks** (7.4x improvement over the base policy) and **87% on articulated part tasks** (9.6x improvement). This performance significantly surpassed DynaGuide and ITPS. ITPS struggled with tasks involving variable object positions, while DynaGuide's DINO-feature-based heuristics lacked the expressiveness to capture task-specific spatial requirements. VLS's VLM-conditioned, differentiable guidance proved more effective in handling spatial variability and complex task constraints.

- **Q3. What is the contribution of each component in the VLS framework?**
 - **Finding:** All components are crucial for optimal performance, with gradient guidance being the most critical.
 - **Effect of gradient-based guidance:** Removing gradient guidance led to a severe performance collapse, confirming its role as the primary driver of VLS's effectiveness in providing dense, trajectory-differentiable steering.
 - **Role of FK resampling and RBF diversity:** While their removal had a smaller impact on overall success rate, it consistently degraded efficiency and stability. These components are important for sample efficiency, preventing premature collapse to suboptimal modes, and maintaining broad coverage of the action manifold during early denoising.
 - **Scaling with sample batch size:** Increasing the batch size improved success rates and reduced episode length, but at the cost of higher inference latency, demonstrating a practical compute-performance trade-off.
 - The ablation study concluded that robust inference-time adaptation requires a combination of gradient-free global exploration (from FK and RBF) and gradient-based local refinement (from reward gradients).

- **Q4. Can VLS adapt policies in the real world with minimal computational overhead?**
 - **Finding:** Yes, VLS enables efficient real-world deployment. Experiments on a Franka Emika robot showed that VLS significantly improved real-world task success rates over the frozen π0.5 baseline, both in-distribution and under challenging OOD conditions (appearance shifts, position shifts, and novel object substitutions). For instance, in the most challenging "object shift" scenario (replacing a banana with an unseen mug), VLS achieved a 40% success rate, whereas the baseline failed entirely. VLS maintained stable execution and substantially higher success rates across all OOD variants, demonstrating its practical applicability and effectiveness in real robotic systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VLS framework presents a highly significant advancement in the field of robot learning, with several potential impacts:

- **Unlocking Generalization of Pretrained Policies:** VLS directly addresses a critical bottleneck in deploying modern robot policies: their brittleness to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions. By providing a training-free, inference-time adaptation mechanism, VLS unlocks the latent generalization capabilities of powerful pretrained diffusion and flow-matching models without the need for costly and time-consuming retraining or fine-tuning. This dramatically lowers the barrier to deploying robots in diverse and dynamic real-world scenarios.
- **Towards Truly Generalist Robots:** The ability to adapt existing motor skills to novel spatial arrangements and task specifications through high-level vision-language understanding is a crucial step towards building more truly generalist robotic systems. Robots equipped with VLS can handle unexpected changes in their environment or task instructions, moving beyond rigid, in-distribution execution.
- **Efficient Resource Utilization:** By decoupling skill acquisition from OOD adaptation, VLS promotes a more efficient use of computational and data resources. Base policies can be trained once on large datasets to acquire fundamental motor skills, while VLS provides a flexible layer for task-specific and environment-specific adaptation at deployment.
- **Leveraging Vision-Language Models for Embodied AI:** VLS innovatively uses VLMs not just for perception or high-level planning, but to *synthesize differentiable reward functions* that directly guide low-level action generation. This represents a powerful integration of abstract language understanding with concrete motor control, bridging a critical gap in embodied AI.
- **Practical Applicability:** The robust performance demonstrated in real-world experiments on a Franka robot, under realistic OOD shifts, highlights the immediate practical utility of VLS. It offers a viable solution for increasing the robustness and reliability of robotic systems in industrial, service, and personal robotics applications where environments are rarely perfectly controlled.
- **New Research Avenues:** VLS opens up new research directions in areas such as more sophisticated VLM-driven reward generation, strategies for optimizing computational efficiency of steering methods, and further exploration of the interplay between gradient-based and gradient-free guidance mechanisms for complex, long-horizon tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge a key limitation: **computational latency**. The iterative nature of batch sampling, MCMC runs, and Feynman–Kac resampling can introduce significant inference overhead, potentially hindering real-time performance in highly time-sensitive applications.

Future work could focus on:
- **Optimizing Computational Efficiency:** Exploring techniques to reduce the latency of the steering process, perhaps through more efficient sampling strategies, hardware acceleration, or learning more compact guidance functions.
- **Progress-Aware Reward Signal Generation:** Developing more nuanced VLM-generated reward signals that can adapt dynamically based on the robot's ongoing progress, rather than static stage definitions.
- **Generalization to Broader OOD Scenarios:** Further testing and refinement to handle even more extreme OOD conditions, including novel object categories, complex physics interactions, and unexpected environmental dynamics.

Despite its current limitations regarding computational overhead, VLS represents a compelling and effective framework that pushes the boundaries of inference-time adaptation for robot policies, bringing us closer to truly intelligent and adaptable robotic agents.
