# KungfuBot: Physics-Based Humanoid Whole-Body Control for Learning Highly-Dynamic Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> KungfuBot enables humanoid robots to learn and execute highly-dynamic human skills like martial arts and dancing by integrating a physics-based motion processing pipeline with an adaptive motion tracking mechanism. This approach allows zero-shot transfer to real robots, demonstrating superior tracking performance with a global mean per body position error of 53.25mm on easy motions, and robustly executing complex maneuvers on a Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid control algorithms struggle to imitate "highly-dynamic" human actions due to physical infeasibility of raw motion capture data.
- Current methods lack robust adaptive control mechanisms, leading to instability or imprecision when learning complex motions with fixed reward functions.
- Successfully transferring learned policies from simulation to physical humanoid robots remains a significant challenge due to the sim-to-real gap.

## Method

- A multi-step ==physics-based motion processing pipeline== converts raw human videos into ==physically feasible robot reference motions==, filtering unstable sequences and correcting ==contact issues==.
- An ==adaptive motion tracking mechanism== dynamically adjusts the reward's ==tracking factor== (tolerance) during ==reinforcement learning==, allowing the policy to progressively refine precision.
- An ==asymmetric actor-critic RL framework== with ==reward vectorization==, ==reference state initialization==, and extensive ==domain randomization== is used for robust policy training and ==zero-shot sim-to-real transfer==.

## Results

- The physics-based motion filtering successfully rejected untrackable motions, with policies trained on accepted motions achieving high Episode Length Ratios, while rejected motions showed significantly lower ratios (max **54%**).
- **PBHC** outperformed deployable baselines (**OmniH2O**, **ExBody2**) in simulation, achieving a global mean per body position error of **53.25mm** on easy motions compared to baselines exceeding **233mm**.
- Policies trained with **PBHC** demonstrated stable and expressive execution of highly-dynamic martial arts and dance routines on a physical **Unitree G1** robot, with real-world tracking metrics closely matching simulation results, confirming robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pre-processing human motion data with physics-based filtering and contact correction is crucial for generating feasible and high-quality robot reference trajectories, significantly aiding subsequent reinforcement learning.
> - An adaptive tracking factor, dynamically updated based on the instantaneous tracking error, allows the reinforcement learning agent to learn highly dynamic and precise motions more effectively than fixed reward tolerances.
> - Combining a robust reinforcement learning framework with comprehensive domain randomization enables stable zero-shot transfer of complex, agile skills from simulation to real-world humanoid robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics aims to enable robots to acquire and perform diverse skills by imitating human behaviors. Recent advancements in motion capture (Mo-Cap) systems and motion generation methods have led to the creation of extensive datasets of human activities, making it increasingly feasible for humanoid robots to learn whole-body control policies. However, controlling high-dimensional robot actions to achieve human-like performance remains a significant challenge.

A primary difficulty lies in the physical infeasibility of human motion sequences for robots, as human motions may violate robot-specific physical constraints such as joint limits, dynamics, and kinematics. Directly applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to maximize tracking rewards often yields suboptimal policies because the target motions may not exist within the robot's physically achievable solution space.

Previous research has attempted to address these physical feasibility issues. Approaches such as H2O and OmniH2O involve removing infeasible motions using a trained privileged imitation policy. ExBody constructs feasible datasets by filtering via language labels, while Exbody2 trains an initial policy to measure motion difficulty and optimize the dataset. A common limitation of these methods is their capacity to track only low-speed and smooth human motions, struggling with agile and highly-dynamic movements. Furthermore, training initial policies can be computationally expensive, and these methods often lack robust tolerance mechanisms for motions that are inherently difficult to track. ASAP introduced a multi-stage mechanism and learned a residual policy to mitigate the sim-to-real gap for agile motions, but this work focuses on improving motion feasibility and agility primarily within simulation.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by focusing on enabling humanoid robots to master highly-dynamic skills like Kungfu and dancing. It addresses the challenges of physical feasibility and high-agility motion tracking through a two-stage framework involving physics-based motion processing and an adaptive motion tracking mechanism, developed entirely within simulation, before deployment on a physical robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a physics-based humanoid control framework capable of enabling humanoid robots to master highly-dynamic human behaviors, such as Kungfu and dancing.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and opportunities in humanoid robotics:

1. **Exploiting Humanoid Potential:** Humanoid robots, by virtue of their human-like morphology, possess the inherent potential to mimic a wide array of human behaviors. The growing availability of extensive motion datasets from Mo-Cap systems further amplifies this potential, making it a promising avenue for robots to learn complex whole-body control.
2. **Addressing Control Complexity:** Controlling the high-dimensional action spaces of humanoid robots to achieve realistic, human-like performance presents substantial difficulties. This complexity is compounded by the need for precise and coordinated movements across numerous degrees of freedom.
3. **Overcoming Physical Infeasibility of Reference Motions:** A fundamental challenge arises from the disparity between human biomechanics and robot physics. Motion sequences captured from humans often do not inherently comply with the joint limits, dynamics, and kinematics of a target humanoid robot. Directly attempting to imitate such infeasible motions through standard reinforcement learning (RL) frequently leads to policy training failures or suboptimal, unstable behaviors, as a physically achievable solution may not exist for the robot.
4. **Extending Beyond Low-Speed Motions:** Existing whole-body control algorithms, even with sophisticated reward designs and curriculum learning strategies, have largely been restricted to tracking smooth, low-speed human motions. There is a notable gap in the ability of these systems to accurately and stably imitate highly-dynamic, agile movements. The paper explicitly identifies the lack of suitable tolerance mechanisms for difficult-to-track motions in previous training processes as a contributing factor to this limitation.
5. **Improving Training Efficiency and Robustness:** Prior attempts to address motion feasibility, such as filtering infeasible motions or training initial policies to assess difficulty, can be computationally costly. There is a need for more efficient and robust mechanisms that can adapt to the varying quality and complexity of motion data without extensive manual tuning or resource-intensive pre-training.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to push the boundaries of humanoid motion control, enabling robots to perform a wider repertoire of complex and expressive human skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Physics-Based Humanoid motion Control (PBHC), operates through a two-stage pipeline: Motion Processing and Motion Imitation, supported by an asymmetric actor-critic Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework.

#### 3.1. Motion Processing Pipeline

This stage transforms raw human motion data into physically plausible reference motions for the Unitree G1 robot. It consists of four steps:

1. **SMPL-format Motion Estimation from Videos:** The GVHMR model is used to extract SMPL (Skinned Multi-Person Linear) parameters (body shape, joint rotations, global translation) from monocular videos. GVHMR is selected for its ability to align motions with gravity (reducing body tilt) and mitigate foot sliding artifacts by predicting foot stationary probabilities.
2. **Physics-based Motion Filtering:** This step identifies and filters out human motions that violate fundamental physical and biomechanical constraints for a robot. It calculates the projected distance (Δd_t) between the Center of Mass (CoM) and Center of Pressure (CoP) on the ground for each frame. Motions are deemed stable if:
 - Boundary frames (start and end) are stable (Δd_t < ϵ_stab).
 - The maximum length of consecutive unstable frames is below a threshold (max_k (t_k+1 - t_k) < ϵ_N).
 This process ensures that the robot attempts to track only dynamically stable and potentially feasible motions.
3. **Contact-aware Motion Correction:** To refine ground contact, contact masks (binary indicators for foot-ground contact) are estimated by analyzing ankle displacement between consecutive frames, based on the zero-velocity assumption and a height threshold. Small floating artifacts not removed by filtering are corrected by applying a vertical offset (Δh_t) to the global translation (ψ_t,z) if a foot is in contact, ensuring feet are at ground level. Exponential Moving Average (EMA) is then applied to smooth the corrected motion and prevent jitter.
4. **Motion Retargeting:** The processed SMPL motions are retargeted to the G1 robot using an Inverse Kinematics (IK)-based method. This differentiable optimization approach aligns end-effector trajectories while respecting robot joint limits. To expand motion diversity, the pipeline also integrates and processes selected motions from open-source datasets like AMASS and LAFAN.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Motion Tracking

This module addresses the challenge of tracking highly-dynamic motions by dynamically adjusting the learning process.

1. **Exponential Form Tracking Reward:** The reward function comprises task-specific rewards (joint states, rigid body states, foot contact) and regularization rewards (stability, smoothness). Task rewards (except foot contact) are formulated using an exponential form: `r(x) = exp(-x/σ)`, where `x` is the tracking error (e.g., MSE of joint angles) and `σ` is the tracking factor. This form is chosen for its boundedness and intuitive reward weighting, highlighting the importance of `σ` for responsiveness and precision.
2. **Optimal Tracking Factor Derivation:** The paper formalizes the problem of choosing `σ` as a bi-level optimization problem.
 - The **lower-level** optimization represents the standard RL process, where a policy is trained to maximize tracking and other rewards for a *given* `σ`.
 - The **upper-level** optimization aims to select `σ` that minimizes the accumulated tracking error of the *converged* policy.
 Through theoretical derivation (Appendix A), it is shown that the optimal tracking factor `σ*` is the average of the optimal tracking error over the trajectory: `σ* = Avg(x*)`.
3. **Adaptive Mechanism:** Recognizing the circular dependency between `σ*` and `x*` and the impracticality of a fixed `σ` for diverse motions, an adaptive mechanism is designed. It dynamically adjusts `σ` during training using a feedback loop:
 - An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) `(ˆx)` of the instantaneous tracking error is maintained as an online estimate of the expected error.
 - At each step, `σ` is updated to `σ ← min(σ, ˆx)`.
 This ensures `σ` is non-increasing and initialized to a relatively large value. As the tracking error decreases, `σ` tightens, progressively improving tracking precision.

#### 3.3. RL Training Framework

The core policy optimization relies on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with an actor-critic architecture.

1. **Problem Formulation:** The motion imitation task is framed as a goal-conditional RL problem, controlling the 23 DoFs of the Unitree G1 robot. Actions are target joint positions for a PD controller.
2. **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:**
 - The **actor's observation** (`s_actor`) includes the robot’s proprioceptive state (5-step history of joint positions, velocities, root angular velocity, projected gravity, and previous actions) and a time phase variable (`ϕ_t`) indicating motion progress.
 - The **critic's observation** (`s_critic`) augments the actor's observation with privileged information, including root linear velocity, reference motion positions, differences between current and reference body positions, and a set of randomized physical parameters. This privileged information aids in robust value estimation.
3. **Reward Vectorization:** Instead of summing rewards, the critic utilizes reward vectorization, where each reward component (`r_i`) is mapped to an independent value function (`V_i(s)`) that estimates its respective returns. These value functions are then aggregated to compute the action advantage, which is intended to improve value estimation stability.
4. **Reference State Initialization (RSI):** To enhance training efficiency by covering various motion phases, the robot's initial state is sampled from random time phases within the reference motion trajectories.
5. **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To bridge the reality gap, domain randomization is applied during training by varying physical parameters (e.g., friction, PD gains, link masses, CoM offsets, control delay) of the simulated environment and robot. Policies are then directly deployed on the real Unitree G1 robot, aiming for zero-shot transfer without fine-tuning.
6. **Curriculum Learning (Appendix C.5):** Two curriculum mechanisms are employed:
 - **Termination Curriculum:** The threshold for early episode termination (based on motion deviation) is gradually decreased.
 - **Penalty Curriculum:** The weight of regularization terms is progressively increased to promote stable and physically plausible behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of PBHC's components and its overall performance in tracking highly-dynamic motions, both in simulation and real-world deployment.

#### 4.1. Motion Filtering Effectiveness (Q1)

The physics-based motion filtering method was applied to a set of human motion sequences. Among 10 sequences, 4 were rejected, and 6 were accepted. Policies trained on accepted motions consistently achieved high Episode Length Ratios (ELRs), indicating prolonged and successful tracking. In contrast, policies trained on rejected motions achieved a maximum ELR of only 54%, suggesting frequent termination due to instability. This demonstrates that the filtering effectively pre-screens untrackable motions, improving training efficiency by focusing on viable candidates. An additional analysis of the contact mask estimation showed an accuracy of 91.4% on a manually labeled dataset, and visual inspection confirmed its effectiveness in mitigating floating artifacts.

#### 4.2. Simulation Tracking Performance (Q2)

PBHC was compared against three baselines: OmniH2O, ExBody2, and MaskedMimic. Evaluation was performed across motions categorized into easy, medium, and hard difficulty levels, using metrics such as Global Mean Per Body Position Error (E_g-mpbpe), root-relative Mean Per Body Position Error (E_mpbpe), Mean Per Joint Position Error (E_mpjpe), and errors in velocities and accelerations.

- PBHC consistently achieved significantly lower tracking errors across all metrics and difficulty levels compared to the deployable baselines OmniH2O and ExBody2. This improvement is attributed to the adaptive motion tracking mechanism, which adjusts parameters to suit diverse motion characteristics, unlike the fixed, empirically tuned parameters of the baselines.
- While MaskedMimic, a method primarily designed for character animation, performed well on some metrics, it is not directly comparable as it does not account for robot constraints like partial observability and action smoothness. An oracle version of PBHC (which similarly overlooked these constraints) showed performance approaching that of MaskedMimic.

#### 4.3. Impact of Adaptive Motion Tracking Mechanism (Q3)

An ablation study was conducted to assess the adaptive motion tracking mechanism against fixed tracking factor configurations (Coarse, Medium, UpperBound, LowerBound).

- Fixed tracking factors exhibited variable performance; no single setting was optimal across all motion types. Some fixed factors performed well on specific motions but suboptimally on others.
- The adaptive motion tracking mechanism consistently achieved near-optimal performance across all tested motion types, demonstrating its effectiveness in dynamically adjusting the tracking factor to match the specific demands of varying motions.

#### 4.4. Real-World Deployment (Q4)

Policies trained with PBHC were deployed on a physical Unitree G1 robot.

- The robot successfully demonstrated a diverse range of highly-dynamic skills, including complex martial arts techniques (boxing, various kicks), acrobatic movements (360-degree spins), flexible motions (squats, stretches), and artistic performances (dance routines, Tai Chi sequences). This showcases the system's versatility and dynamic control in real-world scenarios.
- A quantitative assessment of Tai Chi motion on the real robot revealed that tracking performance metrics closely aligned with those obtained in MuJoCo simulation. This indicates robust sim-to-real transfer, with policies maintaining high-performance control without fine-tuning.

#### 4.5. Learning Curves

Learning curves for episode length and reward demonstrated that the training process gradually stabilized and converged after approximately 20,000 steps, indicating the reliability and efficiency of the approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of humanoid robotics by introducing PBHC, a reinforcement learning framework that enables whole-body control for imitating highly-dynamic human motions. The framework's core innovations—the physics-based motion processing pipeline and the adaptive motion tracking mechanism—address critical limitations of prior work, which often struggled with the physical infeasibility of human motions and the need for adaptive control in complex, agile scenarios.

The successful real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating stable and expressive execution of skills like martial arts and dancing, suggests a tangible advance in humanoid capabilities. This work provides a foundation for more agile and stable robot behaviors, potentially expanding the applicability of humanoids in various domains.

#### 5.1. Potential Impact

- **Positive Societal Impacts:** The ability of humanoids to accurately imitate complex human movements could have applications in physical assistance, rehabilitation (e.g., precise exercise guidance), education (e.g., demonstrating complex physical skills), and entertainment (e.g., advanced dance or performance robots).
- **Limitations and Future Work:**
 - **Environment Awareness:** The current method lacks environment awareness, such as terrain perception and obstacle avoidance capabilities. This limits its deployment to structured environments and restricts its adaptability in unstructured real-world settings.
 - **Motion Generalization:** Each policy is currently trained to imitate a single motion. This approach may not be efficient for applications requiring a broad and diverse repertoire of skills. Future research could focus on developing methods that maintain high dynamic performance while enabling broader skill generalization across multiple motions or even novel tasks.
- **Broader Societal Concerns (as noted by authors):** The advancement of highly-agile humanoid robots interacting closely with humans introduces potential safety risks that require careful consideration. The increasing capability of robots could also lead to labor displacement in roles requiring physical skill or performance. Furthermore, the misuse of advanced motion imitation technologies, such as in surveillance or military applications, raises security concerns. The authors emphasize the need for clear regulation, robust safety mechanisms, and human oversight in the development and deployment of such technologies, as well as considering the environmental cost of training and operating physical robots.
