# DIJIT: A Robotic Head for an Active Observer

> [!summary] Summary
> DIJIT is a binocular robotic head designed for active observation that biomimics human eye and head movements. It integrates nine mechanical and eight optical degrees of freedom, achieving saccade speeds up to 600 degrees/second and an average saccadic error of 1.13 degrees, comparable to human performance, using a novel data-driven control method.

## Problem

- Existing robotic active vision systems often lack the comprehensive degrees of freedom (DoF) and biomimetic properties (e.g., human-like inter-camera baseline, size, weight) found in human vision, limiting active vision research.
- Achieving robust and accurate saccadic eye movements in robotic heads is challenging, often requiring complex kinematic modeling, precise axis alignment, and extensive calibration, which can be impractical for real-world systems.
- A significant gap exists in understanding how human-like eye and head movements contribute to overall visual ability in both biological and artificial systems, necessitating a more advanced and versatile robotic platform for comparative studies.

## Method

- Designed and constructed **DIJIT**, a biomimetic robotic head with nine mechanical DoF (3 neck, 3 per eye including roll) and eight optical DoF (4 per camera), using 3D-printed parts for a compact and lightweight structure (1.4kg).
- Implemented a rigid body linkage system that enables a human-like inter-camera baseline (115mm) while maintaining individual eye pan, tilt, and roll movements.
- Developed a data-driven saccade control method using a ChArUco board for calibration, homography estimation, and bilinear interpolation to accurately map desired visual fixation points to motor commands, bypassing complex kinematic modeling.

## Results

- DIJIT achieved impressive saccade speeds of up to 600 degrees/second, reaching over **85%** of human peak saccade speed and significantly outperforming most existing robotic heads.
- The data-driven saccade control method yielded an average saccadic error of **1.13** degrees, which is comparable to human saccadic error rates (**0.6**° to **2.89**°) and competitive with or superior to other real-world robotic implementations.
- The head's design successfully integrates nine mechanical and eight optical degrees of freedom into a compact (22x18x12cm head, 1.4kg) and human-like form factor, with an inter-camera baseline of 115mm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A biomimetic robotic head with a full complement of human-like mechanical and optical degrees of freedom can be achieved through innovative mechanical design and material choices, enabling new avenues for active vision research.
> - Precise and rapid saccadic eye movements, comparable to human performance, are feasible on robotic platforms even with lower-resolution motors and without explicit kinematic modeling, by employing a robust data-driven calibration and interpolation strategy.
> - Open-source design and software are crucial for fostering collaboration and accelerating progress in biomimetic robotics, making advanced active vision platforms more accessible to the wider research community.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned at the intersection of robotics, computer vision, cognitive science, and neuroscience, specifically within the paradigm of active vision and biomimetic robotics.

The **active vision** paradigm, pioneered in part by some of the authors, posits that visual tasks are best solved by actively controlling eye fixation and integrating perception with action. This approach has shown benefits in various robotic tasks like Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) and object recognition. However, many existing robotic active vision systems are limited, often employing monocular cameras or fixed binocular pairs on simple pan-tilt systems.

The paper highlights a significant gap between **human and robotic visual systems**. Human vision boasts numerous degrees of freedom (DoF) in eye and head movements, enabling versatile task performance. In contrast, robotic systems are typically designed with fewer DoF for specific, narrow tasks. The DIJIT project directly addresses this disparity by aiming to build a robotic head that closely mirrors the physical properties and functional versatility of the human visual system.

The research fits into the long history of **biomimetic robotic heads**, tracing back to the 1970s. These systems aim to replicate biological structures and functions to improve robotic capabilities and serve as testbeds for understanding biological vision. The authors categorize existing stereo heads into four groups based on their kinematic structure: fixed stereo, individual pan with common tilt, individual pan and tilt, and individual pan, tilt, and roll. DIJIT falls into the most advanced of these categories (individual pan, tilt, and roll for each eye, plus a multi-DoF neck), which is less commonly achieved in fully realized and reported systems. The paper critically reviews existing platforms like iCub, OREO, and Agile, noting their limitations in terms of DoF, human-like inter-camera baseline, size, weight, or comprehensive performance reporting. DIJIT is presented as a system designed to overcome these collective shortcomings, providing a platform with a truly human-like breadth of functionality.

Furthermore, the paper contributes to the specific area of **saccadic motion control** for robotic heads. Saccades are rapid eye movements essential for active vision in humans. Robotic implementation of saccades is challenging due to the complexities of mapping visual targets to precise motor commands. Existing methods range from detailed kinematic/dynamic modeling to neural network training, both of which often require extensive calibration or data. The authors propose a novel data-driven method for saccade control that aims to simplify this process, making it more robust and practical for real-world robotic systems, especially those with inherent mechanical inaccuracies. By comparing DIJIT's saccadic performance to both human capabilities and other robotic platforms, the paper firmly places its contributions within the ongoing efforts to create more agile and biologically plausible robotic vision systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **design and develop DIJIT, a novel binocular robotic head**, specifically engineered to function as an active observer for mobile robotic agents. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Advance Active Vision Research:** A core motivation is to provide a versatile platform that can enable deeper exploration into active vision. By replicating human-like eye and head-neck motions and their intricate interrelationships, DIJIT aims to facilitate studies on how these movements contribute to overall visual ability in both biological and artificial systems.
2. **Bridge Human and Machine Vision Understanding:** The authors are motivated to "explore the differences between how human vision employs eye/head movements to solve visual tasks and current computer vision methods." This comparative study is crucial for gaining insights into the efficiencies and limitations of current AI visual systems and potentially inspiring new, more biologically plausible computational approaches.
3. **Achieve Human-like Biomimetic Properties:** A significant objective is to create a robotic head that closely mirrors the physical characteristics and functional range of the human visual system. This includes replicating the nine mechanical degrees of freedom (three for each eye, three for the neck) and the four optical degrees of freedom per camera, along with human-comparable ranges and speeds of motion and a realistic inter-camera baseline.
4. **Address Limitations of Existing Robotic Heads:** The paper explicitly states that current robotic heads often "fall short in one or more of these aspects," such as insufficient degrees of freedom, non-human-like baselines, excessive size and weight, or inadequate reporting of performance. DIJIT is motivated by the need to overcome these collective shortcomings to provide a more comprehensive and capable research tool.
5. **Develop Robust Saccade Control:** A specific objective within the paper is to present and evaluate a new method for executing saccadic camera movements on DIJIT. This method aims to achieve human-like accuracy and speed without relying on overly complex or time-consuming kinematic modeling and calibration, addressing a practical challenge in biomimetic robotics.
6. **Promote Open Science and Accessibility:** The decision to make DIJIT's design and software open-source is a strong motivation to lower the barrier for other research groups to replicate, utilize, and build upon this platform. This fosters collaborative research and accelerates progress in the field.
7. **Support Future Applications:** Beyond fundamental research, the development of DIJIT is motivated by its potential deployment in real-world autonomous systems, such as assisting wheelchair-bound individuals, indicating a long-term goal for practical societal impact.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of DIJIT and its saccade control methodology involved a multi-faceted approach combining biomimetic mechanical design, off-the-shelf hardware integration, a standard robotic software interface, and a novel data-driven control strategy.

**A. Mechanical Design and Biomimicry:**
The core of DIJIT's design philosophy is biomimicry, aiming for human-like dimensions and articulation.
- **Degrees of Freedom (DoF):** DIJIT incorporates nine mechanical DoF: three for the neck (pan rotation, side-bending, and flexion/extension) and three for each camera (pan, tilt, and roll). This comprehensive set of DoF, particularly the individual camera roll, is critical for emulating human eye movements, including cyclotorsion for convergent stereo vision.
- **Structure and Materials:** To achieve a compact (22cm×18cm×12cm head, 22cm×22cm×26cm with neck) and lightweight (1.4kg) design, the body, frames, and joint parts are 3D printed using PA6-CF, a high-performance thermoplastic. This contrasts with heavier metal constructions common in other robotic heads, facilitating mobile platform integration.
- **Kinematics:** A rigid body linkage system is employed, allowing motors to be placed behind the cameras. This innovative arrangement enables a human-like inter-camera baseline (115mm, comparable to human 45-80mm) despite the individual camera DoF, which often leads to bulkier designs. The neck mechanisms use motors to push/pull metal rods for complex movements.

**B. Hardware Components:**
- **Motors:** Small, lightweight MG90S metal gear servos control individual camera movements. A larger 20kg DC servo manages neck pan, and Hitec HS-422 servos handle neck side-bending/flexion.
- **Control System:** A Teensy 4.0 ARM microcontroller, operating at 400kHz I2C data rate, controls the nine motors via an I2C servo driver interface.
- **Vision System:** Two high-performance iDS U3-3881LE-AF cameras with global shutters capture images at 59 fps (3088×2076 pixels).
- **Optics:** Each camera is equipped with a Corning Varioptic C-S-25H0-096 liquid 9.6mm lens, offering an approximate 40°×30° (H×V) field of view and featuring a focus control board with auto-focus.
- **Optical DoF:** The cameras and lenses provide four optical DoF: focusing distance (analogous to accommodation), exposure time (visual integration), white balance (color constancy), and sensor gain (photoreceptor sensitivity).
- **Sensing:** Two BNO085 IMUs rigidly connected to the camera frames provide rotational state measurements at 60Hz.

**C. Software Interface:**
- A ROS (Robot Operating System) node facilitates communication between the motor controller firmware on the Teensy 4.0 and the rest of the system. This allows for continuous reception, processing, and transmission of information, including motor commands and IMU data. Camera optical parameters can be controlled via software, although they are currently set before startup.

**D. DIJIT Saccade Control Methodology:**
The saccade control strategy developed for DIJIT focuses on a data-driven approach to bypass the complexities and inaccuracies of explicit kinematic modeling and precise axis alignment.
- **Problem Formulation:** The goal is to rapidly shift the camera's fixation from its current image center to a target point `t` in the image, similar to human saccades. The challenge lies in accurately mapping a desired visual shift to the appropriate motor commands.
- **Calibration Data Collection:**
 - A ChArUco board (a standard visual target) is fixed in front of DIJIT.
 - The pan and tilt motors of each camera are moved in discrete steps (e.g., 5 degrees) across their full range.
 - At each motor position `(pi, ti)`, an image `Ii` of the ChArUco board is captured, and the motor values and IMU readings are recorded. This creates a calibration dataset `C = {(Ii, pi, ti, sxi, syi, szi)}`.
- **Fixation Point to Motor State Correspondence:**
 - Given a target point `t` (in image coordinates) in the current view, the method aims to find the corresponding pan and tilt motor values that would center this point.
 - It assumes that `t` can be transferred to an image `Is` from the calibration set.
 - For each calibration image `Ik`, a homography `Hk` is estimated between `Ik` and `Is` using the common ChArUco board markers. Homographies are suitable here because the scene is planar and camera rotation is assumed to be about its optical center.
 - This homography `Hk` is then applied to the center of `Ik` (`p`) to determine its corresponding location `ck` in `Is` (`ck = Hk * p`).
- **Interpolation of Unseen Motor States:**
 - Since the calibration data is discrete, the exact motor state for a desired fixation might not be present.
 - The method identifies the four calibration samples whose `ck` locations are closest to the target point `t` in `Is`.
 - Bilinear interpolation is then applied to the pan and tilt values of these four closest samples to estimate the optimal motor settings required to fixate on `t`. This process is performed independently for each camera.

**E. Experimental Evaluation:**
- The saccade method was evaluated using the left camera, fixating on an ArUco marker whose center was detected via OpenCV.
- Performance was measured by "saccade error," defined as the angular difference between the back-projected ray of the landing point and the image center.
- 191 trials were conducted with target eccentricities distributed similarly to human saccade amplitudes (58% within 6°, 33% between 6-12°, 9% between 12-18°).
- Saccade speed was also measured and compared against human performance and other robotic heads.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research effectively demonstrates DIJIT's capabilities as a sophisticated robotic head and validates its novel data-driven saccade control method through rigorous experimentation.

**A. DIJIT Design Validation:**
- **Comprehensive Degrees of Freedom:** DIJIT successfully implements nine mechanical degrees of freedom (3 for the neck, 3 for each eye) and eight optical degrees of freedom (4 per camera: focus, exposure, white balance, gain). This breadth of functionality is a significant achievement compared to most existing robotic heads.
- **Human-like Baseline and Kinematics:** The design achieves an inter-camera baseline of 115mm, which is closely comparable to the human range of 45-80mm, a feature often compromised in other multi-DoF designs. The rigid body linkage system successfully allows for compact motor placement, keeping the head lightweight (1.4kg) and relatively small (22x22x26cm with neck).
- **Replicability and Accessibility:** The open-source nature of DIJIT's design and software makes it affordable and straightforward for other researchers to replicate, enhancing its potential impact on the community.

**B. Saccade Performance (Speed):**
- DIJIT demonstrates impressive saccade speeds, reaching **up to 600 degrees/second**. This performance significantly surpasses that of most other reported robotic heads (e.g., Manfredi at 120°/s, iCub at 50°/s, COG at 360°/s, OREO at 535°/s). It achieves "over 85% of the peak saccade speed relative to human performance (up to 700°/s)," making it highly biomimetic in its dynamic capabilities.

**C. Saccade Performance (Accuracy):**
- **Average Saccadic Error:** Across 191 trials, DIJIT achieved an average saccadic error of **1.13 degrees**. This error rate is remarkably close to human saccade errors, which are reported to vary between 0.6° and 2.89° depending on various factors like target eccentricity and task conditions.
- **Comparison with Other Robotic Systems:**
 - DIJIT's 1.13° error is favorable compared to other real robot implementations (e.g., Manfredi at 1.57°).
 - While some simulated systems reported lower errors (e.g., Van Opstal at 1.47°), the authors note that simulated results often don't account for real-world mechanical inaccuracies.
 - iCub reported <1° error but *after* a corrective saccade. DIJIT's error after a corrective saccade drops to 0.96°, maintaining competitiveness.
 - Systems reporting pixel errors (e.g., COG, Tombatossals) were converted to angular errors where possible, showing DIJIT's performance to be comparable or superior (COG's 1 pixel error translates to 0.93°-1.52°).
- **Error Distribution and Amplitude Dependence:**
 - The error distribution was consistent with human performance: 58% of saccades had an error less than 1°, and 90% had an error less than 2°.
 - As with humans, saccade accuracy was influenced by amplitude: average error for <6° amplitude was 0.93°, for 6-12° was 1.41°, and for 12-18° was 1.62°.
- **Directional Accuracy:** Average horizontal error was 0.90° and vertical error was 0.54°, indicating good performance across different directions, with a slight advantage in vertical movements, which is interesting compared to human tendencies.
- **Correction Saccades:** A corrective saccade reduced the average error to 0.96°, demonstrating the potential for refined visual targeting.

**D. Validation of Data-Driven Control Method:**
- The results validate the effectiveness of the proposed data-driven saccade control method. Despite using motors with lower resolution than some other biomimetic heads and intentionally avoiding complex kinematic models, the method achieved high accuracy. This indicates that the homography-based calibration and interpolation approach is robust and practical for real-world robotic systems where perfect mechanical alignment is difficult to achieve.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DIJIT robotic head and its associated saccade control method represent a significant contribution to the fields of active vision, biomimetic robotics, and cognitive science, with profound potential impacts:

**A. Advancing Active Vision Research:**
DIJIT provides an unprecedented research platform for active vision studies. Its comprehensive set of nine mechanical and eight optical degrees of freedom, combined with human-like ranges, speeds, and an appropriate inter-camera baseline, allows for more realistic and complex experiments. Researchers can now rigorously investigate how different eye and head movements (including vergence, version, and cyclotorsion) interact and contribute to various visual abilities, moving beyond the limitations of simpler pan-tilt systems. This can lead to new insights into the mechanisms of active perception in both biological and artificial intelligence.

**B. Bridging Human and Machine Vision:**
By closely mimicking the human visual system, DIJIT serves as an ideal testbed for comparing human and computer vision strategies for solving visual tasks. It enables researchers to:
- Evaluate computational theories of human vision (e.g., how the brain controls saccades, vergence, and attention).
- Identify fundamental differences in how humans and current AI perceive and interact with the world.
- Develop new computer vision algorithms inspired by the robustness and versatility of biological vision.

**C. Open Science and Community Empowerment:**
The decision to make DIJIT an open-source platform (including design files, parts lists, and software code) is a critical impact. This significantly lowers the barrier to entry for other research institutions, enabling wider adoption, replication, and further development of the technology. It promotes transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative innovation within the robotics and computer vision communities, potentially accelerating advancements that would otherwise be confined to a few well-funded labs.

**D. Robust and Practical Saccade Control:**
The data-driven saccade algorithm offers a practical solution to a persistent problem in biomimetic robotics: achieving precise movements without relying on perfectly aligned hardware or complex kinematic models. Its demonstrated accuracy and speed, comparable to human performance, validate a methodology that can be applied to a wider range of robotic platforms, especially those built with off-the-shelf components where perfect mechanical precision is not feasible. This reduces calibration time and complexity, making high-performance active vision more accessible.

**E. Foundation for Future Robotic Applications:**
DIJIT lays the groundwork for numerous future applications. The authors explicitly mention ongoing work on binocular saccades and active binocular 3D reconstruction algorithms, which are crucial for advanced robotic navigation, manipulation, and interaction. Furthermore, the vision of deploying DIJIT within an autonomous system to "support wheelchair-bound individuals" highlights a powerful potential for direct societal impact, enabling greater independence and quality of life through advanced robotic assistance. Its lightweight and compact design also make it suitable for integration onto a variety of mobile robotic platforms.

In summary, DIJIT is more than just another robotic head; it is a meticulously designed, open-source scientific instrument that embodies a comprehensive approach to active vision. Its development and validated performance will undoubtedly serve as a catalyst for new discoveries in how biological and artificial systems see and interact with their environment, with far-reaching implications for both fundamental science and practical applications.
