# iVISPAR -- An Interactive Visual-Spatial Reasoning Benchmark for VLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> The iVISPAR benchmark evaluates interactive visual-spatial reasoning in Vision-Language Models (VLMs), revealing that even state-of-the-art models significantly underperform humans in 3D environments due to struggles with precise object localization and complex planning. Closed-source models achieved higher success rates, with 2D visual inputs generally yielding better performance than 3D or text.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) consistently demonstrate weaknesses in spatial reasoning and visual alignment, which are critical for real-world physical agent tasks.
- Existing VLM benchmarks primarily rely on static 2D inputs, failing to capture the dynamic, interactive, and multi-step complexity of real-world 3D spatial problems.
- This lack of appropriate evaluation hinders the development of VLMs capable of effective visuomotor reasoning and spatial decision-making in grounded contexts.

## Method

- Researchers introduced ==iVISPAR==, an interactive multimodal benchmark based on a ==3D sliding geometric puzzle (SGP)==, enabling VLMs to act as agents in dynamic environments.
- Models interact via a natural language prompt-based API, executing commands and receiving updated board states in an ==iterative action-perception loop== across 3D vision, 2D vision, or text modalities.
- The benchmark utilizes ==procedural generation== for scalable complexity and includes human, optimal, and random agent baselines for comprehensive performance comparison.

## Results

- Closed-source models (e.g., Sonnet-3.5) dramatically outperformed open-source models, with Sonnet-3.5 achieving an **89.7%** success rate in the 2D visual modality.
- Performance consistently degraded with increasing task complexity (number of objects, shortest path length), with 3D vision showing a steeper decline.
- An auxiliary board state inference task showed models correctly identified an average of **4.2** objects in 2D images but only **1.4** in 3D images, primarily due to coordinate prediction errors in 3D.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLMs consistently fall short of human-level spatial reasoning and performance, especially in complex 3D scenarios, indicating a substantial gap in cognitive capabilities.
> - Models exhibit a strong bias towards 2D visual inputs, achieving their best performance with 2D vision and worst with 3D vision, potentially due to training data characteristics.
> - The primary cause for VLM failure in 3D environments is an inability to precisely localize objects, leading to frequent coordinate prediction errors and ineffective moves during problem-solving.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid evolution of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has opened new frontiers for AI applications, enabling them to perform complex tasks by integrating visual and linguistic information. These models are increasingly seen as general reasoning systems for real-world scenarios, including physical agent-based tasks and planning. However, a critical consensus in the field is that current VLMs still exhibit significant weaknesses in **spatial reasoning** and **visual alignment**. Spatial reasoning, essential for understanding and manipulating objects in a physical environment, encompasses tasks like mentally rotating shapes, predicting object movements, and recognizing patterns. Visual alignment refers to the model's ability to accurately link visual inputs with their corresponding linguistic representations.

Existing VLM benchmarks have largely fallen short in evaluating these crucial capabilities, primarily due to their reliance on static, text- or image-based setups. These benchmarks often oversimplify spatial contexts, focusing predominantly on 2D environments and lacking the interactive, dynamic, and multi-step complexity characteristic of real-world spatial problems. This limitation has hindered meaningful progress in developing VLMs that can truly operate in realistic 3D environments.

iVISPAR directly addresses this gap by introducing an **interactive multimodal benchmark** designed to evaluate VLMs as active agents in dynamic 3D environments. It builds upon previous efforts in simulation-based benchmarks for physical understanding and visual reasoning but distinguishes itself by offering a robust language interface, fine-grained 3D problem generation, and support for interactive planning and action execution by language agents. While other works have explored spatial reasoning in Large Language Models (LLMs) through textual or symbolic environments, and in VLMs via visual question-answering, iVISPAR pushes the boundary by demanding iterative action-perception loops and complex planning in a visually rich, interactive setting. This work is particularly relevant to the burgeoning fields of embodied AI and robotics, where VLMs are increasingly expected to perform visuomotor reasoning and spatial decision-making within grounded contexts. By focusing on 3D spatial reasoning, iVISPAR aims to bridge the gap between static evaluations and the demands of real-world interaction, thereby advancing the development of more capable and cognitively aligned VLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **systematically evaluate and benchmark the spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) acting as interactive agents in dynamic 3D environments.** The authors are motivated by the persistent struggle of current VLMs with spatial reasoning and visual alignment, despite their general advancements. They argue that existing benchmarks are insufficient, mainly relying on static 2D contexts that fail to capture the multi-step, interactive, and inherently 3D nature of real-world spatial problem-solving.

The key motivations driving the development of iVISPAR are:
- **Address limitations in current VLM evaluation:** To overcome the shortcomings of existing benchmarks that oversimplify spatial contexts and lack interactivity, especially in 3D.
- **Develop a dynamic and interactive benchmark:** To introduce a benchmark that allows VLMs to act as agents, engaging in an iterative action-perception loop to solve problems.
- **Utilize a formalized problem with scalable complexity:** To base the benchmark on a well-established problem (the sliding tile puzzle) that demands logical planning, spatial awareness, and multi-step reasoning, while offering robust control over task difficulty and access to optimal solutions.
- **Incorporate multimodal input:** To enable comprehensive assessment of VLMs across different input modalities (3D vision, 2D vision, and text) to understand their strengths and weaknesses in processing diverse forms of spatial information.
- **Provide a human baseline:** To contextualize VLM performance against human capabilities, offering a realistic target for human-level cognition in spatial reasoning.
- **Identify critical gaps in VLM capabilities:** Through rigorous evaluation, to pinpoint specific challenges VLMs face in complex spatial configurations, particularly concerning visual alignment in 3D environments.

Ultimately, the research aims to lay a foundation for guiding future VLM research towards overcoming these critical gaps and achieving more human-like reasoning and alignment capabilities in physical environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The iVISPAR benchmark is an interactive, multimodal puzzle simulator designed to test VLMs as agents. Its methodology is meticulously structured:

**Benchmark Design (iVISPAR):**
- **Modality Support:** Agents receive board states in one of three input modalities: a 3D rendered image, a 2D top-down view, or a text-based representation. This allows for systematic comparisons across modalities.
- **Interactive API:** VLMs interact with the environment through a prompt-based API, issuing natural language commands. This establishes an iterative action-perception loop where agents receive updated board states after each move.
- **Procedural Generation:** The simulator supports procedural generation of puzzle instances, allowing for scalable task complexity with controlled parameters.

**The Sliding Geom Puzzle (SGP):**
- **Problem Basis:** SGP is a variant of the classic sliding tile puzzle, a well-studied problem in developmental psychology and AI planning.
- **Geometric Objects:** Instead of numbered tiles, SGP uses "geoms"—geometric objects uniquely defined by combinations of color and shape. This increases visual-spatial complexity and mimics real-world scenarios where objects are distinguished by features beyond simple identifiers.
- **Objective:** Agents must rearrange geoms on a 4x4 board by moving them into free spaces to match a given goal configuration.
- **Action Space:** Agents issue commands like "move <color> <shape> <direction>" (e.g., "move blue sphere right"). Actions are validated for legality (e.g., no overlaps, within bounds).

**Complexity Scalability:**
- SGP inherits the NP-hard complexity of the Generalized Sliding Tile Puzzle (GSTP).
- Difficulty is scaled by: board size, number of objects (geoms), object variability (colors/shapes), shortest path length, and geom interference factor (how much objects obstruct each other).
- For experiments, a fixed 4x4 board was used, varying the number of objects (2-11) and shortest path length (2-11), with a geom interference factor of 0. Optimal path solutions are pre-computed using the A* algorithm.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Dataset:** 300 diverse board configurations were generated for the specified complexity ranges, resulting in 900 episodes for each VLM (300 per modality).
- **Baselines:**
 - **Human Baseline:** 30 participants interacted with the 3D vision modality via a web app GUI using text commands, mirroring VLM interaction.
 - **Optimal Agent:** An A*-driven agent providing shortest path solutions.
 - **Random Agent:** Performs uninformed but valid actions, uniformly sampled from those leading to new board states.
- **Models Evaluated:** A broad suite of state-of-the-art open-source and closed-source VLMs: Sonnet-3.5, Gemini-2.0-flash, GPT-4o (closed-source), InternVL2.5-78B, LLaVA-OneVision-72B, Qwen2-72B (open-source).
- **Prompting Strategy:**
 - **Context-Aware Zero-Shot Reasoning:** Models interpret task requirements without examples, relying on pre-trained knowledge, task instructions, and limited past interactions.
 - **Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** Models are prompted to break down problems into sub-tasks to enhance accuracy and interpretability.
 - **Context Window:** Constrained to the past two steps (state representations + model's action responses) to manage computational overhead and maintain sequential coherence.
 - **Uniform Prompt Template:** A single, minimal, and clear prompt template was used for all models and modalities to ensure consistent comparison and reproducibility, avoiding prompt engineering for specific models.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Success Rate:** The percentage of episodes completed.
- **Mean Step-Deviation from Optimal Path:** Measures how far an agent's path deviates from the shortest path, quantifying efficiency even in unsolved episodes (calculated as Regret = `d(st, s*) - (d(s0, s*) - t)`).
- **Error Pattern Analysis:** Classification of actions based on their effects: effective, ineffective, occupied destination, out-of-bounds, and illegal commands.
- **Auxiliary Task (Board State Inference):** Models are given an image and instructed to predict the corresponding board configuration in text form. This evaluates visual interpretation and representation accuracy. Errors are categorized as correctly inferred, missed, hallucinated, coordinate errors, color/shape mismatches, and format errors.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a robust, multi-faceted evaluation of VLM spatial reasoning, highlighting both task-solving proficiency and underlying perceptual and planning weaknesses.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VLMs on the iVISPAR benchmark revealed several key insights into their spatial reasoning capabilities across different modalities and complexities:

**Overall Performance:**
- **Basic Task Understanding:** All models demonstrated basic task understanding and showed goal-directed behavior, progressing towards the goal state.
- **Significant Performance Gaps:** VLMs consistently struggled with complex spatial configurations and fell significantly short of human performance. Human participants solved tasks perfectly with near-optimal paths.
- **Closed-Source vs. Open-Source:** Closed-source models (Sonnet-3.5, Gemini-2.0-Flash, GPT-4o) dramatically outperformed open-source models (InternVL2.5-78B, LLaVA-OneVision-72B, Qwen2-72B).
 - Sonnet-3.5 achieved the highest success rate at 89.7% in the 2D visual modality.
 - Open-source models performed closer to the random baseline, especially on visual inputs.

**Modality Impact:**
- **2D Vision Dominance:** All closed-source models performed best on 2D vision, followed by text, and worst on 3D vision. This suggests a potential bias in training data, where 2D visual inputs are more prevalent in existing spatial benchmarks.
- **Text Modality Robustness:** Text input, despite being challenging for humans, ranked second for closed-source models, indicating some robustness in linguistic reasoning for spatial tasks.
- **Open-Source Modality Struggle:** Open-source models performed poorly across all modalities, with relatively stronger performance on text potentially indicating a reliance on superficial pattern recognition rather than grounded spatial understanding.

**Error Patterns and Visual Alignment Challenges:**
- **Instruction Following:** Models rarely issued illegal commands or exhibited format errors, indicating a general understanding of how to follow instructions and interact with the environment's API.
- **Precise Localization (The Core Problem):** The board state inference auxiliary task revealed a sharp performance drop from 2D to 3D inputs.
 - Models correctly identified an average of 4.2 objects in 2D but only 1.4 in 3D.
 - This decline was primarily due to a substantial increase in **coordinate prediction errors** and moderate rises in color and shape mismatches in 3D.
 - This inability to precisely localize objects in 3D explains the weaker performance in 3D vision, leading to more ineffective moves, such as attempts to place objects out-of-bounds or onto occupied cells.
- **Ineffective Moves:** Error analysis showed that ineffective moves (occupied destination, out-of-bounds) were common, aligning with the poor visual alignment. Out-of-bounds errors were particularly frequent in the text condition, suggesting difficulties in understanding board dimensions from textual representations alone.

**Complexity Scaling:**
- **Performance Degradation:** Performance consistently dropped with increasing complexity in both dimensions (number of objects and shortest path length).
- **Modality-Specific Trends:**
 - **3D Vision:** Performance declined more steeply with increasing geom count in 3D, indicating that sequential planning under complex visual conditions (with more objects) poses a major challenge.
 - **Text Modality:** In the text-only setting, the number of geoms had less impact, with errors mostly determined by the length of the shortest path. This highlights limitations in deriving comprehensive spatial references solely from language.
- **Coordinate Errors and Object Count:** Data from the auxiliary task showed that coordinate prediction errors significantly increased with the number of geoms on the board, further underscoring the difficulty models have with object localization in crowded scenes.

In summary, iVISPAR demonstrates that while advanced VLMs can handle basic interactive spatial tasks, their capabilities are severely limited by challenges in visual alignment and precise object localization, especially in 3D environments. This gap widens significantly with increasing task complexity, indicating that current VLMs are still far from achieving human-level spatial cognition.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of iVISPAR is a significant contribution to the field of Vision-Language Models, with several key implications and potential impacts:

**1. Advancing VLM Evaluation:** iVISPAR provides a much-needed robust and comprehensive benchmark for evaluating interactive visual-spatial reasoning in VLMs. By moving beyond static 2D image-text pairs, it pushes the evaluation paradigm towards dynamic, multi-step problem-solving in realistic 3D environments, which is crucial for developing truly intelligent agents. This will help standardize evaluations and foster more targeted research.

**2. Highlighting Critical Gaps:** The benchmark unequivocally demonstrates that state-of-the-art VLMs, particularly in 3D vision, struggle with fundamental aspects of spatial reasoning, such as precise object localization and complex sequential planning. The performance disparity between 2D and 3D visual inputs, and the consistent underperformance relative to human baselines, underscores persistent limitations in visual alignment and cognitive capabilities. This provides concrete evidence of areas where significant future research and architectural improvements are needed.

**3. Guiding Future VLM Development:** The detailed error analysis and complexity scaling insights offer a roadmap for VLM researchers. The prevalence of coordinate prediction errors, for instance, points to the need for models with enhanced spatial perception mechanisms, possibly integrating more sophisticated 3D scene understanding or explicit spatial representations. The findings suggest that merely increasing model size or training data might not suffice; fundamental architectural changes or novel training methodologies might be required to bridge the gap in 3D spatial cognition.

**4. Bridging Cognitive Science and AI:** By grounding the benchmark in a classic problem from developmental psychology and including a human baseline, the research effectively bridges insights from cognitive science with AI development. This interdisciplinary approach can inspire AI researchers to think more deeply about the cognitive mechanisms underlying human spatial reasoning and attempt to replicate or approximate them in artificial systems.

**5. Impact on Embodied AI and Robotics:** The benchmark is highly relevant for embodied AI and robotics, where VLMs are increasingly being deployed as agents to interact with the physical world. The demonstrated limitations in 3D spatial reasoning directly impact the reliability and safety of robots tasked with manipulation, navigation, and complex planning in real-world environments. Addressing these shortcomings through benchmarks like iVISPAR will be critical for the safe and effective deployment of autonomous systems.

**6. Future Research Directions:** The authors explicitly outline future work, including expanding the benchmark to incorporate additional tasks focused on scene understanding, rotation, and transformation challenges. This indicates a commitment to continuously evolve the benchmark to address emerging needs and push the boundaries of VLM capabilities.

In conclusion, iVISPAR is a timely and impactful contribution that not only provides a rigorous tool for assessing current VLM capabilities but also serves as a critical diagnostic for identifying the conceptual and technical barriers to achieving human-level spatial intelligence in AI. Its findings will likely stimulate focused research efforts toward more robust, spatially aware, and cognitively aligned Vision-Language Models.
