# Learning Agile Intruder Interception using Differentiable Quadrotor Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and the Artificial Intelligence Integration Center developed a control policy for autonomous quadrotor interception of intruder drones, relying solely on the intruder's 3D unit direction vector and the interceptor's own state. This policy, trained using differentiable nonlinear quadrotor dynamics, achieved significantly higher interception success rates, up to 60% against intruders moving at 8 m/s, demonstrating an average improvement of over 30% compared to methods using simplified dynamics.

## Problem

- Existing Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) methods for drone interception typically require the intruder's full 3D relative position or distance, which is difficult to obtain using passive monocular cameras.
- Prior differentiable physics models for quadrotor navigation often used simplified point mass dynamics, limiting their effectiveness for agile and dynamic tasks like drone interception.
- There is a growing need for effective, cost-efficient, and covert countermeasures against unauthorized drone activity, particularly for airspace security and critical infrastructure protection.

## Method

- An end-to-end control policy, implemented as a ==recurrent neural network==, was developed to generate interception commands using only the interceptor's linear velocity, rotation matrix, and the 3D unit direction vector to the intruder.
- The policy parameters were optimized using the ==Analytical Policy Gradient (APG)== method, ==backpropagating gradients through a differentiable nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model==, a departure from simplified point mass dynamics. This model is trained via **Quad APG** (APG with nonlinear quadrotor dynamics).
- Training involved a ==composite objective function== that minimized ==guidance terms== (e.g., aligning relative velocity with line-of-sight) and ==regularization terms== (e.g., penalizing acceleration and jerk) to promote smooth, feasible trajectories.

## Results

- The Quad APG policy achieved approximately **60%** interception success rates against intruders moving at speeds up to **8 m/s**, significantly outperforming PMD APG (around **30%** success) at similar speeds.
- Quad APG improved interception accuracy by an average of **37%** for ellipse, **33%** for spiral, and **31%** for lemniscate intruder trajectories compared to PMD APG, and was capable of intercepting intruders at speeds up to **10 m/s**.
- Policies trained with nonlinear quadrotor dynamics (Quad APG) exhibited lower average acceleration and jerk for successful interceptions, indicating more precise and efficient control compared to those trained with simplified point mass dynamics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training with a high-fidelity nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model in a differentiable simulation framework yields substantially superior interception performance for agile maneuvers compared to policies trained with simplified point mass dynamics.
> - Analytical Policy Gradient (APG) methods demonstrate higher sample efficiency and achieve faster interception times compared to model-free Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for this continuous control problem.
> - A recurrent policy architecture can implicitly estimate unobserved intruder states like velocity and acceleration from a sequence of noisy direction measurements, enabling effective control under practical sensing constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The proliferation of unmanned aerial systems (UAS), particularly low-cost commercial drones, has introduced challenges for airspace security and critical infrastructure protection. This necessitates the development of effective and cost-efficient countermeasures, among which autonomous drone interception through forced collision is a strategy under investigation.

Prior research leveraging Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for drone interception has typically relied on the availability of the intruder's 3D relative position or distance during inference. Obtaining this information in real-world scenarios, particularly with passive monocular camera sensors, is technically challenging. Alternative sensing modalities, such as LiDAR, offer distance information but are active, more expensive, and potentially detectable. Other counter-drone strategies, including jamming Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) signals or deploying nets, encounter limitations such as ineffectiveness in GPS-denied environments or the requirement for precise multi-agent coordination and external target tracking.

Within the domain of learning-based quadrotor navigation, differentiable physics models, often combined with Analytical Policy Gradient (APG) methods, have demonstrated improved sample efficiency and performance compared to imitation learning approaches. However, much of this prior work has utilized simplified point mass dynamics models for tasks like navigation to a fixed setpoint.

This paper addresses a gap in the literature by developing an interception policy that operates under realistic sensing constraints, specifically using only the 3D direction unit vector to the intruder and the interceptor's own state. It extends the application of differentiable simulation to the dynamic and agile interception problem, departing from the simplified point mass dynamics typically used in differentiable navigation, and instead incorporating a more comprehensive nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model. This approach aims to achieve agile interception maneuvers while adhering to practical sensing limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing demand for effective countermeasures against unauthorized drone activity, particularly considering the potential disruption and damage these systems can inflict on critical infrastructure and operations. The authors propose a solution involving the interception of intruder drones through forced collisions.

The core problem identified by the researchers is the disconnect between the data requirements of existing deep reinforcement learning (DRL) interception methods and the capabilities of commonly available, passive sensing modalities. Prior DRL approaches typically assume access to the intruder's full 3D position or distance information, which is not readily obtained from passive, monocular cameras. Such cameras, however, can provide a 3D unit direction vector to an intruder through object detection algorithms.

Therefore, the key objective is to develop and evaluate a control policy capable of learning agile interception maneuvers that relies solely on the 3D direction unit vector to the intruder and the interceptor's own state (linear velocity and rotation matrix) as inputs during inference. This objective is driven by the aim to enhance the practicality and real-world applicability of autonomous drone interception systems.

To achieve this, the authors state three specific contributions:
1. **Development of an end-to-end control policy:** This policy is designed for interception and trained using differentiable simulation, integrating sensing and control.
2. **Extensive comparative evaluation:** The approach is evaluated against existing state-of-the-art reinforcement learning algorithms and different dynamics models within a simulation environment.
3. **Open-source software release:** Making the developed software publicly available aims to facilitate further research and development in this field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on designing and training a learning-based control policy for a quadrotor interceptor. This policy is tasked with intercepting a dynamic intruder in a 3D environment.

**Policy Inputs and Outputs:**
At each timestep, the interceptor observes its linear velocity ($\mathbf{v}_t$), its body-to-start rotation matrix ($\mathbf{R}_t$), and the 3D unit direction vector to the intruder ($\hat{\mathbf{d}}_t$). All observations are expressed in the interceptor's starting frame. The policy outputs a 3D commanded mass-normalized thrust vector ($\mathbf{a}^{\text{cmd}}_t$) and a scalar yaw angle ($\psi_t$). These commands are then tracked by an onboard PD attitude controller.

**Policy Network Architecture:**
The policy ($\pi_{\theta}$) is implemented as a recurrent neural network (RNN) (Fig. 2). Proprioceptive state components ($\mathbf{v}_t$, $\mathbf{R}_t$) and the intruder direction vector ($\hat{\mathbf{d}}_t$) are initially processed by separate Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) encoders, each producing a 192-dimensional embedding. These embeddings are summed and fed into a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with 192 hidden units. The GRU's output then passes through a single hidden layer before a linear head generates the four-dimensional action command. The recurrent structure allows the policy to integrate temporal information, implicitly estimating intruder velocity and acceleration from the sequence of direction measurements, which is necessary given that these are not directly observable inputs.

**Differentiable Dynamics Model:**
The policy parameters are optimized using the Analytical Policy Gradient (APG) method, which involves backpropagating gradients through time (BPTT). This method requires a differentiable dynamics model. Unlike some prior works that used simplified point mass dynamics, this study employs a nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model. This decision was made to achieve higher interception accuracy for agile maneuvers.

The quadrotor is modeled as a rigid body with state variables for position, linear velocity, attitude quaternion, and body-frame angular velocity. The model incorporates standard rigid body dynamics, including collective thrust, body torque, inertia matrix, gravity, and an aerodynamic drag model. Rotor dynamics are included, converting high-level policy commands into desired rotor speeds, with a first-order delay to simulate real-world latency. The ordinary differential equations of the dynamics are integrated using a fixed-step 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method, which is differentiable.

**Objective Functions:**
The training objective minimizes a weighted sum of guidance and regularization terms ($L_t$). Crucially, the intruder's position and velocity are utilized as "privileged information" for loss computation during training but are *not* provided as inputs to the policy during inference or deployment.

- **Guidance Terms:** Inspired by parallel navigation principles, these terms encourage interception.
 - `L_align`: Penalizes the sine of the angle between the relative position vector to the intruder and the relative velocity vector, aiming for alignment (relative velocity along the line-of-sight).
 - `L_close`: Maximizes the closing speed along the line-of-sight.
- **Regularization Terms:** These terms promote smooth and feasible trajectories.
 - `L_acc`: Penalizes the magnitude of commanded acceleration.
 - `L_jerk`: Penalizes the magnitude of jerk (derivative of acceleration) to enforce temporal smoothness.
 - `L_vmax`: A one-sided penalty that constrains the interceptor's speed to a desired maximum, allowing freedom below this limit.
- **Yaw Alignment Term:** `L_yaw` encourages the interceptor's body x-axis to align with the intruder's direction.

**Experimental Design and Implementation:**
Experiments were conducted using a custom-built, parallelized, GPU-accelerated simulator developed in PyTorch.
- **Interception Criteria:** Success is defined as the intruder being within a capture radius ($c_r$) of 0.3 meters. Failure occurs if the episode length ($T_{ep} = 600$ timesteps or 12 seconds) is exceeded or if the distance between agents surpasses an escape radius ($e_r$) of 100 meters.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success rate, episode length, and control effort (acceleration and jerk profiles).
- **Training and Evaluation:** Policies were trained for 1500 updates over 512 parallel environments (approximately 49.15 million environment steps). Evaluations utilized 110,000 environments.
- **Intruder Trajectories:** Intruder trajectories included ellipse, spiral, and lemniscate shapes (Fig. 3). Ellipse trajectories were used for training, while all three types were used for evaluation to assess generalizability. Trajectory parameters, including speed and geometry, were randomized. Evaluation trajectories incorporated dynamic feasibility checks based on the quadrotor model.
- **Baselines:** The proposed approach (Quad APG, APG with nonlinear quadrotor dynamics) was compared against:
 - **PMD APG:** APG trained with a simplified Point Mass Dynamics model.
 - **PMD PPO:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a model-free RL algorithm, trained with simplified Point Mass Dynamics. Domain randomization was applied to PMD policies to improve transferability.
- **Platform Agility:** For initial APG vs. PPO comparisons, the interceptor mass was 2.65 kg. For the PMD APG vs. Quad APG comparison, the interceptor mass was reduced to 1.0 kg to simulate a more agile platform capable of higher speeds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the performance of the proposed differentiable quadrotor dynamics approach for agile intruder interception.

**Comparison of APG and PPO using Point Mass Dynamics (PMD):**
- **Sample Efficiency:** Training performance plots (Fig. 4a) indicated that the Analytical Policy Gradient (APG) method, when utilizing a simplified point mass dynamics model (PMD APG), demonstrated higher sample efficiency during training compared to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) using the same dynamics model (PMD PPO). PMD APG achieved high success rates in fewer environment steps.
- **Episode Length:** The PMD APG policy also resulted in shorter episode lengths (Fig. 4b), indicating faster interception times compared to PMD PPO.
- **Interception Accuracy:** During evaluation across various intruder trajectories (ellipse, spiral, lemniscate) and speeds up to 4 m/s (Fig. 5), PMD APG consistently outperformed PMD PPO. The performance disparity widened as intruder speeds increased, suggesting greater robustness of APG to more dynamic scenarios.
- **Control Effort:** Analysis of successful rollouts (Appendix Fig. 9) revealed that policies trained with PPO generally exhibited higher average acceleration and jerk values compared to APG policies, implying a more aggressive and potentially less efficient control effort. The initial interceptor mass of 2.65 kg limited agility, leading to motor saturation at intruder speeds exceeding 4 m/s.

**Comparison of PMD APG and Quadrotor Dynamics APG (Quad APG):**
- To enable higher intruder speeds and greater agility, the interceptor mass was reduced to 1.0 kg for this set of experiments.
- **Training Convergence:** Both PMD APG and Quad APG policies showed similar convergence rates in terms of environment steps during training (Fig. 6), suggesting that the increased complexity of the nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model did not substantially impede training speed in this metric.
- **Interception Accuracy at Higher Speeds:** The Quad APG policy, trained with the nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model, demonstrated substantially higher interception accuracy compared to the PMD APG policy (Fig. 7). Quad APG successfully intercepted approximately 60% of intruders at speeds up to 8 m/s, whereas PMD APG achieved only about 30% success at similar speeds.
 - Specifically, Quad APG outperformed PMD APG by an average of 37% for ellipse trajectories, 33% for spiral trajectories, and 31% for lemniscate trajectories.
 - The Quad APG policy was capable of intercepting intruders moving at speeds up to 10 m/s.
- **Control Effort:** For successful interceptions, policies trained with the nonlinear quadrotor dynamics (Quad APG) exhibited lower average acceleration and jerk compared to those trained with simplified point mass dynamics (PMD APG) (Appendix Fig. 10). This indicates that leveraging the higher-fidelity dynamics model allows for more precise and efficient control, requiring less aggressive maneuvers to achieve interception.
- **Training Times:** The Quad APG policy required approximately 137 minutes for training, significantly longer than PMD APG (approximately 10 minutes) and PMD PPO (approximately 7 minutes). This indicates a trade-off between training speed and the fidelity of the dynamics model, with the higher-fidelity model providing superior performance despite longer training times.

Qualitative examples of rollouts (Fig. 8) illustrate the agile pursuit behaviors of the Quad APG policy, with collisions occurring at the designated points.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a methodological advancement in autonomous drone interception, particularly by addressing a significant practical constraint in real-world deployments. The development of a control policy that relies solely on the 3D unit direction vector to an intruder and the interceptor's own state, rather than full 3D relative position or distance, aligns with the capabilities of passive, monocular camera systems. This is a critical step towards realizing cost-effective and covert counter-UAS solutions.

The primary significance of the findings lies in demonstrating that training with a high-fidelity nonlinear quadrotor dynamics model, within a differentiable simulation framework, yields substantially superior interception performance. The Quad APG policy achieved higher success rates (an average improvement of over 30%) and the ability to intercept more agile intruders (up to 10 m/s) compared to methods based on simplified point mass dynamics. This suggests that incorporating realistic physical dynamics during training is important for developing robust controllers for agile robotic tasks.

The work also reinforces the observed benefits of Analytical Policy Gradient (APG) methods, showing them to be more sample-efficient than model-free PPO for this complex, continuous control problem. This efficiency can reduce the computational resources and time required for policy development.

Potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Counter-UAS Capabilities:** The methodology provides a foundation for the development of autonomous interception drones capable of operating with practical sensor configurations, contributing to the defense of restricted airspaces and critical infrastructure.
- **Advancements in Agile Robotics:** The success in learning agile interception maneuvers under constrained observations contributes to the broader field of robotics, particularly in areas requiring high-speed, dynamic interactions.
- **Influence on Differentiable Simulation Research:** By demonstrating the performance benefits of using high-fidelity differentiable dynamics for complex tasks, the research underscores the utility of these simulation paradigms beyond simpler navigation problems.

The authors acknowledge limitations, including the assumption of constant intruder speed and the absence of sensor uncertainty or hardware validation. Addressing these aspects in future work will be important for transitioning the developed policies to real-world adversarial scenarios and physical systems. The open-source release of the software facilitates further research and development in this area.
