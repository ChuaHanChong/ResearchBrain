# SteadyTray: Learning Object Balancing Tasks in Humanoid Tray Transport via Residual Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces ReST-RL, a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework that enables bipedal humanoid robots to robustly transport unsecured objects on a tray while maintaining stable locomotion. This approach decouples gait control from object stabilization, demonstrating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 humanoid, successfully carrying diverse items like fluid-filled glasses and medical tools under external disturbances.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face challenges in reliably transporting unsecured payloads on a tray in service environments due to inherent oscillations from bipedal gait.
- The nonrigid coupling between a robot's end-effectors and an unsecured payload complicates stabilization, requiring coordinated whole-body adaptation.
- Existing methods often struggle to simultaneously achieve robust bipedal locomotion and precise, dynamic stabilization of unsecured objects.

## Method

- ==Developed ReST-RL==, a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework that separates locomotion learning from payload stabilization.
- A base policy is first trained for robust locomotion, then a residual module is learned to provide corrective actions for object stabilization using privileged observations.
- A student encoder, conditioned on non-privileged, camera-based object observations, is distilled from a teacher to enable real-world deployment without privileged information.

## Results

- ReST-RL achieved substantial improvements in object stability (reduced Object Projected Gravity XY Tilt) while maintaining locomotion quality in simulation across various disturbance scenarios.
- The framework demonstrated high robustness against diverse body-level and payload-level push disturbances, with success rates of **74.4%** for Push Robot and **73.9%** for Push Object, and generalized to varying object geometries.
- Achieved reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, successfully transporting diverse real-world objects (e.g., fluid-filled wine glass, medical tools) under external forces without retraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling locomotion and object stabilization into a hierarchical learning architecture effectively manages competing objectives, leading to superior robustness compared to end-to-end approaches.
> - Incorporating observation delays for object-centric sensing during training significantly improves sim-to-real transfer reliability and overall stabilization performance.
> - Extensive domain randomization, including variations in robot, tray, and object physical parameters, and application of push perturbations, is crucial for achieving robust real-world generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of humanoid robots in service industries, such as healthcare and food services, is motivated by the need to address labor shortages and automate routine tasks. These environments are often cluttered and designed for human navigation, where humanoid robots, with their compact footprint and active stabilization capabilities, may offer advantages over wheeled or quadrupedal platforms. A critical technical challenge for humanoids in these contexts is the reliable transport of unsecured payloads, such as fluid-filled glasses or fragile instruments on a tray, during dynamic locomotion. The inherent oscillations from bipedal gait and foot impacts can propagate disturbances throughout the robot's kinematic chain, necessitating coordinated whole-body adaptation.

Prior research in humanoid loco-manipulation has made progress in tasks like pick-and-place, door opening, and package carrying, often employing sim-to-real reinforcement learning with various forms of supervision (e.g., imitation learning, teacher-student distillation). End-effector stabilization methods, such as SoFTA, have demonstrated success in reducing oscillations for objects either hung from or rigidly attached to a robot's hand. However, these methods typically do not extend to stabilizing *unsecured* objects on a tray, which presents a more complex problem due to the nonrigid intermediate coupling between the robot's end-effectors and the payload. The dynamic maneuvers associated with walking, turning, accelerating, or decelerating, coupled with external disturbances, further complicate maintaining the tray level and preventing object slip or toppling. This paper addresses this specific gap by introducing a hierarchical learning architecture designed to manage the competing objectives of robust locomotion and precise payload stabilization for unsecured objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the pressing demand for automation in service sectors, where humanoid robots are envisioned to perform tasks requiring reliable object transport in human-centered environments. Tasks such as spill-free meal delivery, sterile instrument transport in operating rooms, and assistance in elder-care facilities necessitate robots to move and carry objects with a level of reliability comparable to humans. Humanoid robots are considered potentially well-suited for these environments due to their ability to navigate narrow passages and actively stabilize against disturbances, unlike wheeled platforms which lack active stabilization, or quadrupedal robots which may have larger footprints.

The core technical challenge, termed "SteadyTray," involves a bipedal humanoid robot transporting unsecured objects on a tray using both arms. This problem is particularly difficult because the tray acts as a nonrigid link between the robot and the payload. This setup creates an objective conflict: the robot must generate agile whole-body gaits for locomotion while simultaneously maintaining a near-level end-effector orientation to stabilize the tray and its contents. Disturbances generated by foot impacts during walking propagate up the robot's kinematic chain, requiring both the upper body and the gait to adapt dynamically. Existing approaches, which often focus on attached objects or monolithic end-to-end learning, have not effectively addressed this dual challenge.

The key objectives of this work are:
1. **Formulate the SteadyTray Problem:** Define the task of transporting unsecured objects on a tray with a bipedal humanoid robot as a bimanual loco-manipulation problem, representative of real-world service scenarios.
2. **Develop a Hierarchical Learning Architecture:** Introduce Residual Student-Teacher Reinforcement Learning (ReST-RL), a framework that explicitly decouples locomotion from payload stabilization. This architecture aims to integrate a robust base locomotion policy with a dynamic residual module designed to cancel gait-induced perturbations at the end-effector.
3. **Ensure Robust Stabilization:** Achieve stable dual-arm load transport by maintaining the tray level and resisting object tipping, even under external disturbances and during dynamic locomotion maneuvers (e.g., turning, accelerating, decelerating).
4. **Preserve Locomotion Quality:** Ensure that the payload stabilization efforts do not degrade the underlying bipedal stability or the quality of the robot's gait.
5. **Achieve Sim-to-Real Generalization:** Develop a policy that can reliably transfer from simulation to real-world hardware (Unitree G1 humanoid) without extensive fine-tuning, generalizing across diverse objects and external disturbances.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces ReST-RL, a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework designed to address the SteadyTray problem. The methodology involves a staged training process, separating robust locomotion learning from object stabilization.

**Problem Statement:** The task is framed as a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning problem, where the policy takes a desired linear and angular velocity goal (G) and current observations (S) to output target joint positions (A). Observations include H-step histories of proprioceptive data (joint positions, velocities, robot angular velocity, projected gravity, past actions) and object data (pose in the camera frame).

**A. Base Policy Training:**
The initial step involves training a base policy, $\pi_{base}$, to achieve robust locomotion while carrying a tray in a level orientation. This policy takes proprioceptive observations and velocity goals as input, outputting base actions. It is optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a reward function primarily focused on locomotion objectives, such as velocity tracking, torso stability, and gait regularity, along with initial tray-related terms for holding configuration and contact.

**B. Residual Module Learning:**
After the base policy is trained and frozen, a residual module (comprising an encoder and an adapter) is trained to provide corrective adjustments for payload stabilization. This module is initially trained using *privileged observations*, which include detailed, noise-free information about the robot's linear velocity, tray pose and gravity, and object pose, linear and angular velocities, and projected gravity, all within the robot's frame. These privileged observations are concatenated with proprioceptive history and the goal for the encoder input. A longer temporal window (H=32 steps) is used for observations to capture subtle destabilization trends.

Two types of residual adapters are explored:
1. **Residual Action Adapter:** This adapter outputs a corrective action ($\tilde{a}_t$) that is linearly combined with the base policy's action ($a_t$) and a default joint position to form the final refined action: $\hat{a}_t = \alpha_{base} a_t + \alpha_{residual} \tilde{a}_t + q_{default}$.
2. **Residual FiLM Adapter:** This adapter uses Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers to modulate intermediate activations within the frozen base policy. For each modulated layer, the adapter predicts scale ($\gamma_t$) and bias ($\beta_t$) parameters based on the latent feature from the encoder, which are then applied to the base policy's activations.

Both adapter types are initialized to output zero correction, ensuring the initial behavior matches the pretrained base policy and preserves its locomotion skills. The residual module's encoder and adapter parameters are jointly optimized using PPO to maximize a combined reward, $r_{residual} = r_{base} + r_{obj}$, where $r_{obj}$ specifically promotes payload stability.

**C. Residual Module Distillation:**
To enable real-world deployment, where privileged observations are unavailable, a non-privileged *student encoder* is distilled from the privileged *teacher encoder*. This process uses DAgger. The adapter component remains frozen during distillation. The student encoder receives only object-centric observations from a camera (s\_obj), along with proprioceptive data and the goal. The student is trained to align its latent features (z\_t) and refined actions ($\hat{a}_{student,t}$) with those generated by the teacher encoder ($\hat{z}_t$) and the frozen adapter, using a joint loss function that minimizes the squared difference in both latent features and actions.

**Domain Randomization and Environment:**
Extensive domain randomization is applied during training to enhance robustness and sim-to-real transfer. This includes randomizing physical parameters of the robot (mass, friction, CoM offset), tray (mass, friction, restitution), and objects (mass, friction, restitution, radius, height, CoM offset). Control delay and observation noise are introduced to mimic real-world latencies and sensor imperfections. Crucially, an observation delay for object-related observations is implemented to account for perception latency, which is typically higher than proprioceptive sensing. Random push perturbations are applied to both the robot and the object during training to encourage learning recovery behaviors.

**Reward Design:**
The reward function is composed of several terms across three training stages (Pre-train Locomotion, Tray Holding Fine-tune, Residual Object Stabilization).
- **Base Reward ($r_{base}$):** Primarily includes locomotion objectives (velocity tracking, torso stability, low joint velocities/accelerations, action rate penalties, joint limits, energy consumption, arm/waist deviation, foot slide/clearance/impact, undesired contacts). It also includes tray-related terms like maintaining tray-end-effector contact and alignment.
- **Object Reward ($r_{obj}$):** Added during the residual module training stage, this sparse reward focuses on payload stability. It includes terms rewarding the object being upright (aligned with gravity) and maintaining contact with the tray. This design allows the adapter to learn emergent recovery strategies without explicit position or velocity targets for the object. Failure conditions (tray or object falling, bad orientation) are handled with a delayed-timeout mechanism to allow observation of post-failure states and facilitate learning stable locomotion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ReST-RL was conducted through simulation studies in Isaac Lab and real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot (29 Degrees of Freedom), equipped with custom end-effectors and a RealSense D435 camera for object pose estimation using AprilTags. The experiments addressed three key questions: the impact of ReST-RL's components, its robustness and generalization, and its sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

**Ablation Study (Q1: Component Impact):**
- **Performance Comparison:** Across three simulation tasks (Command Track, Push Robot, Push Object), all ReST-RL instantiations (FiLM WB, Action WB, Action JT) demonstrated significant improvements in success rates and reduction in Object Projected Gravity XY Tilt compared to the base policies. Importantly, these improvements in object stability were achieved while maintaining comparable locomotion command tracking errors. This indicates that the hierarchical, residual design effectively enhances payload stability without compromising the robot's underlying gait performance.
- **Architectural Equivalence:** The performance differences between FiLM WB, Action WB, and Action JT were minor, suggesting that the primary benefit arises from the structured residual adaptation and its conditioning on object-related features, rather than the specific mechanism of action modification.
- **End-to-End Limitations:** The End-to-End baseline achieved reasonable performance in the Command Track task but showed significantly lower robustness in Push Robot and Push Object scenarios. An analysis of reward components revealed that while End-to-End policies achieved comparable *total* rewards, a much smaller portion was attributed to object stabilization, indicating that direct, monolithic optimization of both locomotion and object stability objectives is challenging. ReST-RL's separated approach provided a more reliable path to robust stabilization.
- **Observation Delay:** An ablation on observation delay demonstrated that training ReST-RL with delayed object observations consistently led to higher success rates across various perception latency settings, even in zero-latency cases. This indicates that accounting for sensing latency not only improves sim-to-real transfer but also enhances overall stabilization performance.

**Simulation Results (Q2: Robustness and Generalization):**
- **Disturbance Recovery:** Analysis of velocity tracking (Fig. 6) showed that ReST-RL enables the robot to closely follow commanded torso velocities while keeping object velocity tightly coupled. Deviations caused by external pushes (both robot-level and object-level) were quickly corrected without prolonged oscillations, demonstrating robust recovery capabilities.
- **Robustness to Push Disturbances:** ReST-RL maintained high success rates (74.4% for Push Robot, 73.9% for Push Object) across a wide range of push directions and magnitudes (Fig. 7), with only moderate performance degradation under extreme forces. This confirms its robustness against diverse body-level and payload-level perturbations.
- **Generalization to Object Scales:** The policy exhibited high success rates across a broad range of cylinder radius-to-height ratios (Fig. 8), with only a slight decrease for extremely slender objects. This indicates the policy's robustness to variations in payload geometry.

**Real-World Results (Q3: Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
- **Zero-Shot Transfer:** ReST-RL was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating "highly reliable zero-shot sim-to-real generalization" across various objects and external force disturbances.
- **Real-World Disturbance Handling:** In real-world experiments (Fig. 9a, 9b), the robot demonstrated timely whole-body recovery behaviors when subjected to external disturbances, such as being kicked or having the object pushed. The coordination of upper and lower-body joints enabled smooth re-stabilization of the tray and prevention of payload tipping.
- **Generalization to Diverse Objects:** ReST-RL successfully transported a variety of real-world objects, including a coffee cup, a water-filled wine glass, medical tools, and a sealed food container (Fig. 9c, 9d). This generalization was achieved without retraining or fine-tuning, despite variations in mass distributions, geometries, and contact properties, confirming the robustness to real-world perception and dynamics challenges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a critical challenge in humanoid robotics: the stable transport of unsecured objects on a tray during dynamic bipedal locomotion. This problem, termed SteadyTray, represents a significant bottleneck for deploying humanoids in service roles requiring interaction with fragile or unstable items.

The introduction of ReST-RL, a hierarchical reinforcement learning architecture, offers a notable advancement by explicitly decoupling the inherently conflicting objectives of locomotion and payload stabilization. This architectural separation allows the system to maintain a robust gait while concurrently learning precise object stabilization, a capability that monolithic end-to-end learning approaches struggled to achieve. The modular design, coupled with strategic training considerations like delayed object observations, control latency modeling, and extensive domain randomization, significantly enhances the policy's robustness, generalization capabilities, and reliability during sim-to-real transfer. The demonstrated zero-shot generalization across diverse objects and under external disturbances in a real-world Unitree G1 humanoid highlights the practical applicability of this work.

The potential impact of this research extends to various domains:
- **Service Robotics:** Enabling humanoids to reliably perform tasks like serving meals, transporting medical supplies, or assisting in care facilities, thereby addressing labor shortages and enhancing automation in human-centric environments.
- **General Loco-Manipulation:** The ReST-RL framework is adaptable beyond the SteadyTray problem. Its principle of augmenting a pre-trained locomotion policy with a task-specific residual module, conditioned on specialized sensing, could be extended to other complex loco-manipulation skills. Examples include contact-rich manipulation tasks (e.g., door opening, pushing carts) or whole-body adaptation using vision or tactile feedback, all while preserving the integrity of a robust gait.
- **Reinforcement Learning Research:** The findings on the effectiveness of hierarchical structures over monolithic end-to-end learning for tasks with competing objectives, and the importance of specific domain randomization strategies for sim-to-real transfer, contribute to the broader understanding of practical RL system design.

While promising, the paper acknowledges certain limitations and outlines future work. These include the current object encoding being limited to a single object and lacking fine geometric or physical properties, suggesting the integration of advanced pose-estimation models or vision-based RL. Hardware constraints, such as a fixed head-mounted camera with a narrow field of view, also limit versatility in object holding poses. The broader challenge of reward shaping and simulator refinement in complex contact-rich tasks for pure sim-to-real RL remains an area for continued exploration. Nonetheless, this work provides a foundational step towards deploying highly capable humanoid robots in diverse and demanding real-world applications.
