# CityCube: Benchmarking Cross-view Spatial Reasoning on Vision-Language Models in Urban Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> CityCube is a new benchmark designed by researchers from the National University of Defense Technology and Tsinghua University to evaluate cross-view spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models in complex urban environments. It reveals a substantial performance gap between current state-of-the-art VLMs and human intelligence, with VLMs struggling particularly with geometric and multi-view consistency tasks.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language Model (VLM) benchmarks primarily focus on indoor settings or restricted street-level environments, overlooking the unique complexities of open-ended urban spaces.
- Embodied AI agents (e.g., autonomous vehicles, drones) operating in cities lack systematic evaluation tools for their spatial understanding and planning capabilities across diverse, multi-scale urban viewpoints.
- Current VLMs often prioritize semantic understanding, leading to limitations in precise geometric reasoning, perspective taking, and mental simulation required for complex urban navigation and interaction.

## Method

- CityCube introduces a systematic benchmark with ==5,022 multi-view Question-Answer pairs==, integrating real-world (nuScenes, GeoText-1652) and simulated urban imagery (EmbodiedCity, MatrixCity) from over 70 cities.
- The benchmark is structured along ==four dimensions==: Viewpoint Dynamics (rotation, orbit, ego-allocentric, dynamic translation), View Perspectives (ground-level, low-altitude, bird-eye), five cognitive Benchmark Tasks (Mental Reconstruction, Perspective Taking, Spatial Relation, Comprehensive Reasoning, World Knowledge), and Spatial Relation Expressions.
- A human-AI collaborative approach, leveraging ==Gemini-2.5 Pro== with contextual role-playing and geometric reference injection, along with a two-stage human-AI quality control pipeline, ensures high-quality and challenging QA instances.

## Results

- The best-performing proprietary VLM achieved only **54.1%** accuracy on CityCube, compared to human performance of **88.3%**, indicating a substantial **34.2%** gap.
- Fine-tuning on CityCube significantly improves performance; custom 'CityBot' models, even at **2B** parameters, surpassed strong proprietary baselines, with CityBot-8B (CoT) achieving **61.4%** accuracy.
- Error analysis revealed common VLM failure modes including limited sensitivity to small-scale urban entities, failures in egocentric spatial reasoning, insufficient cross-view consistency, and misinterpretation of motion/scale dynamics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current Vision-Language Models exhibit a significant performance gap compared to human spatial reasoning in urban environments, underscoring the fundamental difficulty of cross-view spatial intelligence for AI.
> - VLMs demonstrate a bias towards semantic understanding (e.g., World Knowledge tasks) while struggling with tasks demanding precise geometric reasoning, viewpoint transformation, and multi-view consistency (e.g., Perspective Taking, Mental Reconstruction).
> - The low correlation between human and VLM task difficulty suggests that current models may not be leveraging spatial cues and reasoning strategies in the same way as humans, highlighting a divergence in cognitive approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of CityCube is a significant step in addressing critical limitations within the existing research landscape of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and spatial intelligence benchmarks. Current VLMs are increasingly adopted as the "cognitive backbone" for embodied AI agents, such as autonomous vehicles, drones, and mobile robots, which operate dynamically in the physical world. For these agents to function effectively in complex environments, they require robust spatial understanding, mental simulation, and planning capabilities.

However, as highlighted by the authors, existing benchmarks primarily emphasize either **indoor settings** (e.g., Matterport3D, ScanNet-based benchmarks) or **restricted street-level environments** (e.g., driving datasets like nuScenes). While these have advanced VLM capabilities, they largely overlook the unique and formidable challenges posed by **open-ended urban spaces**. Urban environments are characterized by:
- **Richer semantics:** Dense and repetitive instances (e.g., signage, vehicles, diverse building types) demand sophisticated spatial grounding and disambiguation.
- **Complex geometries:** Intricate 3D structures and road networks necessitate advanced spatial mental reconstruction.
- **Cross-scale viewpoint variations:** Diverse perspectives, ranging from egocentric (ground-level) to top-down (drone, satellite), require rigorous cross-scale reasoning to maintain spatial consistency.

Prior works, such as ViewSpatial, UrbanVideo, and Urbench, have touched upon embodied urban spaces, but they often focus on single or restricted perspectives, lacking the comprehensive multi-view and cross-scale evaluation that real-world urban agents require. CityCube directly addresses this gap by introducing a systematic benchmark that explicitly integrates multi-source imagery (pedestrian, vehicle, drone, satellite) with multi-centric perspective modeling (egocentric, exocentric, allocentric) and supports a rich set of cognitive task types. It fills a crucial void by moving beyond purely geometric measures to encompass relational reasoning, perspective taking, mental simulation, dynamic perception, and world knowledge within the complex urban fabric. This benchmark therefore serves as a vital tool for evaluating whether current VLMs possess the "requisite capabilities for expansive urban spaces," a question that has largely remained unanswered due to the lack of appropriate diagnostic tools.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **introduce CityCube, a systematic and comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the cross-view spatial reasoning (CvSI) capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in urban environments.**

The core motivations behind CityCube are:
1. **Bridging the gap for embodied AI:** Embodied AI agents operating in urban settings (e.g., autonomous vehicles, delivery drones) require advanced spatial intelligence to perceive, understand, and navigate complex real-world scenarios. Current VLMs, while powerful, lack systematic evaluation for these specific urban challenges.
2. **Addressing unique urban complexities:** Existing spatial reasoning benchmarks typically focus on indoor scenes or simpler outdoor environments. Urban spaces present distinct challenges, including:
 - **High semantic density:** Numerous similar objects and detailed information require precise grounding.
 - **Intricate 3D structures:** Complex building facades and road networks demand robust 3D reconstruction from 2D views.
 - **Dynamic and multi-scale viewpoints:** Agents need to reason consistently across widely varying perspectives (e.g., ground-level, aerial, drone-level) and understand how scenes change with different scales and motions.
3. **Comprehensive diagnostic tool:** The authors aim to create a benchmark that not only evaluates overall performance but also provides a fine-grained diagnosis of VLM strengths and weaknesses across various cognitive dimensions of spatial intelligence (e.g., mental reconstruction, perspective taking, world knowledge). This diagnostic capability is crucial for guiding future model development.
4. **Challenging current state-of-the-art:** By designing tasks that are inherently difficult for existing VLMs but intuitive for humans, CityCube intends to reveal the fundamental limitations of current models and spur innovation in spatial reasoning architectures and training methodologies.

In essence, the motivation is to elevate the evaluation of spatial intelligence in VLMs from generalized settings to the highly demanding and context-rich domain of urban environments, thereby accelerating the development of truly intelligent embodied AI for smart cities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CityCube benchmark is meticulously constructed through a multi-stage process involving extensive data collection, sophisticated image pre-processing, AI-assisted QA generation, and rigorous human quality control. Its design is rooted in four key dimensions that systematically model multi-view urban spatial reasoning.

**A. Data Collection and Pre-processing:**
CityCube integrates a substantial collection of 18.1K urban images from both **real-world datasets** and **high-fidelity urban simulators**, spanning over 70 representative cities globally (e.g., Singapore, Boston, Beijing).
- **Real-world Scenes:**
 - **nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020):** Provides ground-level panoramic views from autonomous vehicles. To reduce redundancy from continuous driving, a two-stage temporal subsampling and appearance-based deduplication strategy is applied.
 - **GeoText-1652 (Chu et al., 2024):** Offers satellite and drone imagery with arbitrary viewpoints. A rule-based sampling strategy with manual intervals extracts "orbit views" (small viewpoint changes) and "spiral views" (altitude/scale changes) around landmarks, ensuring diverse multi-view observations.
- **Simulated Scenes:**
 - **EmbodiedCity (Gao et al., 2024) and MatrixCity (Li et al., 2023):** Used to generate high-fidelity virtual images. For EmbodiedCity, manual recording of agent trajectories captures "dynamic translation" views (e.g., zooming in/out, vertical/horizontal movement) and panoramic rotations. MatrixCity, with its precise camera poses, enables geometry-aware cross-view matching to construct reliable aerial-street image pairs, focusing on spatial overlap and viewpoint consistency between egocentric and allocentric perspectives.
All pre-processing ensures that multiple views correspond to the same underlying urban scene, either through temporal alignment, landmark proximity, or geometric projection.

**B. Four Dimensions of the Benchmark:**
CityCube systematically categorizes its multi-image QA instances along four dimensions:
1. **Viewpoint Dynamics (Dimension 1):** Mimics camera movements and observation behaviors:
 - **Rotation:** Observing from a fixed position with changing camera orientation.
 - **Orbit:** Circling a landmark with planar or volumetric movements.
 - **Ego-Allocentric View:** Aligning first-person (egocentric) street views with third-person (allocentric) aerial views.
 - **Dynamic Translation:** Simulating coarse-to-fine movements or scale changes (e.g., zooming from skyscraper to billboard).
2. **View Perspectives (Dimension 2):** Standardizes three acquisition protocols for robustness:
 - **Ground-level Panorama:** From ground vehicles (egocentric).
 - **Low-altitude Oblique Imagery:** From drones (exocentric/allocentric).
 - **Bird-Eye View:** From satellites or high-altitude mapping (allocentric).
3. **Benchmark Tasks (Dimension 3):** Defines five fundamental cognitive categories with 59 subtasks:
 - **Mental Reconstruction (MR):** Inferring spatial transformations between views.
 - **Perspective Taking (PT):** Maintaining spatial consistency and inferring relationship shifts.
 - **Spatial Relation (SR):** Quantifying distance, direction, and topological relations.
 - **Comprehensive Reasoning (CR):** Multi-step inference for hypothesis testing, like hypothetical navigation.
 - **World Knowledge (WK):** Probing urban common-sense (e.g., object geometry, affordance, visibility).
4. **Spatial Relation Expressions (Dimension 4):** Unifies spatial reference frames in QA expressions:
 - **Object-to-Object (Exocentric):** Relationships between external objects.
 - **Camera-to-Object (Egocentric):** Relations between the agent's viewpoint and a target.
 - **Camera-to-Camera:** Relative transformations between two observation positions.

**C. Question-Answer Generation:**
A total of 5,022 meticulously annotated multi-view QA pairs are generated using a human-AI collaborative approach, primarily leveraging Gemini-2.5 Pro.
- **Context Engineering:**
 - **Contextual Role-Playing:** The VLM is prompted to act as an "urban embodiment" or "expert evaluator" with background knowledge of observation behaviors and perspectives (e.g., "Multi-scale Role," "Egocentric–Allocentric Role," "Surrounding Multi-view Role," "Rotation-based Viewpoint Role").
 - **Template Coverage:** 59 distinct templates are provided to ensure comprehensive coverage of CvSI tasks across all structural dimensions.
 - **Geometric Reference Injection:** Ground-truth geometric information (camera position, orientation, image count) is explicitly supplied to enhance answer credibility and mitigate hallucinations.

**D. Quality Control and Refinement:**
A rigorous two-stage refinement pipeline ensures high quality and mitigates construction biases:
1. **Blind Filtering:** Multiple VLMs (six open-source models) are used to answer questions without visual input. Questions correctly answered by all VLMs are discarded as they don't require visual reasoning. Similarly, questions where all VLMs fail are also removed as potentially ill-posed. This ensures the retained questions genuinely demand complex image-grounded reasoning.
2. **Human Verification:** A team of ten expert annotators (master's/doctoral students in urban science) is divided into a "refinement group" and a "verification group." Each QA pair is reviewed by at least one annotator from each group. Annotators verify rationality, authenticity, resolve ambiguities, correct invalid options, and add human-authored reasoning processes. This dual-review and adjudication process ensures high reliability and consistency of annotations.

This systematic methodology ensures that CityCube is a challenging, interpretable, and fine-grained diagnostic benchmark for urban CvSI.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive evaluation of 33 VLMs on CityCube, including state-of-the-art proprietary, mainstream open-source, and specialized spatial models, yielded several critical findings:

1. **Significant Performance Disparity with Humans:** Current VLMs, regardless of their scale or proprietary status, exhibit limited cross-view spatial intelligence. The best-performing proprietary model achieved only **54.1% accuracy**, while humans reached **88.3%**, revealing a substantial gap of **34.2%**. This "consistent gap" suggests that the challenge lies not merely in model architecture or scale, but in the fundamental difficulty of multi-view spatial reasoning in urban environments.
2. **Effectiveness of Fine-tuning on CityCube:** Fine-tuning models on the CityCube training set significantly improves performance. The custom "CityBot" models, even at smaller scales (e.g., CityBot-2B), surpassed strong proprietary baselines, achieving over 60.0% accuracy (CityBot-8B (CoT) achieved 61.4%). The incorporation of human annotations, particularly Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, further boosted performance (+0.6% to +3.6%), emphasizing the value of explicit reasoning guidance.
3. **Reasoning-Oriented VLMs Struggle with Spatial Tasks:** Models specifically designed for "reasoning" (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking) did not consistently outperform non-reasoning counterparts on multi-view spatial tasks like Perspective Taking. This indicates that generic reasoning supervision alone is insufficient to induce robust spatial reasoning, which likely requires explicit modeling of view-dependent geometry and spatial transformations.
4. **CityCube Reveals Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** General-purpose spatial models (e.g., SpaceOM) performed sub-optimally on CityCube's urban embodied scenarios. This validates the benchmark's unique value in evaluating distinct CvSI challenges not covered by standard VQA or existing spatial intelligence benchmarks.
5. **Uneven Difficulty Across Spatial Dimensions:** Models performed relatively better on World Knowledge (WK) and Comprehensive Reasoning (CR) tasks, which primarily rely on semantic understanding and logical inference. Conversely, performance dropped significantly on Perspective Taking (PT), Spatial Relation (SR), and Mental Reconstruction (MR), tasks demanding precise geometric reasoning and viewpoint transformation across multiple views. This suggests a VLM bias towards semantic priors over robust spatial representations.
6. **Task Correlation Analysis:**
 - **Strong Correlations Across Categories:** A Pearson correlation analysis across 59 tasks revealed substantial correlations between CvSI categories, particularly between Mental Reconstruction (MR) and Perspective Taking (PT) (r = 0.536), indicating shared underlying cognitive mechanisms.
 - **Dense Cross-Category Correlations:** 81.4% of high-correlation pairs spanned different CvSI dimensions (e.g., "Behind View" (SR) and "Left-turn Prediction" (CR) with r = 0.954), highlighting the interconnectedness of spatial perception and dynamic reasoning.
 - **Weak Correlation for Metric Estimation:** Tasks involving precise metric estimation (e.g., Height Estimation) showed negligible correlation with most other tasks, suggesting it's a distinct capability.
7. **VLMs Error Analysis:** Qualitative investigation revealed four primary failure modes:
 - **Limited Sensitivity to Small-Scale Urban Entities:** Models often failed to recognize low-salience objects or details in dense urban scenes.
 - **Failures in Egocentric Spatial Reasoning:** Inability to correctly predict changes in relative position/orientation from a shifting first-person viewpoint ("mental rotation").
 - **Insufficient Cross-View Consistency:** Difficulty establishing correct correspondences across different perspectives, leading to misalignments (e.g., matching a street building to a wrong aerial structure).
 - **Misinterpretation of Motion and Scale Dynamics:** Struggles in interpreting camera movements (e.g., forward/backward translation, turns) and the resulting scale changes, hindering scene reconstruction and navigation reasoning.
8. **Human-AI Difficulty Bias:** A weak correlation (r = 0.098, R² = 0.010) between human baseline accuracy and average VLM performance across tasks indicates that what is difficult for VLMs is not necessarily difficult for humans, and vice versa. This divergence confirms that CityCube captures "unique spatial challenges that are non-trivial for current model architectures, despite being intuitive for humans."

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CityCube represents a crucial contribution to the fields of computer vision, natural language processing, and embodied AI, with significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Advancing Embodied Urban Intelligence:** By providing the first systematic benchmark for cross-view spatial reasoning in urban environments, CityCube directly supports the development of more intelligent and robust embodied AI agents (e.g., autonomous vehicles, drones, service robots) that can effectively perceive, understand, and interact with complex cityscapes. Its detailed diagnostic capabilities will guide researchers in building agents with human-like spatial cognition.
2. **Diagnostic Tool for VLM Development:** The benchmark's fine-grained task categories and comprehensive evaluation reveal fundamental limitations of current VLMs, including their struggles with geometric reasoning, cross-view consistency, and dynamic spatial understanding. This diagnosis is invaluable for VLM developers, highlighting specific areas where architectural innovations, training paradigms, and post-training strategies (e.g., explicit spatial supervision, Chain-of-Thought for geometry) are most needed.
3. **Driving Research Beyond Semantic Priors:** The finding that VLMs prioritize semantic priors over robust spatial representations, alongside the uneven performance across different spatial task types, signals a need to move beyond purely semantic-driven approaches. CityCube will encourage research into integrating more explicit 3D geometry, viewpoint transformation mechanisms, and multi-view fusion strategies into VLM architectures.
4. **Foundation for Spatially Grounded Learning:** The benchmark provides a rich dataset and challenging tasks that can serve as a foundation for exploring novel spatially grounded learning paradigms. This includes investigating how models can learn to build coherent 3D mental models from diverse 2D observations, perform mental simulations of movement, and align different reference frames, all crucial for true spatial intelligence.
5. **Revealing Human-AI Cognitive Divergence:** The low correlation between human and AI task difficulty is a profound finding. It suggests that current VLMs might be solving problems using strategies fundamentally different from human spatial cognition, potentially relying on superficial visual cues rather than a deep understanding of spatial relationships. This insight opens new avenues for research into bridging this cognitive gap and developing AI that reasons more intuitively about space.
6. **Standardization and Reproducibility:** CityCube offers a standardized framework for evaluating urban spatial reasoning, allowing for fair comparison and reproducible research across different VLM architectures and training methodologies. The open-source nature of the benchmark will foster community engagement and accelerate progress in this challenging domain.

In conclusion, CityCube is not just another dataset; it is a meticulously crafted diagnostic instrument designed to push the boundaries of spatial intelligence in AI. Its comprehensive scope and challenging nature are poised to stimulate significant advancements in VLMs, ultimately leading to more capable and spatially aware embodied AI systems that can seamlessly operate in the dynamic and complex environments of our urban future.
