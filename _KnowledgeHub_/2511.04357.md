# GraSP-VLA: Graph-based Symbolic Action Representation for Long-Horizon Planning with VLA Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The GraSP-VLA framework enables robots to perform complex, long-horizon tasks by automatically deriving symbolic action representations from human demonstrations using a Multi-Layer Continuous Scene Graph. This neuro-symbolic approach orchestrates existing low-level Vision-Language Action policies, achieving up to 0.4 task accuracy on 6-skill tasks where direct end-to-end VLA fine-tuning yielded 0.0 accuracy.

## Problem

- End-to-end Vision-Language Action (VLA) models lack high-level planning capabilities, limiting their performance on complex, long-horizon robotic tasks.
- Traditional symbolic Action Model Learning (AML) requires extensive human effort to define symbolic representations and domain knowledge, hindering its generalization and scalability.
- A gap exists in effectively integrating low-level perception and control from neural networks with high-level symbolic planning for robust autonomous robotics.

## Method

- A Multi-Layer Continuous ==Scene Graph== (ML-CSG) is introduced, extending scene graph generation with temporal aggregation and state refinement to create a dynamic, persistent symbolic representation.
- An algorithm automatically generates PDDL planning actions (preconditions and effects) by identifying changes in agent-object functional and topological relations within the Continuous Scene Graph during demonstrations.
- A client-server orchestrator maps the automatically generated PDDL actions to a bank of pre-trained low-level Vision-Language Action policies, enabling complex task execution by chaining atomic skills.

## Results

- The SGG backbone achieved R@**50/100** of 31.**4/34**.5, although it demonstrated limitations in detecting fine-grained action-related predicates (e.g., Recall@100 for 'holding' was 0.29).
- Automatic PDDL action generation, especially with informative selection, demonstrated improved detection of action-related predicates from the Continuous Scene Graph, yielding an average Action Accuracy of 0.96 in real-time robotic scenarios.
- GraSP-VLA substantially improved long-horizon task success rates compared to end-to-end VLA fine-tuning, achieving 0.6 vs 0.2 for 2-skill tasks, 0.4 vs 0.1 for 4-skill tasks, and 0.4 vs 0.0 for 6-skill tasks on a SO-101 robot arm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scene Graph Generation, particularly with multi-layered and continuous temporal aggregation, offers a viable, low-cost method for automatic symbolic knowledge extraction for action model learning.
> - Decomposing long-horizon tasks into orchestrated sequences of atomic Vision-Language Action policies, guided by automatically generated PDDL plans, scales more effectively than training a single end-to-end VLA policy.
> - Real-time monitoring of the environment state via the Continuous Scene Graph allows for pre-condition validation and a robust fallback mechanism, enhancing task execution reliability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the evolving landscape of autonomous robotics, specifically addressing the challenge of enabling robots to learn and execute complex, long-horizon tasks from human demonstrations. This field, known as imitation learning, has seen significant advancements, but also faces distinct challenges.

Traditionally, two main paradigms have dominated:
- **End-to-end imitation learning with Vision-Language Action (VLA) models:** These models learn directly from raw sensor data and language commands, offering impressive generalization and ease of deployment. However, the authors highlight a critical limitation: VLAs typically struggle with "long-horizon tasks" due to a lack of high-level symbolic planning capabilities. They often learn reactive policies that perform well for short, atomic actions but degrade significantly when chaining multiple actions or requiring complex reasoning.
- **Symbolic approaches with Action Model Learning (AML):** AML focuses on modeling symbolic representations of actions (preconditions, effects) from observations, which can then be used with classical planning solvers to compose long-horizon tasks. While powerful for planning, the authors point out that traditional AML suffers from high "human labor cost" in designing symbolic representations (predicates and domain knowledge), limiting "generalization and scalability."

GraSP-VLA aims to bridge this "neuro-symbolic gap" by combining the strengths of both approaches while mitigating their weaknesses. It leverages recent advancements in **Scene Graph Generation (SGG)** and **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)** as "low-cost experts" for symbolic knowledge extraction, addressing the human labor bottleneck in AML. SGG, which models visual relationships as grounded graph structures, is identified as a robust, lightweight, and hallucination-free alternative to general VLMs for extracting symbolic representations directly from visual data. The paper claims to be the "first implementation of SGG in the context of Action Model Learning," marking a significant step in this specific intersection.

Furthermore, the work addresses the critical limitation of current SGG models (generating graphs only from still images) by introducing the concept of a **Continuous Scene Graph**, extending the symbolic representation into the temporal domain to capture changes over time. This temporal aggregation is crucial for identifying actions and their effects, moving beyond static scene understanding to dynamic process understanding. By combining this novel symbolic representation with the low-level execution capabilities of pre-trained VLA policies, GraSP-VLA seeks to enable robots to autonomously generate planning domains and orchestrate sequences of atomic VLA behaviors to complete complex tasks, thereby enhancing the performance of VLAs in long-horizon scenarios. This approach represents a shift towards modularity in VLA policy learning, where a collection of simple, atomic skills can be composed, rather than training a single, monolithic policy for an entire complex task.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind GraSP-VLA stems from the dual limitations observed in modern autonomous robotics: the struggle of current Vision-Language Action (VLA) models with long-horizon tasks due to a lack of high-level planning, and the generalization and scalability challenges of traditional symbolic Action Model Learning (AML) due to the heavy reliance on human-designed domain knowledge.

The overarching objective of this research is to **develop a neuro-symbolic framework, GraSP-VLA, that enables autonomous robots to learn and execute long-horizon tasks from single human demonstrations by automatically extracting symbolic action representations and orchestrating low-level VLA policies.**

More specifically, the key objectives and contributions outlined by the authors are:

1. **Develop a new approach to Scene Graph Generation (SGG) by extending the Scene Graph representation to a Multi-Layer Continuous Scene Graph (ML-CSG).** This aims to provide a more robust and temporally consistent symbolic representation of the environment, crucial for understanding dynamic changes during a demonstration.
2. **Propose a new algorithm for the automatic generation of planning domains from the Continuous Scene Graph.** This objective targets overcoming the manual predicate and action definition bottleneck in AML by autonomously inferring PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language) actions (preconditions and effects) directly from observed demonstrations.
3. **Create a Continuous Scene Graph-based orchestrator to decompose complex tasks into sequences of atomic behaviors.** This aims to leverage the extracted symbolic knowledge to intelligently sequence calls to low-level VLA policies, thereby enabling the robot to perform long-horizon tasks by chaining simpler, learned skills.
4. **Implement a client-server execution framework utilizing a bank of pre-trained Vision-Language Action model policies.** This objective focuses on the practical deployment aspect, ensuring efficient and real-time execution of the orchestrated skills by a robotic agent.

In essence, GraSP-VLA seeks to empower robots with a richer, self-generated understanding of their operational environment and the actions performed within it, allowing them to autonomously reason about and execute multi-step tasks that are beyond the current capabilities of end-to-end VLA models alone.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GraSP-VLA is a two-phase neuro-symbolic architecture designed to extract symbolic action descriptions from human demonstrations and then use these descriptions to orchestrate low-level VLA policies for task execution.

**Phase I: Task Modeling (Automatic PDDL Action Extraction)**

1. **Multi-Layer Scene Graph Generation (ML-SGG):**
 - **SGG Backbone:** The REACT model, based on the YOLOv8m architecture for object detection, is used to extract entities (objects with bounding boxes, class labels, and confidence) and their relations from raw visual input.
 - **Multi-Layer Representation:** Unlike traditional SGG, this approach classifies relations into four distinct non-exclusive types (layers): Functional (e.g., `human holding cup`), Topological (e.g., `cup on top of table`), Physical Part-Whole (e.g., `hand part of person`), and Attributive (e.g., `person wearing shirt`). This is achieved by applying an `argmax` function on subsets of predicates for each category, and a fine-tuned Large Language Model (LLM) is used for triplet classification. This multi-layered structure ($G' = \{V, E, L\}$) provides a richer and more organized symbolic representation.

2. **Continuous Scene Graph (CSG) Aggregation:**
 - **Temporal Extension:** The ML-SGG is extended to a Continuous Scene Graph ($G^+_k$) that serves as the robot's internal memory. It aggregates scene graph information over time.
 - **Object Tracking:** A Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) algorithm (OC-SORT) is employed to assign persistent tracking IDs to objects, ensuring nodes in the graph are continuous through time.
 - **State Refinement:** To enhance robustness and filter out false positives, a state refinement mechanism is introduced. It uses a sliding window ($\theta=3$) to validate detected relations, confirming a state change only if the same label is detected multiple times consecutively.
 - **Confidence Weighting:** Each relation is assigned a confidence weight ($\omega_r$), which is updated over time based on repeated detections, with low-confidence relations being removed.

3. **Automatic Action Description Generation (PDDL):**
 - **Agent-Object Distinction:** To facilitate PDDL action generation, nodes in the CSG are categorized as 'agent' (labeled 'person') or 'object' (all others).
 - **Action Definition:** An action is defined as the difference in sets of relations before ($\Pi^-_k$) and after ($\Pi^+_{k+\gamma}$) a graph update, specifically focusing on changes influenced by agents.
 - **Preconditions and Effects Extraction (Algorithm 1):** The core algorithm identifies PDDL actions by observing changes in relations. It iterates through functional relations (e.g., `person holding glass`) at time $k$. For each functional relation, it identifies preconditions (Topological relations involving the object in question *before* the functional relation, within a sliding window $\zeta=10$) and effects (Topological relations involving the object *after* the functional relation, within a sliding window $\zeta=10$). If preconditions and effects differ, a PDDL action is generated, including positive and negative effects (e.g., `not(on(glass, table))` when `on(glass, shelf)` becomes true).

**Phase II: Task Reproduction (Scheduling of VLA Policies)**

1. **Action Orchestrator:**
 - During inference, the Orchestrator continuously extracts PDDL action descriptions from the current CSG state.
 - It maps these PDDL actions to pre-trained low-level skills based on predicate and object involvement (e.g., `pick knife`, `place left`).
 - Before executing a skill, the Orchestrator validates its preconditions against the current CSG state. If satisfied, the corresponding VLA policy is called. If not, the next policy in the decoded sequence is considered.
 - The system operates in an "Open-Ended Imitation Learning" paradigm, where the goal is not explicitly known in advance, allowing for online task composition.

2. **Policy Bank:**
 - A collection of pre-trained low-level Vision-Language Action (VLA) policies (e.g., SmolVLA) is maintained on a skill execution server. These policies are described in natural language (e.g., "Pick up the knife from the table").
 - Communication between the Orchestrator (client) and Policy Bank (server) is synchronous. Policies are loaded in memory for real-time execution. Upon completion, a signal is sent back, and the Orchestrator proceeds to the next action.

The methodology integrates perception (SGG, MOT), symbolic reasoning (CSG, PDDL generation), and low-level control (VLA policies) into a cohesive neuro-symbolic framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were structured into three main parts: SGG backbone evaluation, Continuous Scene Graph and Action Description Generation validation, and full GraSP-VLA architecture evaluation in real-world settings.

1. **Scene Graph Generation Evaluation (on IndoorVG dataset):**
 - The REACT SGG model achieved R@50/100 of 31.4/34.5 and mR@50/100 of 17.5/19.7, with a latency of 26.6 ms.
 - A significant finding was the SGG model's **struggle with fine-grained action-related predicates** (e.g., Recall@100 for 'holding' was 0.29), performing better on coarse-grained ones like 'next to' or 'on'. This is acknowledged as a known issue in the SGG community and represents a limitation impacting subsequent stages.

2. **Continuous Scene Graph & Action Descriptions (on DAHLIA dataset):**
 - **Baseline (without Informative Selection):** Showed high Recall (0.69) but a very low number of identified actions (average 2.6 per video). This confirmed the SGG model's difficulty in detecting action-related predicates.
 - **With Informative Selection:** Applying Informative Selection (re-weighting predictions by semantic importance) significantly increased the number of generated actions, demonstrating improved detection of action-related predicates. However, it also led to a higher number of False Positives, likely due to incorrect/missing object detections and the SGG's low relation prediction recall. Overall Recall was 0.51, indicating a trade-off between quantity and precision of detected actions.

3. **Evaluation of GraSP-VLA (Real-world with SO-101 robot arm):**
 - **Atomic VLA Policy Accuracy:** SmolVLA policies were fine-tuned for 6 atomic behaviors (pick knife, fork, spoon; place left, right, inside). Placing policies achieved 100% accuracy, while picking policies had lower success rates (0.8 for knife, 0.6 for fork, 0.7 for spoon), attributed to fewer training episodes for individual picking actions.
 - **GraSP-VLA Performance vs. Full Fine-Tuning:**
 - **Action Accuracy (PDDL generation in real-time):** GraSP-VLA achieved an average Action Accuracy of 0.96 (1.0 for 2 and 4 skills, 0.9 for 6 skills), a significant improvement over previous experiments, likely due to a simpler, controlled scenario and the use of Qualitative Spatial Reasoning (QSR) for relations instead of the SGG relation head.
 - **Overall Task Accuracy:** GraSP-VLA demonstrated a much higher success rate in chaining skills compared to directly fine-tuning SmolVLA for the entire long-horizon task. For 2 skills, GraSP-VLA achieved 0.6 accuracy vs. 0.2 for full fine-tuning. For 4 skills, it was 0.4 vs. 0.1, and for 6 skills, 0.4 vs. 0.0.
 - This conclusively shows that GraSP-VLA's modular approach, by decomposing tasks and orchestrating atomic VLA policies, **substantially improves performance on long-horizon tasks,** where end-to-end VLA models fail rapidly with increasing task length and limited demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GraSP-VLA framework represents a significant advancement in neuro-symbolic robotics, with the potential to impact several key areas:

1. **Enabling Long-Horizon Task Learning:** The most profound impact of GraSP-VLA is its demonstrated ability to tackle long-horizon tasks, which is a major bottleneck for current end-to-end Vision-Language Action (VLA) models. By orchestrating a sequence of atomic VLA policies based on symbolic planning, GraSP-VLA allows robots to perform complex multi-step tasks that would otherwise be intractable or require vast amounts of demonstrations for direct VLA training. This unlocks new possibilities for deploying robots in more complex real-world scenarios requiring sequential decision-making.

2. **Reduced Reliance on Human Expert Knowledge:** GraSP-VLA significantly reduces the human labor traditionally required for Action Model Learning (AML). By automatically generating planning domains (PDDL actions) from visual observations using Continuous Scene Graphs, it mitigates the need for human experts to manually define predicates and actions. This has a direct impact on the scalability and generalization of symbolic AI in robotics.

3. **Novel Neuro-Symbolic Integration:** The work provides a strong example of effective neuro-symbolic integration, leveraging the strengths of deep learning (SGG for perception, VLA for low-level control) with symbolic reasoning (Continuous Scene Graphs for world representation, PDDL for planning). This hybrid approach offers a robust path forward for developing more intelligent and capable robotic systems. The introduction of the Multi-Layer Continuous Scene Graph as a dynamic, persistent internal memory is a particularly novel and impactful contribution to environmental representation.

4. **Enhanced Flexibility and Generalization:**
 - **Single-Shot Learning of New Tasks:** GraSP-VLA can potentially learn a new complex task from a single demonstration, provided its constituent atomic skills are already present in the policy bank. This dramatically improves the efficiency of robot skill acquisition.
 - **Task Decomposition:** The framework's ability to decompose tasks into unitary behaviors makes it inherently more flexible. Instead of retraining for every new complex task, new tasks can be learned by composing existing skills, mirroring how humans often approach novel problems.

5. **Improved Robustness through Monitoring:** The Continuous Scene Graph (CSG) allows for real-time monitoring of the environment state. As highlighted in the discussion, this enables a "fallback mechanism": if the expected effects of a VLA policy (as defined by the PDDL action) are not observed in the CSG after execution, the skill can be re-called. This significantly enhances the robustness and reliability of robot task execution in dynamic and uncertain environments.

6. **Extraction of Ontologies and World Knowledge:** Beyond action descriptions, the paper demonstrates the potential of GraSP-VLA to infer higher-level knowledge, such as object ontologies (e.g., distinguishing "movable" from "static" objects) by analyzing patterns of change in the Topological layer of the CSG. This opens avenues for robots to build a more comprehensive and meaningful understanding of their environment autonomously.

While acknowledging limitations related to SGG accuracy, the overall significance of GraSP-VLA lies in its innovative solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics. Its potential impact includes accelerating the development of truly autonomous robots capable of complex reasoning and interaction in unstructured environments, reducing development costs, and making advanced robotics more accessible. The proposed future work of infusing latent graph representations into VLA models also indicates a promising direction for even tighter integration and improved performance of the perception and action components.
