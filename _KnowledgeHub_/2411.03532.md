# A Behavior Architecture for Fast Humanoid Robot Door Traversals

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Florida Institute for Human and Machine Cognition (IHMC) developed an integrated behavior architecture enabling their Nadia humanoid robot to perform rapid door traversals in 20-30 seconds. This framework combines GPU-accelerated perception, a tree-based behavior coordination system with layered concurrency, and a momentum-based whole-body controller to efficiently navigate diverse door types.

## Problem

- A lack of research on bipedal humanoid robots performing fast, whole-body loco-manipulation tasks like door traversals.
- Existing methods for door interaction by humanoids were slow and often required significant human intervention, hindering practical deployment.
- Developing robust perception for door hardware (handles, knobs) in unstructured environments was a challenge.

## Method

- An integrated behavior architecture using a tree-based runtime with layered concurrency, allowing actions to overlap and execute as soon as dependencies are met.
- A novel perception system combining YOLOv8 for object detection and segmentation with GPU-accelerated planar region extraction and stability filtering for robust door hardware identification.
- A sophisticated operator interface based on ==Coactive Design== principles, enabling rapid, 'on-the-fly' authoring and modification of behaviors, significantly accelerating development and iteration.

## Results

- The Nadia humanoid robot successfully traversed various door types (push/pull, different mechanisms) in 20-30 seconds, achieving speeds multiple times faster than prior IHMC methods.
- The system demonstrated the ability to execute three consecutive door traversals and integrate debris clearing behaviors, showcasing robustness and complex task integration.
- The novel perception system reliably detected and tracked four distinct door opening mechanisms (push bar, lever handle, knob, pull handle) outside of controlled lab environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Layered concurrency in behavior execution is crucial for dramatically increasing the speed of complex loco-manipulation tasks in humanoids.
> - A modular and reusable action primitive library, combined with a flexible tree-based runtime, facilitates the rapid development and adaptation of behaviors for various, previously unencountered door types.
> - The integration of advanced hardware, robust whole-body control, and accurate GPU-accelerated perception is essential for achieving high performance in real-world humanoid tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose humanoid robots capable of operating in human-designed environments is a grand challenge in robotics. One critical aspect of this challenge is enabling robots to interact with common architectural features, such as doors. This paper identifies a significant gap in the existing literature: the ability of *bipedal humanoid robots* to *quickly and effectively* perform whole-body loco-manipulation tasks, specifically door traversals. While there is a substantial body of research on door traversals by *wheeled-base robots with arms*, the unique mobility and balance constraints of bipedal humanoids present fundamentally different and more complex planning and control problems. The authors explicitly state, "This is currently no body of research available that explores door traversals with bipedal humanoid robots," highlighting their contribution as filling a crucial void.

The work builds upon and integrates several state-of-the-art concepts from different sub-fields of robotics:
- **Behavior Coordination:** The paper leverages and extends established paradigms like **Affordance Templates (ATs)**, which IHMC has historically contributed to and utilized, for defining general action plans for high-level tasks. It also adopts **Behavior Trees (BTs)**, a popular model for orchestrating complex behaviors, but distinguishes its application by focusing on *loco-manipulation in bipedal humanoids*, an area where BTs have not been extensively explored. The architecture further incorporates **Coactive Design (CD)** principles, an IHMC-developed methodology for structuring human-machine interdependence, which informed the design of their operator interface and interaction strategies.
- **Perception:** The paper integrates modern computer vision techniques, specifically **YOLO-based object detection** and **planar region extraction**, which have shown promise in detecting and localizing environmental features like doors and handles. Their novel combination and refinement allow for robust perception outside controlled lab environments.
- **Whole-Body Control:** The foundation of the robot's physical capabilities rests on IHMC's advanced **momentum-based whole-body control framework** and **dynamic walking algorithms**, which enable stable bipedal locomotion and simultaneous manipulation. This established low-level control expertise is critical for the reliable execution of complex behaviors.
- **Hardware Design:** The paper implicitly contributes to the broader landscape by demonstrating the capabilities of the Nadia humanoid robot, an advanced platform developed by IHMC and Boardwalk Robotics, showcasing the potential of next-generation hardware in facilitating faster, more robust robotic actions.

By integrating these diverse components—from advanced hardware and low-level control to sophisticated perception and high-level behavior coordination—and applying them to a previously under-researched problem for humanoids, this work significantly pushes the boundaries of autonomous and semi-autonomous humanoid robot capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research stems from the envisioned role of humanoid robots as "squad mates in urban operations and other domains." In such scenarios, doors are "ubiquitous in many environment domains," yet the capability for robots to "quickly and effectively perform useful whole-body loco-manipulation tasks automatically" to traverse them is severely lacking in the literature. The authors emphasize that the humanoid form factor is "uniquely suited" for such operations in human-designed spaces.

Given this context, the key objectives of this paper are:
1. **Develop a Behavior Architecture:** To present a comprehensive software architecture that enables humanoid robots to navigate and interact with various types of doors.
2. **Achieve Fast Door Traversals:** The central aim is to demonstrate and enable *rapid* door traversals, significantly improving upon existing methods and moving towards human-like operational speeds.
3. **Integrate State-of-the-Art Components:** To seamlessly combine GPU-accelerated perception, an interactive tree-based behavior coordination system, and a robust whole-body motion and walking controller into a cohesive system.
4. **Enable Rapid Behavior Authoring and Reusability:** To create a system that facilitates quick generation of behaviors for novel or unseen door types and promotes the reusability of these authored behaviors.
5. **Develop Robust Perception:** To implement a novel perception system capable of accurately detecting door opening mechanisms (handles, knobs, push bars) outside of controlled laboratory environments.
6. **Analyze Human-Robot Interdependence:** To formally explore and document the collaboration between human operators and the robotic system using Interdependence Analysis (IA) charts, a key aspect of IHMC's Coactive Design methodology.
7. **Demonstrate Real-World Performance:** To present and discuss concrete results from real robot experiments of fast door traversals on the Nadia humanoid robot.

The authors explicitly list three novel contributions: (1) a system for operators to compose *concurrent sequences* of loco-manipulation action primitives to *increase speed*; (2) an integrated behavior framework for *reusable, fast, and resilient* whole-body loco-manipulation behaviors for *unencountered door types*; and (3) a novel detection system combining YOLO-based object detection and segmentation, depth correspondence, and rapid planar region extraction for robust door hardware detection *outside a lab environment*. These objectives are geared towards creating practical, deployable humanoid robotics systems that can adapt to diverse and dynamic human environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented is a holistic, integrated system spanning three major domains: behavior coordination, perception, and whole-body control, all managed through an advanced operator interface.

**Behavior Coordination:**
The core of the behavior system is a **tree-based runtime** that orchestrates robot actions. Unlike conventional Behavior Trees (BTs) with strict success/failure return types, this implementation offers a more flexible structure where all nodes are updated each tick. Logical nodes enable reactive decision-making based on environmental queries (from perceived object frames), allowing for re-execution of sub-sequences or invocation of alternative strategies upon failure. A critical feature for achieving speed is **layered concurrency**, where action primitives include an "execute after" field. This allows actions to commence as soon as their dependencies are met, overlapping execution and eliminating unnecessary pauses. Behaviors are stored in hierarchical JSON files, enabling real-time authoring, modification, and execution ("on the fly"), significantly accelerating the development and iteration cycle.

**Action Primitives:**
These are the leaf nodes in the behavior tree, representing fundamental robot capabilities and acting as the interface to the low-level controller. They include:
- **Footstep Plan:** Manages sequences of footsteps, either pre-specified or planned online using IHMC's footstep planner. Features like "turn-walk-turn" mode and snapping goal stances to the ground enhance robustness.
- **Arm Trajectory:** Controls arm and hand movements to preset joint angles or through inverse kinematics (IK) solutions for frame-relative hand poses. Interactive 3D gizmos aid in authoring these poses.
- **Screw Primitive:** A parameterized helical trajectory, inspired by existing work, designed for actions like turning door handles or swinging door panels. It is "grasp-invariant," adapting to the current hand pose.
- **Chest and Pelvis Pose Trajectories:** These coordinate with arm movements to extend reachability and enhance whole-body motion.
- **Hand Control:** Manages gripper actions, including finger trajectories, preset configurations (open/closed), and adjustable grip strength.
Crucially, all action primitives implement entry and exit conditions to ensure stability and reusability, operating within task-relative frames.

**Whole Body Control:**
The behavior layer interfaces with IHMC's well-established **momentum-based control framework**, which employs a quadratic program (QP) to reconcile motion tasks and maintain stability. This controller handles foot-ground contact, force-limited grasping, and executes jointspace and spatial acceleration objectives. The dynamic walking behavior relies on Instantaneous Capture Point (ICP) and Centroidal Moment Pivot (CMP) for balance, with foot swings adaptable to terrain. State estimation uses a combination of kinematic foot-ground contact proprioception and an IMU, with inherent awareness of potential drift.

**Operator Interface:**
The system includes a sophisticated operator interface, built on **Coactive Design (CD)** principles, which features a digital twin of the robot and environment, teleoperation controls, and a behavior management interface. The "online creation, modification, and execution" capability is a highlight, enabling rapid iteration without redeployment. It provides real-time previews of planned motions (transparent robot graphics) and live tracking plots and log messages to monitor execution progress and identify issues. Interdependence Analysis (IA) charts are used to explicitly model and optimize human-robot team performance.

**Perception System (Door Handle Detection):**
A novel 8-step process detects and tracks door opening mechanisms (push bars, lever handles, knobs, pull handles) using a ZED 2i stereo camera:
1. Color data from the camera is processed by a **YOLOv8** neural network (retrained with synthetic and real data) to generate a segmentation mask of the mechanism.
2. The segmentation mask is combined with depth data to calculate the **centroid** of the mechanism, providing its position.
3. A **planar region extractor** (GPU-accelerated, from previous IHMC work) identifies the door panel from depth data.
4. The mechanism's orientation is derived from the normal of the associated door panel plane.
5. A **stability detection filter** alpha-filters the centroid and requires consistent detection (YOLO and planar region association) over a time window to ensure a stable and reliable pose estimate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling results demonstrating the successful implementation and effectiveness of their behavior architecture on the Nadia humanoid robot.

**Demonstrated Capabilities:**
- The system successfully executed **six variations of door traversals**, encompassing different door types (push/pull), hinge sides (left/right), and opening mechanisms (knob, push bar, lever handle). This highlights the versatility and adaptability of the architecture.
- **Videos** showcasing these traversals on the Nadia humanoid robot are provided online, offering concrete evidence of the system's performance in real-world scenarios.
- A "right pull lever door traversal" (Figure 1) is detailed, illustrating the sequence of concurrent actions (approach, unlatch and open, walk through).
- The system demonstrated advanced composability by executing **three consecutive door traversals** (push bar, push knob, pull lever handle) in a single, continuous autonomous behavior (Figure 22), indicating robustness and higher-level task integration.
- A **debris clearing behavior** (e.g., pushing a recycling bin) was successfully integrated into a door traversal sequence (Figure 23), showcasing the robot's ability to handle obstacles and adapt to cluttered environments.

**Performance Metrics:**
- The most significant finding is the **speed of door traversals**, which execute on the timescale of **tens of seconds (20-30 seconds)**. This is a dramatic improvement, described as "many times faster than the prior methods used by the IHMC Robotics lab," including their DRC "Best in Door" performance.
- **Push door behaviors** were the fastest, completing in less than 20 seconds, due to the robot's ability to maintain forward progress.
- **Pull door behaviors** took approximately 10 seconds longer, as they require the robot to stop, move out of the door's swing path, open it fully, and then proceed.
- Analysis of the **Center of Mass (CoM)** and **Instantaneous Capture Point (ICP)** trajectories (Figure 21) during traversals with spring-loaded door closers confirmed the whole-body controller's ability to maintain balance despite significant external disturbances.
- The **perception system** demonstrated its capability to detect and track four distinct door opening mechanisms (push bar, lever handle, knob, hands-free pull handle) reliably outside of laboratory settings.
- The **rapid authoring capabilities** allowed the team to create over twenty diverse loco-manipulation behaviors (including door traversals and debris clearing) on the real robot within a single year.

These results, particularly the achieved traversal speeds, signify a major leap forward in the practical application of humanoid robots for complex loco-manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a substantial advancement in the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in practical task execution and human-robot teaming. Its primary significance lies in achieving **unprecedented speed for bipedal humanoid robot door traversals**, demonstrating completion times of 20-30 seconds, which the authors claim is "the fastest presented such system in the literature." This dramatic increase in speed, multiple times faster than previous IHMC methods (including their award-winning DRC performance), is attributed to several factors:
- **Advanced Hardware:** The Nadia humanoid robot, with its high maximum joint velocities and robust, high-torque, and compliant cycloidal drive electric arms, enables faster locomotion and manipulation.
- **Concurrent Action Execution:** The novel implementation of layered concurrency, where actions overlap once dependencies are met, significantly reduces idle time and doubles execution speed.
- **Rapid Iteration through Coactive Design:** The operator interface, designed using Coactive Design principles, facilitates "on the fly" authoring and modification, allowing researchers to quickly test, iterate, and optimize behaviors, leading to faster strategies.
- **GPU-Accelerated Perception:** The integrated perception system swiftly and accurately identifies door features, minimizing pauses for environmental understanding.

The **integrated architecture** presented here serves as a powerful framework for developing "fast, resilient, and reusable loco-manipulation behaviors" for humanoids. Its ability to support rapid authoring for unseen door types is crucial for deploying robots in varied, unstructured environments. The formal analysis of human-robot interdependence through IA charts also contributes to a deeper understanding of designing effective human-robot teams.

**Potential Impact:**
The immediate impact is a significant step towards realizing humanoid robots as **truly useful general-purpose agents** in human-centric environments. Faster door traversals are not merely a benchmark; they are critical for applications such as:
- **Urban Operations and Disaster Response:** Enabling robots to keep pace with human teams in complex, confined, and dynamic environments.
- **Space Exploration:** Assisting astronauts or operating autonomously within modules, habitats, and spacecraft, which often involve doors and confined passages.
- **Industrial and Service Robotics:** Performing tasks in factories, warehouses, or public spaces designed for human interaction.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
Despite the significant achievements, the authors candidly acknowledge limitations and propose directions for future work:
- **Further Speed Optimization:** Human speeds are still a target. Future work aims to eliminate unnecessary footsteps and pauses, increase maximum walking speed, and blend footstep plans between actions.
- **Force- and Impact-Based Control:** Transitioning from position-space trajectory definitions to force- and impact-based actions could simplify definitions, increase disturbance robustness, and leverage proprioceptive perception, especially for tasks involving constrained motions like operating doors.
- **Increased Autonomy and Reduced Human Dependency:** While rapid authoring is powerful, the reliance on a human operator for behavior generation is still high. Future efforts will integrate more advanced planners (e.g., stance, grasp, collision-free motion planning) and potentially reinforcement learning to reduce the cognitive burden on the operator.
- **Enhanced Perception:** The current system assumes doors are initially closed. Detecting door frames and handling doors in various initial states (e.g., ajar) is a necessary next step, requiring additional perception algorithms and planning heuristics.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust, integrated, and empirically validated behavior architecture that dramatically enhances the speed and versatility of humanoid robots in performing complex loco-manipulation tasks like door traversals. It lays critical groundwork for future autonomous and semi-autonomous humanoid applications in diverse real-world settings, solidifying IHMC's position at the forefront of humanoid robotics research.
