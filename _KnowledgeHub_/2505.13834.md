# Toward Real-World Cooperative and Competitive Soccer with Quadrupedal Robot Teams

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley and Tsinghua University developed a fully learning-based, decentralized multi-quadruped robotic system capable of playing cooperative and competitive soccer in the real world, leveraging only onboard sensors and computation. The system achieved a 98.3% win rate against a static opponent in simulation and demonstrated complex emergent team strategies, including coordinated passing and dynamic role allocation, in real-world robot-robot and robot-human matches.

## Problem

- Extending advanced single-robot behaviors to multi-agent legged robot systems is challenging due to complex dynamics, high-dimensional control, and strategic reasoning.
- Deploying fully learning-based, competitive, and cooperative soccer systems on real legged robots is difficult due to the need for precise motor skills for dynamic ball interaction.
- Such systems also require long-horizon strategic reasoning and decentralized execution without relying on external infrastructure or predefined high-level rules.

## Method

- A hierarchical ==Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)== framework was developed, decoupling the soccer policy into a library of low-level motor skills (Walk, Dribble, Kick) and a high-level scheduler.
- ==Fictitious Self-Play (FSP)== was employed for co-evolutionary training, allowing attacker and defender policies to alternately adapt to diverse opponent populations.
- The learned policies were deployed zero-shot on real Unitree Go1 quadruped platforms, utilizing onboard LiDAR for perception and real-time ==LiDAR-inertial odometry== for decentralized localization.

## Results

- Achieved the first fully decentralized multi-quadruped robotic system capable of playing cooperative and competitive soccer in the real world, relying solely on onboard sensing and computation.
- The hierarchical method demonstrated a **98.3%** win rate against a static opponent in simulation, substantially outperforming a flat end-to-end policy which achieved **37.5%**.
- Real-world experiments showcased emergent cooperative play (e.g., passing, scoring) and competitive behaviors (e.g., interceptions, counterattacks) in **2v1** robot-robot and robot-human scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The hierarchical MARL framework provides significantly more stable and efficient learning for complex multi-robot tasks compared to end-to-end approaches.
> - Fictitious Self-Play effectively induces diverse and sophisticated emergent cooperative and competitive behaviors in adversarial multi-agent settings, preventing policy collapse to local optima.
> - Complex multi-agent strategies, such as coordinated passing and dynamic role allocation, can emerge from sparse task-driven rewards without explicit coordination mechanisms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) have enhanced the capabilities of single legged robots, allowing them to perform complex behaviors such as agile locomotion, dynamic manipulation, and long-horizon navigation. However, extending these capabilities to multi-agent systems, particularly with legged robots, presents significant challenges due to the inherent nonlinear dynamics, high-dimensional control spaces, and the requirement for strategic reasoning over extended time horizons.

Robot soccer serves as a prominent benchmark for multi-agent robotic systems, integrating real-time control, decentralized decision-making, and long-horizon strategic planning. Prior research in robot soccer has predominantly adopted rule-based pipelines or focused on simplified 1v1 game scenarios. Many learning-based approaches have been confined to simulation, demonstrating capabilities like emergent passing, zone defense, and role assignment in virtual environments for various team sizes. While single-robot skills such as dribbling, kicking, and goalkeeping have seen considerable development using learning-based control, real-world deployments of cooperative and competitive multi-agent soccer systems, especially with legged robots, remain limited. Existing real-world implementations typically involve 1v1 matches under controlled sensing conditions, often relying on external motion capture systems or vision-based tracking with restricted fields of view. Furthermore, while hierarchical frameworks have been explored to decompose complex tasks into reusable sub-policies, these often depend on manually crafted high-level strategies, requiring expert heuristics and extensive tuning.

This work addresses a significant gap in the current research by demonstrating a fully learning-based, competitive, and cooperative soccer system on real legged robots. It specifically tackles the challenges of precise motor control for ball manipulation in dynamic environments and the need for decentralized, long-horizon strategic reasoning in multi-agent settings, without relying on external infrastructure or predefined high-level rules. By employing a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework and co-evolutionary training, the paper aims to enable autonomous and adaptive team play in real-world scenarios, thereby pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in multi-legged robot coordination.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to achieve coordinated teamwork among legged robots, requiring both fine-grained locomotion control and long-horizon strategic decision-making. Robot soccer is chosen as a compelling testbed due to its combination of dynamic, competitive, and multi-agent interactions.

The motivation for this work stems from two main difficulties in deploying fully learning-based, competitive, and cooperative soccer systems on real legged robots:

1. **Precise Motor Skills and Dynamic Ball Interaction:** Legged robots must execute precise motor skills, such as dribbling and kicking, while maintaining balance and responding to the highly dynamic motion of the ball. These skills demand high-frequency joint-level control and are sensitive to contact dynamics, where even minor errors can lead to instability, collisions, or loss of ball control.
2. **Long-Horizon Strategic Reasoning and Decentralized Execution:** Coordinated team play necessitates long-horizon strategic reasoning under decentralized execution to ensure robustness in real-world systems. Without a centralized coordinator, each robot must infer the intentions of teammates and opponents in a dynamically changing environment, which complicates coordination and strategic planning.

To address these challenges, the authors propose a hierarchical MARL framework with the following key objectives:

- **Develop a Novel Hierarchical Framework:** The framework aims to compose multiple learned low-level legged locomotion skills (walking, dribbling, kicking) with a high-level strategic planning policy. This modular design is intended to facilitate fully learned cooperative and competitive behaviors for robot soccer, enhancing stability and sample efficiency compared to end-to-end approaches. The use of Fictitious Self-Play (FSP) is incorporated to promote strategic policy evolution in adversarial settings.
- **Systematically Analyze Emergent Multi-Agent Behaviors:** The research seeks to understand how the proposed learning method, particularly FSP, induces diverse and sophisticated emergent behaviors in multi-agent cooperation and competition, such as coordinated passing, interception, and dynamic role allocation.
- **Demonstrate Real-World Deployment of a Decentralized Multi-Quadruped System:** A crucial objective is to achieve the first fully decentralized multi-quadruped robotic system capable of playing soccer in the real world. This includes supporting both robot-robot and robot-human games without relying on external hardware infrastructure (e.g., motion capture systems), instead using only onboard proprioception and decentralized localization.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to advance the capabilities of multi-legged robotic systems in complex, dynamic, and adversarial environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a hierarchical Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) framework to enable autonomous and decentralized quadruped robot soccer. This framework decouples the soccer policy into a library of reusable low-level motor skills and a high-level scheduler that composes these skills online.

#### 3.1. Simulation Environment and Robot Platform

- **Simulator:** All policies are trained in the GPU-accelerated IsaacGym simulator.
- **Robot Platform:** The learned policies are deployed zero-shot on real-world Unitree Go1 quadruped platforms.
- **Game Setup:** The study primarily focuses on 2v1 settings, with additional validation in 1v1 and 2v2 configurations. Robots are divided into Attackers (scoring) and Defenders (defending, counterattacking). Games terminate upon a goal, ball out of bounds, or timeout. A restricted zone along field boundaries prevents robots from exiting.

#### 3.2. Low-Level Skill Control Policies

A set of three highly dynamic low-level skills—Walk, Dribble, and Kick—are trained to achieve robust locomotion and ball interaction. A "Receive" skill was initially explored but later removed due to observed inefficiency.

- **Unified Command:** All skills share a two-dimensional velocity command `c = (vx, vy)` defined in the world frame.
 - **Walk:** `c` specifies desired base velocity; the robot is trained to align its yaw with the velocity direction.
 - **Dribble:** `c` represents the target velocity of the ball.
 - **Kick:** `c` is a unit vector determining the desired post-kick ball velocity direction.
- **Execution:** Each skill is implemented as a neural network policy `πθ(a | o, c)` outputting desired joint positions at 50 Hz. These positions are tracked by proportional-derivative (PD) controllers operating at 200 Hz.
- **Training:** Model-free reinforcement learning using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
 - **Observations:** Low-level policies receive a sequence of 25 recent observation frames, including proprioceptive data (base linear/angular velocity, projected gravity, joint positions/velocities), task-relevant features (forward vector, relative ball position, velocity command), and previous joint actions. An auxiliary estimator network predicts privileged information (e.g., motor stiffness, ball state, environment friction) in the simulator.
 - **Reward Shaping:** Standard rewards for Walk and Dribble. For Kick, a state-conditioned multi-stage reward design is used, dividing behavior into "Pursue & Strike" and "Hold" stages to encourage kicking with sufficient force and then stabilizing.

#### 3.3. High-Level Multi-Agent Strategy Policy

Building on the low-level skills, a decentralized high-level policy is trained to compose these skills for cooperative and competitive play.

- **Action Space:** The high-level policy selects a skill type (Walk, Dribble, Kick, Stop) and a 2D command. The continuous command `c` is discretized into eight equally spaced unit vectors, with magnitude pre-specified per skill.
- **Decision Frequency:** Decisions are made every 10 low-level steps (0.2 seconds, 5 Hz).
- **Architecture:** The policy uses a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) backbone to maintain a hidden state for long-term memory. Two Softmax heads independently parameterize categorical distributions over skill primitives and directions.
- **Observations:** The high-level policy receives only the current proprioceptive state and egocentric relative positions of teammates, opponents, the ball, and goals.
- **Policy Sharing:** All agents on the same team share a common high-level policy network.
- **Reward Structure:** Combines sparse outcome rewards (positive for scoring, negative for conceding) with dense auxiliary rewards for safe and purposeful behaviors (minimizing collisions, avoiding out-of-bounds, promoting forward ball progression). Crucially, no explicit reward is given for coordinated behaviors; cooperation emerges implicitly.
- **Transition Rules:** Rule-based transitions convert high-level actions into low-level commands, ensuring context-appropriate skill activation (e.g., Dribbling/Kicking only when close to the ball, mapping to walking or stopping otherwise, collision avoidance logic).

#### 3.4. Co-Evolution of Teams via Fictitious Self-Play (FSP)

To address the mixed cooperative-competitive nature of soccer and promote robust strategy learning, an FSP framework is implemented.

- **Mechanism:** Attacker and defender policies are trained alternately using Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO). When one side is updating, the opponent population pool is frozen.
- **Procedure:** Training begins with attackers. The initial defender population comprises random and a manually crafted "ball-chasing" policy. After each evaluation phase, if the focal team's score (win rate + 0.5 * draw rate) exceeds a predefined threshold, the current policy snapshot is added to its respective population. During training, each environment samples an opponent policy uniformly from the population, encouraging continuous adaptation and preventing collapse to local optima.

#### 3.5. Real-World System Details

- **Hardware:** Unitree Go1 robots equipped with Livox MID-360 LiDAR and NVIDIA Orin NX onboard computers.
- **Perception & Localization:**
 - Ball detection: Achieved by filtering high-intensity LiDAR points from the ball's reflective surface.
 - Human detection: Filtering points based on height.
 - Self-localization: Real-time LiDAR-inertial odometry (FAST-LIO) within a pre-mapped environment, allowing independent global pose estimation.
- **Communication:** Minimal pose information is broadcast among agents via a wireless network for mutual localization.
- **Decentralized Control:** Each robot constructs its observation, feeds it into its learned policy (integrating high-level strategy and low-level skills), and executes joint position commands via its onboard microcontroller unit. This setup enables fully autonomous, decentralized play without external infrastructure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed hierarchical MARL framework, including ablation studies, analysis of emergent behaviors, and real-world deployment.

#### 4.1. Ablation Study

- **Hierarchical vs. End-to-End Policy:**
 - A comparison between the proposed hierarchical method ("Ours") and a flat, end-to-end policy ("End2End") showed that "Ours" achieved significantly more stable and coordinated ball control. The "End2End" policy exhibited erratic ball motions and frequent out-of-bounds events. Quantitatively, against a static opponent, "Ours" achieved a 98.3% win rate compared to 37.5% for "End2End" over 1000 episodes, indicating the effectiveness of skill abstraction.
- **Contribution of Low-Level Skills:**
 - Ablating the low-level skills (Walk-only, Walk+Dribble, Ours with Walk+Dribble+Kick) demonstrated that skill diversity is crucial for efficient learning. "Walk+Dribble" improved over "Walk" in ball control, but the absence of a kicking skill severely limited offensive capabilities, leading to higher training variance and occasional failure to score. "Ours," incorporating all three skills, converged significantly faster to the predefined score threshold (s\_thres\_att = 0.88), underscoring the importance of a comprehensive skill set.

#### 4.2. Policy Evolution with Fictitious Self-Play

- **Co-Evolution in 1v1:** The FSP training regime induced co-evolutionary dynamics. Attackers evolved from naive shooting to sophisticated tactics like rebounding and adaptive shooting as defenders improved. Simultaneously, defenders learned dynamic interceptions and multi-stage defensive strategies.
- **Multi-Modal Behaviors in 2v1:** FSP enabled the high-level strategy policy to discover diverse, multi-modal behaviors, preventing collapse into single-modal local optima. For instance, in 2v1 scenarios, attackers learned to dynamically choose between passing and solo running based on the defender's reactions and ball positions.
- **Emergent Cooperative and Competitive Strategies:** The FSP framework facilitated the emergence of complex team behaviors:
 - **Defender:** Demonstrated effective defenses, including anticipating shots, proactively blocking, breaking up passes, and counterattacking.
 - **Attacker:** Exhibited coordinated attacks, such as one attacker luring the defender while another moved to receive a pass and shoot. More sophisticated collaboration included an attacker actively blocking an opponent's path to enable a teammate to pass from behind and score.
- **Scalability:** The framework with FSP also scaled effectively to 2v2 simulations, yielding stable and coordinated attacking and defensive behaviors.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments and Behavior Analysis with Value Function

- **Decentralized Deployment:** The learned high- and low-level policies were deployed on three Unitree Go1 quadrupeds on a 10m x 8m field, relying solely on onboard LiDAR for perception (Livox MID-360) and onboard computation (NVIDIA Orin NX). Localization was achieved via real-time LiDAR-inertial odometry (FAST-LIO) in a pre-mapped environment, with minimal pose information shared among robots for mutual localization. This setup enabled fully autonomous, decentralized soccer without external motion capture, functioning indoors and outdoors under varying lighting conditions.
- **Robot-Robot and Robot-Human Interaction:** The system supported both robot-robot and robot-human soccer matches.
 - In a 2v1 scenario with trained attackers against a handcrafted ball-chasing defender, attackers successfully coordinated passes and scored. Critic value maps from the attackers' perspectives showed higher expected returns when one attacker attempted a pass and the other moved to receive.
 - In another 2v1 experiment, a trained defender played against one trained attacker and one human teammate. The trained defender successfully intercepted the ball and executed a counterattack, with its value map indicating higher critic values near the attackers' goal. The robot attacker also successfully passed the ball to the human teammate, resulting in a goal.
- **Robustness in 1v1:** Real-world 1v1 experiments showed attackers utilizing the wall to rebound the ball into the goal, while defenders exhibited anticipatory behavior to intercept. The policies also demonstrated robustness, with attackers successfully scoring with follow-up shots after initial failures.
- **Interpretability through Value Maps:** Visualization of value maps provided insights into the learned strategies, demonstrating how agents perceive and value different states in relation to their objectives (e.g., high values for passing opportunities or positions favoring counterattacks).

In summary, the experimental results indicate that the hierarchical framework with a diverse set of low-level skills provides stable and efficient learning. FSP promotes strategic diversification and mitigates local optima, leading to the emergence of complex cooperative and competitive behaviors. The zero-shot transferability of the learned policies to real robots, coupled with interpretable value functions, validates the design choices and capabilities of the proposed system.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of multi-robot systems, particularly in the context of robot soccer. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Demonstration of Fully Decentralized, Learning-Based Multi-Robot Soccer:** The work provides the first demonstration of a fully decentralized multi-quadruped robotic system capable of playing cooperative and competitive soccer in the real world, relying solely on onboard sensing and computation. This eliminates the need for external motion capture systems or centralized planners, which have traditionally been prerequisites for such complex multi-robot tasks. This capability marks a step toward truly autonomous robotic teams operating in unstructured or outdoor environments.
- **Validation of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning for Complex Tasks:** The proposed hierarchical MARL framework effectively addresses the dual challenge of fine-grained motor control and long-horizon strategic decision-making. By decoupling low-level skills from high-level strategy, the framework enhances learning stability and sample efficiency, outperforming flat and restricted baselines. This approach offers a valuable blueprint for developing complex behaviors in other multi-robot domains.
- **Effectiveness of Fictitious Self-Play in Adversarial Environments:** The integration of Fictitious Self-Play (FSP) for co-evolutionary training demonstrates its utility in fostering versatile competitive and cooperative behaviors. This method enables agents to develop sophisticated strategies, such as coordinated passing, dynamic role allocation, blocking, and counter-attacking, by continually adapting to a diverse population of opponents. This has implications for training agents in other adversarial multi-agent settings where adaptive strategies are critical.
- **Robust Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer of policies from simulation to real quadruped robots (Unitree Go1) highlights the robustness of the learned policies and the fidelity of the simulation environment. This capability reduces the substantial effort typically required for real-world fine-tuning, accelerating the deployment of complex robotic systems.
- **Insights into Emergent Behaviors and Interpretability:** The systematic analysis of emergent behaviors, aided by the visualization of critic value maps, provides insights into how cooperative and competitive strategies arise from task-driven pressures rather than explicit coordination rewards. This interpretability can inform the design of future multi-agent learning systems.
- **Foundation for Future Robot Teams:** This work lays a foundation for the development of autonomous robot teams that could potentially rival human teams in complex physical sports. Beyond soccer, the principles demonstrated here could be extended to other real-world applications requiring coordinated multi-robot operation, such as search and rescue, logistics, or industrial automation, where decentralized control, robust perception, and strategic cooperation are essential.

While the work acknowledges limitations in scaling to larger team sizes and extending to humanoid platforms, the achievements in decentralized, learning-based multi-quadruped soccer represent a significant contribution to robotics and artificial intelligence, pushing the boundaries of autonomous multi-agent systems in dynamic, interactive environments.
