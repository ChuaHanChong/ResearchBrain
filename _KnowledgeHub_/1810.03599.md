# SFV: Reinforcement Learning of Physical Skills from Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> SFV learns controllers for physically simulated characters directly from monocular YouTube video through a three-stage pipeline: 2D and 3D pose estimation with rotation-augmented training, latent-space motion reconstruction fusing both estimators with temporal smoothing, and PPO motion imitation using an adaptive initial-state distribution. It reproduces over 20 dynamic skills, including backflips, cartwheels and kip-ups, on a humanoid and an Atlas robot, and retargets them to new terrains.

## Problem

- Mocap-based character animation needs heavily instrumented studios and actors, so activities like large-scale outdoor sports remain impractical or impossible to record.
- Monocular pose estimators predict each frame independently, so sequenced poses jitter and violate physics, making raw predictions poor reference motions for imitation.
- Earlier video-imitation controllers used finite-state machines, silhouette likelihoods and task-specific mocap priors, yielding robotic motions and a narrow repertoire of skills.

## Method

- ==Rotation-augmented pose estimation==: 2D **OpenPose** and 3D **HMR** estimators retrained on images rotated uniformly over the full circle, fixing upside-down acrobatic poses.
- ==Motion reconstruction==: a trajectory optimized in the 3D estimator's latent pose space under weighted 2D reprojection, confidence-weighted 3D consistency, and inter-frame smoothness losses.
- ==Adaptive state initialization==: the initial-state distribution, phase-conditioned Gaussians, is learned by policy gradient jointly with a **PPO** imitation policy under a pose/velocity/end-effector/center-of-mass reward.

## Results

- Over **20** skills learned from single video clips; humanoid normalized returns span **0.487** for push to **0.932** for walk, using roughly 150 million samples per policy.
- Adaptive state initialization beat reference and fixed initialization on every skill tested, e.g. Backflip A at **0.741** versus **0.602** and **0.086** respectively.
- Motion reconstruction raised frontflip return from **0.403** to **0.708** under adaptive initialization; skills retargeted to a 169.5 kg Atlas robot and to slopes and gaps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physics simulation plus RL imitation acts as a denoiser, absorbing pose-estimation errors that kinematic post-processing alone cannot remove.
> - Learning the initial-state distribution is a cheap automatic curriculum, and pays off most exactly when the reference data is noisy or low-fidelity.
> - Dataset orientation bias is a silent failure mode; uniform rotation augmentation is a trivial fix that unlocks entire classes of extreme poses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Data-driven methods have been fundamental in character animation for several decades, with motion capture (mocap) serving as a prevalent source of motion data. Mocap data is utilized in both kinematic and physics-based character animation, enabling the development of diverse and naturalistic character controllers through imitation. However, the acquisition of mocap data typically necessitates heavily instrumented environments and actors, which can be costly, logistically challenging, and prohibitive for certain activities, such as large-scale outdoor sports.

Monocular video offers an alternative and abundant source of motion data, with platforms like YouTube providing a vast repository of visual demonstrations. Despite its accessibility, extracting accurate and physically plausible motion information from monocular video presents significant challenges. Prior methods that attempted to learn character controllers from video, such as those relying on 2D silhouette likelihoods and finite-state machines, often produced motions that appeared robotic and lagged in quality compared to mocap-based systems. These methods also frequently required task-specific pose priors, necessitating the availability of similar mocap data, or manual annotation.

Concurrently, the field of computer vision has witnessed substantial advancements in 2D and 3D human pose estimation from monocular images and videos, largely driven by deep learning techniques. While these pose estimators can predict reasonable human poses, their outputs often contain errors, physical inconsistencies, and temporal inconsistencies that accumulate, leading to unnatural behaviors when directly applied to character animation.

This research integrates state-of-the-art deep pose estimation with deep reinforcement learning (RL) to bridge the gap between abundant video data and high-fidelity, physics-based character animation. It builds upon previous work in motion imitation using RL with mocap data (e.g., Peng et al., 2018) and recent breakthroughs in weakly-supervised 3D pose estimation (e.g., Kanazawa et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2016). By employing RL, the proposed method aims to refine imperfect and noisy pose sequences extracted from video, compensate for missing frames, and adhere to the physical constraints of simulated characters and their environments. The work positions itself as a system that leverages the accessibility of video data to produce robust and adaptable physically simulated character controllers, addressing limitations present in both traditional mocap workflows and earlier video-based animation techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that enables physically simulated characters to learn a wide range of dynamic physical skills directly from monocular video demonstrations. This involves synthesizing robust control policies that allow simulated characters to reproduce behaviors observed in video clips.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations inherent in current data-driven character animation approaches:
- **Mocap Limitations:** Traditional motion capture, while producing high-quality data, is often prohibitively expensive and logistically complex. It requires specialized equipment, instrumented environments, and trained actors, making it inaccessible for many practitioners and unsuitable for capturing certain types of activities (e.g., large-scale outdoor sports, or motions of non-human animals).
- **Abundance of Video Data:** Monocular video clips are an abundant and easily accessible source of motion data, with a vast and continuously growing repository available online. Leveraging this resource could significantly reduce the barriers to creating diverse character animations.
- **Challenges of Video-to-Motion Conversion:** Despite advances in computer vision for pose estimation, direct extraction of motion information from monocular video for animation often results in low-fidelity and physically inconsistent motions due to errors, depth ambiguities, and lack of temporal consistency in raw pose predictions. Prior video-based animation systems have struggled to achieve the naturalism and robustness of mocap-based methods.

To overcome these challenges and achieve its primary objective, the research set out to accomplish several specific technical goals:
- **Integration of Vision and Control:** To create a unified framework that effectively combines deep pose estimation from monocular video with deep reinforcement learning for physics-based character control.
- **Improved Reference Motion Quality:** To develop a motion reconstruction method that processes raw, noisy pose estimations from video to produce higher-fidelity, temporally consistent, and physically plausible reference motions suitable for imitation by simulated characters.
- **Robust Reinforcement Learning:** To introduce novel reinforcement learning techniques, specifically an adaptive state initialization method, that enhance the agent's ability to learn from the inherently lower-fidelity and imperfect reference motions derived from video, enabling the acquisition of challenging, long-horizon skills.
- **Generalizability and Adaptability:** To demonstrate that the resulting controllers are robust to physical perturbations, can be retargeted to characters with different morphologies, and can perform skills in varied environmental conditions (e.g., irregular terrain, object interaction).
- **Novel Application Development:** To explore and demonstrate a new application, physics-based motion completion, where learned controllers can predict plausible future motions from a single still image.

In essence, the research aims to democratize access to diverse motion data for character animation by transforming readily available video content into robust, physically simulated character skills, thereby extending the capabilities and applications of data-driven animation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, termed SFV, comprises a three-stage pipeline designed to learn physical skills from monocular video. These stages are pose estimation, motion reconstruction, and motion imitation.

**A. Pose Estimation Stage:**
The initial stage involves extracting the pose of the human actor from each frame of the input video clip. This is achieved by leveraging an ensemble of deep learning-based pose estimators:
- **2D Pose Estimation:** OpenPose (Wei et al., 2016) is utilized to predict 2D joint locations ($\hat{x}_t$) and associated confidence scores for each frame. These 2D predictions are generally considered more accurate in screen coordinates.
- **3D Pose Estimation:** Human Mesh Recovery (HMR) (Kanazawa et al., 2018) is employed to directly predict the 3D pose ($q_t$, represented as local joint rotations in axis-angle form) and shape of a human model, along with camera parameters, from a single image. HMR operates by encoding an image into a latent space and then decoding it to a 3D pose. Its weakly-supervised adversarial training framework allows it to generalize to "in-the-wild" images, which is crucial for handling the varied content of internet videos.

A key challenge for dynamic and acrobatic skills is that human poses can deviate significantly from the upright orientations dominant in most training datasets. To address this, the pose estimators are trained with a data augmentation technique where standard datasets are augmented with rotated versions of existing images, sampled uniformly over [0, 2$\pi$]. This strategy aims to improve the estimators' performance on extreme and inverted poses common in acrobatic movements. The output of this stage is a 2D pose trajectory and a 3D pose trajectory for the entire video.

**B. Motion Reconstruction Stage:**
Since the pose estimators process frames independently, simply concatenating their predictions often results in temporally inconsistent motions with artifacts like jitter and sudden pose changes, which are non-physical and difficult for a simulated character to imitate. The motion reconstruction stage aims to produce a high-fidelity, temporally consistent 3D reference motion {q\*t} by reconciling the predictions from both 2D and 3D estimators and enforcing temporal smoothness.

Instead of directly optimizing in the 3D pose space, the method leverages HMR's encoder-decoder structure and optimizes a trajectory $Z = \{z_t\}$ in the latent pose space, which is designed to capture the manifold of plausible 3D human poses. The optimization minimizes a reconstruction loss function ($l_{rec}$) composed of three terms:
- **2D Consistency Loss ($l_{2D}$):** Penalizes the reprojection error between the predicted 2D joint locations ($\hat{x}_{t,j}$) and the 2D projections of the 3D joints obtained from the decoded latent pose ($q(z_t)$). This term is weighted by the confidence of the 2D predictions ($c_{t,j}$).
- **3D Consistency Loss ($l_{3D}$):** Encourages the optimized 3D pose trajectory to remain close to the initial 3D predictions ($\hat{q}_t$) from HMR. This term is weighted by an exponential confidence score that decreases with the discrepancy between the initial 2D and 3D predictions. This ensures that accurate 3D predictions are largely preserved, while inconsistent ones are adjusted.
- **Smoothness Loss ($l_{sm}$):** Penalizes large changes in 3D joint positions between adjacent frames, promoting temporal consistency and mitigating high-frequency jitter.
The final optimized latent trajectory is then decoded to yield the reference motion {q\*t}.

**C. Motion Imitation with Reinforcement Learning Stage:**
With a reconstructed reference motion {q\*t}, the goal is to train a control policy ($\pi$) for a physically simulated character to reproduce this motion. This is achieved using a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017).
- **Policy and Value Networks:** The policy is modeled as a feedforward neural network outputting a Gaussian action distribution, while a separate network estimates the value function.
- **State and Action Space:** The character's state ($s$) is a 197D vector including relative joint positions, rotations (quaternions), linear and angular velocities in the character's local coordinate frame, and a scalar phase variable ($\phi$) to synchronize with the reference motion. Actions ($a$) are 36D target rotations for PD controllers at each joint.
- **Reward Function:** A composite reward function ($r_t$) incentivizes the simulated character to match the reference motion. It comprises weighted terms for:
 - **Pose Similarity ($r_{p_t}$):** Based on quaternion differences of joint orientations.
 - **Velocity Similarity ($r_{v_t}$):** Based on differences in local joint angular velocities.
 - **End-effector Position ($r_{e_t}$):** Based on positional differences of hands and feet relative to the root.
 - **Center-of-Mass Position ($r_{c_t}$):** Based on the difference in the character's center-of-mass from the reference.
- **Adaptive State Initialization (ASI):** A crucial component is the learning of an initial state distribution ($\rho_\omega(s_0)$) alongside the policy. This addresses the challenge that low-fidelity reference motions might contain physically implausible states from which recovery is difficult. ASI treats the initial state distribution as a second agent in a cooperative multi-agent RL problem, optimizing it via policy gradients to propose initial states that maximize the expected return. The distribution is modeled as a collection of independent Gaussian distributions over character states, conditioned on discrete phase values. This acts as an automatic curriculum, providing initializations that are challenging but achievable for the current policy, facilitating learning for long-horizon and dynamic skills.
- **Training Process:** Episodes are simulated until a fixed time horizon or early termination (e.g., character falling). Policy and value networks are updated using PPO with collected data batches, while the initial state distribution is updated less frequently.

**D. Motion Completion (Application):**
As an application, the framework supports physics-based motion completion. Given a library of learned controllers and a single still image of a human actor, the system predicts plausible future motions. This involves:
1. Extracting the actor's 3D pose ($\bar{q}$) from the image.
2. Comparing $\bar{q}$ to all frames of all reference motions in the library to identify the most similar reference pose ($q^{i^*}_t*$) and its corresponding learned policy ($\pi^{i^*}$).
3. Initializing the simulated character with pose $\bar{q}$ and the velocity from $q^{i^*}_t*$.
4. Simulating the character's motion using the selected policy $\pi^{i^*}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that the SFV framework is capable of learning a broad range of highly dynamic and acrobatic physical skills from monocular video demonstrations.

**A. Learning Diverse Skills:** The system successfully trained controllers for over 20 distinct skills, encompassing locomotion (walk, run, jump), acrobatics (backflips, cartwheels, frontflips, handsprings, kip-ups, rolls, spins), martial arts (kick, punch, spinkick), and dance moves (Gangnam Style, general dance). These learned skills are exhibited by simulated humanoid characters, achieving average normalized returns for most skills above 0.6, indicating a significant degree of imitation of the reconstructed reference motions.

**B. Retargeting Capabilities:**
- **Morphological Discrepancies:** The learned skills could be successfully retargeted to an Atlas robot, which possesses a morphology significantly different from that of a human (e.g., different limb proportions, substantially greater mass). Despite these differences, the Atlas robot was able to reproduce various skills, including walking, running, vaulting, and even cartwheels, albeit with generally lower normalized returns compared to the humanoid, reflecting the inherent challenge of the morphological mismatch.
- **Environmental Adaptability:** Policies were adapted to new environments not present in the original video demonstrations, which were recorded on flat terrain. The simulated characters were able to perform skills on irregular terrains (slopes) and navigate obstacles (gaps). For object interaction, a pushing skill was retargeted to push a heavy box uphill and downhill on a sloped surface. This adaptability was facilitated by incorporating a heightmap of the surrounding terrain into the policy's input state.

**C. Impact of Adaptive State Initialization (ASI):**
Comparative experiments evaluated the contribution of Adaptive State Initialization (ASI) against Fixed State Initialization (FSI) and Reference State Initialization (RSI).
- **Improved Performance and Speed:** ASI consistently outperformed both FSI and RSI across all evaluated skills. For example, for "Backflip A," FSI yielded a normalized return of 0.086, RSI 0.602, while ASI achieved 0.741. This improvement was consistent for other complex skills like "Cartwheel A," "Frontflip," and "Handspring A." ASI also demonstrated faster convergence and improved learning speed, particularly for more challenging, dynamic motions.
- **Robustness to Hyperparameters:** The performance of ASI was found to be relatively robust to the number of Gaussian components used to model the initial state distribution, suggesting its practical applicability is not overly sensitive to this choice.

**D. Impact of Pose Estimation and Motion Reconstruction:**
The quality of the reference motion was shown to be critical for effective imitation.
- **Rotation Augmentation for Pose Estimators:** Training the underlying 2D and 3D pose estimators with rotation augmentation significantly improved their ability to predict extreme and upside-down poses common in acrobatic movements. Without this augmentation, the estimators frequently failed to correctly predict such orientations.
- **Motion Reconstruction (MR):** The motion reconstruction stage was demonstrated to be effective in correcting errors and enforcing temporal consistency in the raw 3D pose predictions from the estimators. Policies trained with reference motions produced by MR showed improved performance and learning speed compared to those trained directly on the raw, unrefined 3D pose estimates. This improvement was more pronounced when using RSI, which is more sensitive to reference motion artifacts. The results indicate that MR generates reference motions that are more amenable to being mimicked by a simulated character, leading to higher quality imitation.

**E. Motion Completion:**
The motion completion technique successfully predicted plausible future motions from single still images. Given an image, the system could identify a matching skill from its library of learned controllers and simulate the continuation of the observed pose. This demonstrates a novel application for physics-based character animation, leveraging the learned control policies to infer and predict dynamic behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the fields of computer graphics, reinforcement learning, and computer vision by presenting a comprehensive framework for learning physics-based character skills directly from monocular video. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Democratization of Character Animation:** By enabling skill acquisition from readily available video clips, the framework significantly lowers the barrier to entry for creating complex character animations. It reduces the dependence on expensive, specialized motion capture equipment and instrumented environments, making high-fidelity animation more accessible to a broader range of developers, artists, and researchers.
- **Expanded Data Sources for Motion:** The work effectively harnesses the vast and continuously growing reservoir of online video content as a rich source of diverse human motion data. This addresses a major limitation of traditional mocap, which is often constrained by the limited volume and specificity of professionally acquired datasets. It opens possibilities for learning motions from unconventional sources, such as large-scale outdoor activities or non-human animals, which are traditionally difficult or impossible to motion capture.
- **Enhanced Realism and Robustness in Simulation:** The physically simulated characters trained using this method exhibit inherent robustness to physical perturbations and can adapt to new environments and morphologies. This is crucial for developing interactive applications, realistic virtual agents in games, and robust control policies for robotics, where characters must respond dynamically and plausibly to unforeseen circumstances.
- **Advancements in Reinforcement Learning and Vision Integration:** The research demonstrates a practical and effective integration of advanced deep learning techniques from computer vision (pose estimation) with deep reinforcement learning. It specifically addresses the challenge of learning from noisy and imperfect "real-world" data, which is a common problem in imitation learning. The proposed adaptive state initialization method represents a notable contribution to curriculum learning in RL, enabling agents to learn complex, long-horizon skills more effectively from imperfect demonstrations.
- **Novel Applications and Capabilities:** The introduction of physics-based motion completion, where plausible future motions can be predicted from a single still image, showcases a new capability with potential applications in human-computer interaction, predictive analytics, and content generation. This allows for dynamic interpretation of static visual information, inferring potential actions and outcomes.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The modular design of the SFV framework allows for straightforward incorporation of future advancements in either pose estimation (e.g., more accurate 3D tracking) or reinforcement learning algorithms. This provides a clear pathway for further development, including the exploration of more end-to-end learning approaches that reduce reliance on explicit pose estimation stages, as well as scaling to even more complex interactions and environments.
