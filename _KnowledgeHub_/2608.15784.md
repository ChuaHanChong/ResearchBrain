# Reliable Piezoresistive Strain Sensing Through Physical Limits and Uncertainty Monitoring

> [!summary] Summary
> PARM pairs a physics-guided Gaussian Process inverse model (resistance, resistance-rate, and viscoelastic memory-state features) with a risk factor fusing epistemic uncertainty and physical strain/strain-rate limits into a three-state reliability monitor for piezoresistive soft strain sensors, needing no labeled failure data. On Nitinol wire and silver-coated polyamide thread it reaches 0.90-0.95 fit score, 0.15-0.26% RMSE, 96% coverage, and detects 95% out-of-range and 100% abnormal conditions.

## Problem

- Data-driven soft-sensor models report average accuracy metrics like RMSE or fit score that say nothing about whether a given reading should be trusted at deployment.
- Fault-detection methods need labeled faulty examples or injected failures, which are expensive or impractical to collect for soft piezoresistive materials without damaging them.
- Probabilistic regressors track epistemic uncertainty but ignore whether the predicted sensor state is physically plausible against strain and strain-rate operating limits.

## Method

- Physics-guided input features derive a causal memory state from a ==Standard Linear Solid viscoelastic model==, combined with relative resistance and its rate to capture hysteresis and short-term history.
- **PARM**'s ==Gaussian Process== with a ==Rational Quadratic kernel== and ARD lengthscales regresses strain from the physics-guided features, giving a posterior mean and calibrated predictive standard deviation.
- A ==risk factor== fuses per-timestep physical-constraint violation probability with an epistemic out-of-distribution probability via a union rule, driving a five-sample-debounced ==reliable/warning/fault state machine==.

## Results

- Adding resistance-rate and memory-state features raised nominal fit score from 0.90 to **0.95** and lowered RMSE from 0.26% to **0.15%**, with PICP-95 coverage of **0.96** against the 95% target.
- Epistemic uncertainty alone detected only **40%** of out-of-range conditions while the model still scored 0.87 fit score there; physical risk alone reached **92%**, and the combined risk factor reached **95%**.
- Under nominal operation all risk variants gave **100%** reliable-state occupancy and zero false alarms, while abnormal conditions (noise, fatigue, wire breakage) were detected at **100%** by every variant.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A confident-looking predictive mean is not evidence of trustworthiness; a model can stay accurate and low-uncertainty while operating past its physical operating limits.
> - Physical constraints and epistemic uncertainty catch complementary failure modes, so fusing both types of risk beats either alone for full-coverage reliability monitoring.
> - Reliability monitoring can be built from nominal operating data and known physical limits alone, avoiding the need for hard-to-collect labeled failure examples in soft-material sensing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Piezoresistive strain sensors, characterized by their flexibility and compliance, have become prevalent in soft robotics and wearable technologies. These sensors offer advantages such as simple readout electronics, low cost, lightweight nature, and ease of integration into deformable structures. However, their electrical response is complex, exhibiting nonlinearities, hysteresis, viscoelasticity, strain-rate dependence, drift, and susceptibility to fatigue and degradation. These characteristics make accurate and reliable modeling challenging.

Traditional approaches for modeling soft sensors often employ machine learning (ML) techniques like Neural Networks, Support Vector Machines, Gaussian Processes, and transformers. While these data-driven methods are capable of learning complex nonlinear mappings and demonstrate proficiency in estimating physical quantities (e.g., strain, curvature), they primarily focus on prediction accuracy. Standard performance metrics like Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) or correlation coefficients quantify average prediction error but do not inherently ensure the reliability or trustworthiness of a measurement during real-world deployment. A prediction, even if numerically accurate on average, might originate from a sensor operating outside its calibrated range or experiencing an internal fault.

Existing solutions for reliability typically involve training dedicated fault-detection algorithms. These supervised methods require extensive datasets of labeled faulty behavior, which are often expensive, impractical, or even impossible to collect for soft materials without intentionally damaging them or reproducing rare operating conditions.

Another line of research utilizes uncertainty-aware ML models, such as Gaussian Processes (GPs) and Bayesian neural networks. These probabilistic models estimate not only the output but also the epistemic uncertainty associated with the prediction. This uncertainty can signal when a model operates outside its training domain, potentially indicating reduced confidence. However, a key limitation addressed by this paper is that epistemic uncertainty alone does not confirm the physical plausibility of the predicted sensor state or account for the sensor's operating limits. A confident prediction might still violate physical constraints or come from an unphysical state. This lack of physical context in uncertainty quantification leaves a critical gap, particularly in closed-loop control systems where incorrect sensor measurements can compromise stability, safety, and performance.

This work contributes to the landscape by integrating physics-informed modeling with probabilistic uncertainty quantification and explicit physical operating limits. It aims to bridge the gap between accurate prediction and reliable measurement by providing a framework that not only estimates strain but also reports a reliability state, effectively creating a "self-monitoring" sensor without requiring labeled failure data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reliability monitoring framework for piezoresistive soft sensors that ensures trustworthy strain measurements during operation. This framework aims to provide a real-time strain estimate coupled with a clear indication of its reliability, moving beyond simple prediction accuracy.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in current soft sensor modeling and reliability assessment practices:

- **Complex and Unreliable Sensor Behavior:** Piezoresistive soft sensors exhibit inherent nonlinearities, hysteresis, viscoelasticity, and sensitivity to environmental factors and material degradation. These characteristics make their electrical resistance-to-strain mapping challenging to model reliably, particularly under varying operating conditions or repeated use.
- **Inadequacy of Prediction Accuracy for Reliability:** While data-driven machine learning models can achieve high prediction accuracy for soft sensors, this accuracy alone does not guarantee that the reported measurements are reliable or trustworthy during deployment. A model might generate confident predictions even when the sensor is operating outside its calibrated workspace, experiencing internal failure, or reporting physically implausible states.
- **Limitations of Supervised Fault Detection:** Traditional fault detection methods rely on collecting and labeling examples of faulty behavior. This process is often impractical, costly, and time-consuming for soft materials, as inducing failures typically requires damaging the sensor or simulating rare operational scenarios. The proposed framework seeks to obviate the need for such labeled failure data.
- **Insufficient Information from Epistemic Uncertainty Alone:** Probabilistic machine learning models provide an estimate of epistemic uncertainty, indicating how well the current input aligns with the training data. However, this uncertainty does not inherently incorporate the sensor's physical operating limits. A prediction might have low epistemic uncertainty but still correspond to a physically impossible or undesirable state. The model might confidently extrapolate beyond its training data, leading to an unplausible physical state.
- **Criticality in Closed-Loop Systems:** In applications such as soft robotics control or medical devices, incorrect or unreliable sensor readings can have significant consequences, including system instability, safety hazards, and compromised performance. A mechanism to monitor measurement reliability in real-time is therefore crucial.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes a framework that couples a physics-informed probabilistic inverse model with a risk factor. This framework is designed to turn strain estimation into a self-monitoring process, reporting not only a strain value but also a reliability state at every measurement timestep.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Physics-Aware Reliability Monitor (PARM), integrates physics-guided input features, Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) for inverse modeling and uncertainty quantification, and a multi-component risk factor for reliability assessment.

**A. Sensor's Physical Domain Characterization:**
The approach begins by defining the sensor's physical domain, which encompasses all possible operating conditions up to sensor failure. This domain is partitioned into four regions based on physical and quality constraints:
- **Physical Constraints:** Hard limits on sensor state variables, such as strain ($\epsilon(t)$) and strain rate ($\dot{\epsilon}(t)$), which must remain below predefined thresholds ($\theta_k$). These are expressed as $g_k(\epsilon(t), \dot{\epsilon}(t)) \leq \theta_k$.
- **Quality Constraints:** Evaluate the sensor's resistance response against a specific score metric, ensuring it exceeds a threshold ($\theta_m$). These are expressed as $G_m(C_{ij}) \geq \theta_m$.
Based on these constraints, the domain is divided into:
- **Nominal Region:** Both physical and quality constraints are met, suitable for training and operation.
- **Degraded Region:** Physical constraints are met, but quality constraints are not.
- **Out-of-Range Region:** Physical constraints are violated, but quality constraints are met.
- **Rejected Region:** Neither constraint is met.
This partitioning forms the basis for reliability monitoring, requiring the machine learning input to reflect these domain boundaries.

**B. Physics-Guided Input Features:**
To capture the viscoelastic and hysteretic behavior of piezoresistive sensors, the model incorporates physics-guided input features:
- **Standard Linear Solid (SLS) Model:** This viscoelastic model is used to derive a causal memory state $z(t)$ that reflects the sensor's short-term memory and relaxation effects. The memory state is updated based on the relative resistance $\tilde{R}(t)$ and a time constant $\tau$, with discrete update rule $z_{k+1} = \phi z_k + (1-\phi)\tilde{R}_k$.
- **Feature Vector:** The complete input feature vector $x(t)$ for the inverse model combines static information, dynamic information, and memory:
 $x(t) = [\tilde{R}(t) \quad \dot{\tilde{R}}(t) \quad z(t)]^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$
 where $\tilde{R}(t)$ is the relative resistance and $\dot{\tilde{R}}(t)$ is the relative resistance rate. This ensures that the model can differentiate between identical resistance values that arise from different loading histories.

**C. Gaussian Process Regression (GPR):**
A Gaussian Process is selected as the backbone for the inverse modeling regression. GPR is a non-parametric, probabilistic model that not only predicts the strain but also quantifies the epistemic uncertainty associated with that prediction.
- **Predictive Distribution:** For each input $x(t)$, the GP produces a full posterior predictive distribution $p(\epsilon|x(t))$, characterized by a mean $\mu(t)$ (the strain estimate) and a standard deviation $\sigma_{GP}(t)$ (epistemic uncertainty).
- **Rational Quadratic (RQ) Kernel:** This kernel, $k(x, x') = \sigma_f^2 (1 + \sum_{i=1}^3 (x_i - x'_i)^2 / (2\alpha l_i^2))^{-\alpha}$, is chosen for its ability to capture both fast local variations (e.g., hysteresis) and slower viscoelastic drift. Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) is used to assign different lengthscales ($l_i$) to each input dimension, weighting their relevance. High uncertainty $\sigma_{GP}(t)$ indicates inputs that are poorly represented in the training data, linking the feature domain to the physical domain.

**D. Risk Factor for Reliability Monitoring:**
The core of the reliability monitoring framework is a risk factor $p_{risk} \in$, which combines physical plausibility with epistemic uncertainty.
- **Physical Risk Components ($p_k(t)$):** For each physical constraint $g_k$ (e.g., on strain or strain rate), the probability $p_k(t)$ that the constrained quantity $q_k$ exceeds its limit $q_{max,k}$ is calculated. The quantities (strain, strain rate) are treated as Gaussian with the GP's predicted mean ($\mu(t)$) and standard deviation ($\sigma_{GP}(t)$). For strain rate, uncertainty is propagated from two consecutive strain samples ($\dot{\sigma}(t) = \sqrt{\sigma_{GP}^2(t) + \sigma_{GP}^2(t-\Delta t)} / \Delta t$).
- **Epistemic Risk Component ($p_u(t)$):** This component quantifies how far the current predictive uncertainty $\sigma_{GP}(t)$ deviates from the uncertainty observed during nominal training. It is calculated as a clipped value based on lower ($\sigma_{low}$) and upper ($\sigma_{high}$) bounds of nominal training uncertainty. A high $p_u(t)$ indicates operation outside the well-represented training region.
- **Combined Risk Factor ($p_{risk}(t)$):** The individual risk components are combined using a probability union formula, assuming independence:
 $p_{risk}(t) = 1 - (1 - p_u(t)) \prod_{k=1}^K (1 - p_k(t))$
 This ensures that any single violation or high epistemic uncertainty can drive the overall risk factor.
- **Reliability State Machine:** The $p_{risk}$ factor is fed into a three-state machine: "reliable" (low risk), "warning" (risk > 0.5), and "fault" (risk > 0.75). A state transition requires five consecutive samples in the new state to prevent false alarms from transient spikes.

**E. Piezoresistive Soft Sensors Considered:**
The framework is validated on two materials:
- **Superelastic Nitinol Wire (Primary Case Study):** A Nickel-Titanium alloy (Ø0.2mm) exhibiting superelasticity due to stress-induced martensitic phase transformation, leading to significant strain recovery. Its resistance change is governed by geometric effects (Poisson effect) and piezoresistive effects (different resistivities for austenite $\rho_A$ and martensite $\rho_M$). The relative resistance $\tilde{R}$ is used for normalization.
- **Silver-Coated Polyamide Thread (Secondary Validation):** Used as an additional example to demonstrate method generality, with results in supplementary materials.

**F. Data Acquisition:**
Experiments involved subjecting sensors to 66 different loading and unloading conditions, encompassing both static (step stretching at 0Hz) and dynamic (sinusoidal stretching from 1-6% amplitude, 0.1-1Hz frequency) tests. Signals ($\tilde{R}(t)$, $\dot{\tilde{R}}(t)$, $z(t)$, and ground-truth $\epsilon$) were sampled at 100 Hz. Additionally, three anomaly tests were recorded: manually induced noise, wire breakage, and operation with a fatigued wire.
Sensor suitability was pre-validated using Gauge Factor (GF = $5.98 \pm 0.89$) and Pearson's correlation coefficient ($r_{\epsilon, \tilde{R}} = 0.96 \pm 0.04$), confirming high sensitivity and a strong linear relationship between strain and resistance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The PARM framework was evaluated on the superelastic Nitinol wire, with primary focus on nominal region identification, model performance, and reliability monitoring.

**A. Nominal Region Identification:**
The nominal operating region for the Nitinol wire was defined using:
- **Physical Limits:** Strain $\epsilon(t) \leq 6\%$ and absolute strain rate $|\dot{\epsilon}(t)| \leq 7\%/s$.
- **Quality Constraint:** A fit score $G_1(C_{ij}) \geq 0.88$, where the fit score is $1 - \sqrt{\sum (\epsilon_k - \mu_k)^2 / \sum \epsilon_k^2}$.
Applying these constraints to the experimental dataset resulted in a partitioned workspace (Figure 3). Conditions with low strain amplitude (e.g., 1%) showed reduced fit scores due to noise and hysteretic effects becoming more prominent relative to signal variation. High-frequency conditions often exceeded the strain-rate limit.

**B. Uncertainty Calibration and Model Performance:**
Model performance was assessed using Leave-One-Condition-Out Cross-Validation (LOCOCV) on nominal data and evaluated on both nominal and out-of-range conditions. Metrics included fit score, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP-95).
An ablation study on input features (Table II) demonstrated the following:
- **Relative Resistance ($\tilde{R}(t)$) alone:** Achieved a nominal fit score of $0.90 \pm 0.06$ and RMSE of $0.26 \pm 0.33\%$.
- **Adding Relative Resistance Rate ($\dot{\tilde{R}}(t)$):** Significantly improved nominal performance to a fit score of $0.93 \pm 0.03$ and RMSE of $0.16 \pm 0.08\%$. This feature captured crucial hysteretic dynamics. Out-of-range performance also improved from a fit score of $0.82$ to $0.86$.
- **Adding SLS Memory State ($z(t)$):** When added to $\tilde{R}(t)$, $z(t)$ alone showed marginal improvement.
- **Full Feature Set ($\tilde{R}(t)$, $\dot{\tilde{R}}(t)$, $z(t)$):** Achieved the best nominal performance with a fit score of $0.95 \pm 0.03$, RMSE of $0.15 \pm 0.09\%$, and PICP-95 of $0.96 \pm 0.03$ (close to the 95% target). This set also maintained the best out-of-range performance with a fit score of $0.87 \pm 0.07$ and RMSE of $0.46 \pm 0.42\%$.

The improved predictive accuracy and well-calibrated uncertainty (PICP-95 near target) with the full feature set were deemed critical for the subsequent reliability monitoring, as a more accurate mean reduces misleading strain estimates, and calibrated uncertainty supports accurate epistemic risk assessment.

**C. Reliability Performance:**
The performance of the risk factor was evaluated on nominal, out-of-range, and abnormal conditions (Table III), focusing on state occupancy (reliable, warning, fault) and Detection Rate (DR).
- **Nominal Conditions:** All risk definitions (epistemic $p_u$, physical $p_k$, and combined $p_{risk}$) achieved 100% reliable state occupancy and 0% DR. This indicates no false alarms under normal operation, consistent with the model's calibrated uncertainty.
- **Out-of-Range Conditions:**
 - **Epistemic Risk ($p_u$) alone:** Detected only 40% of out-of-range conditions. The GP's predictive uncertainty often remained low because the mean prediction was still reasonably accurate (fit score 0.87), failing to consistently trigger warning thresholds.
 - **Physical Risk ($p_k$) alone:** Detected 92% of out-of-range conditions. This component effectively identified situations where strain or strain rate exceeded predefined limits, even if the model was confident in its prediction.
 - **Combined Risk ($p_{risk}$):** Achieved the highest detection rate of 95% for out-of-range conditions, with 19% warning state and 24% fault state occupancy. This demonstrated the synergy between physical and epistemic risk components.
- **Abnormal Conditions (noise, fatigue, breakage):** All three risk factor variants achieved a 100% DR.
 - **Epistemic Risk ($p_u$):** Primarily resulted in fault states (31% fault occupancy), as expected when the input signal significantly deviates from the training distribution.
 - **Physical Risk ($p_k$):** Showed more warning time (30% warning occupancy), indicating violations of strain/strain-rate limits without necessarily pushing GP uncertainty to fault levels.
 - **Combined Risk ($p_{risk}$):** Maintained the 100% DR, with 2% warning and 31% fault states, effectively combining the strengths of both components.

**Qualitative Examples (Figure 4):**
- **Nominal (dynamic & static):** Low risk factor, consistent with accurate predictions.
- **Out-of-range:** Risk factor increased, initially triggered by strain-rate risk ($p_k$) as limits were approached, followed by epistemic risk ($p_u$) as the input moved away from the training distribution, leading to warning and fault states.
- **Abnormal Signal Spikes:** Rapid rise in risk, primarily driven by the relative resistance rate component of $p_k$, leading to warning and fault states.
- **Sensor Fatigue:** Characteristic "bump" in relative resistance due to altered phase transformation triggered an increase in the risk factor, identifying the sensor as faulty before mechanical failure.
- **Sensor Breakage:** The circuit opening produced a highly out-of-range input signal, causing the risk factor to rapidly escalate and transition the sensor to a fault state, preventing the system from acting on erroneous data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for enhancing the reliability of piezoresistive soft strain sensors, addressing a critical limitation in current sensor modeling practices. The significance of this work lies in several key aspects:

- **Self-Monitoring Capability:** The proposed PARM framework transforms conventional piezoresistive sensors into self-monitoring units. By reporting a strain estimate alongside a dynamic reliability state at every timestep, the system provides continuous feedback on measurement trustworthiness. This enables intelligent decision-making, allowing a control system or application to withhold action if a measurement is deemed unreliable, thereby enhancing safety and stability.
- **Physics-Informed Reliability:** The fusion of physics-guided input features, a probabilistic inverse model (Gaussian Process), and a multi-component risk factor is a notable advancement. It ensures that reliability is not solely based on statistical uncertainty but also on the physical plausibility of the sensor's state relative to its defined operating limits. This integration provides a more robust and comprehensive assessment of reliability.
- **Reduced Data Requirements for Fault Detection:** A significant practical advantage is that the risk factor is computed from nominal operating data and predefined physical limits alone. This eliminates the need for extensive, often difficult-to-collect, labeled fault examples for soft materials, which are prone to damage during failure data collection. This reduces the cost and complexity associated with deploying reliable soft sensing solutions.
- **Improved Accuracy and Uncertainty Calibration:** The strategic use of physics-guided features, particularly the relative resistance rate and the Standard Linear Solid (SLS) memory state, substantially improved the predictive accuracy and calibration of the Gaussian Process model. This robust base model is foundational for the effectiveness of the reliability monitor, as a well-calibrated model better informs both physical and epistemic risk components.
- **Generalizability Across Materials and Models:** The framework's validation on both a Nitinol wire and a silver-coated polyamide thread, materials with distinct transduction mechanisms, demonstrates its generalizability. The underlying principle of combining a predictive mean, calibrated uncertainty, and physically bounded quantities suggests that the risk fusion rule can be extended to other probabilistic regressors and various physical constraints.
- **Enhanced System Safety and Performance:** By proactively identifying out-of-range operation or anomalous sensor behavior (e.g., noise, fatigue, breakage) and signaling a warning or fault state, the framework can prevent systems from making decisions based on erroneous data. This has direct implications for improving the safety, robustness, and overall performance of soft robotic systems, wearables, and other applications relying on soft sensors.

The potential impact of this research is broad, offering a standardized and efficient method for incorporating reliability into soft sensor design and deployment. This could accelerate the adoption of soft sensing technologies in critical applications where measurement trustworthiness is paramount.
