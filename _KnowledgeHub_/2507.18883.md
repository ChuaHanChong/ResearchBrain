# Success in Humanoid Reinforcement Learning under Partial Observation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Newcastle developed a model-free reinforcement learning method utilizing a novel history encoder, achieving the first stable training of humanoid locomotion policies under partial observability in the Gymnasium Humanoid-v4 environment. The method demonstrated performance comparable to or exceeding full-state observation baselines, even with up to two-thirds of the state information removed.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) performance significantly degrades in high-dimensional systems like humanoids under partial observability, transitioning from Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) to more complex Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs).
- Prior memory-based methods, such as those employing RNNs or LSTMs, have not consistently achieved stable training or effective policy learning for humanoid locomotion in complex, high-dimensional POMDP benchmarks like Gymnasium Humanoid-v4.
- The inherent partial observability of real-world robotic systems, stemming from incomplete sensor data, measurement noise, or bandwidth limitations, poses a major challenge for deploying RL policies learned in fully observed simulations.

## Method

- Introduces a novel model-free reinforcement learning method that integrates a history encoder into a standard RL algorithm.
- The history encoder processes a fixed-length sequence of past observations in parallel, designed to flexibly combine information across the observation window and infer complete latent states from incomplete observation sequences.
- Evaluated the approach by systematically simulating partial observability through the selective removal of velocity, mass/inertia, and force attributes from the 348-dimensional state space in the ==Gymnasium Humanoid==-v4 environment.

## Results

- The proposed method consistently outperformed baseline recurrent memory (RMF, ODERMF) algorithms in all single-attribute removal scenarios, achieving average maximum rewards up to **6680.8**, which surpassed the full-state TD3 baseline (**4547.2**) in some configurations.
- Even with two observation attributes removed (reducing observable state dimensions by up to two-thirds), the method continued to learn effective policies, notably achieving an average maximum reward of **5707.7** when mass/inertia and force were absent.
- Policies trained with the proposed method demonstrated adaptability to dynamically varying robot body part masses during training and maintained high average performance (ranging from **5492.9** to **6697.8**) when evaluated on static mass variations of ±**50%** for individual parts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Stable and effective policy learning for humanoid locomotion under partial observability is achievable in high-dimensional, continuous control environments, demonstrating success in the challenging Humanoid-v4 benchmark where previous attempts struggled.
> - A parallel history encoder, capable of aggregating contextual information from a fixed window of past observations, can reconstruct sufficient latent state information for robust decision-making, outperforming traditional recurrent architectures in this complex domain.
> - Different observation attributes have varying criticality; velocity information is most critical, mass/inertia influences convergence speed, and force information appears least essential for successful humanoid locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robots capable of operating in diverse, unstructured environments represents a longstanding objective in robotics. Humanoid robots, with their anthropomorphic design, are considered suitable for tasks requiring physical dexterity and adaptability in human-centric spaces. While hardware advancements and motion planning techniques have enabled humanoids to perform complex actions such as climbing stairs and folding laundry, their control remains a challenge due to high dimensionality and complex dynamics.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated promise in addressing these control challenges in fully observed simulation settings. However, its efficacy significantly diminishes under conditions of partial observability, particularly in high-degree-of-freedom systems like humanoids. Partial observability is a characteristic of real-world robotic systems, resulting from factors such as incomplete sensor data, measurement noise, or bandwidth limitations. In such scenarios, the problem transitions from a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), necessitating mechanisms for inferring latent states from incomplete observations, often involving memory or belief inference.

Prior research in POMDPs commonly employs recurrent neural networks (RNNs), including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks or more recent architectures like Mamba, to encode temporal dependencies and manage incomplete information. While these memory-based approaches have shown some success in simpler POMDP benchmarks, their application to highly complex and high-dimensional environments, such as humanoid locomotion tasks, has not consistently demonstrated stable training or effective policy learning. The Humanoid-v4 environment in Gymnasium serves as a benchmark for high-dimensional continuous control, and prior to this work, no approach had successfully demonstrated stable training of humanoid policies with incomplete state information in this specific environment. This research addresses this gap by proposing a method capable of learning effective policies under partial observability in this challenging benchmark.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to achieve stable and effective policy learning for humanoid locomotion under conditions of partial observability in a high-dimensional continuous control environment. Specifically, the authors aim to demonstrate success in the Gymnasium Humanoid-v4 environment, a benchmark task where the robot's objective is to walk forward as fast as possible without falling, receiving rewards for upright posture and forward movement, and penalties for excessive actions and external contact forces.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and observations within the current state of reinforcement learning for robotics:
- **Challenge of Partial Observability in High-Dimensional RL:** Despite progress in RL, its effectiveness in high-degree-of-freedom systems like humanoids under partial observability remains largely unproven. Real-world robotic systems inherently operate with incomplete sensory information, making it crucial for control policies to be robust to such conditions.
- **Lack of Prior Success in Benchmark Environments:** The Humanoid-v4 environment is a recognized, complex benchmark. The authors note that no prior work has demonstrated stable training of humanoid policies using incomplete state information in this specific environment, highlighting a significant research gap.
- **Limitations of Existing Memory-Based Approaches:** Common approaches for POMDPs, such as those utilizing RNNs (e.g., LSTMs, Mamba), have shown promise in simpler tasks but have not translated effectively to the complexity of humanoid control. This suggests a need for alternative temporal encoding strategies.
- **Need for Sensor-Efficient and Robust Policies:** Developing policies that can perform effectively with less than full state information can reduce reliance on extensive and potentially expensive sensor setups, improve robustness to sensor failures, and enable more adaptable real-world robotic systems.
- **Hypothesis on Contextual Information Reconstruction:** The authors hypothesize that a mechanism capable of reconstructing essential contextual information from recent observations, even incomplete ones, can enable robust decision-making comparable to, or potentially exceeding, policies trained with full state access.

By addressing these points, the research aims to contribute a novel model-free RL method that can bridge the performance gap between fully observed and partially observed learning in high-dimensional robotic tasks, specifically demonstrating this capability in humanoid locomotion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a novel model-free reinforcement learning method designed to handle partial observability in high-dimensional continuous control tasks. The approach is evaluated in the Gymnasium Humanoid-v4 environment.

#### 3.1. Environment and State Representation

The experiments were conducted in the Mujoco Humanoid-v4 environment, characterized by a 348-dimensional state space and a 17-dimensional continuous action space. The agent's goal is to control a bipedal humanoid robot through continuous joint torques to maintain balance and achieve locomotion.
The full 348-dimensional state is semantically categorized into four attributes:
- **Position**: Position values of body parts (22 dimensions).
- **Velocity**: Velocities of body parts and center-of-mass-based velocities (101 dimensions).
- **Mass/inertia**: Mass and inertia of rigid body parts (130 dimensions).
- **Force**: Actuator force at each joint and external forces on body parts (95 dimensions).

#### 3.2. Simulating Partial Observability

To simulate partial observability, specific attributes were selectively removed from the full state vector. Joint position information was retained in all configurations, as its removal was observed to break the causal link required for learning. The following configurations for partial observability were tested by removing combinations of Velocity (V), Mass/inertia (M), and Force (F):
- **Removal of One Attribute**: Remove V, Remove M, Remove F. These configurations retained approximately 63% to 73% of the original state dimensions.
- **Removal of Two Attributes**: Remove VM, Remove VF, Remove MF. These configurations retained approximately 34% to 44% of the original state dimensions.

#### 3.3. Proposed Method: Novel History Encoder

The core of the proposed method is a novel model-free RL approach that utilizes a history encoder. Unlike traditional memory-based approaches (e.g., RNNs, LSTMs) that process sequences sequentially, this encoder processes a fixed-length sequence of past observations in parallel. The design premise is that by treating each time step in the history as equally important, the network can flexibly combine information across the observation window, potentially inferring complete latent states from successive observation sequences. The specific architecture and mechanisms of this encoder are not detailed in this paper, but are stated to be discussed in a separate report. The encoder is integrated into a standard model-free algorithm.

#### 3.4. Baselines and Comparison

The proposed method was compared against two state-of-the-art memory-based approaches designed for POMDPs:
- **RMF (Recurrent Model-Free RL)**: Utilizes LSTM networks for temporal encoding.
- **ODERMF (ODE-based Recurrent Architecture)**: An ODE-based recurrent network for POMDPs.
As a benchmark for performance under full observability, **TD3 (Twin-Delayed DDPG)**, a standard model-free algorithm, was trained with full state observations.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

- **Implementation Framework**: All models were implemented within a unified training framework to ensure fair comparison.
- **Network Capacity and Hyperparameters**: Network capacity was matched, and consistent hyperparameters were used across all methods. Most hyperparameters followed the original TD3 configuration.
- **Training Duration**: Each method was trained for 1 million (1M) or 3 million (3M) gradient steps across various partially observable settings.
- **Random Seeds**: Experiments were conducted using five different random seeds to account for variability.
- **Performance Metric**: Performance was evaluated using episodic return curves averaged over the seeds. Numerical records included the average maximum reward and the average reward during the last 25% of training.
- **Computational Resources**: Experiments were performed on NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs (32 GB HBM2 memory) and Intel Xeon Gold 6148 CPU cores. Each 3M-step training run required approximately 47.7 hours. GPU utilization was around 10%, with environment interactions being the primary computational bottleneck.

#### 3.6. Adaptability to Varying Robot Properties

To assess the policy's robustness, additional experiments were conducted where the mass of various body parts (hands, shins, thighs, upper arms, pelvis, torso) was varied dynamically between 50% and 100% of their default values every 10,000 steps during training. A learned policy from one such training run (with upper arm mass variation) was then evaluated on static mass variations of ±50% for each body part.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from extensive experimentation in the Gymnasium Humanoid-v4 environment under various partial observability configurations.

#### 4.1. Removal of One Observation Attribute

In configurations where a single observation attribute (Velocity, Mass/inertia, or Force) was removed, the proposed method demonstrated substantial performance advantages over baseline POMDP algorithms.
- **Comparison with Baselines**: RMF and ODERMF algorithms exhibited significantly lower performance, struggling to learn effective policies in this high-dimensional control task. For instance, in the "Remove M" configuration, RMF achieved an average maximum reward of 721.1 and ODERMF 580.7, whereas the proposed method achieved 4844.6 (1M steps).
- **Performance of Proposed Method**: The proposed method consistently outperformed both RMF and ODERMF across all single-attribute removal scenarios. It achieved performance comparable to, and in some cases surpassed, the TD3 baseline trained with full state observations.
 - **Remove V**: With velocity removed, the proposed method (1M steps) achieved an average maximum reward of 4410.3 and a last 25% reward of 3658.2 ± 202.7, comparable to TD3's 4547.2 / 4092.2 ± 133.2. Extending training to 3M steps further improved performance to 5137.1 / 4573.4 ± 209.1.
 - **Remove M**: With mass/inertia removed, the proposed method (1M steps) achieved 4844.6 / 4247.8 ± 230.9, notably surpassing TD3's full observation performance. At 3M steps, it reached 5037.3 / 4387.3 ± 214.2.
 - **Remove F**: With force information removed, the proposed method (1M steps) achieved the highest performance in this category, with 5625.3 / 5017.3 ± 178.8, also surpassing TD3. At 3M steps, it further improved to 6680.8 / 5776.4 ± 238.1.
- **Criticality of Attributes**: Velocity was identified as the most critical attribute for performance, followed by mass and inertia, which influenced convergence speed. Force information appeared to be the least essential.

#### 4.2. Removal of Two Observation Attributes

When two observation attributes were removed, posing a more significant challenge due to the reduction of observable state dimensions to approximately one-third, the proposed method continued to demonstrate learning capabilities, although with varying degrees of success depending on the combination. RMF and ODERMF were not included in these comparisons due to their failure in single-attribute removal scenarios.
- **Remove VM (Velocity and Mass/inertia)**: This configuration proved the most challenging. The proposed method (1M steps) achieved 1339.0 / 974.6 ± 535.3. Even with 3M training steps, performance was 4691.7 / 2978.3 ± 1310.6, indicating slower convergence and greater difficulty in reconstructing dynamic state information from position and force alone.
- **Remove VF (Velocity and Force)**: Similar to "Remove VM," this configuration hindered the estimation of state transitions. The proposed method (1M steps) reached 1781.5 / 1382.1 ± 730.9, and at 3M steps, 1951.5 / 1308.7 ± 505.8. Position combined with mass and inertia was not sufficient to infer full motion states efficiently.
- **Remove MF (Mass/inertia and Force)**: In this scenario, with velocity information retained, the proposed method converged relatively quickly and achieved high performance. At 1M steps, it reached 4964.9 / 4537.3 ± 651.0, and at 3M steps, it attained 5707.7 / 5175.7 ± 691.7, which surpassed the TD3 baseline trained with full observations. This suggests that observable velocity cues are sufficient to model movement and enable successful learning even when other attributes are absent.

#### 4.3. Learning under Varying Body Masses

The research also investigated the adaptability of the proposed method to changes in robot properties, specifically body part masses.
- **Dynamic Mass Variation During Training**: Experiments where the mass of individual body parts (hands, shins, thighs, upper arms, pelvis, torso) was varied randomly between 50% and 100% every 10,000 steps during training demonstrated that the training process successfully adapted to these mass changes.
- **Policy Evaluation with Static Mass Variation**: A policy trained with varying upper arm masses was evaluated by independently varying the mass of each body part by ±50%. The average performance across ten evaluations for different parts ranged from 5492.9 (Thighs) to 6697.8 (Pelvis). These results indicate that the learned policy exhibits adaptability to significant mass variations across all tested body parts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of reinforcement learning for high-dimensional robotic control, particularly under conditions of partial observability.

The primary significance lies in the demonstration of the first successful instance of stable training for humanoid locomotion under partial observability in the benchmark Gymnasium Humanoid-v4 environment. Previous attempts in this specific, complex setting had not yielded comparable results. This success challenges the notion that high-dimensional POMDPs necessarily require full state access or highly complex belief inference mechanisms for effective policy learning.

Key impacts and implications include:
- **Bridging the Reality Gap**: By enabling effective learning with incomplete state information, this work moves RL closer to real-world robotic applications, where full observability is rarely achievable due due to sensor limitations, noise, or occlusions.
- **Reduced Sensor Reliance**: The demonstrated ability to achieve high performance using only one-third to two-thirds of the original state dimensions suggests that complex robotic systems might not require extensive sensor suites. This could lead to simpler, lighter, and more cost-effective robot designs.
- **Enhanced Training Robustness**: The proposed method exhibits robustness across various configurations of missing observations and even demonstrates adaptability to variations in robot physical properties, such as body part masses. This robustness is critical for deploying robots in dynamic and uncertain environments.
- **Novel Temporal Encoding Strategy**: The introduction of a parallel history encoder that flexibly combines information from a fixed-length observation sequence offers an alternative to sequential memory-based methods. Its effectiveness in a complex domain suggests that strategic context aggregation can be a powerful mechanism for latent state reconstruction and may outperform traditional recurrent architectures in certain settings.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The findings motivate further investigation into lightweight temporal encoding strategies. Future work could explore the explicit mechanisms by which the encoder reconstructs missing information and exploits redundancy, or its application to other complex real-world robotic control tasks beyond locomotion, potentially leading to more generalizable and sensor-efficient model-free policies.

In summary, this research contributes to the development of more practical and robust reinforcement learning solutions for humanoid robotics, with implications for designing more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems.
