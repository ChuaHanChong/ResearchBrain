# FELT: Generating Tactile Signals from Vision for Visuo-Tactile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A vision-to-tactile generative network named FELT synthesizes per-finger pressure images from RGB observations, facilitating visuo-tactile policy learning and real-time deployment without physical tactile sensors. The framework demonstrates improved tactile prediction over baselines and enhances robot manipulation success rates across various contact-rich tasks, matching or exceeding real tactile sensor performance in some settings.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation in unstructured environments is limited by the high cost and complexity of collecting diverse robotic data, particularly for tactile information.
- Tactile data, crucial for contact-rich tasks and complementary to vision, is scarce due to specialized, fragile, and non-standardized sensors, making large-scale collection prohibitive.
- Existing large-scale robot learning datasets frequently lack tactile information, creating a data collection bottleneck for developing robust visuo-tactile policies.

## Method

- **FELT**, an attention-based ==cross-modal generative network==, extracts visual features from fisheye RGB images using a frozen ==DINOv2 Vision Transformer==.
- It employs a ==tactile query decoder== with separate processing branches for each finger, augmented with learnable queries and a ==gated cross-panel exchange module== to synthesize physically consistent tactile pressure images.
- A ==panel-aware convolutional readout head== predicts per-cell contact logits and pressure intensity, trained with a composite loss function targeting both contact detection and intensity estimation.

## Results

- FELT achieved superior tactile prediction quality compared to Nearest Neighbor and MAE baselines, demonstrating a lower LPIPS (**0.191** vs. **0.199** for MAE) and a more accurate Energy Ratio (**1.123** vs. **4.597** for MAE).
- Visuo-tactile policies augmented with FELT-generated signals consistently improved success rates over vision-only baselines in four contact-rich tasks, matching real tactile performance in Cup Nesting and Triangle Peg Insertion.
- Policies utilizing FELT's latent tactile features performed competitively, even surpassing real tactile in Triangle Peg Insertion (**90%** vs. **70%** success rate), while enabling sensor-free policy training and deployment with approximately **20 ms** latency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sufficient cues exist within the visual context of a robot's gripper and object to accurately predict detailed pressure distributions on the gripper's fingers.
> - Incorporating the physical topology of dual-finger tactile sensors, such as separate processing branches and cross-panel information exchange, is crucial for generating accurate and balanced tactile signals.
> - Intermediate latent features from the generative model can serve as compact, effective tactile representations for downstream policies, sometimes outperforming raw real tactile data in low-data regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has achieved considerable advancements, yet its widespread deployment in unstructured environments faces limitations, primarily due to the high cost and complexity of collecting diverse robotic data. A significant challenge in this context is the scarcity of tactile data, which is crucial for manipulation tasks, especially when visual information is occluded or ambiguous. Vision and touch provide complementary information: vision offers global context, while tactile feedback delivers local, high-resolution contact signals. However, tactile sensors are often specialized, fragile, and difficult to standardize and calibrate, making large-scale data collection prohibitive.

Previous efforts in robotic tactile sensing have focused on developing various sensor types, improving their open-source accessibility, and benchmarking their performance. Concurrently, research in visuo-tactile manipulation has demonstrated that integrating both modalities enhances task performance compared to using either alone. Despite this, existing large-scale robot learning datasets frequently lack tactile information, leading to a data collection bottleneck for visuo-tactile policies.

Data augmentation has been widely applied in robot learning to expand visual datasets synthetically. While some methods explore tactile world models for predicting future tactile states, or vision-to-touch generation for specific tasks like pushing, there remains a gap in scalable visuo-tactile augmentation that can generate physically consistent tactile pressure images from general RGB observations. This work positions itself within this landscape by developing a method to synthesize tactile observations from visual input, thereby augmenting existing vision-only datasets with tactile information. It aims to reduce the dependency on physical tactile sensors during policy training and deployment, complementing efforts in sensor development and visuo-tactile policy learning by addressing the tactile data scarcity problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning-based framework, named Feature-Extracted Latent Tactile (FELT), that can synthesize per-finger pressure tactile images from RGB observations. This framework aims to reduce the necessity for collecting data with tactile-equipped robots and to augment existing vision-only datasets with tactile information.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges in robotic manipulation:
- **High Cost of Data Collection:** Current large-scale imitation learning approaches require extensive teleoperation data, which is costly due to specialized hardware and expert operators. This data typically captures only visual observations.
- **Complementary Nature of Vision and Touch:** Vision provides global context, while tactile feedback offers local, high-resolution contact signals. Combining these modalities has been shown to improve manipulation performance, particularly in contact-rich tasks where vision alone may be insufficient.
- **Scarcity and Difficulty of Tactile Data Collection:** Tactile data is notably difficult to acquire due to the specialized, fragile, and non-standardized nature of tactile sensors. Their deployment often requires careful calibration, further complicating large-scale data collection.
- **Underexplored Tactile Data Augmentation:** While visual data augmentation is common, tactile data augmentation remains relatively unexplored despite the growing availability of large-scale vision-only datasets that lack corresponding tactile information. This represents a significant missed opportunity to leverage existing data more effectively.

The central insight motivating FELT is the hypothesis that when the robot gripper, the manipulated object, and the contact region are visible in an RGB image (e.g., from a fisheye camera), the surrounding visual context contains sufficient cues to predict the resultant pressure distribution on the gripper's fingers. By leveraging this strong cross-modal correlation, FELT seeks to bridge the data gap and enable more robust visuo-tactile policy learning without the continuous reliance on physical tactile sensors. The ultimate goal is to enable visuo-tactile policy learning for contact-rich manipulation tasks with reduced or eliminated requirements for physical tactile sensors at the policy training or deployment stages.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FELT framework is an attention-based, cross-modal generative network designed to synthesize physically consistent tactile observations from visual context.

#### 3.1. FELT Architecture

- **Visual Input Processing:** Given an RGB image from a fisheye camera, the model first resizes it to 224x224 pixels and applies ImageNet normalization. Invalid border pixels from the fisheye lens are masked, and ArUco tags are inpainted.
- **Visual Feature Extraction:** A frozen, pretrained DINOv2 Vision Transformer (ViT) (specifically, DINOv2-B/14) is used to extract visual tokens. To capture a range of information from low-level texture to high-level semantics, the model averages features from multiple network depths (layers 3, 7, and 11). The CLS token is discarded, retaining 256 spatial patch tokens, each 768-dimensional. The visual encoder remains frozen throughout the training of FELT.
- **Tactile Query Decoder:** To respect the physical topology of dual-finger tactile sensors, FELT employs separate processing branches for the left and right finger panels.
 - Each branch is assigned its own set of 12x32 learnable query embeddings, matching the resolution of the physical sensor grid. These queries are augmented with 2D positional embeddings, factorized by row and column.
 - Each branch features an independent transformer decoder, where its queries attend to the extracted visual tokens via cross-attention.
 - **Gated Cross-Panel Exchange Module:** After each decoder layer, a gated cross-panel exchange module facilitates information sharing between the two branches. These gates are initialized close to zero, allowing the model to learn when and how to coordinate contact information between the fingers.
- **Panel-Aware Convolutional Readout Head:** The decoded queries from each branch are reshaped into 2D feature maps (12x32) and projected to 256 channels. A learned side embedding is introduced to distinguish between the left and right sensors. A shared convolutional trunk, consisting of three residual depthwise-separable convolution blocks, processes these feature maps. Finally, two 1x1 heads predict per-cell contact logits and nonnegative pressure intensity for each tactile grid cell, resulting in a concatenated 12x64 tactile image output (representing two 12x32 panels).
- **Latent Feature Extraction:** For certain deployment scenarios, intermediate feature maps (z_L_t, z_R_t ∈ R^(12x32x256)) are extracted after the convolutional readout blocks but before the final contact and intensity heads. These are spatially mean-pooled, concatenated, and projected into a 768-dimensional token.

#### 3.2. Training Protocol

- **Dataset:** FELT is trained on a large dataset of paired visuo-tactile demonstrations (Zhu et al.), comprising over 2,700 handheld UMI-style demonstrations and 2.6 million RGB-tactile pairs, split 80/20 for training/evaluation.
- **Loss Function:** A composite loss function L(ϕ) is minimized, which decomposes tactile prediction into two components:
 - **Contact Loss (L_contact):** Addresses binary contact detection using a combination of focal loss and Dice loss. This combination is chosen to handle the class imbalance between contact and non-contact regions effectively. A logit regularizer is also included.
 - **Intensity Loss (L_intensity):** Estimates pressure intensity using a value-weighted Huber loss, which prioritizes accuracy for high-pressure cells, thus capturing both the spatial extent and magnitude of contact forces.
 - Two auxiliary regularizers are used: background suppression (penalizing intensity outside dilated contact regions) and a normalized shape loss (comparing pressure distributions after normalizing by total energy).
- **Optimization:** The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2 × 10^(-4), weight decay of 0.003, and a batch size of 128 for 40 epochs. A linear warmup and cosine decay learning rate schedule are employed. An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) checkpoint is used for evaluation.
- **Data Augmentation:** During training, RGB images undergo color jitter, Gaussian noise, gamma perturbation, and small in-plane rotations.

#### 3.3. Deployment Modes

Once trained, FELT can be deployed in three distinct modes:
- **Offline Augmentation:** Synthesized tactile images are generated for existing vision-only trajectories, effectively converting them into visuo-tactile demonstrations for subsequent policy training.
- **Online Deployment:** Generated tactile images replace physical sensor readings in real-time during policy execution, enabling sensor-free deployment of visuo-tactile policies.
- **Latent Feature Deployment:** Instead of generating full tactile images, intermediate latent feature maps from FELT are used as compact tactile representations. These features can be directly fed to a downstream policy, eliminating the need for real tactile data during both policy training and deployment.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Hardware:** An xArm7 robot with a UMI-style gripper instrumented with FlexiTac pressure-array sensors (12x32 per-finger) and a wrist-mounted GoPro Hero 9 fisheye camera.
- **Policy Dataset:** 60 GELLO demonstrations per task (240 total, ~520K frames) were collected on the xArm for training task-specific Diffusion Policies. This dataset is separate from the generator training and testing data.
- **Tasks:** Four contact-rich manipulation tasks: Tube Insertion, Cup Nesting, Eraser Wiping, and Triangle Peg Insertion.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Tactile prediction quality is assessed using LPIPS, Energy Ratio, Frame Accuracy, and Panel Asymmetry Error. Policy success is measured by per-stage and final success rates over 20 real-robot trials per task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results demonstrating FELT's performance in tactile prediction and its impact on downstream manipulation policies.

#### 4.1. Tactile Prediction Quality

- **Performance against Baselines:** FELT consistently outperformed two baselines: Nearest Neighbor retrieval and a Masked Autoencoder (MAE).
 - FELT achieved an LPIPS score of 0.191, compared to 0.199 for MAE and 0.342 for Nearest Neighbor, indicating better perceptual similarity to ground-truth tactile images.
 - Its Energy Ratio was 1.123, which is close to the ideal value of 1, suggesting accurate total-pressure calibration. This contrasts sharply with MAE's Energy Ratio of 4.597, which implies severe pressure overestimation.
 - FELT's Frame Accuracy, measuring panel-level contact accuracy, was 0.816, higher than MAE's 0.746 and Nearest Neighbor's 0.414.
 - While MAE had a slightly lower Panel Asymmetry Error (0.431 vs. FELT's 0.738), its significant pressure overestimation diminishes the practical utility of this metric.
- **Ablation Studies:** Architectural ablations highlighted the importance of FELT's design choices:
 - **Removing the gated cross-panel exchange module:** Led to an increase in Panel Asymmetry Error from 0.738 to 1.175, indicating issues with left-right force balance and capturing asymmetric contact patterns.
 - **Replacing separate per-finger branches with a single dual-branch decoder:** Resulted in a severe underestimation of tactile magnitude, with Frame Accuracy dropping to 0.616 and Panel Asymmetry Error tripling to 2.225. This confirms the importance of respecting the physical sensor topology.
 - **Replacing the panel-aware convolutional readout with pointwise MLP heads:** Reduced Frame Accuracy to 0.665, suggesting that local spatial processing is more effective than pointwise decoding for tactile image generation.
- **Robustness to Thresholds:** A sensitivity analysis of Frame Accuracy showed that FELT's superior performance and the relative ranking of models remained consistent across a wide range of evaluation thresholds.

#### 4.2. Downstream Policy Success Rate

- **Improved Policy Performance:** Across four contact-rich manipulation tasks (Tube Insertion, Cup Nesting, Eraser Wiping, Triangle Peg Insertion), visuo-tactile policies informed by FELT-generated signals consistently improved success rates over vision-only baselines.
 - **Vision + Real Tactile** policies generally outperformed **Vision Only** policies.
 - **Vision + FELT Tactile** (trained with real tactile, deployed with generated tactile) also improved over **Vision Only** on all final task metrics. It matched the performance of **Vision + Real Tactile** on Cup Nesting and Triangle Peg Insertion, while showing slightly lower, but still improved, performance on Tube Insertion and Eraser Wiping. The generator's latency (~20 ms) supported real-time closed-loop control.
 - **Vision + FELT Features** (trained and deployed with latent tactile features from FELT, requiring no real tactile sensor) performed competitively with **Vision + Real Tactile**, notably achieving a higher success rate on Triangle Peg Insertion (90% vs. 70%). This suggests that in low-data settings (60 demonstrations per task), the smoother, averaged contact representation provided by FELT's latent features, pretrained on large datasets, might be more stable and easier for the policy to learn from than potentially noisy real tactile images.
- **Task-Specific Gains:** Tactile information, whether real or generated by FELT, primarily improved performance in contact-critical stages of manipulation tasks (e.g., reorientation, insertion, nesting, wiping), rather than initial grasping, where visual cues are often sufficient. For example, in Cup Nesting, tactile feedback aided cup alignment despite gripper occlusion.
- **Ablation Impact on Policies:** Policies deployed with tactile images generated by ablated FELT models showed reduced performance compared to the full FELT model, particularly for the "no-panel-exchange" and "no-dual-branch decoder" variants. This directly links the quality of tactile generation to downstream policy success.
- **Additional Comparisons (Supplementary Material):**
 - A policy trained with real tactile data but deployed with zero tactile input (**Vision + Zero Tactile**) performed worse than the **Vision Only** baseline on three of four tasks, indicating that policies actively rely on learned tactile representations.
 - Using generic DINOv2 visual features as tactile input (**Vision + DINOv2 Features**) underperformed FELT Features, suggesting FELT's benefit comes from learned contact-relevant representations rather than just an additional visual encoder.
 - A policy trained and deployed entirely with FELT-generated tactile images (**Vision + FELT Tactile Train**) performed comparably to the configuration that used real tactile during training, indicating that distribution shift between real and generated data might not be a major bottleneck.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Observations and Failure Modes

- **Successful Rollouts:** Qualitative examples showed FELT successfully generating tactile images that track evolving contact states accurately during grasping, reorientation, nesting, and wiping, reflecting realistic changes in intensity and pattern.
- **Failure Modes:** Identified failure cases included release-position errors (e.g., tube falling outside receptacle), target-object collisions (e.g., knocking over cups), insufficient contact force during wiping, and object-orientation errors during insertion. While tactile information reduced these failures, residual errors indicate ongoing challenges in fully leveraging contact signals, especially under heavy occlusion or for highly variable task parameters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FELT represents a significant contribution to robot learning and visuo-tactile manipulation, with several potential impacts:

- **Addressing the Tactile Data Bottleneck:** The primary significance of FELT is its ability to synthesize tactile signals from vision, thereby reducing the critical dependency on large-scale, costly, and fragile tactile sensor-equipped data collection. This can accelerate research and development in contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- **Scalable Visuo-Tactile Policy Learning:** By enabling the augmentation of readily available vision-only datasets with synthesized tactile information, FELT paves the way for training more robust and capable visuo-tactile policies on a larger scale. This expands the potential applications of robotic manipulation in diverse and unstructured environments.
- **Enabling Sensor-Free Deployment:** FELT facilitates the deployment of visuo-tactile policies without the need for physical tactile sensors at runtime. This lowers hardware costs, reduces system complexity, and improves the overall robustness and reliability of robotic systems, as they are no longer dependent on potentially delicate physical sensors in operational settings.
- **Leveraging Latent Representations:** The finding that latent tactile features can achieve performance comparable to, or even surpass, real tactile sensing in low-data regimes is particularly impactful. This suggests that learned, compact representations of touch may offer a more stable and effective signal for policy learning than raw tactile sensor data, especially when demonstration data is limited or noisy.
- **Facilitating Future Research:** The open-source release of visuo-tactile datasets, including both real and FELT-generated tactile channels, provides a valuable resource for the research community. This supports further investigation into visuo-tactile policy learning, cross-modal perception, and advanced data augmentation techniques.
- **Foundation for Advanced Perception:** FELT's approach of inferring touch from vision could inspire future work on richer perceptual models that can predict a wider range of physical interactions from visual cues, even in cases of partial occlusion or for different types of tactile sensors. It also highlights the importance of incorporating sensor topology into cross-modal generative models.

While FELT relies on the availability of an initial paired visuo-tactile dataset for generator training and its effectiveness depends on visible contact regions, it offers a promising direction towards more scalable, cost-effective, and deployable visuo-tactile robot learning solutions.
