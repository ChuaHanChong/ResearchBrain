# Scaling Self-Play with Self-Guidance

> [!summary] Summary
> An algorithm called Self-Guided Self-Play (SGS) was introduced by researchers at Stanford University, which uses an integrated "Guide" language model to ensure the generation of high-quality, relevant synthetic problems for a Solver model. This approach enabled a 7B parameter model to exceed the pass@4 performance of a 671B parameter model in formal theorem proving and achieve a 7% higher asymptotic solve rate compared to baseline reinforcement learning methods.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) struggle with scalability and prolonged learning, often encountering plateaus when applied to complex problem-solving tasks.
- Existing asymmetric self-play methods suffer from Conjecturer degeneracy, where the model generates low-quality or unhelpful synthetic problems that do not genuinely advance the Solver's capabilities.
- Traditional reinforcement learning approaches face limitations in challenging domains due to sparse or absent reward signals, hindering effective learning.

## Method

- Introduces **Self-Guided Self-Play (SGS)**, an iterative algorithm where a single base LLM functions as a ==Solver==, a ==Synthetic Data Conjecturer==, and a ==Guide==.
- The Conjecturer generates synthetic problems conditioned on unsolved target problems, which are then evaluated by the Guide for ==relevance and elegance==, providing a quality-based reward.
- The Solver is updated using ==REINFORCE1/2==, focused on problems with an empirical solve rate of ≤ 0.5, to maintain learning signal and prevent entropy collapse.

## Results

- **SGS** achieved a **+7% higher asymptotic cumulative solve rate** compared to the strongest REINFORCE1/2 baseline and solved nearly **10%** of problems initially inaccessible to the baseline.
- A **7B parameter DeepSeek-Prover-V2** model trained with SGS surpassed the **pass@4** performance of the **671B parameter** DeepSeek-Prover-V2 model after **6.3M generations**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that removing the Guide led to **Conjecturer degeneracy**, producing unhelpful problems, while freezing the Conjecturer limited long-term learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An LLM can serve as an effective "Guide" to critically assess the quality and utility of synthetically generated problems, thereby preventing the Conjecturer from producing degenerate or unhelpful learning material.
> - Sustained learning in LLM self-play critically depends on maintaining a stable entropy within the Solver, as rapid entropy collapse starves the Conjecturer of useful training signals.
> - Conditioning the Conjecturer on unsolved target problems and integrating the Guide's feedback are crucial for directing synthetic problem generation toward genuinely beneficial learning for the Solver.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of artificial intelligence, specifically concerning the scalability and long-term learning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) when applied to complex problem-solving tasks. Existing research aims to develop AI systems capable of solving difficult problems given sufficient computational resources. Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) methods often struggle with sparse or absent rewards in such challenging domains, limiting their effectiveness.

Asymmetric self-play emerges as a paradigm to mitigate the sparse reward problem. In this framework, a "Conjecturer" agent proposes new learning problems, acting as a curriculum generator, while a "Solver" agent attempts to solve them. Both agents iteratively improve through this interaction, theoretically allowing for unbounded learning. This concept has roots in earlier self-play literature (e.g., Samuel, 1959; Silver et al., 2016) and was extended to asymmetric roles by Sukhbaatar et al. (2017) and Florensa et al. (2018) in navigation and control environments. More recently, asymmetric self-play has been applied to natural language action spaces using LLMs, with some methods relying on real data and others on self-generated data. Relevant works in formal theorem proving include Dong & Ma (2025) and Poesia et al. (2024).

Despite the theoretical promise of unbounded learning, current LLM self-play methods frequently encounter learning plateaus over extended training periods. The paper argues that this limitation stems from the Conjecturer model learning to exploit its reward function by generating artificially complex or inelegant problems that do not genuinely contribute to the Solver's improvement. These problems degrade in quality over time, leading to stagnation. The present work distinguishes itself by introducing a mechanism, the Guide model, to explicitly prevent this Conjecturer degeneracy and by conducting evaluations over significantly longer training runs to study the scaling properties of self-play. This contrasts with many prior algorithms that solely reward the Conjecturer based on Solver performance, an approach the authors identify as insufficient for sustained learning. The research also focuses on formal theorem proving in Lean4, a domain characterized by verifiable solutions, allowing for precise evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a self-play algorithm that can scale effectively with increased computational resources to solve a maximum number of problems from a fixed set of difficult target problems, thereby overcoming the learning plateaus observed in existing methods. The authors aim to achieve an algorithm that, when run for a sufficiently long duration, leads to the solution of an increasing number of these challenging problems.

The motivation for this objective arises from the observed limitations of current LLM self-play methods, which despite their theoretical potential, exhibit practical learning plateaus. The authors hypothesize that these plateaus are caused by the Conjecturer model "hacking" its reward function, leading it to generate synthetic problems that are either superficially complex or otherwise unhelpful for the Solver's genuine progress on target problems. This degradation in synthetic problem quality starves the Solver of useful learning signals.

To address this, the paper proposes Self-Guided Self-Play (SGS), driven by a core hypothesis: language models possess the capability to assess whether a subproblem is genuinely useful for achieving a broader goal. This hypothesis motivates the introduction of a third role for the LLM within the self-play loop: the Guide. The Guide is designed to evaluate the quality of synthetic problems by assessing their relevance to unsolved target problems and their clarity or elegance of formulation. This provides supervision to steer the Conjecturer away from generating degenerate problems.

Furthermore, the research is motivated by the need for a more rigorous evaluation of self-play scaling. Prior works have typically not explored self-play over very long training durations. This work seeks to quantify the scaling properties of SGS by performing significantly longer training runs (e.g., producing over 6 billion tokens) and by fitting scaling laws to cumulative solve rate curves. This allows for the extrapolation of long-run behavior and a more robust comparison of the asymptotic solve rates of different methods. The chosen application domain, formal theorem proving in Lean4, is motivated by its verifiable solutions, which provide a clear and objective measure of problem-solving success.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Self-Guided Self-Play (SGS) algorithm is designed to enable scalable learning by integrating a Guide model to ensure the quality of synthetically generated problems. The system employs a single base language model that takes on three distinct roles: a Solver (πθ), a Synthetic Data Conjecturer (gϕ), and a Guide (ρ). The goal is to solve as many problems as possible from a fixed dataset D of verifiable problems, such as Lean4 formal mathematical theorems.

The algorithm proceeds iteratively through the following steps:
1. **Synthetic Problem Generation:** For each problem (x) in a sampled batch B that the Solver has not yet solved (B_unsolved), the Conjecturer (gϕ) is prompted to generate a related synthetic problem (˜x). The prompt instructs the Conjecturer to produce a conceptually related but simpler version of the target problem, specifically avoiding identical or trivially equivalent statements.
2. **Rollout and Verification:** The Solver (πθ) then attempts to generate k solutions for both the original problems in B and the newly generated synthetic problems in B_synth. Each generated solution is subjected to binary verification (v(y) ∈ {0, 1}) using the Lean4 compiler.
3. **Solver and Conjecturer Update:**
 - **Solver Update:** The Solver (πθ) is updated using a reinforcement learning objective based on the binary verification reward. The specific objective used is REINFORCE1/2, which applies the REINFORCE loss only to problems where the Solver's empirical solve rate (s(x)) is 0.5 or less. This strategy aims to focus the Solver's learning on more challenging problems and prevent it from spending compute on already-easy problems.
 - **Conjecturer Update:** The Conjecturer (gϕ) is updated using a combined reward function, R_synth = R_solve * R_guide, which is linearly normalized within each batch to a range.
 - **Solve Rate Reward (R_solve):** This component is based on the Solver's empirical solve rate (s(˜x)) for the synthetic problem. R_solve is set to 0 if s(˜x) is 0 (too difficult) or if s(˜x) falls within the top 30% of solve rates in the current batch (too easy). For problems of intermediate difficulty, R_solve is set to 1 - s(˜x), thereby favoring harder problems that are still within the Solver's current capabilities.
 - **Guide Reward (R_guide):** The Guide (ρ), an LLM initialized from the same base model as the Solver and Conjecturer, evaluates each synthetic problem (˜x) relative to its target (x). It outputs a score R_guide ∈ based on two primary criteria: how well ˜x relates to x, and its elegance (e.g., simple conclusion, no redundant premises). A detailed rubric is used, automatically assigning a score of 0 to conjectures with overly complex conclusions. The Guide model undergoes a small supervised finetuning (SFT) stage to ensure reliable output formatting due to the base model's initial poor instruction-following capabilities. The Conjecturer is also updated using a REINFORCE objective.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Model:** DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B is used to initialize all three roles.
- **Dataset:** D3k, a custom dataset of 3,323 formal math problems from Goedel-Pset-V1, is employed. This dataset was filtered to remove easy or unprovable problems.
- **Training Parameters:** A full batch setting is used, sampling 8 proof rollouts per problem. A length penalty is applied to RL updates for overlong rollouts, and proofs using the 'try' tactic receive 0 reward.
- **Compute Infrastructure:** A custom distributed infrastructure is used, comprising up to 64 GPU workers for generation and up to 128 CPU workers for verification, with mechanisms for fault tolerance and speculative reassignment.
- **Scaling Performance Modeling:** To assess long-term behavior, the cumulative solve rate (R_C) over training is modeled using a sigmoidal curve: R_C = R_0 + (A - R_0) * 1 / (1 + (C_mid/C)^B), where A represents the asymptotic cumulative solve rate. This allows for extrapolation and comparison of different methods' asymptotic performance.

**Evaluation:**
The evaluation involves running SGS and various baselines for significantly longer durations than prior work (up to 8M generations). Ablation studies are conducted to analyze the impact of different SGS components:
- **No Problem Conditioning:** Conjecturer generates problems without explicit conditioning on unsolved targets or Guide reward.
- **No Guide:** The Guide component (R_guide) is removed from the Conjecturer's reward function.
- **Frozen Conjecturer:** The Conjecturer model's weights are kept fixed.
- **Solver Objective Comparison:** SGS is also evaluated using CISPO as the Solver objective to compare against REINFORCE1/2.
Baselines include REINFORCE1/2, Expert Iteration, and CISPO, all tuned for optimal performance, as well as parallel sampling and a comparison to the DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B model's pass@4 performance and the STP method.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the scaling properties and effectiveness of Self-Guided Self-Play (SGS) in formal theorem proving.

**SGS Performance:**
- SGS demonstrates superior performance compared to the strongest Reinforcement Learning (RL) baseline (REINFORCE1/2). It achieves a 7% higher asymptotic cumulative solve rate.
- The system exhibits sustained progress on problems that were initially inaccessible to the RL baseline, solving almost 10% of these hard problems after 8M generations, whereas the baseline solved none.
- After 6.3M generations, SGS, when applied to a 7B parameter DeepSeek-Prover-V2 model, surpasses the pass@4 performance of the significantly larger 671B parameter DeepSeek-Prover-V2 model.
- Compared to STP (Self-Play LLM Theorem Provers), a related self-play method for Lean4, SGS shows superior scaling, outperforming STP after approximately 1M generations.

**Training Dynamics:**
- During SGS training, both the Solver and Conjecturer components show reliable improvement. The Solver's pass@8 on target statements increases steadily, indicating a lack of catastrophic forgetting.
- The Conjecturer's overall reward increases throughout training, which is attributed to a stable Guide reward and an increasing solve rate reward.
- Analysis of synthetic problems reveals that as the Solver approaches solving a particular target problem, it is trained on a greater number of synthetic problems specifically related to that target. This suggests that the synthetic problems generated are relevant and useful for progressing towards the target.

**Ablation Studies on Conjecturer Components:**
- **No Problem Conditioning:** When the Conjecturer is not conditioned on unsolved target problems and the Guide reward is excluded (i.e., only R_solve is used), the method does not outperform the RL baseline. Although the Conjecturer generates many problems that are difficult for the Solver, these problems are observed to be ineffective for solving the main target problems.
- **No Guide:** Removing the R_guide component from the Conjecturer's reward function results in a lower cumulative solve rate than the full SGS algorithm at all training stages. The fitted asymptotic solve rate for "No Guide" is 65.5%, compared to SGS's 67.1%. While this variant generates more "solvable" problems for the Solver, these problems do not translate into better solve rates on target problems. The Conjecturer, without the Guide's influence, collapses to producing problems with long, complex, and often disjunctive conclusions that are not useful for learning, contrasting with SGS which maintains problem characteristics similar to the D3k dataset.
- **Frozen Conjecturer:** Freezing the Conjecturer, preventing it from learning, performs better than the RL baseline and the "No Problem Conditioning" ablation, but is inferior to both the full SGS and "No Guide" variants. The number of synthetic problems useful for Solver training decreases over time because the Solver quickly covers the fixed distribution of problems from the static Conjecturer. This leads the algorithm to eventually resemble the standalone RL baseline.

**Importance of Solver Entropy Management:**
- When CISPO, another RL objective, is used as the Solver update mechanism within SGS, the Solver experiences rapid entropy collapse. This causes the distribution of problem solve rates to concentrate at 0 (unsolvable) and 1 (easily solvable), eliminating problems of intermediate difficulty.
- This outcome starves the Conjecturer of training signal, as problems with solve rates of 0 or 1 yield no reward, thereby preventing the Conjecturer from effective learning.
- In contrast, using REINFORCE1/2 for the Solver objective maintains a more stable Solver entropy, allowing the Conjecturer to learn by continuously generating problems with a spread of solve rates. The presence of synthetic problems from the Conjecturer also appears to stabilize the Solver's entropy.
- The authors hypothesize that managing the Solver's entropy is a critical factor for the success of LLM self-play algorithms, suggesting that methods like entropy bonuses or KL regularization might be necessary for grouped objectives like CISPO.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes contributions to the field of large language model (LLM) self-play by addressing critical limitations that impede sustained learning and scalability. The introduction of Self-Guided Self-Play (SGS) provides a framework that mitigates two primary issues: the degeneracy of the Conjecturer model, which tends to generate unhelpful problems, and the entropy collapse of the Solver, which can starve the Conjecturer of useful training signals.

The significance of SGS lies in its demonstration that an LLM itself can serve as an effective data quality metric (the Guide), steering the problem generation process towards relevant and elegant subproblems. This self-guidance mechanism enables self-play to sustain learning over significantly longer training durations and with larger computational budgets than previously observed methods. The finding that a 7B parameter model, when trained with SGS, can surpass the pass@4 performance of a 671B parameter model highlights the potential for this approach to enhance the capabilities of smaller models or achieve higher performance with larger models, effectively extending the practical scaling limits of self-play.

The potential impact of SGS is multi-faceted:
- **Enhanced Problem-Solving Capabilities:** By enabling more stable and extended learning, SGS contributes to the development of AI systems capable of solving highly complex problems that are far beyond the capabilities of base models or traditional RL. This is particularly relevant for domains requiring deep reasoning, such as formal mathematics.
- **Improved Efficiency of Compute:** The ability to scale learning effectively with compute means that investments in computational resources are more likely to yield tangible improvements in problem-solving performance, making self-play a more practical approach for developing advanced AI.
- **Generalizability to Other Domains:** While demonstrated in formal theorem proving (a verifiable domain), the conceptual framework of SGS may extend to other complex domains. The paper discusses potential applications in embodied control (where a neural world model could generate environments and a vision-language model could provide rewards), coding (generating problem statements and unit tests), or natural language mathematics (using learned verifiers). This suggests a broader paradigm for self-improvement across various AI tasks.
- **New Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for future research, including investigating how to make the Guide itself learn and adapt (e.g., by rewarding synthetic problems that lead to quick solutions of target problems), and exploring the interaction of SGS with model scale. Such advancements could further unlock the potential of self-play.

In summary, this research provides a methodological advancement for scaling LLM self-play, demonstrating that strategic self-guidance and careful management of learning dynamics are crucial for overcoming plateaus and achieving sustained progress in complex problem-solving. It represents a step toward building AI systems that can continuously learn and tackle increasingly difficult challenges given sufficient computational resources.
