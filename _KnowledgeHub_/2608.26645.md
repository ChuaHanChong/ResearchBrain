# FLARE: A Failure-Aware Framework for Autonomous Correction and Recovery in Visual-Language Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> FLARE makes vision-language-action policies recover from execution errors through a Retry/Reset paradigm. Perturbation-and-bridging augmentation injects random arm displacements plus recovery segments into demonstrations, decoupling robot pose from environment state, while an offline MLLM mines catastrophic failures to bootstrap object-centric reset skills trained as LoRA adapters and arbitrated by an online MLLM monitor. Average RoboMimic success reaches 84.0%.

## Problem

- VLAs train only on trajectory-monotonic, failure-free demonstrations, so policies learn spurious correlations between their own arm configuration and task progress rather than environment state.
- Success-centric datasets contain no recovery examples, and recomposition augmentation only restitches successful segments, so catastrophic states like a toppled cup have no corrective behavior.
- MLLM self-correction systems depend on fixed skill libraries with poor low-level adaptability, while reinforcement or instruction-based recovery suffers sample-efficiency and long-horizon generalization limits.

## Method

- An ID/OOD failure taxonomy separates errors where the environment stays valid but arm pose is novel (retry) from non-recoverable environment states needing a dedicated reset skill.
- Perturbation-and-bridging augmentation injects random pose perturbations between task segments plus kinematically interpolated bridging returns; only bridging-to-task subsequences are trained, breaking pose-state coupling.
- An offline MLLM analyst parses failure videos into JSON error type, reset target, error group and timestamp, bootstrapping object-centric reset demonstrations trained as separate LoRA adapters swapped by an online monitor.

## Results

- Averaged 84.0% success over nine contact-rich RoboMimic tasks, beating Phoenix at 57.8%, the π0.5 backbone at 72.2%, and even human-corrected Phoenix at 78.9%.
- Ablation: removing reset skills costs 3.5% average success, while oracle human reset instructions add 7%; real-robot Piper trials improved 62.5% to 75.0% and 45.0% to 55.0%.
- Gemini-2.5-Pro failure analysis hit 88%/96% retry-versus-reset accuracy; perturbation peaked around 30 degrees rotation and 0.7 translation, with larger perturbations lowering data-generation efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Brittleness can originate in the data regime rather than architecture: deliberately injecting off-manifold poses and their recoveries teaches policies to read progress from the environment.
> - Recovery is worth treating as separate learned skills, not one monolithic policy; modular adapters avoid conflicting gradients and let new reset behaviors be appended cheaply.
> - Augmentation diversity trades against valid-demonstration yield, and reset success collapses for fine repositioning (24%/20%), exposing gripper dexterity as the binding constraint.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm for Embodied Artificial Intelligence, integrating perception, language, and control to enable robots to execute complex, long-horizon tasks in open-world environments. Recent advancements, exemplified by models such as π₀ and OpenVLA, have demonstrated capabilities in generalizing to diverse manipulation tasks. However, a significant limitation of these systems is their brittleness. They exhibit a lack of robustness, failing due to minor perturbations, unexpected object contacts, or slight execution deviations, and do not possess an inherent ability for continuous self-correction. This fragility constitutes a barrier to the widespread deployment of truly autonomous robotic systems.

The authors identify the primary source of this brittleness not in model architecture, but in the nature of the training data. Current datasets for VLA models are typically costly to collect, leading to sparsity, a bias towards successful trajectories, and a lack of diversity in robot-environment configurations. Specifically, two fundamental issues are highlighted:
1. **Trajectory-monotonic data:** Demonstrations show the robot following a narrow manifold of poses correlated with task progress. This causes models to learn spurious associations between the robot's specific configuration and task success, making them unable to recover from valid but previously unseen robot poses (In-Distribution, or ID, errors).
2. **Success-centric data:** Datasets contain few, if any, examples of recovery from catastrophic failures (e.g., a toppled object). This absence of recovery data leaves the VLA unable to handle Out-of-Distribution (OOD) error states where the environment itself is invalid and requires restoration.

Previous research efforts to address robotic self-correction have explored several avenues. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have been used to provide semantic feedback and diagnose high-level failures (e.g., REFLECT), but these approaches often rely on fixed skill libraries, limiting their low-level adaptability. Reinforcement learning methods aim to explicitly learn recovery behaviors (e.g., SeRO) but frequently face challenges related to sample efficiency and generalization in complex, real-world scenarios. Data-driven paradigms, such as augmentation methods, have been employed to enhance robustness (e.g., Phoenix) but may not fully address the semantic aspects of failure reflection or generalize across diverse tasks and environments.

FLARE positions itself to address the limitations of existing VLAs and self-correction methods by proposing a unified, data-centric framework. It specifically targets the identified data-regime issues by introducing distinct mechanisms for handling both ID and OOD errors, thereby enabling robust and generalizable self-recovery capabilities that are currently absent in the broader research landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to augment Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models with robust and generalizable error recovery capabilities, thereby transforming them into resilient embodied agents. This aims to overcome the inherent fragility observed in current VLA systems, which limits their effectiveness in real-world, open-ended manipulation tasks.

The motivation for FLARE stems from a detailed analysis of the limitations of existing VLA models, which are summarized as follows:

1. **Brittleness of VLAs in Dynamic Environments:** While VLA models demonstrate proficiency in executing complex tasks, their performance degrades significantly in the presence of minor perturbations, unexpected contacts, or slight deviations from learned trajectories. Unlike humans, who can adapt and self-correct, current robotic systems trained with VLAs often experience irreversible failures when encountering such anomalies. This lack of resilience is a critical bottleneck for achieving true robotic autonomy.

2. **Data Regime as the Root Cause of Brittleness:** The authors identify that the fundamental issue contributing to VLA brittleness originates from the characteristics of their training data rather than solely architectural limitations.
 - **Trajectory-Monotonicity in Demonstrations:** Human demonstrations, which form the basis of VLA training, are typically collected along "perfect", failure-free trajectories. This results in datasets where the robot consistently follows a narrow manifold of poses that are strongly correlated with task progress. Consequently, the VLA model tends to learn a spurious association between the robot's specific configuration (s_r) and the environment's state (s_e). When deployed, if the robot encounters a situation where the environment state (s_e) is valid for the task but the robot's pose (s_r) is novel or slightly perturbed from the training distribution, the policy incorrectly interprets this as an "out-of-distribution" state. This type of failure, termed an **In-Distribution (ID) Error**, prevents the robot from performing a simple "retry" or minor adjustment, leading to task failure despite the environment being recoverable.
 - **Success-Centric Nature of Datasets:** Standard VLA training datasets are devoid of examples illustrating how to recover from catastrophic failures. Such failures, where the environment itself enters an unstable or "broken" state (e.g., a toppled cup, a jammed component), are termed **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Errors**. Since the model has never observed corrective behavior for these situations, it lacks the necessary skills to restore the environment to a task-valid state, leading to irreversible task abandonment.

To address these motivations, FLARE outlines specific objectives:
- To establish a clear **ID/OOD error taxonomy** to categorize failures and guide the development of distinct, complementary recovery mechanisms.
- To equip VLAs with an **innate "Retry" capability** for ID errors by systematically decoupling the robot's pose from the environment's state during training. This aims to enable the VLA to robustly continue task execution from diverse robot configurations.
- To facilitate the learning of **dedicated "Reset" skills** for OOD errors through an efficient, MLLM-driven process that identifies catastrophic failure states and bootstraps the collection of targeted recovery demonstrations.
- To integrate these learned capabilities into a **unified VLA system** with a closed-loop inference mechanism, where an online MLLM monitor arbitrates between standard task execution and the invocation of appropriate recovery skills.

By pursuing these objectives, FLARE aims to advance embodied intelligence from imitation-driven execution towards resilience-driven autonomy, enabling robots not only to perform tasks but also to autonomously recover from errors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FLARE introduces a "Retry" and "Reset" framework designed to endow VLAs with robust error recovery capabilities. This framework is built upon a taxonomy that classifies errors as either In-Distribution (ID) or Out-of-Distribution (OOD), offering distinct solutions for each type within a unified VLA system.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The VLA agent is modeled as a Markovian policy π_θ, predicting an action chunk a_t based on the current visual observation o_t and language instruction I. The policy is typically trained via imitation learning on successful human demonstrations. The authors decompose the true world state s_t into the environment state s_e_t (object poses, states) and the robot state s_r_t (end-effector pose, gripper status), with o_t being a rendering of s_t. A key challenge is the spurious correlation between s_e_t and s_r_t in success-biased demonstration data, leading to VLA brittleness.
- **ID Error:** Occurs when the environment state s_e_t is valid for the task, but the robot state s_r_t is outside the typical demonstrated configurations (low-probability region of P(s_r_t | s_e_t, D_demo)). The task is recoverable, requiring a "Retry."
- **OOD Error:** Occurs when the environment state s_e_t is itself non-recoverable by the standard task policy (e.g., a toppled object). This requires a distinct "Reset" skill.
The goal is to learn a unified policy system with innate "Retry" for ID errors and a library of "Reset" skills for OOD errors.

#### 3.2. Enabling Robust "Retry" via Perturbation & Bridging

To address ID errors and the trajectory-monotonic nature of demonstrations, FLARE introduces a Perturbation & Bridging augmentation pipeline that decouples robot pose (s_r_t) from environment state (s_e_t).
1. **Trajectory Composition:** A high-level task trajectory (T_task) is composed from human-demonstrated subtask segments, kinematically transformed to match new scene object poses.
2. **Perturbation & Bridging Segments:** "Perturbation-bridging" segments (d_i) are systematically injected between task segments (a_i) in T_task to create an augmented trajectory (T_aug).
 - **Perturbation (d_A_i):** A random motion sequence moves the robot arm to an arbitrary, out-of-distribution pose (e.g., random joint velocities, end-effector movements). This phase deliberately breaks the pose-state correlation.
 - **Bridging (d_B_i):** A recovery action sequence moves the robot from its perturbed pose (end of d_A_i) back to the valid starting pose required for the subsequent task segment (a_i+1). This is generated using planner-free kinematic interpolation.
3. **Training Data Generation:** The perturbation actions (d_A_i) are not used as training targets. Instead, new training sequences are formed by combining the bridging actions with the subsequent task actions (e.g., (d_B_i, a_i+1, a_i+2...)). This process explicitly trains the VLA to execute the correct task action from a wide variety of initial robot poses, fostering a generalizable "retry" capability.

#### 3.3. Learning "Reset" Skills via MLLM-driven Failure Mining

For OOD errors, where the environment is in a catastrophic state, FLARE employs an efficient pipeline for mining, labeling, and learning object-centric "reset" skills.
1. **Failure Collection:** The "retry-capable" model (trained from Sec. 4.2) is executed on its tasks, and all execution videos and trajectory data (including failures) are collected.
2. **Offline MLLM Failure Analysis:** A Multimodal LLM (e.g., Gemini) acts as an "offline failure analyst." It processes failure videos and outputs structured JSON metadata, including `error_type` ("ID-Retryable" / "OOD-Reset Required"), `reset_target` (object needing reset), `error_group` (interacting objects forming the error, preserving relative poses), and `failure_timestamp`. The `error_group` concept is critical for ensuring semantic integrity during reset skill generalization.
3. **Bootstrapping the Reset Dataset:** For identified "OOD-Reset Required" failures, the `failure_timestamp` allows extracting precise environment states (s_t) from logs. These (error state, skill label) pairs serve as starting configurations for human demonstration collection.
4. **Demonstrations and Augmentation:** A small set (10-20) of human demonstrations (D_reset_human) is collected for each object-centric reset skill, starting from a mined error state s_t and restoring the `reset_target` to a valid state. This small dataset is then massively expanded into D_reset_aug using the same Perturbation & Bridging augmentation, but applied to the entire `error_group` to maintain the failure's semantic integrity across varied scene contexts.

#### 3.4. Unified Training and Closed-Loop Inference

FLARE adopts a modular approach for training and deployment to manage the combined task and recovery skills.
1. **Training an Expert Policy Library:** Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is used to fine-tune specialized policy adapters on top of a shared, pre-trained VLA backbone (e.g., π_0.5).
 - A main task adapter (π_task_LoRA) is trained on D_task_aug (original and "retry-augmented" task data) with the main task instruction.
 - Distinct reset skill adapters (π_reset,j_LoRA) are trained for each object-centric reset skill j, using specific reset prompts (e.g., "reset the cup") and D_reset_aug data.
 This modularity prevents gradient conflicts and facilitates scalability.
2. **Closed-Loop Orchestration:** An online MLLM monitor orchestrates task execution and recovery during deployment.
 - The system starts with the main task adapter (π_task_LoRA).
 - **ID Error:** The VLA's innate "retry" robustness handles autonomous recovery without MLLM intervention.
 - **OOD Error:** The online MLLM monitors execution, identifies the OOD error, determines the required reset skill, and directs the control system to swap the active LoRA adapter to the corresponding π_reset,j_LoRA.
 - The VLA executes the recovery skill. Once the MLLM confirms the environment is restored, it reloads π_task_LoRA, and the VLA seamlessly resumes the main task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results in both simulated and real-world environments to evaluate the FLARE framework.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted on 9 contact-rich manipulation tasks in RoboMimic simulation, following established protocols. The VLA backbone utilized was π_0.5. For "retry" augmentation, 10 human demonstrations were expanded to 500 augmented demonstrations per task, using a maximum perturbation rotation of 45° and translation of 0.5m. For "reset" skills, Gemini-2.5-Pro analyzed failure videos, and 20 human demonstrations for each object-centric reset skill were augmented to 500.

- **Comparison Results (Table 1):**
 - FLARE achieved an average success rate of 84.0%, which significantly surpasses previous leading error recovery methods such as Phoenix (57.8%) and the VLA backbone π_0.5 alone (72.2%).
 - The framework demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on 8 out of 9 tasks. Even on the Threading D0 task, where it was not the top performer, it substantially improved upon the π_0.5 backbone and outperformed Phoenix.
 - Notably, FLARE (84.0%) also outperformed Phoenix-Human (78.9%), which involves manual human instruction for subgoal correction, indicating a comprehensive advantage over prior self-reflection approaches, even when provided with human guidance.
 - For tasks with higher object randomization (D1 suffix), FLARE's performance improvement was more pronounced compared to D0 versions, which is attributed to the decoupling effect of the perturbation-and-bridging strategy, enhancing robustness to environmental variations.

#### 4.2. Real-World Validation

To verify the effectiveness and transferability of FLARE beyond simulation, real-world experiments were conducted on a Piper arm using RealSense D435i cameras for two challenging tasks: "Stack Three Blocks" (long-horizon) and "Insert U-shaped Block" (contact-rich).
- **Success Rates (Table 2):** FLARE consistently outperformed the π_0.5 baseline. For "Stack Three Blocks," FLARE achieved a 75.0% success rate compared to 62.5% for the baseline. For "Insert U-shaped Block," FLARE reached 55.0% versus 45.0% for the baseline.
- The real-world implementation used only 10 human demos (augmented to 50) for each task/reset skill and employed Any6D for object pose estimation to facilitate data augmentation, demonstrating its deployability without privileged simulator states and its reliance on visual feedback and approximate failure reconstruction.

#### 4.3. Ablation and Analysis

- **Analysis of Perturbation & Bridging (Fig. 4):**
 - Investigation of hyperparameters (rotation `r` and translation `t`) for perturbation demonstrated that optimal performance on "Coffee D1" was achieved with `r`=30° and `t`=0.7.
 - Larger perturbations generally led to higher task success rates but decreased demonstration generation efficiency. Excessively large perturbations eventually degraded performance, suggesting a balance between variance and generalizability for the training dataset size.
- **Ablations and Analysis for Reset Skills Learning (Tables 3, 4, 5):**
 - **Ablation of Reset and Human Instruction (Table 3):**
 - Removing the "reset" skills ("Ours w/o Reset") reduced the average success rate by 3.5%, underscoring the necessity of this component.
 - An "Ours-Oracle" variant, which replaced the MLLM-generated reset instructions with human-provided feedback, yielded an additional 7% improvement. This suggests that stronger MLLMs for online monitoring could further enhance performance.
 - **Reset Skill Learning Analysis (Table 4):**
 - Reset skill success rates varied based on task difficulty. For example, resetting a coffee machine lid had an 84% success rate, while resetting a toppled coffee pod was 24%. Similarly, resetting a T-shaped block was 88%, while a U-shaped block was 20%. These variations highlight challenges in manipulating complex object poses with the robot hand used.
 - Demonstration generation efficiency for reset skills correlated with the task success rates, indicating more difficult resets also had lower efficiency.
 - **Failure Case Identification with Multi-modal LLM (Table 5):**
 - Gemini-2.5-Pro demonstrated strong performance in identifying failure cases from videos.
 - Retry/Reset classification accuracy was 88% for "Coffee" and 96% for "ThreePieceAssembly."
 - Reset object identification achieved 88% for "Coffee" and 78% for "ThreePieceAssembly."
 - Timestamp identification accuracy was 78% for "Coffee" and 66% for "ThreePieceAssembly," validating the MLLM's capability to bootstrap a high-quality reset dataset.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FLARE framework represents an advancement in the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically in enhancing the robustness and autonomy of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation.

**Significance:**
- **Addressing VLA Brittleness:** FLARE directly confronts a critical limitation of current VLA models: their brittleness when encountering minor perturbations or catastrophic failures. By identifying the data regime (trajectory-monotonic and success-biased demonstrations) as a primary root cause, the framework provides a targeted solution.
- **Unified Error Recovery Paradigm:** The introduction of a clear ID/OOD error taxonomy coupled with distinct "Retry" and "Reset" mechanisms offers a comprehensive and structured approach to failure recovery. This dual paradigm allows for both autonomous minor adjustments and MLLM-guided interventions for more severe, state-breaking errors.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The perturbation-and-bridging augmentation strategy is significant for decoupling the robot's pose from the environment's state, leading to a VLA policy that is inherently more robust to execution deviations. This contributes to higher success rates in diverse and randomized environments, as demonstrated in both simulation and real-world experiments.
- **Efficient Skill Acquisition:** The MLLM-driven failure mining pipeline is a data-efficient method for bootstrapping object-centric "reset" skills. By leveraging MLLMs to analyze failure videos and pinpoint critical error states, the framework reduces the need for extensive manual data collection for recovery behaviors.
- **Scalable Architecture:** The use of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to train a modular library of expert policies allows for the efficient integration of new recovery skills without requiring complete retraining of the VLA backbone. This promotes scalability and extensibility of the system.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancing Robotic Autonomy:** FLARE moves robotic systems from simply executing learned trajectories to possessing genuine resilience and self-correction capabilities. This is a crucial step towards enabling robots to operate reliably in unstructured, open-world environments with reduced human oversight.
- **Increased Efficiency and Safety:** By autonomously recovering from errors, robots can complete tasks more efficiently, minimize downtime, and operate in potentially hazardous conditions without constant human intervention. This has implications for industrial automation, service robotics, and exploration.
- **Broader Deployment of VLAs:** The improved robustness conferred by FLARE could accelerate the transition of VLA models from research laboratory demonstrations to practical, large-scale deployment in various applications, making robots more dependable collaborators.
- **Future Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for future research, including the development of more generative recovery policies that can infer novel recovery actions, integrating online adaptation for lifelong learning of new failure modes, and extensive validation in highly dynamic and complex real-world scenarios, potentially involving deformable objects or human-robot collaboration.
- **Ethical Considerations:** The authors acknowledge that increased autonomy necessitates attention to safety constraints, interpretability, and human oversight, ensuring that self-correcting robots operate responsibly.

In conclusion, FLARE represents a substantial contribution by providing a principled and effective framework for endowing VLA models with critical failure-aware capabilities, thereby advancing the field closer to robust, autonomous robotic manipulation.
