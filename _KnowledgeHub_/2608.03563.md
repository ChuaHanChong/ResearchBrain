# Unified Visuomotor Targets: Supervising VLAs Beyond Physical Actions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Irvine developed Unified Visuomotor Targets (UVT), a latent representation blending robot actions and visual dynamics from Latent Action Models, which serves as a refined supervision signal for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This strategy improves VLA training efficiency, enhances robustness, and boosts performance on manipulation tasks by up to 16.2% in early training stages, without modifying existing VLA architectures.

## Problem

- VLA models typically fine-tune Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by supervising low-level robot actions, creating a mismatch with the VLM's high-level semantic representations.
- This discrepancy hinders training efficiency and limits policy robustness, as low-level actions lack the structured, task-relevant information VLMs are proficient at handling.
- Existing solutions often involve architectural modifications to VLA systems or require additional data, which can degrade pretrained VLM representations or be computationally expensive.

## Method

- Introducing **Unified Visuomotor Targets (UVT)** as a novel latent prediction target that fuses low-level robot actions with high-level visual scene transition information from pretrained ==Latent Action Models (LAMs)==.
- A ==Multimodal Variational Autoencoder (MVAE)== is trained to encode both robot actions and discrete dynamics codes into a shared 32-dimensional UVT space, ensuring it captures information from both modalities.
- During VLA fine-tuning, the policy predicts the UVT, and a trainable decoder maps the predicted UVT back to executable robot actions, supervised by both ==UVT alignment and action reconstruction losses==.

## Results

- UVT significantly improved VLA training efficiency, achieving up to **16.2%** higher success rates on LIBERO Spatial tasks and **12.6%** on Long tasks at early training checkpoints (**10k steps**).
- Enhanced policy robustness under environmental perturbations (LIBERO-Plus), with Spatial tasks improving from **34.0%** to **81.5%** and Long tasks from **41.7%** to **63.0%** at 10k steps compared to baselines.
- Demonstrated improved performance in real-world bimanual manipulation, increasing Lift Pot success from **38%** to **54%** and achieving **12** full successes for Plate Handover (vs. **7** for baseline).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervising VLA policies with a unified latent target that integrates both motor control and visual dynamics information is more effective than direct low-level action supervision.
> - A joint encoding of action and dynamics within a single latent space (UVT) yields significantly larger performance gains and better final performance compared to using auxiliary dynamics prediction heads.
> - The UVT space creates a more structured, task-dependent representation for robot actions, implicitly filtering out low-level actuator noise and leading to smoother, more stable decoded actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control has increasingly leveraged Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to create Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models aim to map visual observations and natural language instructions to robot actions, benefiting from the rich, high-level representations learned by VLMs on vast internet-scale datasets.

Early VLA approaches extended VLM capabilities to predict discretized action tokens, treating binned joint values or frequency-domain codes as language tokens. While this aligned with autoregressive VLM pretraining, it introduced limitations in motion precision. Subsequent advancements moved towards predicting raw continuous actions, utilizing direct regression or flow matching. However, VLMs are not inherently trained to produce such low-level, continuous actuator signals. This mismatch has led to various architectural modifications in VLA systems, including the addition of MLP heads, action expert transformers, or cross-attention modules atop VLM backbones. Such architectural specialization, while aiming to adapt the model to the action target, can sometimes degrade the VLM's pretrained representations, leading to further research into techniques for their preservation.

A parallel development in robotics research is Latent Action Models (LAMs). These models learn to encode visual state transitions between image pairs into compact, discrete codes. LAMs abstract action dynamics beyond explicit robot actions, enabling generalization across different robot embodiments and even human video data. They have primarily been employed in large-scale robotics pretraining to bootstrap generalist policies from diverse video and cross-embodiment data. Examples include LAPA, UniVLA, ConLA, and MVP-LAM, which focus on learning structured dynamics code representations as more abstract and transferable motion priors.

This paper positions itself by identifying a fundamental disconnect: while VLMs excel at encoding semantic, high-level information about scenes and goals, VLA policies are typically trained to predict low-level robot actions that lack this task-relevant structure. Instead of focusing on architectural adaptations, this work investigates whether modifying the *prediction target* itself can improve VLA training efficiency and performance. By proposing a unified latent prediction target that integrates both motor control and visual scene transition information, the authors aim to bridge the gap between VLM representations and robot action supervision, without requiring architectural changes or additional data. This approach represents a shift from adapting the architecture to the target, to adapting the target to better suit the architecture's inherent representational capabilities. The work differentiates itself from previous LAM research by exploring their utility not for large-scale pretraining, but as a fine-tuning supervision signal for downstream VLA policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from an identified mismatch in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model training paradigms. VLA models leverage pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as backbones to interpret visual and language inputs for robotic control. VLMs are designed to process and encode rich, high-level semantic information from scenes and goals, such as object presence, spatial relations, and desired end states. However, conventional VLA training often supervises policies to predict raw, low-level robot actions, such as joint angles or Cartesian poses.

The authors highlight that these low-level actions lack the structured, semantic information that VLMs are proficient at handling. For instance, a particular wrist motion or gripper adjustment might recur across various tasks with entirely different objectives, making it difficult for a semantic predictor like a VLM to efficiently map high-level understanding to these low-level kinematic signals. This discrepancy can impede convergence speed and limit the robustness of VLA policies during training.

Therefore, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a prediction target better aligned with VLM representations:** The primary goal is to create a supervision signal that captures both the motor control aspects and the task-relevant visual changes, thereby providing a more semantically consistent target for VLA models.
2. **Improve VLA training efficiency:** By offering a more aligned supervision signal, the authors aim to accelerate the learning process, leading to faster convergence and requiring fewer training steps to achieve high performance. This is particularly relevant under limited training data or computational budgets.
3. **Enhance final task performance and policy robustness:** The research seeks to demonstrate that a more structured prediction target can lead to policies that not only perform better in terms of success rate but are also more robust to environmental variations and challenging conditions.
4. **Achieve these improvements without architectural changes or additional data:** A significant objective is to demonstrate that such benefits can be realized through a reformulation of the supervision target alone, avoiding the complexities and potential degradation of pretrained VLM representations associated with architectural modifications or the high computational cost of collecting new, extensive datasets.

In summary, the motivation is to address the "what to predict" question for VLA models, rather than solely "how to adapt the architecture to the target," by proposing a unified representation that bridges the gap between high-level semantic understanding and low-level action execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the Unified Visuomotor Target (UVT) as a latent prediction target for supervising VLA policies. This approach aims to integrate low-level robot actions with high-level visual scene transitions, leveraging pretrained Latent Action Models (LAMs).

**A. Preliminaries**
The standard VLA training involves fine-tuning a model to predict a future ground-truth action chunk, $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$, from multimodal inputs $x_t$ (visual observations and language instructions). Simultaneously, a pretrained LAM operates on visual observations $v_t$ and $v_{t+k}$ to produce a discrete dynamics code $z_t = \text{LAM}(v_t, v_{t+k})$. This $z_t$ encodes image-level dynamics across the action window without explicit motor grounding.

**B. Method Overview**
The core idea is to replace direct supervision on $a_t$ with a unified visuomotor target, $u_t$, which fuses $a_t$ and $z_t$. This $u_t$ is a compact latent representation that preserves motor control fidelity while incorporating visual dynamics information. During VLA fine-tuning, the policy predicts $\hat{u}_t$ from $x_t$. A trainable decoder then maps $\hat{u}_t$ back to executable robot actions.

**C. Unified Visuomotor Target Construction**
The construction of $u_t \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ is achieved using a Multimodal Variational Autoencoder (MVAE) trained once on the demonstration dataset.

1. **Encoders:** Two separate encoders map each modality ($a_t$ and $z_t$) into diagonal Gaussian distributions in a shared 32-dimensional UVT space:
 - Robot action encoder: $(\mu_{a_t}, \log \sigma^2_{a_t}) = f_a(a_t)$
 - Discrete dynamics encoder: $(\mu_{z_t}, \log \sigma^2_{z_t}) = f_z(z_t)$
 These encoders are trained jointly to ensure their outputs are aligned in the UVT space.

2. **Fusion:** The two Gaussian predictions are combined using a weighted precision fusion mechanism. Precision ($\tau = 1/\sigma^2$) is used as a weighting factor, allowing dimensions with smaller predicted variance to contribute more strongly. Learnable scalar weights, $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_z$, provide a global balance between the action and dynamics signals. The fused mean, $\mu_t$, becomes the unified visuomotor target $u_t$.

3. **Decoders:** Two decoders are trained to reconstruct their respective modalities from the fused UVT:
 - Action decoder: $\hat{a}_t = g_a(u_t)$
 - Dynamics decoder: $\hat{z}_t = g_z(u_t)$
 This forces $u_t$ to encode information necessary for recovering both the robot action and the discrete dynamics code.

4. **Training Objective for MVAE:** The MVAE is trained to minimize a combined loss:
 - Action reconstruction loss: $L_{ACT} = \|a_t - \hat{a}_t\|_1$
 - Dynamics code reconstruction loss: $L_{DYN} = CE(z_t, \hat{z}_t)$ (Cross-Entropy for discrete codes)
 - KL divergence loss: $L_{KL} = D_{KL}(N(\mu_t, \text{diag}(\sigma^2_t)) \| N(0, I))$ (regularizes $u_t$ towards a standard normal prior).
 The overall MVAE objective is $L_{UVT} = L_{ACT} + L_{DYN} + L_{KL}$. This pre-training step generates the unified visuomotor targets $u_t$ for all demonstration samples.

**D. UVT for Imitation Learning with VLAs**
After pre-computing $u_t$ for the entire demonstration dataset, VLA fine-tuning is performed using these targets.

1. **UVT Objective:** The VLA policy, $f_\theta(x_t)$, is trained to predict $\hat{u}_t$ from the multimodal input $x_t$. The primary supervision loss is an L2 loss: $L_{UVT-align} = \|\hat{u}_t - u_t\|_2^2$. This loss guides the policy's internal representation towards the unified visuomotor target space.

2. **Trainable Action Decoding:** The MVAE's action decoder, $g_a$, is kept trainable during VLA fine-tuning. It maps the policy's predicted UVT $\hat{u}_t$ to an executable action $\hat{a}_t = g_a(\hat{u}_t)$. An additional action decoding loss, $L_{dec} = \|\hat{a}_t - a_t\|_1$, is applied. This allows the decoder to adapt to the distribution of $\hat{u}_t$ produced by the VLA policy, preventing amplification of reconstruction errors from minor deviations in $\hat{u}_t$.

3. **Final VLA Objective:** The combined objective for VLA fine-tuning is $L_{VLA} = L_{UVT-align} + \gamma L_{dec}$, where $\gamma = 0.5$ balances the UVT alignment and direct action reconstruction.

This entire framework modifies only the prediction target and loss formulation, leaving the backbone VLA architecture (e.g., regression or flow-matching heads) unchanged.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated UVT across various benchmarks and real-world scenarios, demonstrating its effects on training efficiency, task performance, and policy robustness.

**A. Experimental Setup**
The evaluation was conducted on:
- **Benchmarks:** LIBERO and the more challenging LIBERO-Plus benchmark, which introduces greater visual and object state variations. Tasks are categorized into Object, Spatial, Goal, and Long.
- **VLA Models:** Two representative VLA systems were used: VLA-Adapter (regression-style head) and $\pi^{0.5}$ (flow-matching-based head). UVT was integrated without changing their backbone architectures.
- **UVT Configuration:** The unified visuomotor target $u_t$ was 32-dimensional.
- **Real-World Tasks:** Three bimanual manipulation tasks: Lift Pot (grasping and lifting a pot with two arms), Close Marker (aligning and closing a marker with its cap), and Plate Handover (transferring a thin plate between two arms). Each task used 30 human demonstrations and 50 evaluation trials.

**B. Training Efficiency Analysis**
- **Early-Stage Performance:** UVT significantly improved success rates at early training checkpoints (10k steps). On LIBERO, UVT improved Spatial tasks from 78.8% to 95.0% (+16.2%) and Long tasks from 56.2% to 68.8% (+12.6%) compared to VLA-Adapter.
- **Robustness under Perturbation:** Under the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, which features increased environmental perturbations, UVT showed substantial gains at 10k steps, improving Spatial tasks from 34.0% to 81.5% and Long tasks from 41.7% to 63.0% against the VLA-Adapter baseline.
- **Saturation Performance:** At 100k training steps, UVT either matched or slightly exceeded baseline performance across all suites (e.g., Object suite: 97.6% for baseline vs. 99.6% for UVT), indicating that the efficiency gains did not compromise final accuracy.

**C. Benchmark Results**
- **LIBERO Benchmark:** UVT consistently improved performance on the LIBERO benchmark when applied to both VLA-Adapter and $\pi^{0.5}$. For VLA-Adapter, the overall average success rate increased from 94.6% to 96.7%. For $\pi^{0.5}$, the average increased from 96.2% to 96.9%, demonstrating compatibility with different VLA architectures.

**D. Real-World Evaluation**
UVT demonstrated improvements in real-world bimanual manipulation tasks, particularly in object acquisition and grasp stability.
- **Lift Pot:** UVT improved lift success from 38% to 54%. Baseline failures often involved unstable or missed grasps leading to slippage.
- **Close Marker:** The baseline achieved 0% success (full or half), while UVT achieved 5 full successes and 28 half-successes (successful grasping but failed insertion). This task highlighted UVT's ability to consistently grasp objects, though precise insertion remained challenging.
- **Plate Handover:** The baseline achieved 7 full successes and 4 half-successes, while UVT achieved 12 full successes and 16 half-successes. UVT improved the initial approach (sliding gripper finger under the plate) and the handover stage.
- **Decoded Action Behavior:** UVT policies exhibited fewer corrective micro-motions before contact, leading to more stable grasps and reduced accidental grazing.

**E. LAM Regularization Ablation**
- **Two-Head Regularization:** An alternative approach of using an auxiliary dynamics prediction head (Villa-X or UniVLA) showed moderate early performance gains (e.g., 78.8% baseline to 83.2% or 86.4% at 10k steps on LIBERO-Spatial). However, final performance at 100k steps remained similar to the baseline.
- **Unified Visuomotor Target:** UVT, by contrast, achieved significantly larger gains (78.8% baseline to 95.0% at 10k steps on LIBERO-Spatial) and improved final performance (98.6% at 100k steps), suggesting that joint encoding of action and dynamics information is more effective than independent auxiliary prediction.

**F. Unified Visuomotor Target Analysis**
- **Structured Representation:** t-SNE visualizations of $u_t$ showed clear and smooth grouping by LIBERO task suite, even without explicit suite labels. In contrast, raw robot actions $a_t$ appeared intermixed, reflecting their low-level, task-agnostic nature. This suggests $u_t$ encodes high-level, task-dependent visual dynamics information.
- **Smoother Decoded Actions:** Actions decoded from predicted $\hat{u}_t$ exhibited smoother temporal profiles with fewer high-frequency fluctuations compared to ground-truth demonstration actions, while preserving overall movement trends. This indicates that UVT filters out low-level actuator noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Unified Visuomotor Targets (UVT) as a reformulation of the supervision signal for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The primary significance of this work lies in demonstrating that modifying *what* a policy predicts, rather than *how* its architecture is designed, can yield substantial improvements in VLA training.

The core contribution is the introduction of a compact latent prediction target that unifies robot actions with visual state-transition information derived from a pretrained Latent Action Model (LAM). This unified target, $u_t$, provides a supervision signal that is hypothesized to be more aligned with the high-level semantic representations encoded by VLMs, which contrasts with the low-level, kinematically focused robot actions typically used for supervision.

The potential impacts are multi-fold:

1. **Enhanced Training Efficiency:** The empirical results consistently demonstrate that UVT accelerates VLA fine-tuning, achieving higher success rates at earlier training checkpoints and under limited computational budgets. This efficiency gain can reduce the time and resources required to train effective robotic policies, which is particularly relevant for practical deployment and iterative development cycles.
2. **Improved Performance and Robustness:** UVT led to improved final task performance on standard benchmarks (LIBERO) and demonstrated increased robustness under challenging environmental conditions (LIBERO-Plus). In real-world bimanual manipulation tasks, UVT policies exhibited more reliable object acquisition and stable grasps. These improvements suggest that policies trained with UVT are not only faster to train but also more capable and resilient in varied scenarios.
3. **Architectural Flexibility:** A key aspect of UVT is its compatibility with existing VLA architectures (demonstrated with VLA-Adapter and $\pi^{0.5}$) without requiring any architectural modifications or additional data. This makes UVT a widely applicable method that can be integrated into various VLA designs, preserving the benefits of pretrained VLM backbones while improving their fine-tuning process.
4. **Semantic Alignment:** The analysis of the UVT space indicates that it creates a more semantically meaningful representation, grouping actions by task suite even without explicit labels. This suggests that UVT implicitly guides the VLA policy to learn high-level, task-dependent motion patterns rather than merely replicating low-level actuator signals, which can lead to better generalization. The smoother decoded action trajectories also imply that UVT helps filter out low-level noise in demonstrations.

Looking ahead, the concept of a better-conditioned prediction target could extend beyond tabletop VLAs to other domains in embodied AI, such as language-driven navigation, multi-agent coordination, and addressing classical imitation learning challenges like compounding errors. By focusing on a prediction target that aligns more closely with the strengths of VLM representations, this work contributes a method to make robot learning more efficient, robust, and scalable.
