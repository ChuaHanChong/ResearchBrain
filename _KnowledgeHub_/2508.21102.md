# GENNAV: Polygon Mask Generation for Generalized Referring Navigable Regions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Keio University and Honda developed GENNAV, a system that addresses the Generalized Referring Navigable Regions (GRNR) task by generating polygon masks for "stuff-type" target regions in an image, including scenarios with no, single, or multiple targets. GENNAV achieved a mean stuff Intersection over Union (msIoU) of 46.35% on the new GRiN-Drive benchmark, outperforming existing baselines while maintaining a fast inference speed of 31.31 ms/sample.

## Problem

- Existing Referring Navigable Region (RNR) methods primarily focus on "thing-type" objects, struggling with "stuff-type" regions (e.g., road, grass) that have ambiguous boundaries.
- Prior models often lack the ability to explicitly handle scenarios where no target is present or multiple targets are referenced in a natural language instruction.
- Pixel-based segmentation methods, common in RNR, are computationally intensive and can be inefficient for real-time autonomous driving applications.

## Method

- Introduced **GENNAV**, a model integrating ==polygon-based segmentation== with spatially grounded multimodal representations and ==landmark-distribution-driven patchification==.
- Developed an **Existence Aware Polygon Segmentation Module (ExPo)** to explicitly predict target existence (no, single, multi) and generate efficient polygon-based segmentation masks.
- Created the novel **GRiN-Drive** benchmark, a dataset specifically designed to include no-target, single-target, and multi-target scenarios for generalized navigable regions to support evaluation.

## Results

- GENNAV achieved the highest msIoU of **46.35%** on the GRiN-Drive benchmark, an **8.45-point** improvement over the best baseline, TNRSM (**37.90%**).
- The model demonstrated strong zero-shot transfer performance in real-world driving environments, achieving an msIoU of **34.32%**, outperforming baselines like LAVT (**30.44%**) and Qwen2-VL (**20.48%**).
- GENNAV provided a fast inference speed of **31.31 ms/sample**, outperforming all MLLM baselines (e.g., Gemini at **1700-3500 ms/sample**) and proving more efficient than pixel-based decoders in ablation studies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Polygon-based segmentation significantly improves computational efficiency and performance compared to pixel-based methods for generalized referring regions.
> - Integrating pseudo-depth and road region information (via VLSiM) is crucial for enhancing spatial understanding and constraining mask generation to appropriate navigable areas.
> - A Landmark Distribution Patchification Module (LDPM) effectively captures fine-grained visual representations of potential landmarks, especially for distant or small objects, improving overall model performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a specific challenge within the intersection of vision-and-language tasks and autonomous driving, focusing on the interpretation of natural language navigation instructions to identify target regions in a vehicle's front camera image. It extends existing work on Referring Navigable Regions (RNR) by introducing the Generalized Referring Navigable Regions (GRNR) task.

Existing research in this domain can be categorized into:
- **Visual Grounding:** This involves localizing objects or regions described by natural language.
 - **Referring Expression Comprehension (REC)** tasks aim to predict rectangular bounding boxes for "thing-type" objects (countable objects with clear boundaries, e.g., a car).
 - **Referring Expression Segmentation (RES)** tasks extend REC by generating pixel-level masks for referred regions, often for "thing-type" objects. Recent advancements include polygon-generation frameworks for efficiency. Some RES models also handle multiple or no targets, but primarily for "thing-type" scenarios.
 - **Referring Navigable Regions (RNR)** tasks, which are closely related to the current work, require segmenting a destination region indicated by a navigation instruction. Previous RNR methods (e.g.,) primarily focus on single target regions and often rely on pixel-based segmentation, which can be computationally intensive and may underperform for "stuff-type" regions.
- **Vision and Language Navigation (VLN):** While VLN focuses on generating a sequence of steps to reach a destination, RNR and GRNR are distinct in that they require the direct generation of segmentation masks within an image as the designated goal. Most VLN studies have focused on indoor environments, with more recent efforts exploring outdoor autonomous driving contexts.

The current work differentiates itself by addressing several limitations of prior approaches:
1. **Stuff-type target regions:** Unlike "thing-type" objects, "stuff-type" regions (e.g., road, grass) have ambiguous boundaries, making segmentation more challenging. Existing methods often struggle with these.
2. **Generalized scenarios:** Many existing methods are designed for single-target scenarios and do not explicitly account for cases where no target exists or multiple targets are present.
3. **Computational efficiency:** Pixel-based segmentation methods can incur significant computational overhead, which is a concern for real-time autonomous driving applications.

To support this generalized task, the paper also contributes a novel benchmark, GRiN-Drive, which specifically includes no-target, single-target, and multi-target scenarios, unlike existing datasets such as Talk2Car-RegSeg, Refer-KITTI-V2, or general visual grounding datasets which typically lack comprehensive coverage of these diverse scenarios, particularly for "stuff-type" regions with natural language *instructions*. This work, therefore, pushes the boundaries of semantic understanding for autonomous driving by focusing on more complex and realistic navigation scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a system capable of accurately identifying the location of target regions from a natural language instruction and a front-camera image captured by a moving mobility device. This task is formally defined as the Generalized Referring Navigable Regions (GRNR) task.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several challenges and societal needs:
- **Facilitating User-Friendly Autonomous Mobility:** The development of autonomous personal mobility systems is crucial for addressing labor shortages in transportation and providing equitable access, particularly for individuals with mobility impairments. Effective, intuitive user interaction, especially in understanding navigation instructions, is paramount for such systems. Current systems often have limited capability in interpreting nuanced navigation instructions relative to the surrounding environment.
- **Addressing Complex Real-World Scenarios:** Autonomous driving environments present complex visual and linguistic challenges.
 - **Stuff-Type Regions:** Target regions for navigation often involve "stuff-type" areas (e.g., "park on the grass," "go near the road"), which inherently lack the clear, countable boundaries of "thing-type" objects (e.g., "park next to the car"). Existing segmentation methods frequently underperform in these ambiguous boundary scenarios.
 - **Target Existence Variability:** In dynamic driving environments, landmarks referenced in instructions may become absent due to vehicle movement, or multiple relevant landmarks might be present. Current referring expression models often lack explicit mechanisms to predict "no-target" responses or handle "multi-target" scenarios effectively.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** Pixel-based segmentation, while precise, can be computationally demanding, which is a significant drawback for real-time applications in autonomous vehicles.
- **Limitations of Current Models:** Existing methods, including multi-modal large language models (MLLMs), have shown insufficiencies in handling the aforementioned generalized scenarios, particularly for stuff-type regions and in cases of absent or multiple targets. Models that do support multiple or no-target outputs are primarily designed for "thing-type" objects.

To address these motivations, the authors propose GENNAV, a system designed to explicitly predict target existence and generate segmentation masks for multiple stuff-type target regions. The key contributions proposed to achieve this are:
- Introducing an **Existence Aware Polygon Segmentation Module (ExPo)** for existence prediction and computationally efficient polygon-based mask generation.
- Incorporating a **Landmark Distribution Patchification Module (LDPM)** to capture fine-grained visual representations of potential landmarks.
- Constructing the novel **GRiN-Drive benchmark** to provide a comprehensive evaluation dataset covering no-target, single-target, and multi-target scenarios for generalized navigable regions.
- Validating the zero-shot transfer performance of GENNAV in diverse real-world driving environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces GENNAV, a model designed for the Generalized Referring Navigable Regions (GRNR) task. The GRNR task requires the model to determine if a target region specified by a natural language instruction exists in a given front-camera image. If one or more target regions exist, the model must generate a segmentation mask for each. The target regions can be "stuff-type" and involve zero, one, or multiple targets.

GENNAV integrates polygon-based segmentation with spatially grounded multimodal representations and landmark-distribution-driven patchification. It comprises three modules:

1. **Landmark Distribution Patchification Module (LDPM):**
 - **Purpose:** To capture fine-grained visual representations related to potential landmarks. This is crucial because standard low-resolution processing can lead to insufficient pixel allocation for distant or small landmarks.
 - **Mechanism:** Instead of uniform image partitioning, LDPM employs a landmark-distribution-based patchification strategy, focusing on regions where landmarks are likely to be densely distributed. Each patch (e.g., 560x315 regions resized to 224x224) is encoded using a frozen DINOv2 visual backbone, followed by a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The resulting features across all patches are integrated to form the final landmark-distribution-aware feature, `h_ldp`.

2. **Visual-Linguistic Spatial Integration Module (VLSiM):**
 - **Purpose:** To integrate visual, linguistic, and spatial information, and to mitigate the generation of masks in inappropriate regions (e.g., sky, occluded areas).
 - **Mechanism:**
 - **Visual Features:** Raw image `x_img` is processed by a frozen DINOv2 and a CNN to obtain `f_vis(x_img)`.
 - **Pseudo-Depth Integration:** A pseudo-depth image `x_depth` is generated from `x_img` using a monocular depth estimation model (e.g., Depth Anything V2). This `x_depth` is overlaid with `x_img` and then processed by DINOv2 and an additional CNN (as DINOv2 is not trained for overlaid images) to obtain `f_depth(x_img)`. This aims to enhance spatial understanding.
 - **Road Region Integration:** A mask of the road `x_road` is obtained from `x_img` using a semantic segmentation model (e.g., PIDNet), followed by a CNN to get `f_road(x_img)`. This helps constrain mask generation to navigable areas.
 - **Multimodal Integration:** The linguistic feature `h_inst` (obtained from `x_inst` using GISTEmbed) is integrated with `f_vis(x_img)` and `f_depth(x_img)`. Separately, `f_road(x_img)` is integrated with `f_depth(x_img)`. These integrated features are then combined using the Hadamard product to produce the multimodal representation `h_mm`: `h_mm = h_inst ⊙ {f_vis(x_img) + f_depth(x_img)} ⊙ {f_road(x_img) + f_depth(x_img)}`.

3. **Existence Aware Polygon Segmentation Module (ExPo):**
 - **Purpose:** To explicitly predict the existence of target regions (no, single, multi) and generate polygon-based segmentation masks efficiently.
 - **Mechanism:**
 - **Feature Combination:** `h_inst` and `h_ldp` are combined with `h_mm` to form `h_cmm = (h_ldp ⊙ h_inst) + h_mm`.
 - **Classification Head:** A classification head predicts the probability `p(y_ex)` over categories {no-target, single-target, multi-target} based on `h_cmm` and `f_road(x_img)`.
 - **Regression Head:** A regression head generates the polygon vertices `c_i` for target regions using an MLP, taking `h_cmm` and `f_road(x_img)` as input. Polygons are predicted as a sequence of 2D coordinates.
 - **Loss Function:** The overall loss `L` combines a cross-entropy loss `L_CE` for existence prediction and an L1 loss `ℓ_1` for polygon vertex prediction. The polygon loss is applied conditionally only when `y_ex` is `single-target` or `multi-target`.

**GRiN-Drive Benchmark Construction:**
- **Foundation:** Built upon Talk2Car-RegSeg (which provides single-target, stuff-type RNR data) and Refer-KITTI-V2 (which provides multi-object tracking data with bounding boxes for "thing-type" objects).
- **No-Target Samples:** Created by swapping instructions between Talk2Car-RegSeg images, followed by MLLM assessment (GPT-4o) and manual verification for landmark absence.
- **Multi-Target Samples:** Refer-KITTI-V2 images containing multiple landmarks were selected. MLLMs were used to generate instruction templates from Talk2Car-RegSeg, singularize noun phrases from Refer-KITTI-V2, and then combine them to create new multi-target instructions. Annotators then provided polygon masks for each target region using a custom interface (Imagetagger). Manual filtering ensured quality.
- **Scale:** The benchmark contains 17,114 samples.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **mean stuff Intersection over Union (msIoU):** A novel primary metric proposed to provide a balanced evaluation for single-target, multi-target, and no-target samples. It addresses the bias of standard IoU metrics that might unfairly favor correct "no-target" predictions (IoU=1.0) over accurate mask generations (IoU < 1.0). msIoU assigns a score of 1 if IoU exceeds a threshold K and normalizes scores below K, also incorporating TP, TN, FP, FN for existence prediction.
- **Precision@K (P@K):** Standard metric for referring expression tasks, measuring the percentage of samples where IoU exceeds a threshold K (set at 0.1 and 0.2).
- **Accuracy (Acc.):** Measures the accuracy of predicting target region existence.
- **Inference Speed:** Measures computational efficiency in milliseconds per sample.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Baselines:** Two groups were used: pixel-based methods (Rufus et al., LAVT, TNRSM, GSVA-Vicuna-7B) and large multimodal models (MLLMs) (Gemini, GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL). Baselines were evaluated at various resolutions (224x224, 640x640, 1024x1024, 1600x900). MLLMs were evaluated zero-shot with carefully designed prompts for bounding box or polygon output.
- **Real-World Experiments:** GENNAV's zero-shot transfer performance was validated using four automobiles in five distinct urban areas, collecting 20 video recordings across 120 episodes (40 each for single, no, and multi-target scenarios).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results, including ablation studies and real-world experiments, to evaluate GENNAV's performance.

**Quantitative Results on GRiN-Drive Benchmark (Table 1):**
- **Overall Performance:** GENNAV achieved the highest mean stuff Intersection over Union (msIoU) of 46.35%, significantly outperforming all baseline methods. The best-performing baseline, TNRSM (224x224), achieved 37.90% msIoU, indicating an 8.45-point improvement by GENNAV.
- **Precision and Accuracy:** GENNAV also demonstrated superior performance in Precision@0.1 (49.60%) and Accuracy (75.41%), surpassing TNRSM (224x224) by 5.55 and 7.52 points, respectively. These improvements were statistically significant (p < 0.05).
- **Inference Speed:** GENNAV achieved an inference speed of 31.31 ms/sample. While LAVT (224x224, 9.29 ms/sample) was faster, GENNAV delivered substantially higher performance across other metrics. All MLLM baselines (Gemini, GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL) exhibited significantly slower inference speeds (e.g., 1700-3500 ms/sample).
- **Resolution Impact:** For pixel-based baselines (Rufus et al., LAVT, TNRSM), increasing input resolution from 224x224 to 640x640 did not consistently improve performance across msIoU, P@K, and accuracy. This was attributed to backbones pre-trained at lower resolutions not being optimized for higher-resolution inputs, and it also increased computational costs.
- **Human Performance:** Human performance on the GRiN-Drive test set was 56.08% msIoU, 56.00% P@0.1, and 88.00% Accuracy, providing a benchmark for the task's inherent difficulty.

**Qualitative Results on GRiN-Drive Benchmark (Figure 3, 7, 9):**
- GENNAV consistently generated appropriate segmentation masks for both single-target and multi-target scenarios. For example, in a "multi-target" instruction like "Pull up right next to pedestrian on the left," GENNAV successfully generated masks for each of the two pedestrians, while baselines like TNRSM only predicted a single region or produced inappropriate masks.
- In "no-target" cases, GENNAV correctly predicted the absence of a target, whereas several baseline methods often generated erroneous masks in inappropriate regions.
- The model demonstrated the ability to generate different predictions for the same image when provided with different instructions.

**Ablation Study (Table 2):**
- **LDPM (Landmark Distribution Patchification Module):** Removing LDPM (Model i) resulted in a 3.3-point decrease in msIoU compared to the full GENNAV (Model v), confirming its contribution to understanding landmarks via patchification.
- **VLSiM (Visual-Linguistic Spatial Integration Module) Components:**
 - Removing the `f_depth` subnetwork (Model ii) led to a 2.22-point decrease in msIoU, indicating the pseudo-depth images assist in aligning linguistic and spatial information.
 - Removing the `f_road` subnetwork (Model iii) resulted in a 1.82-point decrease in msIoU, showing its role in improving mask generation by leveraging road representations.
- **Decoder Type (Polygon vs. Pixel):** A pixel-based decoder (Model iv) underperformed GENNAV (Model v) by 11.09 points in msIoU and had a slower inference speed (47.92 ms/sample vs. 31.31 ms/sample). This validates the efficiency and effectiveness of the polygon-based approach.

**Real-World Experiments (Zero-Shot Transfer, Table 3, 7, Figure 4, 12):**
- **Zero-Shot Performance:** In real-world driving scenarios across diverse urban areas, GENNAV achieved the highest msIoU of 34.32%, outperforming baselines such as TNRSM (224x224) at 23.11%, LAVT (640x640) at 30.44%, and Qwen2-VL (bbox) at 20.48%.
- GENNAV also maintained higher P@0.1 (28.75%) and Accuracy (67.50%) compared to baselines in these zero-shot settings.
- Qualitative results showed GENNAV successfully generating appropriate masks in complex real-world conditions, including multi-target scenarios and adapting predictions based on different instructions for the same scene.

**Error Analysis (Figure 11):**
- **Existence Prediction Failures:** The main bottleneck was "Multimodal Language Understanding for landmarks" (MLU), suggesting insufficient semantic understanding of landmarks.
- **Polygon Generation Failures:** The primary issue was "Relative Position Misunderstanding" (RPM), indicating an inadequate understanding of 3D environmental structure and object relationships.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method for Generalized Referring Navigable Regions (GRNR), which holds significance for the advancement of autonomous driving systems and human-robot interaction.

The key contributions and their potential impacts are:
- **Addressing Critical Gaps in Navigation Instruction Understanding:** By explicitly handling "stuff-type" target regions, "no-target" scenarios, and "multi-target" instructions, GENNAV extends the capabilities of referring expression segmentation beyond the limitations of prior work primarily focused on single, "thing-type" objects. This generalization is crucial for enabling autonomous vehicles to interpret the diverse and often ambiguous natural language commands encountered in real-world driving.
- **Enhancing Computational Efficiency for Real-Time Systems:** The adoption of a polygon-based segmentation approach, validated through ablation studies, demonstrates improved computational efficiency compared to pixel-based methods. This efficiency is critical for autonomous driving applications that require real-time processing and rapid decision-making, contributing to safer and more responsive systems.
- **Introducing a Comprehensive Benchmark (GRiN-Drive):** The construction of the GRiN-Drive benchmark, which integrates no-target, single-target, and multi-target scenarios with natural language instructions for "stuff-type" regions, provides a valuable resource for the research community. This benchmark is expected to facilitate more rigorous and realistic evaluations of future models, driving further innovation in the field by addressing previously underrepresented complexities.
- **Demonstrating Robust Zero-Shot Transfer in Real-World Environments:** The successful validation of GENNAV's zero-shot transfer performance across diverse real-world urban areas and varying hardware setups indicates its robustness and practical applicability. This suggests that GENNAV can be effectively integrated into operational autonomous systems without extensive re-training for every new environment, accelerating deployment and adoption.
- **Improving User-Autonomous System Interaction:** By enabling autonomous mobility devices to better understand human-given navigation instructions, GENNAV has the potential to make these systems more intuitive and user-friendly. This can significantly enhance the experience for all users, particularly assisting people with mobility impairments by providing them with more reliable and accessible transportation options.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as restricted semantic understanding of landmarks (MLU) and difficulties with relative positional relationships (RPM), provide clear directions for future research. Proposals to integrate more robust semantic segmentation models or incorporate 3D structural understanding tasks offer pathways for continued improvement, fostering further development in robust multimodal scene understanding.

In summary, GENNAV represents an advancement in vision-language grounding for autonomous mobility, offering a more generalized, efficient, and robust solution for interpreting complex navigation instructions in dynamic environments. Its validated performance and the introduction of a new benchmark contribute to both theoretical research and practical application in autonomous driving technology.
