# Nautilus: From One Prompt to Plug-and-Play Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> NAUTILUS, a domain-specific harness for robot learning, automates complex research workflows by interpreting natural language prompts, reducing the engineering burden associated with integrating policies, benchmarks, and diverse robotic hardware. It provides standardized interfaces, chambered execution, and domain-specific procedural knowledge, enabling improved reproducibility and seamless real-robot deployment.

## Problem

- Robot learning research suffers from significant fragmentation across policy families, benchmarks, and robot embodiments, leading to substantial manual engineering effort for integration and reproduction (estimated Θ(N·M·K) complexity).
- Dependency conflicts, interface mismatches, and the lack of standardized validation protocols impede the reproducibility and trustworthiness of robot learning research.
- General-purpose LLM coding agents struggle with end-to-end reproduction in complex robot learning workflows due to the absence of a robust 'harness' encompassing orchestration, tools, and domain-specific priors.

## Method

- NAUTILUS is a two-layer harness with a 'substrate' for engineering invariants (typed interface contracts, chambered execution for dependency isolation, uniform inter-module communication) and a 'content layer' for robotics-specific procedures.
- It integrates 'Guides' to direct agent behavior, 'Sensors' for post-generation output validation, and a 'State' registry to store verified module knowledge, providing reusable agent skill sets and reducing integration complexity.
- The system provides unified execution environments that abstract away underlying complexities, allowing generated artifacts to run in isolated 'chambers' and communicate via a lightweight WebSocket protocol.

## Results

- NAUTILUS-generated benchmark wrappers faithfully reproduced published reference results for various policies (IL, VLA, WAM) across diverse benchmarks (LIBERO, ManiSkill, RoboCasa), with most wrapped outcomes closely matching published figures.
- The harness enabled shared-interface onboarding with similar or lower costs compared to a vanilla coding agent, showing efficiency gains (fewer agent turns) with predefined structures and greater benefits with weaker LLM backbones.
- Policies wrapped once by NAUTILUS were seamlessly deployed from simulation benchmarks to heterogeneous real-world robots (Franka arm, Unitree H1 humanoid) without requiring separate policy-integration efforts or modifications to the policy code.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The primary bottleneck for automated robot learning workflows with LLMs is the absence of a robust domain-specific 'harness' rather than inherent limitations of the LLM models themselves.
> - Explicit, standardized interface contracts and uniform communication protocols are crucial, with an ablation study showing a significant drop in success (55.3%) without a stable inter-module transport mechanism.
> - Incorporating domain-specific procedural knowledge via guides, sensors, and a verified state registry significantly enhances an agent's ability to achieve end-to-end success in complex robotics research workflows.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is characterized by a high degree of fragmentation across various policy families (e.g., imitation learning, reinforcement learning, vision-language-action models, world action models), benchmark suites (e.g., LIBERO, RoboCasa, ManiSkill), and robot embodiments (e.g., single-arm, bimanual, humanoid). This fragmentation results in significant engineering effort for researchers to integrate, reproduce, evaluate, or deploy different combinations of policies, benchmarks, and robots. Each integration typically requires distinct, hand-written "glue code," leading to dependency conflicts, interface mismatches, and reproducibility challenges. The paper estimates this integration burden to be of complexity Θ(N·M·K) for N policies, M benchmarks, and K robots.

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as tools for code generation and software engineering workflows. In robotics, LLMs have been applied to task-level embodied pipelines, such as policy code generation and reward design. However, general-purpose coding agents often encounter difficulties in achieving end-to-end reproduced results in complex robot learning research workflows that span multiple repositories, simulators, benchmarks, and hardware interfaces. The authors suggest that this limitation stems not primarily from a lack of LLM capability, but from the absence of a robust "harness" – the surrounding infrastructure encompassing orchestration, tools, verification protocols, guardrails, state management, and procedural priors necessary for successful agentic project execution.

Existing robot learning frameworks (e.g., LeRobot, Isaac Lab, robosuite) consolidate some common workflows but are often coupled to specific policies or benchmarks. Conversely, general-purpose coding agents (e.g., Claude Code, OpenHands, SWE-agent) provide the agentic loop but lack the domain-specific procedural knowledge required for robotics research workflows. NAUTILUS is presented as a domain-specific harness designed to bridge this gap, specializing the harness perspective for robot learning. It aims to provide a stable, structured environment for LLM agents to operate within the fragmented robot learning ecosystem, thereby reducing the engineering overhead and increasing the feasibility of cross-family reproduction and evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for NAUTILUS stems from the significant engineering challenges inherent in robot learning research due to its fragmented nature. Researchers frequently encounter an "engineering nightmare" when attempting to port or combine different components—policies, benchmarks, and robots—as each implementation is often tightly coupled and entangled with others. This results in repetitive, manual efforts for tasks such as Docker container setup, observation adapter development, smoke tests, and trust-validation procedures across various papers, laboratories, and reproduction initiatives. This leads to a substantial integration cost, estimated at Θ(N·M·K), which impedes progress and reproducibility.

While general-purpose LLM coding agents have shown promise in code generation, they often fail to achieve end-to-end reproduction in robot learning. The authors contend that this persistent failure is not a fundamental limitation of the LLM models themselves, but rather a "harness problem." A robust harness, encompassing orchestration, tool integration, verification protocols, guardrails, state management, and domain-specific procedural priors, is considered crucial for the success of agentic systems. Such a general-purpose research harness is currently lacking in robot learning.

The key objectives of the NAUTILUS project are:

1. **Reduce Engineering Burden:** To transform a single user prompt (e.g., "Evaluate policy A with benchmark B") into automated, ready-to-use workflows for reproduction, evaluation, fine-tuning, and deployment. The overarching goal is to reduce the field-level integration cost from Θ(N·M·K) to Θ(N+M+K) by introducing a shared harness.
2. **Provide Domain-Specific Priors:** To distill the tacit procedural knowledge and validation practices characteristic of robotics research into explicit, reusable agent skill sets. These skills aim to guide the agent's behavior effectively within complex robot learning workflows.
3. **Establish Standardized Interfaces:** To introduce typed contracts that define clear interfaces among policies, simulators/benchmarks, and real-world robots. These contracts serve as stable surfaces for agent reasoning and integration.
4. **Ensure Unified Execution:** To provide unified interfaces and execution environments that abstract away underlying system complexities and dependency incompatibilities. This includes isolating components in "chambered units" and standardizing inter-module communication.
5. **Enable Trustworthy Automation:** To develop an agentic coding workflow characterized by explicit, automated validation and testing at each milestone. This aims to increase the reliability and trustworthiness of automated integrations.
6. **Facilitate Extensibility and Community Contributions:** To design an open-source, extensible community platform that can automatically generate required adapters for existing implementations, and also wrap and onboard new or user-provided policies, simulators/benchmarks, and robots via uniform interfaces, without requiring modifications to the core framework code.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NAUTILUS is designed as a domain-specific harness for robot learning research, implemented as a Claude Code plugin. It operates by interpreting natural language requests and orchestrating robot learning workflows across various components. The system's architecture is structured into two main layers: a substrate and a content layer.

**Substrate (Engineering Invariants):**
The substrate provides a stable foundation for the agent, built upon three core invariants:

1. **Typed Interface Contracts:** NAUTILUS defines three primary typed interfaces that represent the modular boundaries of a robot learning experiment:
 - **Policy:** Exposes an `infer(obs) -> actions` method (with an optional `reset`).
 - **Benchmark/Environment:** Exposes `reset(seed)` and `step(action)`, along with task definition, success criteria, and deterministic seeding.
 - **Robot:** Exposes `reset`, `get_observation`, `apply_action`, and `safe_stop`.
 These contracts standardize the unit of integration, requiring new components to conform to these specifications rather than necessitating framework modifications.

2. **Chambered Execution:** To manage incompatible dependencies often found in robot learning (e.g., distinct CUDA, PyTorch, MuJoCo/Isaac/SAPIEN, ROS 2 stacks), NAUTILUS executes generated artifacts within isolated, disposable "chambers" (containers). Each chamber maintains its own dependency environment, preventing conflicts while exposing the same external contract. This enables parallel building of policy and benchmark modules.

3. **Uniform Inter-module Communication:** Communication between modules is divided into two tiers:
 - **Tier 1 (Robot-local):** Handles high-bandwidth sensing and control within the native robot stack (e.g., ROS 2, shared memory), operating below the agent's visible boundary.
 - **Tier 2 (Off-robot):** This is the only transport visible to the agent, implemented as a lightweight WebSocket protocol. It provides a shared observation/action schema for both simulated and real-robot endpoints, ensuring consistency for agent-level reasoning and policy interaction.

**Content Layer (Robotics-Specific Procedures):**
Built atop the substrate, the content layer incorporates robotics-specific procedures and abstractions:

1. **Guides:** These components direct the agent's behavior before code generation. They include routing rules, subagent roles, schemas, and validation protocols derived from robotics research conventions. Guides are implemented across different layers (L2 for command routing, L3 for task-specific subagents, L5 for shared reference libraries) to shape agent actions explicitly.

2. **Sensors:** Sensors are designed to validate the agent's generated outputs post-generation. At the L4 boundary, NAUTILUS implements six types of checks: pre-action filtering, render-time auditing, interface verification, spec comparison, tiered smoke testing, and cross-run verification. These sensors trigger local corrections or block runs if issues are detected, ensuring generated artifacts meet specified criteria. For instance, spec comparison checks policy and benchmark interface compatibility before module launch.

3. **State:** The L4 layer includes a persistent, MCP-served, JSON-schema-validated registry that stores verified module knowledge. This registry allows NAUTILUS to reuse information about repository layouts, environment constraints, and policy/benchmark/robot interfaces across runs, binding pinned code and environment artifacts to typed specs. This mechanism reduces integration complexity by providing machine-readable specs and a shared dispatch protocol.

4. **Workspace Artefacts:** The L6 layer records evidence for each run. This includes append-only process logs in a `.nautilus/run-log` directory and regenerated summary "receipts" at the repository root (e.g., `install.md`, `history.md`, `benchmark.md`). These artifacts document the source commit, image ID, environment choices, benchmark protocol, key decisions, and rerun recipes, contributing to reproducibility.

**Registry Curation and Hardware Support:**
The registry is designed as a shareable index of reproducible entries, with a "verified" status reserved for benchmark entries that demonstrate cross-policy evidence (i.e., multiple released policies reproducing their published reference results through the same wrapper). Hardware support is integrated via `nautilus-collect`, a chambered data-collection platform that implements the `Robot` typed contract and exposes the uniform Tier-2 WebSocket transport. This allows policies wrapped once by NAUTILUS to be seamlessly deployed from benchmarks to real robot endpoints. Real robot implementations involve a two-container setup (hardware container for manufacturer drivers, platform container for the robot learning stack) communicating via ROS 2 and shared memory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings across three primary studies: benchmark trust validation, an ablation study on onboarding cost, and real-robot deployment demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Benchmark Trust Validation (RQ1)

This study investigated whether the generated benchmark wrappers faithfully reproduced reference results for public policies. The protocol involved running several released policies with their official checkpoints through the NAUTILUS-generated wrappers and comparing the resulting scores against published references. This covered imitation learning (IL), vision-language-action (VLA), and world action model (WAM) policies across LIBERO, ManiSkill, RoboTwin, ALOHA, and RoboCasa benchmarks.

- **Findings:** The results (Table 2) indicated that most wrapped outcomes for IL, VLA, and WAM policies remained close to their published references. Deviations were typically small, suggesting that the benchmark adapters preserved the intended evaluation protocols. This cross-policy validation supported the reuse of the generated benchmark wrappers for the initial verified entries, with deviations transparently retained for future calibration.

#### 4.2. Ablation: From Pairwise Wiring to Reusable Extending (RQ2)

This study assessed the cost and effectiveness of onboarding new implementations into NAUTILUS, comparing it to a baseline raw coding agent. A controlled "leave-one-benchmark-out" testbed was created where existing benchmark support was surgically removed from public policy repositories, and agents were tasked to reconstruct the missing integration. This allowed for validation against a task-level oracle (matching reference success rates).

- **Findings (Table 3):**
 - **Cost Parity with Vanilla Agent:** NAUTILUS achieved shared-interface onboarding within a similar cost envelope as a vanilla coding agent for one-off integrations. For Claude Opus 4.7, token and peak context usage increased slightly (13.8M vs 12.2M, +30k peak context), while agent turns decreased (152.4 vs 161.9), indicating efficiency gains from predefined structures. Inference time was longer due to waiting for validation tool calls. With Claude Sonnet 4.6, NAUTILUS demonstrated lower costs (115.5 vs 158.0 turns, 9.5M vs 12.4M tokens, $4.76 vs $5.19), suggesting that the harness provides greater benefits with weaker underlying models.
 - **Impact of Typed Interface Template:** An ablation without the Tier-2 policy WebSocket schema resulted in a significant drop in wrap success (55.3% for Opus), a large increase in agent turns (607.5), and substantially higher costs ($74.94). This indicated that the absence of a stable, standardized inter-module transport mechanism forced the agent to re-discover and hand-roll pairwise communication plumbing, confirming the value of the substrate's design.
 - **Impact of Verified Registry:** Removing access to the verified State registry (forcing regeneration of benchmark sides) approximately doubled the workload. Opus turns nearly tripled (152.4 to 431.2), and cost increased by 2.3x. Nevertheless, a portion of trials still succeeded, suggesting that the underlying substrate could support unaided dual-side wrapping, albeit at a much higher cost.

#### 4.3. Real-Robot Deployment Path

This section demonstrated the portability of the NAUTILUS substrate for real-robot deployment, rather than focusing on new benchmark scores.

- **Protocol and Demonstration:** A VLA manipulation checkpoint, previously wrapped for benchmark evaluation, was deployed on a single-arm Franka robot. The same policy artifact was used, with only the simulation backend replaced by a hardware container, keeping the policy-facing WebSocket endpoint and platform-side container unchanged. Similarly, an RL-trained locomotion policy was deployed on a Unitree H1 humanoid, with only the H1 hardware container and launch namespace altered.
- **Findings:** These demonstrations supported the substrate's portability. They showed that any checkpoint wrapped behind the shared Policy interface could be transitioned from benchmark evaluation to heterogeneous real hardware (Franka, Unitree H1) without requiring a separate policy-integration effort or modifications to the policy code. Policy-family and embodiment-specific details remained encapsulated below the `Robot` contract, while the agent-facing and policy-facing interfaces remained consistent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The NAUTILUS project presents a contribution to the field of robot learning by addressing a fundamental challenge: the fragmentation and high engineering burden associated with integrating policies, benchmarks, and robots. The work reinforces the argument that in the context of agentic automation for robot learning, the primary constraint is the absence of a suitable "harness" rather than limitations of the large language models themselves. The ablation studies provide empirical support for this claim, demonstrating that the structural components of the harness (Guides, Sensors, State) are more impactful on end-to-end success than variations in LLM backbones.

The potential significance and impact of NAUTILUS include:

1. **Reduced Engineering Overhead:** By collapsing the integration cost from Θ(N·M·K) to Θ(N+M+K), NAUTILUS aims to significantly reduce the manual effort required for cross-family reproduction, evaluation, and deployment. This could make rigorous benchmarking and exploration of diverse robot learning components more accessible, particularly for smaller research groups with limited infrastructure teams.
2. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Trustworthiness:** The system's emphasis on typed contracts, chambered execution, uniform communication, and explicit validation protocols, combined with the generation of detailed reproducibility artifacts, is designed to improve the reliability and auditability of empirical robot learning research. Pinned registry entries enable third-party verification of published results, fostering greater confidence in the scientific literature.
3. **Foundation for Auto-Research:** The structured substrate and content layer (Guides, Sensors, State) provide a robust framework that could serve as a foundation for future "auto-research" loops in robot learning. This envisions agents not only executing predefined workflows but also proposing experiments, performing ablations, interpreting results, and refining hypotheses autonomously.
4. **Extensible Community Platform:** NAUTILUS is designed as an open-source, extensible platform. Its contract-centric approach allows researchers to onboard new policies, benchmarks, or robot interfaces without modifying the core framework, fostering community contributions and expanding cross-validation coverage.
5. **Sim-to-Real Portability:** The demonstrations of real-robot deployment illustrate that the shared Policy and Robot contracts enable seamless transitions from benchmark evaluation to heterogeneous real hardware without separate integration efforts, streamlining the sim-to-real transfer process.

The paper acknowledges current limitations, such as restricted workflow coverage, environment-API level unification (rather than physics-level), and inherited platform churn. Future work includes fully closing the train-eval-deploy loop with automated hyperparameter selection, multi-seed sweeps, and report generation, as well as exploring automatic skill induction and expanding hardware support with human-in-the-loop safeguards. The broader implication is that expert-built harnesses can make domain expertise executable, reusable, and inspectable, facilitating a co-evolutionary relationship between human experts and AI models in scientific research.
