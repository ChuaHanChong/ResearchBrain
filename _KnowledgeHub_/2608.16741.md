# Semantic- and Density-Aware Planning for Accessibility-Preserving Multi-Object Placement

> [!summary] Summary
> SDPP plans sequential shelf placements for household objects by scoring candidate poses on a semantic-density term (GloVe similarity weighted by spatial proximity) minus an Accessibility Map penalty for lost reachable workspace. Filters unreachable candidates before motion planning. Achieves highest semantic organization and shelf density among baselines, with much faster feasible-pose search.

## Problem

- Stowing/packing methods achieve dense geometric storage but ignore semantic organization of objects entirely, treating placement as pure space optimization.
- Semantic and preference-aware placement methods pick reference objects via relations or context but ignore how repeated placements erode manipulator accessibility over time.
- No prior method jointly optimizes semantic organization, dense space utilization, and accessibility preservation under unknown future object arrivals in confined shelves.

## Method

- **Accessibility Map (AM)**: binary reachability mask built from dilated object footprints, shelf walls, rear-facing cones, and gripper-clearance morphological closing, used to reject unreachable candidates pre-motion-planning.
- **Footprint-Constrained/Center-Constrained (FC/CC)** sampling: two rejection strategies of differing conservativeness, applied FC-first-then-CC-fallback to prune candidates before expensive IK/motion checks.
- ==Semantic-density score==: ==GloVe-embedding similarity== (split into semantic-word and form-word cosine terms, contrast-normalized) weighted by inverse spatial distance, combined with an AM-based space-preservation penalty into one placement score.

## Results

- SDPP places **30.7±3.9** additional objects with semantic sum **61.6±11.5** and density **0.261±0.016**, beating SPS++ (28.2, 48.7, 0.245) and Dengler density baseline (28.6, 32.4, 0.248).
- AM-based FC to CC filtering saves **52.8s** mean accumulated IK/motion-planning time under score-ranked ordering versus no filtering (paired t-test **p<0.05**).
- Ablation: removing accessibility penalty (w2=0) drops placement count by ~**1.5** objects/trial; raising w2=5 boosts density/capacity but lowers semantic score from **1.440** to **1.295**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local compactness alone (semantic-density score) does not guarantee preserved future capacity; explicit accessibility modeling recovers placements a purely dense/semantic score would forfeit.
> - Cheap reachability pre-filtering before expensive IK/motion planning is a general pattern for cutting sequential manipulation planning cost in cluttered scenes.
> - Semantic and space objectives trade off tunably via one scalar weight, letting a single scoring framework interpolate between denser-but-less-organized and organized-but-sparser regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation often requires robots to interact with and organize objects within environments over extended periods. This work addresses the specific challenge of multi-object placement in confined storage spaces, such as household shelves, under online conditions where future object arrivals are unknown.

Previous research in robotic manipulation has explored various aspects relevant to this problem:
- **Object Stowing and Bin Packing:** Much work has focused on maximizing geometric storage efficiency in bounded containers, often relying on vertical stacking or robust object manipulation for logistics and industrial settings (e.g., Amazon Robotics Challenge). These approaches typically prioritize density and throughput, often without considering semantic organization or the specific constraints of shelf environments.
- **Shelf Replenishment and Arrangement:** Other studies have focused on supermarket shelf replenishment, addressing robust manipulation strategies, retrieval cost optimization, arrangement stability, and insertion feasibility. While these methods tackle geometric and manipulation challenges in shelf settings, they generally do not jointly consider semantic organization, dense space utilization, and spacial accessibility.
- **Semantic Placement Methods:** A distinct line of research focuses on inferring suitable placement locations based on object relations, scene context, or user preferences. These methods often use techniques like contextual inference, foundation models, or word embeddings to quantify semantic relationships. Some semantic placement approaches account for geometric feasibility, but they often do not concurrently optimize for dense space utilization and long-term manipulator accessibility as a shelf is progressively filled.

The current work identifies a gap in existing methodologies: the lack of an integrated approach that simultaneously addresses semantic organization, dense space utilization, and manipulator accessibility within an online, sequential multi-object shelf-placement context where future object information is unavailable. This research aims to bridge this gap by proposing a planning framework that considers these three factors concurrently to achieve functional, efficient, and future-proof object arrangements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an accessibility-preserving planning approach for online multi-object shelf placement that jointly optimizes semantic organization, dense space utilization, and manipulator accessibility. This is motivated by the requirements of household service robots assisting users with tasks like storing groceries in partially occupied, multi-level shelves.

The specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Online and Sequential Planning:** Robots must make placement decisions one object at a time without prior knowledge of future object identities, arrival order, or geometries. Each decision must be based solely on the current shelf state and the incoming object.
- **Semantic Organization:** From a user's perspective, functionally or categorically related objects should be placed close to one another to facilitate future retrieval and maintain an organized environment. The planner needs to infer and apply these semantic relationships effectively.
- **Dense Space Utilization:** Available storage space in household shelves is typically limited. The planner must efficiently use this space, maximizing the number of objects that can be stored without creating clutter or instability.
- **Accessibility Preservation:** Each placement decision alters the available free space and the regions reachable by the manipulator. A key challenge is to avoid decisions that locally seem optimal but restrict future placement opportunities or render large shelf regions inaccessible for subsequent objects. This requires reasoning about how current actions affect long-term manipulability and the feasibility of future placements.
- **Balancing Conflicting Objectives:** The objectives of semantic organization, dense space utilization, and accessibility preservation can conflict. For example, semantic grouping might suggest a location far from a dense area, or a dense placement might block access to a semantically relevant region. The planner must find a balance among these competing criteria.
- **Addressing Geometric and Manipulation Challenges:** The method must handle objects with varied shapes and ensure collision-free placements on shelf boards, while also accounting for the physical constraints of robotic grippers and manipulator reach. This includes avoiding unstable stacked placements and navigating confined spaces.

To achieve these objectives, the authors propose a planning pipeline that integrates a semantic-density scoring mechanism with an accessibility map to guide candidate pose selection and penalize actions that reduce future accessibility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, Semantic-Dense Placement Planning (SDPP), formulates placement planning as an optimization problem that balances semantic consistency, spatial compactness, and long-term accessibility. The methodology comprises several integrated components:

#### 3.1. A. Environmental Representation

Each shelf board is modeled as a 2D occupancy grid. For objects already placed, their 2D footprint is projected onto the shelf plane, along with their height, center position, and semantic class.

#### 3.2. B. Accessibility Map (AM)

The AM serves two main purposes: to guide placement sampling towards reachable regions and to estimate the loss of accessible workspace after a hypothetical placement.

1. **Map Generation:**
 - The AM is constructed from the 2D footprints of existing objects.
 - Initially, only projected object footprints are marked as inaccessible.
 - To account for manipulator clearance and difficult-to-access areas, object footprints, shelf walls, and a rear-facing cone for each object are dilated.
 - Morphological closing is applied to the dilated occupancy mask using an elliptical structuring element, derived from the gripper width, to represent the clearance required for gripper operation.
 - The resulting binary mask identifies regions where candidate object centers should not be placed.

2. **Object Placement Sampling:**
 - For each new object, candidate placement poses (2D position and 12 orientations) are sampled on each shelf board.
 - The AM is used to filter these candidates before expensive motion planning.
 - **Footprint-Constrained (FC) Strategy:** A candidate is rejected if any part of its projected footprint overlaps the inaccessible region. This is a conservative strategy, restricting sampling to entirely accessible areas.
 - **Center-Constrained (CC) Strategy:** A candidate is rejected only if its footprint center lies within the inaccessible region or if its footprint overlaps existing objects or the shelf boundary. This strategy is less restrictive, allowing placements closer to inaccessible boundaries.
 - The system first attempts FC. If no executable placement is found, it falls back to CC to find a less constrained but potentially feasible pose.
 - Sampling continues until a predefined number of valid placements (n=250) is found or no additional feasible candidates can be generated.

3. **Space-Preservation Penalty:**
 - For each candidate placement, the AM is recomputed under the hypothetical shelf configuration.
 - A penalty `V_AM` is calculated based on the difference in inaccessible cells before and after the hypothetical placement, normalized by the total shelf size.
 - Placements that significantly increase the inaccessible workspace receive a higher penalty, discouraging actions that unnecessarily reduce future accessibility.

#### 3.3. C. Local Semantic-Density Scoring

This component aims to create semantically organized arrangements while maintaining spatial compactness.

- **Semantic Similarity:** Pairwise semantic similarity `s(o_i, o_j)` between objects is computed using GloVe word embeddings. Object labels are split into semantic words and combined with the full form word embedding. A weighted average of cosine similarity between these embeddings (α=0.8 for semantic words) defines the initial similarity score.
- **Contrast Normalization:** Raw similarity values are contrast-normalized by subtracting the average similarity of both objects to all other object classes. This enhances the distinction between related and unrelated objects.
- **Spatial Proximity Integration:** The semantic similarity score `ŝ(ô, o_j)` is weighted by a spatial proximity term `max(0, 1 - d(x, o_j)/d_max)`. This ensures that nearby objects have a stronger influence on the placement decision, promoting compact local clusters of related objects, while distant objects have a weaker influence, maintaining broader shelf context. `d(x, o_j)` is the shortest 2D Euclidean distance, and `d_max` is the maximum influence distance. The final local semantic-density score is `V_SemDen`.

#### 3.4. D. Placement Scoring

The final placement score `V_p(x | ô)` combines the semantic-density score and the space-preservation penalty:
`V_p(x | ô) = V_SemDen(x | ô) - w_2 * V_AM(x | ô)`
where `w_2` is a weight controlling the trade-off between semantic organization and accessibility preservation.

#### 3.5. E. Pick-and-Place Execution

The highest-scoring feasible placement is executed. This involves:
- **Grasp Generation:** Building upon GoalGrasp, grasp candidates are generated from an object's bounding box. The object's point cloud is projected onto the support plane, a minimum-area bounding rectangle of its convex hull is computed, and then lifted into 3D.
- **Grasp Pose Representation:** All grasp poses are represented in the object's local coordinate frame, allowing direct transformation to any candidate placement pose.
- **Motion Planning:** NVIDIA's cuRobo motion planner is used to check for collision-free pick-and-place trajectories for selected grasp-place pairs.
- Robust execution is supported by filtering grasp candidates based on gripper width, clearance, height, and rotation, followed by clustering. The inaccessible region is slightly reduced along the shelf depth to avoid overestimation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted in simulation using a Neobotix MMO-700 mobile manipulator and qualitatively on a real-world UR5e manipulator, addressing three main questions: arrangement quality, AM filtering efficiency, and real-world applicability.

#### 4.1. A. Shelf-Arrangement Performance

The performance of SDPP was compared against four baselines: Semantic Proximity Sampling (SPS), Semantic Proximity Sampling++ (SPS++), Density-driven placement, and Random placement. Metrics included additional objects placed, semantic sum, semantic score, proximity score, and density score.

1. **Semantic Organization:**
 - SDPP consistently achieved the highest semantic scores among all evaluated methods, with the difference becoming more pronounced as the shelf filled. This indicates SDPP's effectiveness in maintaining local semantic organization despite randomized object arrival.
 - SPS and SPS++ showed semantic scores, but SPS exhausted placement candidates earlier due to its restricted placement choices around the most semantically related object.
 - Random and Density-driven baselines exhibited minimal improvement in semantic scores.

2. **Shelf Utilization and Placement Capacity:**
 - SDPP, SPS++, and the Density-driven method achieved high space utilization as the shelf filled. SPS and Random placement resulted in substantially lower final densities.
 - SDPP achieved both the highest average shelf density and the largest average number of additional objects placed (30.7 ± 3.9), outperforming all baselines. This suggests that SDPP's integrated approach allows for dense packing without sacrificing semantic quality or future placement capacity.

3. **Final Arrangement Quality (Table I Summary):**
 - SDPP significantly outperformed all baselines in both semantic metrics (Semantic Sum: 61.6 ± 11.5; Semantic Score: 1.440 ± 0.180; Proximity Score: 0.788 ± 0.036).
 - SDPP achieved significantly higher shelf density (0.261 ± 0.016) than all baselines, except for the dedicated density-driven method, where the difference was not statistically significant.
 - Overall, SDPP demonstrated strong performance across placement capacity and semantic metrics, producing arrangements that were simultaneously dense, semantically organized, and capable of accommodating more incoming objects.

4. **Scoring-Component Ablation:**
 - Removing the AM-based accessibility-preservation term (w_2 = 0) resulted in slightly fewer objects placed (29.2 ± 2.7 vs. 30.7 ± 3.9 for full SDPP) and a slightly lower density, while maintaining high semantic scores. This indicates that local compactness alone is insufficient for fully preserving future placement opportunities.
 - Increasing the accessibility-preservation weight (w_2 = 5) yielded the highest average number of additional placements (31.1 ± 3.5) and highest shelf density (0.262 ± 0.023) but resulted in a reduction in both semantic metrics. This highlights the trade-off between accessibility preservation and semantic quality. The ablations suggest complementary roles for the two scoring components.

5. **Per-Object Planning Time:**
 - SDPP required an average of 10.42 ± 3.06 seconds per successful placement. While slower than SPS++ (7.12 ± 3.34 s) and Random (2.43 ± 0.43 s), the authors argue this computational overhead is acceptable given the improvements in shelf arrangement quality and storage efficiency. Random's lower runtime was associated with substantially lower placement capacity and arrangement quality.

#### 4.2. B. Accessibility-Map Filtering Efficiency

This evaluation focused on the candidate-search stage to assess whether AM-based filters reduce the time for inverse kinematics (IK) and motion planning.

1. **Filtering Performance:**
 - Both Footprint-Constrained (FC) and Center-Constrained (CC) filtering strategies substantially reduced the accumulated IK and motion-planning time required to identify the first executable placement compared to a no-filter baseline.
 - The FC→CC strategy, which falls back to CC if FC fails, provided mean savings of 52.8 seconds under score-ranked ordering and 7.4 seconds under randomized ordering, both statistically significant. This suggests AM-based filtering significantly reduces the expensive candidate-feasibility search, particularly when combined with score-based ranking.
 - FC occasionally over-pruned the candidate set in heavily occupied shelves, which the FC→CC fallback mechanism addressed.

2. **Effect of Shelf Occupancy:**
 - Candidate-search time did not increase monotonically with shelf occupancy. It initially decreased as the semantic score provided guidance, then increased as the shelf filled due to more IK/motion planning failures. At very high occupancy, search time decreased again as inexpensive early-stage checks rejected more candidates.

#### 4.3. C. Qualitative Real-World Experiment

A qualitative experiment on a UR5e robot arm demonstrated the applicability of the SDPP pipeline in a realistic two-level shelf scenario, with the robot placing 24 additional objects into an initially occupied shelf. This indicates the method's potential for real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Semantic-Dense Placement Planning (SDPP), an integrated approach that addresses the complex challenge of multi-object placement in confined shelf environments for household service robots. The work contributes to the field by jointly considering semantic organization, dense space utilization, and manipulator accessibility under online, sequential conditions with unknown future object arrivals.

The significance of this work lies in:
- **Integrated Optimization:** SDPP demonstrates that it is feasible to simultaneously optimize for multiple, often conflicting, objectives (semantic organization, density, and accessibility) in robotic placement tasks. This moves beyond approaches that focus on only one or two of these aspects.
- **Improved Arrangement Quality:** The proposed method generates shelf arrangements that are not only more semantically organized but also denser and capable of accommodating a larger number of objects compared to existing baselines. This directly translates to more functional and efficient storage solutions for users.
- **Enhanced Planning Efficiency:** The introduction of the Accessibility Map provides a practical mechanism for pre-filtering unfeasible placement candidates, thereby substantially reducing the computational burden associated with inverse kinematics and full motion planning. This efficiency is critical for online robotic operation.
- **Long-Term Accessibility:** By explicitly incorporating an accessibility-preservation penalty, SDPP encourages placements that maintain viable workspace for future interactions, mitigating the risk of a robot inadvertently cornering itself or rendering parts of the shelf unusable.
- **Real-World Applicability:** The qualitative demonstration on a physical robot system suggests that the concepts and algorithms developed are robust enough for real-world domestic scenarios, paving the way for more capable and user-friendly service robots.

The potential impact of this research extends to various domains requiring intelligent object organization, including household assistance, retail shelf management, and warehouse automation. By enabling robots to make more informed, context-aware decisions about object placement, this work contributes to the development of autonomous systems that can effectively manage and organize complex environments, thereby enhancing their utility and integration into human daily life and industrial processes.
