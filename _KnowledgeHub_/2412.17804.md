# GausSim: Foreseeing Reality by Gaussian Simulator for Elastic Objects

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from S-Lab Nanyang Technological University, Fudan University, and The University of Hong Kong developed GausSim, a neural network-based simulator that integrates continuum mechanics with Gaussian Splatting to accurately predict the dynamic behaviors of real-world elastic objects. The model achieved significantly lower L2 errors (e.g., 1.85 vs. 11.32 for DreamGaussian4D) and reduced kernel-wise computations by 95%, resulting in faster prediction speeds and lower GPU memory usage.

## Problem

- Existing generative models for dynamic objects often lack physical realism and fail to adhere to fundamental physical laws, leading to inaccurate long-term predictions.
- Current physics-integrated models (e.g., MPM, PBD) rely on idealized assumptions or discrete particle treatments, limiting their ability to capture complex real-world elastic deformations and often suffering from instability or high computational cost for densely represented objects.
- A lack of real-world datasets specifically designed to evaluate physically plausible dynamic simulations hinders the development and validation of robust simulators.

## Method

- **GausSim** treats each Gaussian kernel as a "Center of Mass System" (CMS) representing a continuous piece of matter, integrating continuum mechanics principles to model deformations using a deformation gradient and Polar SVD decomposition.
- A hierarchical structure clusters kernels into CMSs, enabling a coarse-to-fine simulation approach that reduces kernel-wise computations by 95% while maintaining fidelity.
- Explicit physics constraints, including mass and momentum conservation, are incorporated into the neural network's loss function to ensure robust, physically plausible, and interpretable dynamic predictions.

## Results

- GausSim consistently outperformed baselines, demonstrating substantially lower L2 errors across all tested real-world and synthetic objects (e.g., L2 error of **1.85** vs. **11.32** for DreamGaussian4D on 'Mothorchids' unseen 83 frames).
- The hierarchical structure reduced kernel-wise computations by 95%, leading to faster per-frame prediction times (**0.13**±0.01s) and lower GPU memory consumption (**2.1** GB) compared to baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that explicit mass and momentum conservation significantly contribute to maintaining object shape, volume, and dynamic fidelity, with the model also exhibiting strong generalization across diverse object types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling Gaussian kernels as continuous Centers of Mass Systems, rather than discrete particles, allows for a more accurate and physically consistent representation of elastic deformations by directly integrating continuum mechanics.
> - A hierarchical simulation approach significantly enhances computational efficiency for densely represented objects without sacrificing detail, by propagating dynamics from coarser to finer levels.
> - Enforcing explicit physics constraints like mass and momentum conservation is crucial for robust, long-term physically plausible predictions that surpass purely data-driven or simplified physics-based methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The simulation of dynamic processes is fundamental across various scientific and applied domains, including computer graphics, robotics, and physical modeling. A significant challenge in this area is accurately bridging the gap between the complexities of real-world phenomena and the often-idealized assumptions of computational models.

Previous research approaches to simulating object dynamics can generally be categorized into two main streams. One stream utilizes generative models, often video-based, to predict the movements of complex objects. Examples include methods based on video diffusion models such as DreamGaussian4D and 4D-fy. While these approaches have demonstrated progress in creating dynamic content, they primarily rely on visual supervision without explicit physics constraints. Consequently, they frequently fail to adhere to fundamental physical laws, such as conservation of mass or momentum, and exhibit limitations in generating accurate long-term predictions.

The second stream integrates physics priors into reconstructed 3D representations, such as Gaussian Splatting. These methods, exemplified by approaches employing the Material Point Method (MPM) or Position Based Dynamics (PBD), typically treat Gaussian kernels as discrete particles or simulation units within an analytical physics framework. While incorporating physics principles, these analytical models are often derived under idealized conditions or simplified assumptions. For instance, MPM-based simulations might suffer from instability in long-term predictions due to explicit Euler time integration or error accumulation from frequent particle-grid interpolations. Similarly, mass-spring systems offer only approximations of true elasticity. Such limitations restrict their ability to fully capture the intricate complexities of real-world scenarios.

GausSim positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of both generative and physics-integrated models. It proposes a novel neural network-based simulator that specifically integrates principles from continuum mechanics with Gaussian Splatting representations. By treating Gaussian kernels as continuous pieces of matter rather than discrete particles and incorporating explicit physics constraints, GausSim aims to learn and simulate real-world elastic dynamics without relying on idealized assumptions, thereby enhancing both physical plausibility and long-term prediction accuracy. Its introduction of a hierarchical structure further aims to improve computational efficiency and fidelity for densely distributed kernels, a common issue in realistic object representations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel neural network-based simulator capable of accurately capturing and predicting the dynamic behaviors of real-world elastic objects, particularly those represented using Gaussian kernels. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims:

- **Bridging the Reality-Simulation Gap:** A fundamental motivation is to overcome the disparity between the complex dynamics observed in the real world and the simplified conditions often assumed by existing simulation models. Current generative models often lack physical realism, while analytical physics models frequently operate under idealized assumptions that do not fully capture real-world intricacies. GausSim aims to bridge this gap by learning directly from real-world dynamic data while enforcing physical consistency.

- **Integrating Continuum Mechanics with Neural Networks:** The authors are motivated to leverage the principles of continuum mechanics, which describe materials as continuous rather than discrete entities, to model deformations more accurately. By treating each Gaussian kernel as a "Center of Mass System" (CMS) representing a continuous piece of matter, the objective is to capture realistic deformations without relying on idealized particle-based assumptions. The integration of this physics framework within a neural network architecture is intended to allow the system to learn underlying physics laws directly from data.

- **Improving Computational Efficiency and Fidelity:** Reconstructed objects often comprise a large number of densely distributed Gaussian kernels, leading to significant computational overhead for kernel-wise simulations. A key objective is to enhance computational efficiency while maintaining high fidelity in dynamic predictions. This motivated the development of a hierarchical structure that organizes kernels into CMSs, enabling a coarse-to-fine simulation approach that reduces redundant computations.

- **Ensuring Physical Plausibility and Interpretability:** The research aims to ensure that the simulation results are not only visually realistic but also physically plausible and interpretable. This is achieved by explicitly incorporating fundamental physics constraints, such as the conservation of mass and momentum, into the GausSim framework. These constraints are intended to lead to more robust performance and results that adhere to physical laws.

- **Developing a Realistic Evaluation Dataset:** To validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach on real-world dynamics, the authors were motivated to create a new dataset, named READY. This dataset, comprising multi-view videos of various real-world elastic objects undergoing complex deformations, provides a benchmark specifically grounded in real-world physics, contrasting with datasets focused solely on motion quality from video diffusion models.

In summary, the core motivation is to create a robust, efficient, and physically interpretable simulator for elastic objects represented by Gaussian Splatting, capable of accurately forecasting real-world dynamic behaviors by integrating continuum mechanics and explicit physics constraints within a hierarchical neural network framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GausSim is structured as a neural network-based physics simulator that models the dynamic behaviors of elastic objects represented by Gaussian kernels, integrating principles from continuum mechanics. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

Given an object reconstructed via Gaussian Splatting, represented as a set of kernels $G = \{x_k, \sigma_k, c_k, \alpha_k\}_{k \in K}$ at times $\{t-1, t\}$, along with invariant attributes $A = \{\rho_k, a_k\}_{k \in K}$ (density $\rho_k$ and material attribute vector $a_k$), GausSim aims to predict the kernels' states at time $t+1$: $G_{t+1} = \psi_\theta(G_t, G_{t-1}, A)$. Here, $\psi_\theta$ is the neural network-based GausSim. The initial states $G_0$ define the material space. To enable predictions from $t=0$, initial positions $\{ \bar{x}_{k,-1} \}$ are trainable, and corresponding covariances and colors are predicted. Unknown attributes $\bar{A}$ are also trainable. Training supervises the rendered images of deformed Gaussian kernels against ground truth multi-view videos $\{I^d_t\}$.

#### 3.2. Integrating with Continuum Mechanics

Instead of treating Gaussian kernels as discrete particles, GausSim regards each kernel as a Center of Mass System (CMS) that describes a continuous piece of matter. This aligns with continuum mechanics principles by accounting for the volumes represented by the kernels.
- **Deformation Map:** The deformed states $x$ are derived from undeformed states $X$ in material space via a deformation map $x = \phi(X, t)$. For kernels, a first-order approximation is used: $x_k \approx x_r + F_k(X_k - X_r)$, where $x_r$ is a static root position and $F_k$ is the deformation gradient for kernel $k$, predicted by GausSim based on nearby kernel interactions.
- **Polar SVD Decomposition:** The deformation gradient $F$ is represented using "Polar SVD": $F = U\Lambda V^\top$, where $U$ and $V$ are rotation matrices and $\Lambda$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values representing deformation magnitude. In practice, two quaternions represent $U$ and $V$, and a 3D vector $\lambda = [p, q, r]$ represents $\Lambda$.
- **Kernel Transformations:** The deformation gradient $F_k$ transforms the kernel's covariance: $\sigma_k = F_k \Sigma_k F_k^\top$ (where $\Sigma_k$ is the covariance in material space). The color $c_k$ is updated based on the rotation $R_k = U_k V_k^\top$ and view direction $d$: $c_k = g(R_k^\top d)$. This formulation minimizes error accumulation, directly computes physics variables, and provides interpretable components for regularization.

#### 3.3. Hierarchical Structure

To manage the computational cost of densely distributed Gaussian kernels, GausSim employs a hierarchical structure.
- **Construction:** Kernels are clustered based on proximity to form higher-level CMSs from the bottom up. A CMS at level $l$ (denoted $c_l$) has its position $x_{c_l}$, attributes $a_{c_l}$, mass $m_{c_l}$, and volume $V_{c_l}$ computed as weighted averages or sums of its constituent lower-level CMSs or kernels. An individual Gaussian kernel is considered a CMS at level 0.
- **Simulation and Propagation:** Dynamics are simulated iteratively, starting from the highest (coarsest) level and propagating updates downwards. Deformation gradients predicted at a higher level are used to update the physics properties of the CMSs or kernels at the next lower level. This coarse-to-fine approach is formalized by recursively expanding the deformation equations, where positions, covariances, and colors are updated using the product of multiple deformation gradients from each level in the hierarchy (Equations 8-11).
- **Efficiency:** This hierarchical approach significantly reduces kernel-wise computations by avoiding redundant predictions for closely deforming kernels. The paper claims a 95% reduction in kernel-wise computations. It also provides a physically meaningful interpretation of Gaussian kernels as CMSs.

#### 3.4. Explicit Physics Constraints

GausSim incorporates explicit physics principles to ensure robust and physically plausible simulations.
- **Mass Conservation:** Object density is assumed invariant. Mass conservation requires volumes to remain constant during deformation. In continuum mechanics, $dv = \det(F) dV$. Thus, forcing $\det(F) = 1$ ensures volume (and mass) conservation. This is achieved by normalizing the singular values $\lambda = [p, q, r]$: $\lambda = [p/\sqrt{pqr}, q/\sqrt{pqr}, r/\sqrt{pqr}]$. The hierarchical CMSs also inherently satisfy this due to their construction.
- **Momentum Conservation:** Momentum of a higher-level CMS should be conserved by the sum of momenta of its constituent lower-level CMSs. This is enforced via a squared error loss, $L_{mom}$, constraining the relationship between hierarchical layers.

#### 3.5. Training

- **Supervision:** Training uses multi-view videos $\{I^d_t\}$ as ground truth. The predicted Gaussian kernels $\hat{G}_t$ are rendered into images $\hat{I}^d_t$, and the loss function $L_{I,t}$ combines $L_2$ error and D-SSIM between rendered and ground truth images.
- **Auto-regressive Prediction:** GausSim predicts $T$ steps auto-regressively. $T$ increases as training progresses, starting from $T=1$ and capping at $T=16$. Gradients are back-propagated between adjacent time steps to mitigate gradient explosion.
- **Static Loss:** A self-supervised static loss $L_{static}$ ensures that given static inputs with zero velocities, the predicted kernel states remain unchanged. This aids initialization and is applied with a probability of $1/T$.
- **Total Loss:** The final training objective is a sum of these components: $L = \sum_{t=1}^T L_{I,t} + L_{static} + L_{mom}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated GausSim through experiments on both real-world and synthetic datasets, comparing its performance against established baselines.

#### 4.1. Datasets

- **READY Dataset:** A novel multi-view video dataset, REAl DYnamics (READY), was introduced. It comprises elastic deformations of four real-world objects: moth orchids, carnation, pudding, and a duck. For each object, 30 dynamic sequences (100 frames each, 50 FPS) were captured from 4-6 synchronized camera views, with external forces applied through poking and dragging. Additionally, 200 static images were collected for object reconstruction. Foreground segmentation was applied using SAM. For evaluation, the first 16 frames were used for training, with the remaining 34 or 83 frames for testing unseen dynamics.
- **Synthetic Dataset:** Elastic dynamics of a bunny were synthesized using Blender, following READY's multi-view video settings (24 FPS), with 30 sequences totaling 1800 dynamic frames.

#### 4.2. Baselines

The performance of GausSim was compared against DreamGaussian4D (DG4D) and PhysDreamer (PD). PhysGaussian, which uses a similar MPM engine to PD, was considered represented by PD's performance, as PD estimates material parameters for optimal simulation. All models were trained on the same number of frames.

#### 4.3. Evaluation Metrics

Evaluation primarily focused on pixel-wise $L_2$ errors to assess the faithfulness of predictions against ground truth multi-view videos. Structural similarity (SSIM) and perceptual evaluation (LPIPS) were provided in the supplementary material. All evaluations were conducted exclusively on the foreground objects.

#### 4.4. Dynamic Simulations

- **Quantitative Results (Table 1):** GausSim consistently achieved superior performance, exhibiting lower $L_2$ errors across all objects (Mothorchids, Carnation, Pudding, Duck, Bunny) and evaluation phases (training frames "Seen" and unseen test frames "Unseen 34/83"). For example, on "Mothorchids" (unseen 83), GausSim had an $L_2$ error of 1.85±0.14, compared to DG4D's 11.32±3.09 and PD's 2.59±0.27.
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure A1):**
 - **DG4D:** Capable of reconstructing dynamics within the training set but failed to predict unseen dynamics, often displaying fixed patterns and lacking realistic dissipation.
 - **PD:** Struggled with complex deformations, such as those of "Mothorchids," and produced motions with rapid dissipation across all domains. This led to high errors on the training set and an unusually fast decline in errors on the test set, indicating a failure to maintain realistic dynamics.
 - **GausSim:** Demonstrated faithful and robust prediction of dynamics across all scenarios, accurately mimicking real-world deformation patterns and dissipation, regardless of complexity. Space-time slices further illustrated its ability to capture continuous deformation patterns more effectively than baselines.

#### 4.5. Computational Efficiency (Table 2)

Using "Mothorchids" as an example, the hierarchical structure in GausSim led to a 95% reduction in kernel-wise computations (N_F/N_K = 0.05). This resulted in:
- **Faster Prediction Speed:** GausSim achieved a per-frame forward time of 0.13±0.01 seconds, significantly faster than GausSim without hierarchy (0.36±0.01s) and PD (1.67±0.05s), and comparable to DG4D (0.14±0.01s).
- **Lower GPU Memory Cost:** GausSim required 2.1 GB of GPU memory, which was less than GausSim without hierarchy (3.5 GB), PD (4.6 GB), and DG4D (7.6 GB).

#### 4.6. Ablation Study (Table 3, Figure 5)

Ablation studies on the "Mothorchids" domain investigated the impact of explicit physics constraints:
- **Without Mass Conservation ("w/o mass"):** GausSim exhibited higher errors (2.19±0.52 Seen, 1.90±0.16 Unseen 83) and struggled to maintain the object's shape or volume.
- **Without Momentum Conservation ("w/o momen"):** GausSim yielded blurred details and more static motions, indicating a loss of dynamic fidelity (2.64±0.62 Seen, 1.91±0.17 Unseen 83).
- **Joint Training ("Jointly"):** GausSim trained on all domains (1.90±0.54 Seen, 1.88±0.18 Unseen 83) delivered similar quantitative and qualitative accuracy to the model trained solely on "Mothorchids" (1.78±0.23 Seen, 1.85±0.14 Unseen 83). This indicated strong generalization abilities of GausSim across diverse object types, attributed to its shape-independent designs.

#### 4.7. Interactive Applications (Figure A2)

The method demonstrated support for interactive simulations, where custom external forces could be applied to selected Gaussian kernels, producing vivid and robust dynamic responses.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GausSim represents a notable advancement in the field of physically-based simulation for real-world elastic objects, with several areas of significance and potential impact.

Firstly, a core contribution is its ability to **bridge the gap between simulated environments and real-world dynamics**. By integrating continuum mechanics with a neural network architecture and treating Gaussian kernels as continuous pieces of matter, GausSim can learn and predict realistic deformations without relying on the idealized assumptions often found in traditional analytical physics models or the lack of physical constraints in purely generative approaches. This capability is crucial for applications demanding high fidelity and physical plausibility.

Secondly, the introduction of a **hierarchical structure** for organizing Gaussian kernels significantly enhances **computational efficiency and simulation fidelity**. The coarse-to-fine approach reduces redundant computations by approximately 95% while preserving detailed dynamics. This efficiency makes realistic simulations of complex, densely represented objects more feasible for practical applications. The generalizability of this structure, being agnostic to object shape, further broadens its applicability across various object types.

Thirdly, the **explicit enforcement of physics principles**, specifically mass and momentum conservation, contributes to the model's **robustness and interpretability**. By ensuring that simulation results adhere to fundamental physical laws, GausSim provides more reliable and predictable outcomes compared to models that might drift from physical reality over time. This interpretability is valuable for scientific analysis and engineering applications where understanding the underlying physical processes is important.

Fourthly, the release of the **READY dataset** is a significant contribution to the research community. This multi-view video dataset, featuring real-world elastic deformations of diverse objects, provides a valuable benchmark for evaluating future research in dynamic object simulation. Unlike datasets primarily focused on motion quality from generative models, READY is grounded in real-world physics, fostering the development of more physically accurate simulators.

The potential impact of GausSim extends to various fields:
- **Computer Graphics and Animation:** It can enable the creation of more realistic and physically plausible animations for films, video games, and virtual reality experiences, reducing the need for manual fine-tuning to achieve physical accuracy.
- **Robotics and Haptics:** Accurate simulation of deformable objects is critical for training robots to interact with soft materials and for developing realistic haptic feedback systems.
- **Engineering and Design:** GausSim could be used for prototyping and testing designs of elastic structures under various forces, offering a computational tool for predicting material behaviors.
- **Scientific Research:** The ability to simulate complex physical phenomena with high fidelity could aid in understanding material science, biomechanics, and other fields involving deformable bodies.

Despite its advancements, the paper acknowledges certain limitations, such as the requirement for high-quality input data (e.g., high shutter speed for fast motions) and the dependency on the quality of the initial Gaussian Splatting reconstruction. However, the demonstrated capabilities of GausSim in faithfully reproducing and foreseeing real-world dynamics indicate a promising direction for future research in physically-based simulation.
