# Task-Relevant Feature-Dynamics Fidelity Enables Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Robotic Ultrasound Scanning

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper defines task-relevant feature-dynamics fidelity (TR-FDF), the cross-domain consistency of motion-induced feature changes, and shows by local contraction analysis that its motion sensitivity erodes the real closed-loop contraction margin. A three-stage ultrasound observation generator (shared structural latent, conditional flow matching, reflow distillation) plus fixed trajectory noise trains a raw B-mode SAC policy purely in simulation, reaching 390 of 400 zero-shot real-robot successes at 67.1 Hz.

## Problem

- Learning B-mode servoing policies needs many interaction episodes, yet real-robot ultrasound data collection is safety-constrained and expert annotation is hard to scale.
- Prior methods either feed policies segmentation masks that still require real ultrasound supervision, or keep raw B-mode via unpaired translation optimized only for single-frame realism.
- Single-frame metrics and simulator-side success never check whether the same probe motion induces consistent feature changes, so deployed systems still need real-robot fine-tuning.

## Method

- Stage I learns a ==shared structural latent domain== via twin mask and ultrasound encoders trained with ==distance-matching==, ==smoothness==, ==appearance-invariance==, ==segmentation==, ==alignment== and ==regularization losses==, separating pose-driven structure from speckle appearance.
- Stage II is a ==conditional flow matching== renderer modulating residual blocks with the structural latent through ==spatially adaptive affine layers==, supervised by a ==frozen-encoder reconstruction-consistency loss== on unpaired real images.
- Stage III ==reflow distillation== compresses the high-step teacher into a four-step student, while ==fixed-X0 trajectory rendering== reuses one noise sample per rollout to remove pose-independent frame jitter.

## Results

- A purely simulation-trained SAC policy succeeded in **390 of 400** zero-shot real-robot episodes across four phantom planes, with **2.66 mm** and **3.98 degree** terminal error at **67.1 Hz**.
- Lookup-table interventions held FID between **0.63** and **1.10** while TR-FDF sensitivity rose from **10.99** to **54.82** and real success fell from **100%** to **3%**, correlation about **-0.92**.
- Removing the shared structural domain kept FID at **31.14** but raised sensitivity to **244.26** and cut real success to **5/100**; UVCGAN2's best FID of **21.81** gave **0/100**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Closed-loop transfer hinges on motion-conditioned observation dynamics, not frame realism alone; static bias widens the residual error basin while dynamics mismatch destroys contraction.
> - Simulator-side policy success is a poor transfer predictor: variants all scoring 100% in simulation spanned 0 to 98 percent real-robot success.
> - For learned generators, correlating sampling noise across a trajectory and conditioning on a structural intermediate domain are cheap ways to preserve feedback-relevant dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic ultrasound systems have increasingly adopted machine learning for perception and closed-loop control to automate tasks such as target-plane acquisition and anatomical structure tracking. Training these systems, particularly those relying on high-dimensional B-mode ultrasound images, typically requires extensive interaction data. Acquiring such data from real robots or patients is constrained by cost, safety, and practical feasibility, while obtaining expert demonstrations or high-quality annotations at scale is challenging.

Simulation offers a scalable alternative for training control policies. However, a significant hurdle in this approach is the "sim-to-real gap," which refers to the discrepancy between simulated and real-world environments. For robotic ultrasound, specifically for policies that operate directly on raw B-mode images, achieving "zero-shot" sim-to-real transfer (where a policy trained purely in simulation is deployed without any real-world interaction, online adaptation, or fine-tuning) remains a significant challenge.

Existing approaches to autonomous robotic ultrasound servoing can be broadly categorized:
1. **Structured Intermediate Representations:** Some methods convert raw B-mode images into structured interfaces (e.g., vessel segmentations, anatomical masks) for policy control. While effective, these often require real ultrasound data for training the perception module that maps raw images to these structured representations, thus not strictly achieving zero-shot transfer from raw B-mode inputs.
2. **Raw B-mode Image Generation with Learned Simulators:** Another line of work retains raw B-mode images as policy input, combining simulation frameworks with image-to-image (I2I) generation techniques to create observations. These generators often use unpaired image translation, with single-frame appearance similarity or domain-distribution alignment as primary objectives, aiming for visual realism. However, prior research indicates that single-frame realism does not guarantee that task-relevant features (e.g., vessels, boundaries) undergo consistent changes under probe motion in both simulated and real environments. This *motion-induced observation mismatch* can invalidate the feedback behavior learned in simulation, often necessitating real-robot fine-tuning.

This paper addresses the gap in the latter category by identifying and quantifying a critical property beyond single-frame realism: the *task-relevant feature-dynamics fidelity (TR-FDF)*. It proposes that for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of raw B-mode closed-loop policies, simulated observations must not only be realistic but also reproduce the *task-relevant changes induced by probe motion* consistently with the real world. This study aims to develop a simulator that specifically targets this property to enable reliable zero-shot transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for robotic ultrasound policies trained exclusively on simulated raw B-mode observations. The motivation stems from several critical limitations in current robotic ultrasound research and development:

1. **High Cost and Safety Constraints of Real-Robot Data Acquisition:** Training image-based control policies, especially with reinforcement learning, typically demands a large volume of interaction data. Performing such training on real patients or physical robots is expensive, time-consuming, and raises significant safety concerns.
2. **Limitations of Existing Simulation Approaches for Zero-Shot Transfer:** While simulation offers a scalable alternative, simply generating visually realistic B-mode frames has proven insufficient for achieving robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. Current image-to-image translation methods primarily focus on single-frame appearance similarity (e.g., FID scores) and often fail to preserve the crucial relationship between probe motion and the resulting changes in task-relevant features.
3. **Mismatch in Pose-Observation Relationship:** The paper identifies that a fundamental problem lies in the inconsistency of how probe motion (pose changes) translates into observations (B-mode images) between simulation and the real world. A policy learns to associate actions (probe motions) with expected observation changes. If these *motion-induced feature dynamics* differ significantly between simulation and reality, the learned feedback behavior can become unstable or incorrect on the real robot, even if individual simulated frames look realistic.
4. **Defining and Quantifying Task-Relevant Feature-Dynamics Fidelity (TR-FDF):** To address this, the authors propose the concept of Task-Relevant Feature-Dynamics Fidelity (TR-FDF). TR-FDF refers to the cross-domain consistency of motion-induced feature changes. Specifically, it measures whether the same probe motion produces consistent statistical feature transitions in simulation and in the real system. The *mismatch* in TR-FDF is quantified as D_TR(∆p), the distributional distance between simulated and real feature transitions for a given probe motion ∆p. A key component of this is L_TR, which describes how sensitively this mismatch grows with the magnitude of probe motion.
5. **Theoretical Motivation for Addressing L_TR:** Through a local contraction analysis of closed-loop systems, the paper theoretically demonstrates that the local sensitivity of TR-FDF mismatch to probe motion (L_TR) directly *reduces the effective contraction margin* of the real closed loop. In contrast, static target bias (B) and motion-independent perturbations (d_0, from factors like speckle or generator noise) mainly *enlarge the ultimate error neighborhood* without directly affecting the stability (contraction factor). This theoretical distinction motivates making the reduction of L_TR a primary objective for the simulator.

In summary, the core objective is to develop a robotic ultrasound simulator that explicitly prioritizes preserving the fidelity of motion-induced task-relevant feature dynamics (TR-FDF), alongside single-frame realism and rollout efficiency, to enable stable and reliable zero-shot transfer of raw B-mode control policies to real robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a robotic ultrasound simulator designed to address the challenges of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, specifically by focusing on Task-Relevant Feature-Dynamics Fidelity (TR-FDF). The methodology integrates theoretical analysis, a multi-stage observation generator, and specific rendering techniques.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Analysis

The study begins with a local closed-loop analysis of perception-based control, distinguishing how different types of observation mismatch affect sim-to-real transfer.
- **Observation Mismatch Characterization:** The paper defines `∆y_e_t | ∆p_e_t = ∆p ~ Q_∆p_e` as the conditional distribution of task-feature increments given a probe motion, where `e` denotes the domain (sim or real). TR-FDF is the consistency between `Q_∆p_sim` and `Q_∆p_real`, with its mismatch defined as `D_TR(∆p) = W_2(Q_∆p_sim, Q_∆p_real)`, a Wasserstein-2 distance.
- **Impact on Closed-Loop Stability:** Using a stochastic Lyapunov contraction analysis, the authors derive an inequality for the real closed-loop error `E[V_real_t+1 | s_t]^(1/2) <= ρ_real * V_t^(1/2) + β`.
 - `ρ_real = ρ + C * K * L_TR`: The real-world contraction factor, which is influenced by `L_TR`. `ρ` is the simulator's contraction factor. `L_TR` is the local sensitivity of TR-FDF mismatch to probe motion magnitude. Increasing `L_TR` directly consumes the contraction margin.
 - `β = C * d_0 + B`: An additive error term. `d_0` is the feature-increment mismatch when the probe is stationary (e.g., speckle jitter), and `B` aggregates static feature bias and other residual physical discrepancies. These terms mainly enlarge the ultimate error neighborhood near the target without directly affecting contraction.
- **Design Criteria:** Based on this analysis, three criteria are established for the observation generator:
 1. **TR-FDF:** Reduce `L_TR` to preserve consistent local responses of task features to probe motion.
 2. **Motion-Independent Observation Quality:** Control `d_0` and `B` (e.g., through single-frame realism and stability) to shrink the residual error.
 3. **Rollout Efficiency:** Ensure sufficient generation speed for policy training.

#### 3.2. Generator Architecture

The proposed B-mode observation generator is a three-stage pipeline that integrates a structural renderer, a shared structural intermediate domain, conditional flow matching, and reflow distillation. It takes a policy-generated probe pose `p_t` and outputs a B-mode observation `x_t`.

- **Design Principles:**
 - **Reducing Motion-Related TR-FDF Mismatch (L_TR):** Achieved by Stage I (shared structural domain) and structure-conditioned Stage II.
 - **Motion-Independent Observation Quality (d_0 and B):** Addressed by Fixed-X0 rendering and conditional B-mode generation in Stage II.
 - **Rollout Efficiency:** Provided by Stage III (reflow distillation).

#### 3.3. Stage I: Shared Structural Domain

This stage creates a shared latent space (`z_t`) that is common to anatomical masks and ultrasound images, isolating pose-driven structural information from modality-specific appearance.
- **Purpose:** To provide a structural trajectory to Stage II that varies continuously with probe pose while being invariant to stochastic ultrasound appearance, thus preserving pose-driven anatomical dynamics.
- **Network Architecture:** Two encoders, `E_mask` (for rendered masks `m_t`) and `E_us` (for real ultrasound images `x_t`), map inputs to `z ∈ R^(8x56x56)`. A segmentation decoder `D_seg` is used during training.
- **Training Objectives and Loss Functions:**
 - **TR-FDF Dynamics:**
 - `L_dist`: Matches relative geometry between mask and ultrasound latent spaces to preserve motion-dependent response magnitudes.
 - `L_smooth`: Enforces local continuity, preventing abrupt latent jumps from small structural changes.
 - `L_invar`: Makes `E_us` invariant to speckle, brightness, and gamma perturbations, suppressing pose-independent latent variation.
 - **Non-Collapse and Shared Structure:**
 - `L_seg`: Requires `z_m` and `z_u` to retain anatomical information for segmentation recovery, preventing latent collapse.
 - `L_align`: Brings paired mask and ultrasound representations to the same structural location.
 - `L_reg`: Stabilizes the latent scale.
- **Training Data:** Uses a low-quality paired dataset (sliced 3D volumes) and a small public annotated dataset for pretraining and structural supervision. `E_us` is frozen after training to constrain Stage II.

#### 3.4. Stage II/III: Structure-Conditioned B-mode Rendering and Efficient Distillation

- **Stage II: Conditional B-mode Rendering:**
 - **Purpose:** Renders realistic ultrasound appearance conditioned on the structural latent `z_t` from Stage I. It learns speckle, acoustic-window characteristics, and device style, while transmitting structural changes from `z_t` to the image domain with bounded sensitivity to preserve stable TR-FDF responses.
 - **Method:** Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) is used. Given `z_t`, it learns a conditional velocity field to transform initial noise `x_0` into a B-mode image `x_1`. `z_t` is injected via spatially adaptive affine modulation (`h ← h · (1 + γ(z_t)) + η(z_t)`).
 - **Real-Ultrasound Consistency Constraint:** Trained using unpaired real ultrasound images. `E_us` (frozen from Stage I) extracts `z_u` from a real image `x`, and the CFM reconstructs `x` conditioned on `z_u`, minimizing a reconstruction loss. This enables learning real-ultrasound appearance without paired mask-US data.
- **Stage III: Reflow Distillation for Rollout Efficiency:**
 - **Purpose:** Compresses the high-step CFM teacher (from Stage II) into a low-step student to achieve real-time inference speed for policy rollouts.
 - **Method:** Reflow distillation approximates straight paths between teacher-generated pairs (`x_0`, `x_1`) using a `L_reflow` loss. The distilled student operates at a low number of function evaluations (NFE), typically NFE=4.

#### 3.5. Fixed-X0 Trajectory Rendering

- **Purpose:** To reduce generator-side pose-independent observation jitter, thereby decreasing the `d_0` component of the additive error `β`.
- **Method:** Instead of sampling independent initial noise (`ξ_t`) for each frame, a single initial noise `ξ*` is sampled once at the beginning of each trajectory and reused for all subsequent frames in that trajectory. This ensures that inter-frame changes are primarily driven by probe motion and the Stage-I structural latent, rather than random noise resampling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides comprehensive experimental results demonstrating the efficacy of the proposed simulator in enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for robotic ultrasound.

#### 4.1. Experimental Platform, Policy, and Evaluation Protocol

- **Hardware:** Universal Robots UR5 manipulator, Mindray M9 ultrasound scanner, C5-1s convex probe, force sensor, and a custom abdominal phantom with liver, vessels, and kidneys.
- **Software:** Ubuntu 24.04, ROS2. Policy training in a pure-sim setting (no real-robot data for training or fine-tuning).
- **Policy:** ResNet-50 visual front-end, MLP control head, Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). 6DoF probe servoing (5DoF from policy, 1D axial force control).
- **Evaluation:** 100 independent real-robot episodes per condition, success criteria: final position error < 3 mm, final rotation error < 5°.
- **TR-FDF Sensitivity Proxy:** An empirical estimate of `L_TR` by fitting the relationship between probe-motion magnitude and Wasserstein-2 distance of feature increments (from frozen `Φ_pose` and `Φ_seg` ResNet-50 encoders trained on real images) across motion bins.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Transfer in Multi-Plane 6DoF Servoing (Section V-C)

- **Performance:** The proposed simulator (with four-step inference) achieved an overall real-robot success rate of **97.5% (390/400 episodes)** across four distinct target planes (hepatic vein, right kidney, abdominal aorta, liver tip).
 - Plane A1: 98/100, A2: 100/100, A3: 100/100, A4: 92/100.
 - All failures were timeouts, with no out-of-bounds failures.
 - Mean episode duration: < 30 seconds.
- **Metrics:** TR-FDF sensitivity proxy: 31.61; FID: 29.66; Inference speed: 67.1 Hz (14.9 ms/frame).
- **Implication:** This demonstrates that the proposed simulator can generate raw B-mode observations that support highly reliable zero-shot deployment of a multi-plane 6DoF autonomous ultrasound servoing policy on a real robotic platform.

#### 4.3. Controlled Interventions on TR-FDF Sensitivity (Section V-D)

These experiments directly tested the hypothesis that higher TR-FDF sensitivity leads to lower real-robot transfer success.

- **Real-Image Lookup Intervention (2D):**
 - **Method:** A lookup simulator using real ultrasound images was perturbed by displacing queried positions (`y_d(p) = y_real(p + δ_d(p))`), keeping FID stable but increasing TR-FDF sensitivity.
 - **Results:** As TR-FDF sensitivity increased from 10.99 to 54.82, real-world success *decreased from 100% to nearly 0%*, while FID remained consistently low (0.628–1.100). Simulator success remained high (often 100%) even when real-world success plummeted, illustrating the "sim-real gap" attributed to TR-FDF mismatch. A strong negative correlation (-0.92) was observed between TR-FDF sensitivity and real-world success.
- **Learned-Simulator Structural Intervention (6DoF):**
 - **Method:** Structural perturbations were applied to the learned simulator, altering how neighboring structures evolved with pose, keeping single-frame fidelity comparable.
 - **Results:** As TR-FDF sensitivity increased from 31.61 to 302.64, real-world success *decreased from 98% to 0%*, while FID remained stable (30.35–31.29). Simulator success remained near 100% across these interventions. This confirmed the negative relationship between TR-FDF sensitivity and real-world success, independent of single-frame realism. Trajectories showed divergence and separate failure clusters, consistent with a multiplicative effect on convergence.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Section V-E)

- **Component-Level Ablations:**
 - **Random Frame-Level X_0:** Increased TR-FDF sensitivity (50.78) and FID (43.36), reducing real-robot success to 55/100. This confirms Fixed-X0's role in reducing pose-independent jitter.
 - **No Stage-I Shaping (Direct Mask-to-US):** Severely increased TR-FDF sensitivity (244.26), reducing real-robot success to 5/100, despite comparable FID (31.14). This highlights the crucial role of the shared structural domain.
 - **Weak Stage-I Shaping:** Increased TR-FDF sensitivity (164.61) and FID (53.05), reducing real-robot success to 17/100.
 - **GAN-based Generator:** Increased inference speed but significantly degraded FID (210.83) and reduced real-robot success to 18/100, emphasizing CFM's importance for realism.
 - **No Reflow Distillation:** TR-FDF sensitivity and FID remained comparable to baseline, with high real-robot success (99/100). However, inference speed dropped to 3.07 Hz, confirming reflow's role in efficiency.
 - **Key Insight:** All ablations maintained 100% simulator-side success, but real-world performance varied substantially, indicating that simulator success alone is not a predictor of transferability.

- **Stage-I Loss-Level Ablations:** Removing individual Stage-I losses (e.g., `L_dist`, `L_smooth`, `L_invar`, `L_seg`, `L_align`, `L_reg`) generally increased TR-FDF sensitivity and/or FID, confirming their specific roles in preserving latent dynamics, structural representation, and appearance invariance. Notably, removing `L_smooth` caused the largest increase in TR-FDF sensitivity (to 138.34).

#### 4.5. Comparison with External Baselines (Section V-F)

- **Sequential Screening:** I2I and video-translation methods were evaluated in a sequential screening process (train stably, support policy learning in sim, transfer to real).
- **Results:** Many baselines (UNIT-DDPM, UNest, STABLE) failed to support SAC training. Methods that achieved 100% simulator success (CycleGAN, CUT, UVCGAN2, UNSB) typically exhibited low real-robot success rates (1-7/100).
- **UVCGAN2:** Achieved the best FID (21.81) and high simulator success (92/100) but had the highest TR-FDF sensitivity (904.16) and 0/100 real-robot success.
- **Implication:** This demonstrated that neither single-frame realism nor simulator-side policy success guarantees real-world transfer. The proposed simulator uniquely achieved low TR-FDF sensitivity (31.61), high FID (29.66), usable speed (67.1 FPS), stable SAC training (100%), and reliable zero-shot transfer (98%).

#### 4.6. Task-Level Simulator Consistency under Disturbances (Section V-G)

- **Contact-Force Variation:** The simulator-trained policy achieved 94.0% and 98.0% real-robot success at 5 N and 15 N contact forces, respectively, demonstrating robustness to typical contact-force variations.
- **High-Disturbance Tracking under Phantom Motion:** The robot successfully tracked predefined spiral and square phantom trajectories, demonstrating that the policy maintained closed-loop tracking under sustained target-pose variation in a real environment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of medical robotics and sim-to-real transfer:

1. **Enabling Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Raw B-mode Robotic Ultrasound:** The most significant finding is the successful demonstration of high-fidelity zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for a robotic ultrasound policy trained exclusively on raw B-mode images generated by the proposed simulator. Achieving a 97.5% success rate over 400 real-robot deployments across multiple target planes represents a notable advance in deploying simulation-trained policies directly on physical systems without real-world interaction data.
2. **Introduction of Task-Relevant Feature-Dynamics Fidelity (TR-FDF):** The paper formally defines TR-FDF as a critical property for sim-to-real transfer, moving beyond traditional single-frame realism metrics. The theoretical analysis (local contraction margin) and controlled experimental interventions convincingly demonstrate that `L_TR`, the local sensitivity of TR-FDF mismatch to probe motion, is a key determinant of real-world closed-loop stability and performance, distinct from `d_0` (motion-independent jitter) and `B` (static bias). This provides a new lens for diagnosing and addressing the sim-to-real gap.
3. **Development of a TR-FDF-Oriented Simulator Architecture:** The proposed three-stage simulator architecture, incorporating a shared structural intermediate domain, dedicated loss functions for preserving latent dynamics, fixed-X0 trajectory rendering, conditional flow matching, and reflow distillation, specifically addresses the identified criteria of TR-FDF, motion-independent observation quality, and rollout efficiency. The ablation studies confirm the importance of each component in achieving the overall performance.
4. **Challenging the Sufficiency of Single-Frame Realism and Simulator Success:** The extensive comparisons with external baselines and ablation studies repeatedly show that high single-frame image quality (low FID) or high simulator-side policy success does not guarantee successful real-robot transfer. This finding is crucial for guiding future simulator development, emphasizing the need to evaluate observation generators across multiple metrics and, critically, on real-world deployment.
5. **Robustness to Real-World Disturbances:** The demonstration of robust performance under varying contact forces and dynamic phantom motion suggests that policies trained with this simulator can handle realistic operational variations, further enhancing the practical utility of the approach.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. By enabling reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, it can:
- **Accelerate Robotic Ultrasound Development:** Significantly reduce the time, cost, and safety risks associated with real-world data collection and policy training.
- **Democratize Access to Advanced Robotics:** Lower the barrier for developing and testing complex robotic control policies, particularly in sensitive medical applications.
- **Advance Autonomous Medical Procedures:** Pave the way for more sophisticated and fully autonomous robotic ultrasound scanning systems in clinical settings, improving diagnostic accuracy and patient care.
- **Generalize Transfer Principles:** While developed for ultrasound, the concept of TR-FDF and its theoretical implications may extend to other sensor modalities where actions induce complex, stochastic observation changes (e.g., tactile sensing, specialized optical imaging), prompting further research into action-conditioned observation fidelity.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge that TR-FDF sensitivity complements, rather than replaces, single-frame image quality measures, and that statistical independence between these metrics was not established. Furthermore, while the local analysis in Section III is general, the TR-FDF proxy, simulator architecture, and validation were specifically developed for robotic ultrasound. Its empirical applicability to other observation modalities remains an area for future investigation.
