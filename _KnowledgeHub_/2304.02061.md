# Generating Continual Human Motion in Diverse 3D Scenes

> [!summary] Summary
> This method synthesizes long, animator-guided human motion in arbitrary 3D scenes from sparse action keypoints and a seed sequence. It splits motion into path-following walks plus transitions in and out of actions, trained only on scene-agnostic mocap by predicting motion in a goal-centric canonical frame whose origin holds the next target. It generalizes across Replica, Matterport, HPS, ScanNet and NeRF scenes and is preferred over prior methods.

## Problem

- Prior scene-aware synthesis handles single actions without transitions, so it cannot chain grabbing, sitting and walking into long-range diverse behavior.
- Existing methods condition on scene geometry and need motion registered to 3D scenes and objects, which does not scale to arbitrary real environments.
- Phase-functioned and state-machine controllers demand manual phase or action annotation, and egocentric-frame walkers drift badly over long planned paths.

## Method

- Action keypoints: three or four target joint contacts are turned into anchor poses by ==inverse kinematics== optimizing the ==VPoser== latent space, decoupling scene reasoning from motion synthesis.
- **WalkNet**, an autoregressive ==BERT==-style transformer, predicts motion in a goal-centric canonical frame where the next path waypoint and tangent sit at the origin, avoiding drift.
- **TransNet**, a ==masked-autoencoder== transformer trained with progressively grown masks on mocap, infills transitions between the walking seed and the anchor pose placed at the origin.

## Results

- Perceptual study with 50 participants preferred this method for realism over SAMP **71.8%** to **28.2%**, over GAMMA **95.6%** to **4.4%**, and over Wang et al. **100%** to **0%**.
- Foot skate is **0.91 cm** per frame versus **0.94** for GAMMA, **1.34** for SAMP and **4.53** for Wang et al. on paths through real scanned scenes.
- Ablations: WalkNet beats MoGlow (**0.91** versus **1.88** foot skate, **75.7%** preference) and TransNet beats NeMF (**1.1** versus **1.54**, **66.8%** preference); language-placed keypoints match manual ones.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting motion in a goal-centric frame with the target at the origin removes accumulated drift, so long sequences need no explicit goal input to the network.
> - Sparse contact keypoints are a surprisingly powerful action abstraction: scene reasoning can be decoupled from motion, letting scene-agnostic mocap generalize to unseen geometry.
> - Task-specialized inbetweeners beat general-purpose ones at fixed-length infilling, but the approach assumes flat floors and physically valid user keypoints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of human motion synthesis has seen various approaches, broadly categorized by their conditioning on 3D scenes or lack thereof, and the complexity of motions they generate.

Prior work in **human motion prediction without the 3D scene** has explored methods ranging from classical techniques like Hidden Markov Chains, Gaussian Processes, and physics-based models to more recent applications of recurrent neural networks, Graph Neural Networks, and variational auto-encoders. While these methods have advanced motion prediction and diversity, recurrent approaches often suffer from drift over time, and scene-agnostic models do not account for environmental interactions. Recent advancements have also utilized transformer architectures for unconditional or text/music-conditioned motion sequences. This paper builds on transformer architectures but integrates them with a focus on real 3D scenes.

**Motion Inbetweening** is another classic paradigm, where the goal is to interpolate frames between animator-provided keyframes. This work incorporates elements of motion inbetweening through its `TransNet` component.

In **character control for video games**, methods like motion matching and motion graphs are used to generate kinematic motion sequences constrained by environmental and user inputs. Deep learning variants, such as phase-conditioned RNNs, have also been employed for realistic motion. However, many of these approaches require significant manual effort, or their learned variants often rely on explicit phase information which is non-intuitive for non-periodic motions and requires manual labeling. This work aims to reduce such manual effort.

The study of the **relationship between humans, scenes, and objects** has led to methods for 3D object detection, affordance prediction, and generating plausible static poses conditioned on 3D scenes. While these works address static human-scene interactions, generating coherent motion in complex 3D scenes presents a more significant challenge.

**Scene-aware motion synthesis** has garnered increasing attention. Some methods leverage reinforcement learning for navigation in synthetic 3D scenes, while others focus on single actions like grasping. These often face limitations such as being restricted to synthetic environments or failing to demonstrate transitions between diverse actions. Recent datasets capturing human interaction in 3D scenes have enabled methods that synthesize scene-aware motion. However, these datasets typically feature limited scene variety, constraining the generalizability of methods trained on them to specific or small scenes (e.g., PROX, Matterport). Many existing methods for scene-aware motion synthesis require conditioning on scene geometry and training on datasets where 3D humans interact within 3D scenes and objects. This requirement poses a scalability challenge for deployment in arbitrary real-world scenes. For instance, methods like SAMP and those by Wang et al. and Zhang et al. utilize path planning or explicit object geometry, limiting their generalizability across diverse real 3D scenes or preventing the synthesis of chained actions.

This work addresses the aforementioned limitations by proposing a framework that generates continual, animator-guided, and diverse human motion in arbitrary 3D scenes. It differentiates itself by training solely on scene-agnostic motion capture (mocap) data, thereby enabling deployment in a wide variety of 3D scenes without requiring motion data registered to specific scenes. The approach introduces a novel concept of a goal-centric canonical coordinate frame and action keypoints, enabling long-range motion synthesis and seamless transitions between various actions without explicit conditioning on detailed scene geometry.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method capable of synthesizing animator-guided, rich, and long-term human behavior within arbitrary 3D scenes. This includes generating a diverse range of actions and smooth transitions between them. The authors envision a system that allows users to specify goal-directed human movements, such as walking to a couch to sit, then standing up, approaching a shelf, and grabbing an item. This system should require minimal user interaction while maintaining the realism and expressivity necessary for applications in synthetic data generation, robotics, virtual reality/augmented reality (VR/AR), and gaming.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in the existing research landscape:
- **Restriction to single actions:** Most prior methods for animator-guided motion synthesis in 3D scenes are limited to generating a single action and do not effectively handle transitions between different actions. This prevents them from producing long-range, diverse motion sequences.
- **Limited deployability in diverse scenes:** Many existing techniques synthesize motion by explicitly conditioning on scene geometry, necessitating training on specialized datasets of human interactions within 3D scenes and objects. This approach is not scalable, as it would require collecting motion data registered to an exhaustive variety of potential 3D scenes and objects for generalization.
- **Scalability for real-world scenarios:** Humans demonstrate an ability to navigate novel, cluttered environments, interact with unseen objects, and adapt to new furniture, largely by focusing on immediate goals and relevant contact points rather than detailed scene geometry. The authors hypothesize that motion is primarily driven by avoiding obstacles and reaching immediate targets/contacts, implying that plausible human motion can be generated without accounting for every intricate detail of the 3D scene.

Based on this hypothesis, the key motivations for the proposed method are:
- To develop a framework for animator-guided motion synthesis in 3D scenes that does not rely on scene-registered motion data. This allows training on readily available and abundant scene-agnostic mocap data.
- To achieve a method deployable across 3D scenes with varied geometries, circumventing the need for explicit scene geometry conditioning.
- To enable the synthesis of long sequences of diverse, chained actions (e.g., grabbing, sitting, leaning) in arbitrary order, demonstrating generalizability across different scene datasets (HPS, Replica, Matterport, ScanNet, NeRFs).
- To provide a system that can generate motion without requiring manual phase or action annotations, which are often non-intuitive for non-periodic movements.
- To introduce a flexible interface using sparse keypoints for animator guidance, simplifying the specification of complex interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method synthesizes animator-guided human motion across diverse 3D scenes by decomposing the problem into walking along paths and transitioning into and out of specified actions. It operates without explicit conditioning on scene geometry by using a goal-centric canonical coordinate frame and scene-agnostic motion capture data.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework

The method takes a seed motion sequence and a list of `action keypoints` (sparse 3 or 4 joint locations, e.g., hands and feet) as input. Its goal is to generate a plausible motion sequence starting from the seed motion, transitioning through the specified action keypoints, and satisfying the implied scene constraints. The process involves three stages: walking motion, transition-in (blending walking with a target pose), and transition-out (animating back to a walking pose).

#### 3.2. SMPL Body Model

The human subject is represented using the SMPL body model, a differentiable function that maps global body orientation, pose, translation, and shape parameters to human mesh vertices and 3D joint locations. The shape parameters are assumed static throughout the process.

#### 3.3. Generating Keypoints in a Scene

Action keypoints, representing desired target contacts, can be provided by an animator via a 3D user interface or automatically inferred from scene geometry using action labels or language commands combined with semantic segmentation. For instance, a command like "sit there" could generate keypoints for sitting on a sofa.

#### 3.4. From Action Keypoints to an Anchor Pose

Given sparse keypoints for an action, the method infers a full SMPL anchor pose by optimizing the latent space of VPOSER (a pose prior) and global translation using Inverse Kinematics. This minimizes the squared distance between the desired keypoint locations and the corresponding SMPL joint locations. This process generates a set of anchor poses for each target action.

#### 3.5. Canonical Coordinate Frame

A central idea is the use of a goal-centric canonical coordinate frame. During training, motion sequence clips are canonicalized such that the last frame's planar translation and rotation are moved to the origin. This transforms the sequence so its end pose is at the origin and oriented towards a fixed canonical axis. At test time, by placing the next immediate goal at the origin of this canonical frame, the system can iteratively generate long motions without explicit scene conditioning.

#### 3.6. WalkNet

- **Training:** `WalkNet` is an autoregressive transformer trained on canonicalized walking sequence clips from the AMASS dataset. It takes K motion frames as input and predicts the subsequent K frames. Training sub-clips are randomly sampled with the last pose canonicalized to the origin. The network learns a mapping from an input matrix (vectorized pose and translation parameters) to an output matrix.
- **Test Time:** For path following, an A* algorithm computes an obstacle-free path between the current human location and the first anchor pose. Intermediate goals and tangents are sampled along this path. `WalkNet` then recursively synthesizes motion. The current motion sequence is canonicalized such that the next waypoint and its tangent align with the canonical frame's origin and axis. `WalkNet` predicts future motion in this frame, and the result is uncanonicalized back to the scene frame. This iterative, one-pose-at-a-time prediction strategy (using a stride of 1) ensures motion converges to the origin without drift, avoiding the need to explicitly input goal coordinates.

#### 3.7. TransNet

- **Training:** `TransNet` is a transformer-based motion inbetweener. It is trained to recover masked portions of AMASS sequences placed in the canonical coordinate frame. The mask progressively grows during training to encompass all frames between a seed motion and the final anchor pose, except for the seed motion and the last anchor pose itself.
- **Test Time:** `TransNet` synthesizes transitions by taking the last M/2 frames of a walking motion (the "seed motion") and a target anchor pose. The anchor pose's orientation and position define the canonical frame (placing it at the origin). `TransNet` then infills the missing frames between the canonicalized seed motion and the canonicalized anchor pose.

#### 3.8. Chained Actions

The framework combines `WalkNet` and `TransNet` to chain arbitrary actions. First, anchor poses are computed for all target action keypoints. A* path planning connects these action locations. `WalkNet` generates motion along the path until the human is sufficiently close to the first anchor pose. `TransNet` then predicts the transition into this anchor pose. To transition out, `TransNet` is used again to blend the anchor pose with a sampled walking pose, after which `WalkNet` resumes path following towards the next action. This allows for executing multiple actions at the same location (e.g., grabbing at different heights) by repeatedly using `TransNet`.

#### 3.9. Implementation Details

Both `WalkNet` and `TransNet` are implemented as BERT-style full-attention transformers with 3 attention layers, each comprising 8 attention heads, and an embedding size of 512. An L2 loss is used for training with a learning rate of 1e−5. Specific parameters M=120 (for `TransNet` sequence length) and K=30 (for `WalkNet` prediction window) are used, with M=120 found to minimize foot-skating.

#### 3.10. Datasets

- **Motion Data:** The large-scale AMASS mocap dataset is used for training both `TransNet` and `WalkNet`.
- **Scene Datasets:** The method is evaluated on scenes from Matterport3D, HPS, Replica, and ScanNet datasets, which vary in reconstruction quality and scale. This includes scenes reconstructed from RGB-D or LIDAR scanners.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrates the capabilities of the proposed method in synthesizing continual human motion in diverse 3D scenes, including comparisons with existing baselines and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics

Two primary metrics were used:
- **Perceptual Study:** Participants judged the realism and scene constraint satisfaction of synthesized motion sequences from different methods.
- **Foot Skating (FS):** Quantified foot sliding by measuring the velocity of the right toe vertex when its height is below a threshold (H = 2.5 cm), expressed in cm/frame.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Baselines

The authors compared their method against three existing approaches: SAMP, GAMMA, and Wang et al.. These baselines, while addressing aspects of scene-aware motion or path navigation, required modifications to adapt to the specific task of continual motion synthesis in arbitrary 3D scenes with keypoint guidance.

- **SAMP:** This method is primarily designed for sitting and lying actions in synthetic scenes and explicitly conditions on object geometry. For comparison, the object of interaction was represented synthetically in Unity, and A* paths were ported. SAMP was unable to synthesize chained actions or generalize to arbitrary 3D scenes beyond its training domain (e.g., sitting on stairs or grabbing from a bookshelf).
- **Wang et al.:** This method, trained on the PROX dataset, explicitly conditions on scene geometry for navigational motion. The authors replaced its action label-based anchor pose generation with their inverse kinematics step. However, it did not generalize to 3D scenes beyond its training datasets (HPS, Replica, Matterport).
- **GAMMA:** This method focuses on 3D scene navigation but lacks human-scene interaction capabilities. When used for paths generated by A*, GAMMA struggled to follow exact waypoints, leading to interpenetrations with the 3D scene.

### 5. Quantitative Results (Perceptual Study and Foot Skate)

- **Perceptual Study (Table 1):** Across 50 participants, the proposed method was preferred by a significant majority when compared to baselines.
 - **Vs. SAMP:** 71.8% preferred the proposed method for realism, 76.8% for scene constraint satisfaction.
 - **Vs. GAMMA:** 95.6% preferred the proposed method for realism, 100% for scene constraint satisfaction.
 - **Vs. Wang et al.:** 100% preferred the proposed method for realism and scene constraint satisfaction.
- **Foot-skate (Table 3):** The proposed method achieved a foot-skate score of 0.91 cm/frame, which was lower than SAMP (1.34), GAMMA (0.94), and Wang et al. (4.53), indicating reduced foot sliding.

The perceptual and quantitative comparisons indicate that the proposed method produces more realistic motion, better satisfies scene constraints, and exhibits less foot-skating than the evaluated baselines for the task of continual motion synthesis in diverse 3D scenes.

#### 5.1. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of different components and design choices.

- **TransNet vs. Other Inbetweeners (NeMF):** When comparing `TransNet` with NeMF for transitioning in and out of actions (infilling M/2-1 frames in the canonical frame), `TransNet` produced more natural motion and less foot-skating. A user study with 36 participants showed a preference for `TransNet` (66.8% vs. 33.2% for NeMF), suggesting its specialized design for canonical frame infilling contributes to better performance (Table 2).
- **WalkNet vs. Other Path Following Methods (MoGlow):** Training MoGlow on the walking data and deploying it on long A* paths (150-200 meters) resulted in significant foot-skating after approximately 30 seconds. This was hypothesized to be due to MoGlow synthesizing motion in an egocentric coordinate frame, where rapid changes in control signals from A* lead to drift. In a user study with 36 participants, the proposed `WalkNet` was preferred over MoGlow (75.7% vs. 24.3%), reinforcing its effectiveness for long-range path following without drift (Table 2).
- **Language-based vs. Manual Keypoint Placement:** Comparing motion synthesized using automatically generated keypoints (via language instructions and semantic segmentation) with manually placed keypoints revealed similar quality. A user study with 36 participants indicated a near-even preference (53.8% for language-based vs. 46.2% for manual), suggesting the viability of automated keypoint generation (Table 2).
- **User Effort for Manual Keypoints:** Participants learned to use the provided user interface for navigating 3D scenes and selecting keypoints in an average of 245 seconds. Subsequently, selecting 5 sets of 3 action keypoints (15 total) across 5 different 3D scenes took an average of 125 seconds per scene, indicating a manageable interaction cost.

#### 5.2. Qualitative Results

The method demonstrates the ability to generate motion that generalizes across diverse scene datasets, including Replica, Matterport, HPS, and ScanNet. The use of action keypoints enables high control and diversity, exemplified by generating various grabbing actions (different heights, two hands), sitting on different surfaces, and stretching motions.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a step towards synthesizing continual, animator-guided human motion in complex and diverse 3D environments. The core contributions offer several significant implications:

- **Generalizability and Scalability:** By decoupling scene reasoning from motion synthesis and training solely on scene-agnostic motion capture data, the method achieves generalizability across varied 3D scenes (e.g., HPS, Matterport, ScanNet, Replica, NeRFs). This overcomes a major limitation of prior work that relies on scene-registered datasets, which are difficult and costly to scale to the vast diversity of real-world environments. This allows for deployment in arbitrary 3D scenes without specific pre-training for each environment.
- **Long-Range and Continual Motion Synthesis:** The novel concept of iterative canonicalization, where motion is synthesized towards an immediate goal placed at the origin of a canonical coordinate frame, enables the generation of arbitrarily long sequences of motion without drift. This capability for continual motion, including seamless transitions between diverse actions (walking, sitting, grabbing, leaning), expands the scope of automated animation beyond single-action sequences.
- **Flexible Animator Guidance:** The use of sparse action keypoints provides a flexible and intuitive interface for animators. It allows specifying complex actions like "sit there" or "grab at this height" with minimal interaction, abstracting away intricate pose details. The finding that language-based keypoint placement yields comparable quality to manual placement suggests potential for further automation.
- **Reduced Manual Annotation:** Unlike many existing methods, this approach synthesizes motion without requiring manual phase or action annotations, simplifying the animation pipeline.
- **Applications:** The ability to generate realistic, controlled, and diverse human motion in arbitrary 3D scenes has broad potential impact across several domains:
 - **Synthetic Data Generation:** Facilitates the creation of large, varied datasets for training other computer vision and robotics models, particularly for human-scene interaction.
 - **Robotics:** Can inform the design of more human-like robot behaviors and interactions in complex environments.
 - **Virtual/Augmented Reality (VR/AR) and Gaming:** Enables more immersive and interactive experiences with highly realistic and responsive virtual characters.
 - **Animation Industry:** Provides animators with powerful tools to rapidly prototype and generate complex character behaviors.

### 7. Limitations and Future Work

The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the assumption of a horizontal floor, which restricts its applicability in scenes with uneven terrain. Additionally, the method relies on valid keypoint configurations; unrealistic keypoints can lead to unnatural poses from the IK step. Future work aims to address these limitations by potentially reducing the number of required keypoint inputs, which could make the system more robust to less precise user input.

Overall, the proposed method contributes to the field by offering a robust and scalable solution for generating diverse and continual human motion in 3D scenes, paving the way for further research in dynamic human-scene interaction. The authors' commitment to making their code and models publicly available is expected to facilitate future advancements in this area.
