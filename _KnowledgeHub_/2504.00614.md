# Learning Bipedal Locomotion on Gear-Driven Humanoid Robot Using Foot-Mounted IMUs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Sony developed a reinforcement learning framework that enables robust bipedal locomotion for their gear-driven, torque-sensorless humanoid robot, EVAL-03, across varied terrains by integrating foot-mounted IMUs into the observation space. The method demonstrated improved velocity tracking and high success rates on challenging surfaces and step descents, outperforming baseline approaches without these additional sensors.

## Problem

- Applying reinforcement learning to low-cost, miniature humanoids with high-gear ratio actuators is challenging due to complex actuator dynamics (backlash, friction) and the lack of torque sensors, creating a significant sim-to-real gap.
- Existing solutions for mitigating the sim-to-real gap often require expensive torque sensors or laborious detailed actuator modeling and system identification, which are impractical for cost-constrained platforms.
- The EVAL-03 robot, a miniature humanoid developed by Sony Interactive Entertainment, was previously limited to static poses or flat-ground walking, lacking robust bipedal locomotion capabilities for diverse environments.

## Method

- An RL framework based on ==Legged Gym== integrates linear accelerations and angular velocities from foot-mounted IMUs into the policy's observation space, leveraging their rapid, direct measurement of feet states.
- A multi-stage training process employs a teacher-student model with fine-tuning and uses Random Network Distillation (RND) for enhanced exploration, improving policy learning for complex terrains.
- Sim-to-real transfer is achieved through domain randomization of physical parameters and sensor limitations, along with symmetric data augmentation to promote stable bipedal gaits for the constrained hardware.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved superior velocity tracking, maintaining an average forward velocity of **0.043** m/s for a **0.05** m/s command and successfully tracking fast turn commands (**1.0** rad/s) where baselines failed.
- It demonstrated high robustness on challenging terrains, achieving **100%** success rates on turf, cushion, and bubble wrap, and an **80%** success rate on uneven urethane, significantly outperforming baselines which struggled or failed.
- The robot successfully descended small (10mm, **100%** success) and medium (20mm, **100%** success) steps, and showed tolerance for light payloads (**19%** of body mass), maintaining walking speed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating readily available, low-cost foot-mounted IMUs provides direct and rapid measurements of feet states, effectively compensating for the lack of expensive torque sensors and complex actuator modeling in gear-driven robots.
> - Augmenting the observation space with strategically chosen sensor data offers a pragmatic and powerful alternative to traditional sim-to-real gap mitigation techniques, simplifying system design for constrained hardware.
> - A combination of teacher-student training, RND, and symmetric data augmentation helps develop robust and dynamic bipedal locomotion policies capable of handling varied terrains and disturbances for challenging hardware platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL), robot locomotion, and sim-to-real transfer, with a specific focus on the challenging domain of bipedal humanoid robots.

**Reinforcement Learning for Locomotion:** RL has revolutionized robot locomotion, enabling the development of highly dynamic, agile, and robust gaits for both quadrupedal and bipedal robots. A significant enabler has been zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, where policies trained in massively parallelized physics simulations, are directly deployed on real hardware. However, the success of this approach hinges on effectively mitigating the "sim-to-real gap"—the discrepancies between the simulation model and the real robot/environment.

**Challenges in Sim-to-Real Transfer for Humanoids:**
- **Actuator Modeling:** A primary source of the sim-to-real gap lies in actuator dynamics.
 - **Actuator Networks:** One effective strategy involves embedding an actuator network, trained on real-world data, to infer joint torques in simulation. However, this method requires robots to be equipped with torque sensors, which are often prohibitively expensive, especially for low-cost platforms.
 - **Direct/Quasi-Direct Drive:** Robots with direct or quasi-direct drive actuators, can be accurately modeled using simple PD controllers, simplifying sim-to-real transfer. However, these are often not the chosen actuator type for miniature or cost-constrained robots.
 - **High-Gear Ratio Actuators:** Many low-cost, miniature humanoid robots, like ROBOTIS-OP3 and EVAL-03, utilize high-gear ratio actuators. These actuators amplify the capabilities of small motors but introduce significant challenges: increased backlash, joint friction, and highly nonlinear torque-current relationships. Without torque sensors, methods like actuator networks are unavailable.
- **Prior Approaches for High-Gear Ratio Robots:**
 - Some studies have used high-gain position control and subsequent actuator parameter identification for agile motions, but these were often limited to flat ground.
 - More detailed actuator model identification has been attempted to enable compliant joint control (low-gain PD) for varied terrains. However, these approaches often require careful real-world data collection and have shown limited success, demonstrating only slow walking on tilted planes, not robust locomotion over rough terrain or steps.
 - Incorporating current feedback also necessitates accurate motor parameter identification, which can be laborious.

**This paper's unique contribution to the landscape** is its exploration of an *alternative* strategy to overcome the sim-to-real gap for these challenging high-gear ratio, torque-sensorless platforms. Instead of pursuing complex and costly actuator modeling, detailed system identification, or expensive torque sensors, the authors propose leveraging **additional, readily available sensor observations** in the form of foot-mounted Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs). While foot-mounted IMUs have been used in human motion analysis and state estimation for legged robots, their application in *RL-based locomotion control* for direct policy improvement, particularly for robust sim-to-real transfer, represents a novel direction. By utilizing these sensors to directly measure feet states, the authors aim to enhance the robot's rapid stabilization capabilities over difficult terrains, effectively sidestepping the deep complexities of actuator dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robust and dynamic bipedal locomotion for gear-driven humanoid robots that lack torque sensors, particularly over challenging and varied terrains. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

1. **Address Sim-to-Real Challenges in Low-Cost Humanoids:** The central motivation stems from the inherent difficulties of applying RL to robots equipped with high-gear ratio actuators that lack torque sensors. These robots, exemplified by EVAL-03, are common in low-cost and miniature designs but suffer from complex actuator dynamics (backlash, friction, non-linear torque-current relationships) that create a significant sim-to-real gap. Existing solutions requiring torque sensors or extensive system identification are often impractical for such platforms.

2. **Enhance Locomotion Capabilities of EVAL-03:** The EVAL-03 humanoid robot, developed by Sony Interactive Entertainment for entertainment purposes, has historically been limited to upper body movements, static posing, and walking on flat planes without disturbances. A key motivation is to significantly expand its functional capabilities by enabling robust bipedal locomotion over diverse environments, making it more versatile and interactive.

3. **Propose an Alternative to Complex Modeling:** The authors explicitly ask: "can we improve sim-to-real transfer by introducing additional sensor observations?" This question highlights their motivation to find a less resource-intensive solution compared to detailed actuator modeling, system identification, or the integration of expensive torque sensors. They hypothesize that direct, rapid measurement of feet states via foot-mounted IMUs can provide sufficient feedback for stabilization without needing to perfectly model underlying actuator nonlinearities.

4. **Improve Rapid Stabilization on Challenging Terrains:** A specific target is to improve the robot's ability to maintain balance and recover quickly when encountering non-rigid surfaces, uneven ground, and sudden environmental transitions (like steps). This is critical for practical deployment in varied, human-centric environments.

In essence, the research aims to demonstrate that strategically chosen, additional sensor information can be a powerful and pragmatic tool for closing the sim-to-real gap and achieving high-performance locomotion on hardware with inherent control challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates foot-mounted IMU observations into a reinforcement learning framework, complemented by several sim-to-real transfer techniques and training enhancements.

**A. Reinforcement Learning Framework:**
The foundation of the approach is a model-free RL framework, **Legged Gym**, which leverages massively parallelized physics simulation (e.g., Isaac Gym). The policy is conditioned on 3D velocity commands (longitudinal, lateral, and yaw) and is trained across a variety of procedurally generated terrains (slopes, rough surfaces, stairs, steps) with varying difficulty levels, using a constant curriculum.

- **Robot Hardware:** Experiments are conducted on **EVAL-03**, a miniature-sized, gear-driven humanoid robot (240 mm tall, 1.73 kg) with 12 active degrees of freedom in its legs. It uses low-cost motors with high-gear ratios and lacks torque sensors.
- **Observation Space (Table I):** The key innovation lies here. Beyond standard observations (base velocity command, base IMU data, joint positions/velocities, last actions), the proposed method explicitly includes **linear accelerations and angular velocities from IMUs mounted on both the left and right feet**. The authors hypothesize that these direct feet state measurements improve motion capabilities over varied terrains.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs target joint positions for a low-level PD controller. To ensure smoothness and prevent hardware damage, especially with high-gain PD control, a low-pass filter (Eq. 1) is applied to the target joint positions. The policy runs at 100 Hz, while the PD controller operates at 1000 Hz.
- **Reward Function (Table III):** The reward structure follows Legged Gym defaults, but with hyperparameters tuned for EVAL-03's size. It incorporates bipedal-specific rewards from, including gait reward, swing-foot clearance, and penalties for self-collisions between feet.

**B. Sim-to-Real Considerations:**
To minimize the sim-to-real gap without detailed actuator modeling, several techniques are employed:

- **Domain Randomization (Table IV):** Parameters such as additional base mass, center of mass (COM) displacements, friction/restitution coefficients, PD gains, and sensor offsets (joint encoder, IMU) are randomized during training. System delay is also randomized.
- **Sensor Limitations Replication:**
 - The limited accelerometer range of EVAL-03's low-grade IMUs is replicated in simulation by clipping linear acceleration observations.
 - Since the low-cost motors lack a direct joint velocity interface, real hardware estimates velocities using finite differences (Eq. 2). This estimation method is accurately replicated in simulation for the joint velocity observation, rather than using ground-truth simulator velocities.

**C. Symmetric Data Augmentation:**
To counteract potential asymmetries arising from hardware design (e.g., closely spaced feet leading to excessive self-collision avoidance) and promote efficient bipedal motion, symmetric data augmentation is used. Observations are mirrored with respect to the body center (Fig. 2), including careful coordinate frame transformations for foot-mounted IMU data. This helps enforce a symmetric policy.

**D. Teacher-Student Training with Fine-tuning:**
A three-stage training process is adopted for robust locomotion over rough terrains:

1. **Stage 1 (Teacher Policy):** A "teacher" policy is trained using PPO with a short observation history and privileged observations (e.g., noiseless joint states, base velocity, contact forces, COM displacement, terrain height map – Table I). Symmetric data augmentation is applied.
2. **Stage 2 (Student Policy - Supervised Learning):** A "student" policy is trained via supervised learning to imitate the teacher's actions and reconstruct privileged information. The student uses a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) as a belief encoder to estimate privileged information from a long observation history (100 time steps). Symmetric data augmentation is also used during data collection for this stage.
3. **Stage 3 (Student Policy - RL Fine-tuning):** The entire student policy (including the TCN) is further fine-tuned using PPO. An asymmetric actor-critic approach is used, where the critic receives privileged observations, but the actor (student policy) does not. This is noted to improve performance compared to fine-tuning only the base policy MLP.

**E. Random Network Distillation (RND):**
RND is employed during teacher policy training to enhance exploration. This addresses the tendency of the policy to exhibit minimal swing-foot clearance to avoid falling risks, even with encouraging rewards. A "curiosity state" (Eq. 3), defined by foot positions and height scan, is used for RND to encourage exploration of varied foot placements on terrains. An intrinsic reward based on the prediction error of a randomly initialized network (target network) by a trained predictor network guides this exploration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed method (w/ Feet IMUs) was evaluated through extensive hardware experiments on EVAL-03, comparing it against two baseline policies: one observing linear accelerations and angular velocities from only the base-mounted IMU (w/o Feet IMUs 1), and another observing only angular velocities from the base IMU (w/o Feet IMUs 2).

**A. Walking on Floor:**
- **Velocity Tracking (Table V, Fig. 4):** The proposed method demonstrated superior velocity tracking capabilities. It achieved an average forward velocity (v_x) of 0.043 m/s for a 0.05 m/s command, compared to 0.033 m/s and 0.03 m/s for baselines. Crucially, it was the only policy capable of tracking a fast turn command (w_z,cmd = 1.0 rad/s) without losing balance, whereas the other methods failed. For a 0.5 rad/s turn command, the proposed method showed less deviation between commanded and actual yaw velocity (Fig. 4).
- **Disturbance Rejection (Fig. 5c):** The proposed policy demonstrated reactive motion against external push disturbances on a flat floor.

**B. Walking over a Variety of Terrains (Table VI, Fig. 7):**
- **Enhanced Stability:** The most significant findings emerged on challenging terrains. The proposed method achieved a 100% success rate on turf (thin/thick), cushion (pet), and bubble wrap, and an 80% success rate on an uneven urethane sheet. In contrast, baseline policies showed significantly lower success rates or outright failures on cushion (pet) (40% and 60% for baselines 1 and 2, respectively) and completely failed on the uneven urethane sheet.
- **Consistent Walking Speed:** The proposed method maintained relatively consistent walking speeds (e.g., 71% of floor speed on cushion (pet)) even on challenging surfaces, while other policies struggled and showed greater speed degradation or became stuck.

**C. Descending Steps (Table VII, Fig. 8):**
- **Robustness to Sudden Transitions:** The proposed method achieved a 100% success rate in descending small (10mm) and medium (20mm) steps, and a 60% success rate on large (25mm) steps. This highlights its superior ability to handle sudden environmental transitions. The baseline methods showed very poor performance, largely failing on even small steps.

**D. Walking with Payloads (Table VIII, Fig. 9):**
- **Payload Tolerance:** With a relatively light payload (0.33 kg, 19% of total mass), the proposed method maintained a forward walking speed of 0.04 m/s, comparable to its unloaded performance. Baseline methods exhibited significant speed degradation. With a heavier payload (0.55 kg, 32% of total mass), all three methods experienced substantial speed reduction, indicating a limit to the robustness.

**E. Discussion and Limitation:**
The results strongly suggest that foot-mounted IMUs enable the policy to obtain direct and rapid measurements of feet states, which is crucial for coping with balance challenges arising from complex contacts with various environments. This validation confirms the initial hypothesis that additional sensor observations can significantly improve performance without complex actuator modeling.

However, limitations persist. The proposed method still failed on extremely challenging scenarios like human-sized cushions and, notably, was unable to climb even small obstacles (e.g., a 5mm step). This suggests that while foot-IMUs aid in stabilization, terrain feature estimation solely through them might be insufficient for upward navigation within the current framework. The authors suggest that combining this approach with compliant joint control (lower PD gains) could be a promising direction for future research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics, particularly for practical and cost-effective applications.

1. **Democratizing Bipedal Locomotion:** The most significant impact is providing a viable and effective solution for achieving robust bipedal locomotion on robots with hardware limitations (high-gear ratio, torque-sensorless actuators) that are common in low-cost, miniature, or entertainment-focused platforms. By demonstrating that adding relatively inexpensive foot-mounted IMUs can circumvent the need for complex, costly actuator modeling or expensive torque sensors, the work makes advanced locomotion capabilities more accessible to a wider range of robotic systems.

2. **Enhanced Robustness and Versatility:** The demonstrated ability of EVAL-03 to traverse non-rigid surfaces, handle sudden step descents, and tolerate external disturbances/payloads signifies a substantial leap in the robot's practical robustness. This increased versatility expands the potential deployment environments for such humanoids, moving them closer to operating in unstructured, real-world settings alongside humans.

3. **Advancing Entertainment Robotics:** For Sony Interactive Entertainment, this work directly enhances the core capabilities of their EVAL-03 platform. More robust and dynamic locomotion allows for richer interactions and more engaging performances in entertainment applications, fulfilling the original vision for robots like AIBO and QRIO but with greater mobility in diverse conditions.

4. **Novel Sim-to-Real Strategy:** The paper offers a compelling alternative to traditional sim-to-real gap mitigation techniques. Instead of solely focusing on perfecting the physical model or requiring expensive sensor suites, it highlights the power of intelligently augmenting the observation space with readily available sensor data. This can inspire new research directions in how sensor information, especially direct contact-related data, can implicitly convey environmental and interaction dynamics to an RL policy, simplifying the overall system design.

5. **Blueprint for Future Research:** By clearly outlining its successes and limitations (e.g., inability to climb steps), the paper provides a clear roadmap for future research. The suggestion to integrate compliant joint control with lower PD gains, potentially in combination with foot-IMU data, offers a promising path to overcome current challenges in obstacle negotiation and further enhance adaptive locomotion.

In conclusion, this research presents a pragmatic and impactful solution to a persistent challenge in humanoid robotics. By strategically leveraging foot-mounted IMUs and robust RL techniques, the authors have significantly advanced the capabilities of a constrained hardware platform, paving the way for more versatile and cost-effective bipedal robots in various applications, particularly in the burgeoning field of entertainment robotics.
