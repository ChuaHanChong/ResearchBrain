# Freehand Sketching for End-User Programming of Robot Swarms

> [!summary] Summary
> A sketch-to-swarm system lets non-experts specify robot formations by drawing freehand. Laplacian-of-Gaussian edge detection plus Shi-Tomasi corner detection extract formation points, a rigid graph encodes inter-agent distances, Hungarian assignment maps robots to nodes while excluding mirrored realizations, and a distributed distance-based controller executes it. A 20-participant study scored 84.25 on the System Usability Scale, with all 42 physical formations converging.

## Problem

- Existing human-swarm interfaces target navigation, trajectory generation, task allocation, or coverage, specifying how a swarm moves rather than what geometry it should collectively realize.
- Gesture, augmented-reality, and mixed-granularity abstractions still restrict users to predefined commands or a fixed library of shapes, blocking free specification of arbitrary geometries.
- Prior sketch-to-formation work demonstrated computational feasibility only; whether freehand sketching actually works as an interaction abstraction for users lacking robotics or control expertise stayed unevaluated.

## Method

- ==Sketch-processing pipeline==: ==Laplacian-of-Gaussian edge detection== extracts contours, ==Shi-Tomasi corner detection== picks critical points, then points are scaled to workspace and filtered by minimum separation d_min.
- ==Rigid formation graph== construction, where extracted points define inter-agent distance constraints and ==infinitesimal rigidity== (rank of rigidity matrix equal to 2n-3) fixes geometry up to rigid transformations.
- ==Hungarian-algorithm== binary assignment of robots to nodes minimizing total Euclidean cost, carrying a reflection-free guarantee that preserves signed orientation angles, feeding a ==distributed distance-based formation controller==.

## Results

- Twenty participants gave a mean System Usability Scale score of **84.25** (SD **15.24**, median **87.5**), far above the **68** reference; **17 of 20** exceeded 68 and **16** reached at least 80.
- All **42** self-selected participant formations executed on the physical Robotarium converged, with every prescribed edge distance error within **0.025 m** and no reflected realizations observed.
- Item-level responses favored learnability: **4.55/5** that most people would learn it quickly, **1.35/5** for needing prior learning; **65%** of participants voluntarily ran extra formations (**2.10 ± 1.02** each).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Splitting roles cleanly, human supplies geometric intent while the system resolves node count, assignment, and control, removes the need for users to reason about robot constraints.
> - Distance-based formation control is reflection-ambiguous by construction; orientation must be pinned elsewhere, here through a sign-preserving property of the minimum-cost assignment.
> - High aggregate usability hid real variance (scores 35 to 100, technical-help item widest spread), and an engineering-heavy university sample limits claims about genuinely non-technical users.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper lies at the intersection of swarm robotics, human-swarm interaction, formation control, computer vision, and end-user robot programming. Swarm robotics generally focuses on coordinating many robots through decentralized rules so that the collective system can perform tasks that may be difficult for a single robot. Existing applications include environmental monitoring, exploration, search and rescue, agriculture, logistics, education, and artistic displays.

As robot swarms are deployed in settings involving public users, operators, educators, and domain specialists, researchers have increasingly examined how people can communicate goals to a group of robots. Human-swarm interaction research has explored several interaction abstractions, including gesture control, mixed-granularity commands, augmented and virtual reality, trajectory specification, navigation, task allocation, and coverage planning. These systems reduce the need for users to reason directly about individual robot assignments or low-level control laws.

The paper addresses a more specific interaction problem: allowing users to specify the collective geometry that a swarm should form. Unlike systems based on predefined shapes, gesture vocabularies, or command menus, the proposed interface accepts a freehand drawing. The human supplies a visual representation of spatial intent, while the system determines how that drawing should be converted into formation points, robot assignments, and control inputs.

The work also contributes to end-user robot programming. This field investigates how non-programmers can specify robot behavior through visual programming, programming by demonstration, tangible interfaces, sketches, and other higher-level representations. Prior work has demonstrated that sketches can be used to author individual robot behaviors or multi-robot formations, but the authors identify a gap in evaluating whether freehand sketching is usable for people without robotics or control expertise.

Technically, the paper builds on earlier work by Karam that developed the computational components for transforming sketches into formations. The present paper’s main addition is an integrated human-facing system and a physical user evaluation. It therefore shifts emphasis from whether the pipeline can be implemented to whether people can use it successfully and perceive it as understandable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Objectives

The primary objective is to evaluate freehand sketching as an end-user programming interface for specifying robot-swarm formations. The intended interaction division is straightforward: users draw the desired shape, and the swarm autonomously handles the geometric and control-related details.

The paper has three closely related goals. First, it seeks to provide a formation-specification method that does not require conventional programming, robotics knowledge, or a library of predefined shapes. Second, it evaluates whether users can learn and operate the interface with only minimal instructions. Third, it tests whether the complete pipeline can translate user-generated sketches into physical formations on a multi-robot platform.

#### 2.2. Motivation

The motivation arises from the difference between human and robotic strengths in collaborative systems. Humans are well suited to expressing high-level goals, visual concepts, and task-specific intent. Robot swarms are suited to decentralized coordination, assignment, motion execution, and maintaining geometric relationships among many agents. An effective interface should allow each side to perform the part of the task for which it is most appropriate.

Formation specification is particularly suitable for a sketch-based interface because a shape is naturally communicated through spatial drawing. Users do not need to identify which robot should occupy which location, specify inter-robot distances, select a rigid graph, or tune controller gains. These operations are delegated to the system.

The authors also address a technical issue associated with distance-based formation control. A formation defined only through pairwise distances can be translated or rotated without changing its geometry, but it can also be reflected. Reflection is problematic for asymmetric shapes such as letters or symbols because it can change their meaning. The system therefore incorporates an assignment procedure intended to preserve the orientation of the user’s formation and avoid unintended mirror-image realizations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Sketch Processing

The user draws on a blank graphical interface using a wireless Wacom tablet. The resulting image is denoted by \(I\). The system first applies Laplacian-of-Gaussian edge detection to identify contours while reducing sensitivity to small variations in the hand-drawn input. It then uses Shi-Tomasi feature detection to identify important points, particularly corners or locations with strong intensity changes along the contours.

These detected points serve as candidate formation nodes. They are scaled from image coordinates into the physical workspace of the Robotarium. Points that are too close together are removed using a minimum separation distance, \(d_{\min}\). This filtering step ensures that the resulting formation is compatible with the robots’ physical size, operating area, and safety constraints. The number of retained points, \(n\), must not exceed the number of robots available for deployment, \(N\).

The retained points are represented as a target configuration matrix \(Q\), with each row containing the two-dimensional coordinates of one target point.

#### 3.2. Formation-Graph Construction

A graph \(G=(V,E)\) is constructed from the target points. Each graph node corresponds to a target location, and each edge represents a desired distance between two formation nodes. For an edge connecting points \(q_i\) and \(q_j\), the desired distance is

\[
d_{ij}=\|q_i-q_j\|_2.
\]

The graph is designed to be rigid. In two dimensions, infinitesimal rigidity is characterized using the rigidity matrix \(R\), which must have rank \(2n-3\). A rigid graph ensures that satisfying the selected distance constraints locally preserves the shape, apart from overall translation and rotation.

The paper explains the rigidity condition but does not provide extensive implementation details about how edges are selected or how rigidity is guaranteed for every possible processed sketch. This is an important technical detail for reproducing the complete system.

#### 3.3. Robot-to-Formation Assignment

The extracted points specify geometry but do not identify which physical robot should occupy each location. Let \(p_j\) be the current position of robot \(j\). The cost of assigning robot \(j\) to target \(i\) is the Euclidean distance

\[
c_{ij}=\|p_j-q_i\|_2.
\]

The assignment is formulated as a one-to-one binary linear assignment problem. Each target point receives exactly one robot, and each robot used in the formation is assigned to exactly one target point. The Hungarian algorithm is used to find the assignment minimizing the total robot-to-target distance.

This assignment reduces the amount of motion required to initialize the formation. It also plays a role in preventing reflected formations. The paper invokes a result from the authors’ previous work stating that, under the minimum-cost assignment, the signed orientation of any three non-collinear target points is preserved by the corresponding robot assignment. Consequently, the assignment is intended to exclude a reflected realization while still permitting a global translation or rotation.

#### 3.4. Distributed Formation Control

After assignment, each robot is associated with a target node and participates in a distance-based controller. Robot \(i\) adjusts its velocity according to the distance errors between itself and its graph neighbors:

\[
\dot p_i=-k\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}
\left(\|p_i-p_j\|_2^2-d_{ij}^2\right)(p_i-p_j),
\]

where \(k>0\) is a control gain and \(\mathcal N_i\) is the set of neighboring robots in the formation graph.

The controller uses relative information from neighboring robots rather than centralized absolute-position commands. When the prescribed edge distances are achieved, the swarm realizes the desired formation geometry. Translation and rotation remain unconstrained, so the final formation may appear shifted or rotated relative to the original sketch. The reflection-related assignment condition is intended to preserve orientation.

#### 3.5. Human-Subject Evaluation

Twenty participants were recruited from the University of California, Irvine community. The sample consisted of 12 undergraduate students, six graduate students, and two postdoctoral researchers. Participants came from engineering and science backgrounds, including electrical, computer, mechanical, aerospace, mechatronics, and biological sciences. No prior robotics experience was required, although the sample was not representative of the general population.

Participants received instructions only for the three main interface operations: “New Line” to draw, “Clear” to reset the canvas, and “Done” to submit a sketch. They were not shown an example or given a prescribed shape. Each participant had to complete at least one formation, but could submit additional drawings. The system processed each drawing and executed the resulting formation on the physical Robotarium platform.

Usability was assessed with the ten-item System Usability Scale, or SUS. The questionnaire uses five-point responses and produces a score from 0 to 100. The paper used the conventional reference value of approximately 68 for average usability.

A formation was considered converged when the absolute error for every prescribed graph edge was no more than 0.025 meters:

\[
\left|\|p_i-p_j\|_2-d_{ij}\right|\leq 0.025\text{ m}.
\]

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Usability

All 20 participants completed the SUS questionnaire. The mean score was 84.25, with a standard deviation of 15.24, a median of 87.5, and a range from 35 to 100. Seventeen participants scored above the reference value of 68, and 16 scored at least 80.

The item-level responses indicate favorable perceptions of ease of use and learnability. Participants rated the statement that most people would learn the system quickly at a mean of 4.55 out of 5. The mean response to the statement that substantial learning would be required was 1.35. Participants also rated the system as easy to use at 4.30, felt confident using it at 4.40, and considered its functions well integrated at 4.40.

Negative usability statements received relatively low agreement. Participants rated unnecessary complexity at 1.65, inconsistency at 1.55, and cumbersome operation at 1.50. The item concerning the need for technical support had a higher mean of 1.95 and the largest standard deviation, 1.15, indicating greater variation in participants’ perceptions.

Participants submitted 42 drawings in total, corresponding to an average of 2.10 formations per participant with a standard deviation of 1.02. Thirteen participants completed more than the required single execution. Because additional drawings were optional and not controlled experimentally, this behavior is descriptive rather than a formal engagement measure.

#### 4.2. Physical Formation Execution

All 42 submitted formations met the paper’s convergence criterion. The examples include geometrically distinct formations using between 10 and 20 robots. The resulting configurations could differ from the sketches by a global rotation or translation, as expected from the distance-based controller. The authors report that no reflected realizations were observed.

These results demonstrate that the complete pipeline—from freehand input through image processing, point filtering, graph formation, robot assignment, and distributed control—operated successfully in the physical testbed for the tested drawings.

However, the physical evaluation primarily reports whether formations converged. It does not provide detailed comparisons of convergence time, energy use, trajectory length, positional error after alignment, processing latency, or robustness to failed robots and communication disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides evidence that freehand drawing can serve as an accessible abstraction for specifying static planar swarm formations. Its significance is both interactional and technical. Interactionally, it allows users to express a geometric goal without programming individual robots or managing assignments. Technically, it combines computer-vision-based sketch interpretation, rigid formation graphs, optimal assignment, distributed control, and an orientation-preservation condition in one user-facing system.

Potential applications include education, artistic performances, demonstrations, interactive exhibits, and collaborative design tasks in which users need to communicate shapes to a robot group. The approach could also serve as a foundation for richer end-user programming systems in which sketches specify spatial layouts, task regions, or symbolic patterns.

The evidence should nevertheless be interpreted within the study’s limits. The sample was small, university-based, and weighted toward technical disciplines. The study used self-selected drawings rather than standardized tasks, so it does not establish comparative performance against menus, gestures, or other interfaces. The evaluation focused on static two-dimensional formations and subjective usability. Future work identified by the authors includes testing broader non-technical populations, supporting behaviors beyond formation control, and enabling continuous human-in-the-loop interaction. Further evaluations could also examine sketch-recognition errors, scalability beyond 20 robots, safety under dense drawings, and performance when robots or communication links fail.
