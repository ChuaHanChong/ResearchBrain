# Efficient Estimation of Kernel Surrogate Models for Task Attribution

> [!summary] Summary
> Northeastern University researchers developed Kernel Surrogate Models (KERNELSM) for accurately and efficiently quantifying the influence of individual training tasks on AI model performance, particularly capturing complex nonlinear task interactions. Its efficient gradient-based estimation method avoids costly retraining, yielding a 25% higher correlation with ground truth on average and improving downstream task selection by up to 40% lower loss.

## Problem

- Direct task attribution methods, like Leave-One-Out (LOO) retraining, are computationally infeasible for large-scale AI models due to requiring numerous full training runs.
- Existing surrogate models for task attribution are predominantly linear, failing to capture crucial nonlinear interactions (e.g., synergy or antagonism) between tasks.
- Influence functions, while more efficient than LOO, still involve intensive computation of Hessian-vector products, posing scalability challenges for evaluating many tasks.

## Method

- Introduces Kernel Surrogate Models (KERNELSM) utilizing ==Radial Basis Function== (RBF) kernels to naturally represent and learn complex nonlinear and second-order task interactions.
- Develops an efficient gradient-based estimation procedure that approximates task subset performance by leveraging a first-order Taylor expansion around a pretrained model's parameters, avoiding expensive full model retraining.
- Provides a unified theoretical framework that formally establishes the conditions under which linear surrogate models approximate influence functions, clarifying their operational principles and limitations.

## Results

- KERNELSM consistently achieved an average of **25%** higher correlation with Leave-One-Out (LOO) ground truth compared to existing attribution baselines across diverse domains.
- Demonstrated substantial performance gains in tasks characterized by complex nonlinear interactions, including over **42%** improvement in LDS for modular arithmetic reasoning and **25%** for ICL 'Coin Flip' tasks.
- Improved practical downstream applications, leading to a **40%** lower loss in ICL demonstration selection and a **15%** increase in target rewards for multi-objective reinforcement learning tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Linear surrogate model coefficients approximate influence functions when second-order task interactions are small, explaining their utility in specific contexts while highlighting their inherent limitations for complex interactions.
> - Task influences often exhibit strong nonlinearities, such as synergistic or antagonistic effects, which linear attribution models are fundamentally unable to capture.
> - Approximating fine-tuned model performance for various task subsets can be done accurately and efficiently using a first-order Taylor expansion around a pretrained model, negating the need for repeated full retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, particularly large language models (LLMs), are increasingly trained to perform a wide array of tasks concurrently, ranging from machine translation and code generation to object recognition and complex reasoning. A fundamental question arising from this multi-task capability concerns the quantification of individual training task influence on a model's performance on a specific target task, a problem referred to as "task attribution." This work is situated within the field of AI interpretability, specifically focusing on understanding the contributions of training data components.

Existing methodologies for task attribution primarily fall into two categories. The first, "leave-one-out" (LOO) retraining, provides a direct measure by evaluating performance changes when a task is excluded from training. However, its computational cost, requiring K+1 full retraining runs for K tasks, renders it infeasible for large-scale applications. The second category involves more efficient proxies:
1. **Influence Functions (IFs):** These methods estimate how model predictions change if a training sample (or task in this context) were infinitesimally up-weighted or removed. While more efficient than LOO, they typically involve computing Hessian-vector products, which can still be computationally intensive, especially for evaluating all K tasks.
2. **Surrogate Models (Datamodels/Data Attribution):** This approach constructs simpler models that approximate the black-box behavior of the training pipeline, mapping subsets of training data (or tasks) to target task performance. Once trained, these surrogates offer a faster way to evaluate the impact of various task combinations.

Prior work on surrogate models has predominantly focused on *linear* formulations. While linear surrogates possess theoretical advantages, such as capturing first-order, additive effects and exhibiting linear sampling complexities, they inherently miss complex *nonlinear interactions* between tasks. These interactions could manifest as synergy (tasks enhancing each other's effects), antagonism (tasks hindering each other), or more complex XOR-type relationships that are not simply additive.

This research addresses these limitations by first providing a unifying theoretical framework that elucidates the conditions under which linear surrogate models and influence functions converge. It then extends the surrogate modeling paradigm beyond linearity by introducing *kernel-based surrogate models*, designed to capture these crucial second-order and nonlinear task interactions. A key innovation in this context is the development of an efficient estimation procedure that circumvents the prohibitively expensive repeated retraining traditionally associated with building surrogate models, thereby making the approach scalable for modern AI systems. The work connects to broader applications in multi-task learning (guiding architecture and loss re-weighting), multi-group learning (understanding demographic impacts), in-context learning (demonstration selection), and multi-objective reinforcement learning (analyzing reward signal influences).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to advance the field of task attribution by providing a method capable of accurately and efficiently quantifying the influence of individual training tasks on the performance of modern AI models, particularly in scenarios involving complex, non-additive interactions.

The specific motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Overcome Computational Infeasibility of Direct Methods:** The gold standard for task attribution, Leave-One-Out (LOO) retraining, is computationally prohibitive for contemporary large-scale AI models. More efficient proxies like influence functions, while improvements, still involve repeated Hessian approximations or extensive computation across numerous tasks, presenting significant scalability challenges. This necessitates a more efficient approach that avoids full model retraining.

2. **Address Limitations of Linear Surrogate Models:** Existing surrogate modeling approaches, primarily linear, are limited to capturing only first-order, additive effects. They fundamentally fail to represent complex nonlinear interactions between tasks, such as synergistic benefits (tasks working together to produce a larger effect than their sum), antagonistic interference (tasks undermining each other), or more intricate combinatorial relationships. The paper motivates this by illustrating how linear models cannot capture performance transitions driven by specific sample combinations near decision boundaries (Figure 2). A critical objective is to develop a model that can capture these higher-order interactions.

3. **Establish Theoretical Connections Between Existing Methods:** The paper aims to provide a unified theoretical framework to analyze task attribution methods. Specifically, it seeks to rigorously establish the conditions under which linear surrogate models, estimated through a regression objective, approximate influence functions. This analysis provides insights into the operational principles and inherent limitations of linear approaches by characterizing the error introduced when second-order task interactions are present.

4. **Introduce a More Expressive Task Attribution Model:** Motivated by the shortcomings of linear models, a key objective is to introduce a novel *kernel-based surrogate model* (KERNELSM). This model is designed to naturally represent and learn complex, nonlinear relationships and second-order interactions between tasks, moving beyond simple additive contributions. The RBF kernel is proposed due to its universal approximation capabilities and its alignment with the geometric structure of the task subset space.

5. **Develop an Efficient and Scalable Estimation Procedure:** A major practical hurdle for expressive, nonlinear surrogate models is their estimation cost, which typically requires numerous retrainings of the black-box model on various task subsets. Therefore, a crucial objective is to devise an efficient, gradient-based estimation algorithm for KERNELSM. This algorithm aims to approximate model outcomes for different task subsets by leveraging a first-order Taylor expansion around a pretrained model's parameters, thus avoiding repeated, expensive full retraining. The method also seeks to handle high-dimensional gradient vectors through random projection techniques.

6. **Empirically Validate and Demonstrate Practical Utility:** The research aims to empirically evaluate KERNELSM across a diverse set of domains, including mathematical reasoning with transformers, in-context learning with large language models, and multi-objective reinforcement learning. This validation seeks to demonstrate KERNELSM's superior accuracy compared to existing baselines and its scalability to large numbers of tasks. Furthermore, a critical objective is to show the practical utility of the generated attributions for downstream tasks, such as optimal task selection or demonstration selection, illustrating tangible benefits beyond mere interpretability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a kernel-based surrogate modeling framework for efficient task attribution, building upon an initial theoretical analysis of existing linear methods.

**Unified Task Weighting Framework:**
The problem is formalized using a K-dimensional binary vector $s = [s_1, \dots, s_K]^\top$, where $s_k=1$ indicates the inclusion of task $k$ in training. The model $f_W$ (parameterized by $W$) is trained by minimizing a weighted empirical loss $\hat{L}(f_W, s) = (\sum_j s_j)^{-1} \sum_k s_k \ell_k(f_W, T_k)$, where $\ell_k$ is the task-level loss for task $T_k$. The model's performance on a target test task $T_{test}$ is defined as $F(s) = E[\ell_{test}(f_{c_W(s)}, T_{test})]$, where $c_W(s)$ is the optimal parameter for the given task subset $s$. The ground-truth task attribution score for task $k$ is defined as the Leave-One-Out (LOO) score, $I^{LOO}_k = F(_K) - F(_K - e_k)$.

**Second-Order Analysis of Linear Surrogate Models:**
The paper analyzes linear surrogate models by approximating the performance function $F(s)$ with a second-order Taylor expansion around an anchor point $s^\star =_K$ (all tasks included). This expansion captures first-order linear effects and second-order interaction effects represented by the Hessian matrix $H_s = \nabla_s^2 F(s^\star)$.
Proposition 3.1 demonstrates that the coefficients $\hat{\beta}$ of a linear surrogate model, obtained by minimizing the squared loss against sampled $F(s)$ values, approximate the gradient $\nabla_s F(s^\star)$. This approximation is accurate up to terms induced by the sampling distribution (e.g., Bernoulli for task inclusion) and the second-order Hessian $H_s$, with higher-order errors bounded. Specifically, $\hat{\beta} - \nabla_s F(s^\star)$ is bounded by terms related to $(p-1)H_s_K$ and $(1-2p)/2 \cdot \text{diag}[H_s]$, where $p$ is the Bernoulli probability.
Corollary 3.2 extends this by showing a direct connection to influence functions: when second-order task interactions (i.e., the spectral norm of $H_s$) are sufficiently small, the linear surrogate model coefficients $\hat{\beta}$ approximate the influence function vector $\vec{I} = \nabla_s F(s^\star)$. This provides a theoretical explanation for the empirical utility of linear surrogate models in scenarios where task interactions are largely additive.

**Kernel Surrogate Models (KERNELSM):**
To address the limitations of linear models in capturing nonlinear interactions, KERNELSM is proposed. Instead of a linear function, a nonlinear function $g_\theta: \{0, 1\}^K \to \mathbb{R}$ is learned using Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) within a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). The objective is to minimize $\sum_{i=1}^m (F(s^{(i)}) - g_\theta(s^{(i)}))^2 + \lambda \|g_\theta\|_K^2$, where $s^{(i)}$ are sampled task subsets and $F(s^{(i)})$ are their corresponding performances.
The Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, $k(s^{(a)}, s^{(b)}) = \exp(-\gamma\|s^{(a)} - s^{(b)}\|^2)$, is employed. This choice is motivated by its universal expressivity and its ability to naturally encode the geometry of the binary subset space, reflecting the intuition that subsets with small Hamming distance should have similar performances. The optimal coefficients $\theta$ are computed analytically as $\theta = (\mathbf{K} + \lambda \mathbf{Id})^{-1}\mathbf{F}$, where $\mathbf{K}$ is the $m \times m$ kernel matrix and $\mathbf{F}$ is the vector of observed performances.

**Efficient Estimation via Projected Gradients (Algorithm 1 & 2):**
A significant challenge for kernel surrogate models is the need to compute $F(s^{(i)})$ for $m$ sampled subsets, which would typically involve $m$ full model retrainings. The paper introduces an efficient gradient-based estimation algorithm to approximate $F(s^{(i)})$ without repeated retraining.
1. **First-Order Approximation of Model Output:** The core idea is to approximate the fine-tuned model $f_W(x)$ using a first-order Taylor expansion around the parameters $W_0$ of a *pretrained model*: $f_W(x) \approx f_{W_0}(x) + \langle \nabla f_{W_0}(x), W - W_0 \rangle + \epsilon_W(x)$. Empirical evaluations (Table 2 and 5) show that the residual error $\epsilon_W(x)$ is often negligible (under 2%).
2. **Efficient Fine-tuning Parameter Estimation:** For each subset $s^{(i)}$, instead of retraining the full model, the optimal perturbation $Z^\star_{s^{(i)}} = W - W_0$ is estimated by minimizing the approximated loss: $\min_Z \sum_{k} s^{(i)}_k \sum_{(x,y) \in T_k} \ell_k(f_{W_0}(x) + \langle \nabla_W f_{W_0}(x), Z \rangle, y) + \lambda' \|Z\|^2$. This requires only *one-time computation* of logits $f_{W_0}(x)$ and gradients $\nabla_W f_{W_0}(x)$ for all training samples at the initial $W_0$.
3. **Random Projection for Dimensionality Reduction:** To handle the high dimensionality of gradient vectors, Gaussian random convolutions (i.e., random projection $P \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$ where $k \ll d$) are applied to project the gradients into a lower-dimensional space. This allows for fast optimization of $Z^\star_{s^{(i)}}$ in the reduced space.
4. **Performance Approximation:** The test performance $\hat{F}(s^{(i)})$ for each subset is then approximated using the estimated perturbation: $\hat{f}(x) = f_{W_0}(x) + \langle \tilde{g}, Z^\star_{s^{(i)}} \rangle$, where $\tilde{g}$ is the projected test gradient.
Algorithm 1 (KERNELSM) orchestrates this process: compute initial gradients, use Algorithm 2 (GradEx) to efficiently estimate $F(s^{(i)})$ for $m$ subsets, then train the KRR model. Proposition A.2 provides theoretical guarantees for the accuracy of this gradient-based estimation, assuming small first-order approximation errors and appropriate random projection dimensions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical characterizations and extensive empirical evaluations of the proposed KERNELSM approach.

**Theoretical Insights:**
- **Connection between Linear Surrogates and Influence Functions:** Proposition 3.1 and Corollary 3.2 formally demonstrate that linear surrogate model coefficients approximate the gradient of the performance function, which in turn corresponds to influence functions, under the assumption of small second-order task interactions (i.e., a relatively flat performance landscape). This provides a theoretical justification for the empirical effectiveness of linear surrogate models in certain contexts while simultaneously highlighting their inherent limitations in capturing non-additive effects.
- **Empirical Validation of Theoretical Connection:** Experiments on a binary classification task (Wisconsin Breast Cancer dataset with an L2-regularized logistic regression model) empirically confirmed this connection. Linear surrogate models and influence functions showed high Pearson correlations (0.98 and 0.97, respectively) with ground-truth Leave-One-Out (LOO) scores, and a correlation of 0.96 with each other (Figure 1), when the performance landscape was nearly linear.

**Necessity of Nonlinear Models:**
- A numerical example (Figure 2) using a two-layer MLP on a decision boundary task illustrated that task influence can exhibit strong nonlinearities. In this scenario, linear surrogate models failed to capture crucial transitions in test point predictions, whereas the RBF kernel surrogate model effectively modeled these joint influences and dependencies, demonstrating the need for nonlinear approaches.

**Efficiency of Gradient-Based Estimation:**
- **Low Approximation Error:** The core assumption of the efficient estimation algorithm—that the first-order Taylor expansion error $\epsilon_W(x)$ around a pretrained model $W_0$ is small—was empirically validated. Across diverse datasets (CIFAR-10, modular arithmetic, in-context learning, multi-objective RL), the relative approximation error was consistently below 2% (Table 2). Further tests on Large Language Models (LLMs) ranging from 7B to 34B parameters (DeepSeek-LLM-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-2-13B, CodeLlama-34B) also showed negligible errors, typically in the range of $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ (Table 5 in Appendix), justifying the linear approximation for efficient estimation.
- **Runtime Efficiency:** The gradient-based estimation procedure for KERNELSM maintains runtime comparable to fitting linear surrogate models (around 1-2 minutes for downstream task selection, as shown in Table 4), significantly reducing the computational burden associated with model retraining.

**Superior Attribution Accuracy:**
- **Overall Performance:** KERNELSM consistently outperformed all baseline attribution methods (Influence functions, TracIn, TRAK, SOURCE, BIF, and Linear surrogate models) across all tested domains. On average, KERNELSM achieved a 25% higher correlation with LOO ground truth (measured by Linear Datamodeling Score, LDS) compared to existing attribution methods (Table 3).
- **Complex Tasks:** The advantages were most pronounced in tasks characterized by complex, nonlinear structures:
 - **Modular Arithmetic Reasoning:** KERNELSM improved LDS by over 42% on average compared to linear surrogates (e.g., from 0.18 to 0.30 for Addition, and 0.44 to 0.52 for Quadratic tasks).
 - **In-Context Learning (ICL) with Qwen3-8B:** For the reasoning-heavy "Coin Flip" task, KERNELSM yielded a 25% relative improvement in LDS (from 0.43 to 0.54). On sentiment classification (SST-2), it improved LDS from 0.33 to 0.37.
 - **Multi-objective Reinforcement Learning (Meta-World MT10):** Even in environments where linear baselines performed well (LDS of 0.76), KERNELSM showed a further improvement to 0.80.
- **Scalability to Large K:** KERNELSM demonstrated robustness and maintained comparable performance when scaled to 500 prompt samples in ICL experiments, indicating its ability to handle scenarios with a large number of tasks without significant degradation in accuracy.

**Effectiveness in Downstream Task Selection:**
- The accurate attributions provided by KERNELSM translated into tangible improvements in practical downstream optimization tasks (Table 4):
 - **ICL Demonstration Selection:** KERNELSM achieved a 40% lower loss (better performance) in prompt selection for ICL tasks (e.g., 0.20 to 0.16 loss on SST-2, 0.05 to 0.02 loss on Coin Flip) compared to linear surrogate models.
 - **Multi-objective RL Synergistic Task Selection:** It improved target rewards by 15% when co-training target tasks with top-performing tasks identified by KERNELSM.

**Robustness to Loss Landscape Curvature:**
- Experiments investigating the impact of Hessian regularization (Figure 3) showed that while linear surrogate models' performance (LDS) degraded significantly when the base model was trained with standard SGD (leading to an increasing Hessian trace), KERNELSM remained robust. Both SAM and NSO optimizers, which constrain the Hessian trace, improved linear surrogate performance, but KERNELSM consistently maintained strong performance regardless of the optimizer or regularization applied to the base model, demonstrating its ability to handle diverse and complex loss landscapes.

**Ablation Studies (Appendix B.2):**
- **Kernel Comparison:** The RBF kernel achieved the lowest residual error among tested kernels, outperforming linear and polynomial kernels of degrees 1, 2, and 3 across all tasks (Table 8).
- **Sample Complexity:** KERNELSM consistently outperformed linear models across various surrogate training data sizes (20% to 80% of total subsets, Figure 4), suggesting better sample efficiency for achieving high accuracy.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The approach demonstrated relative robustness to changes in the regularization parameter $\lambda$ and kernel width parameter $\gamma$ (Table 7).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions that significantly advance the field of interpretability in modern AI systems, particularly concerning task attribution. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Interpretability of Multi-task Learning:** By introducing kernel surrogate models, the work moves beyond simple additive explanations of task influence. It enables the identification and quantification of complex nonlinear interactions, such as synergy or antagonism, between training tasks. This deeper understanding is crucial for deciphering how diverse training data shapes the behavior of powerful multi-task AI models, including large language models. This can inform the design of more effective multi-task learning architectures and training strategies, for instance, by identifying tasks that are highly synergistic or detrimental to others.

2. **Scalable Attribution for Large-Scale AI:** The development of an efficient, gradient-based estimation procedure addresses a major bottleneck in task attribution: the prohibitive computational cost of repeated model retraining. By leveraging first-order Taylor approximations around pretrained models and employing random projections, KERNELSM can accurately estimate task attributions without extensive retraining, making it practically applicable to large-scale, high-parameter models like LLMs and in complex environments such as multi-objective reinforcement learning. This scalability broadens the reach of interpretability tools to state-of-the-art AI.

3. **Improved Performance in Downstream Applications:** The research demonstrates that accurate task attributions are not merely an academic exercise but yield tangible improvements in practical downstream applications. For instance, in-context learning benefits significantly from KERNELSM-guided demonstration selection, leading to lower loss in target tasks. Similarly, in multi-objective reinforcement learning, KERNELSM can identify synergistic tasks for co-training, resulting in improved policy performance. This highlights the direct utility of advanced attribution methods for optimizing AI system deployment and performance.

4. **Unified Theoretical Understanding:** The formal connection established between linear surrogate models and influence functions provides a foundational theoretical insight. It clarifies the conditions under which these seemingly disparate attribution methods align, explaining their empirical success in certain contexts while rigorously defining the boundaries of their applicability. This theoretical unification can guide future research in designing attribution methods tailored to specific loss landscape characteristics.

5. **Robustness to Complex Model Dynamics:** The finding that KERNELSM remains robust and accurate even when the underlying model is trained with optimizers that lead to highly curved loss landscapes (i.e., large Hessian traces) is significant. This suggests that KERNELSM is less sensitive to the specific training dynamics of the base model compared to linear approaches, making it a more reliable tool across a wider range of AI training paradigms.

6. **Potential Impact on AI Development and Fairness:** The ability to precisely attribute task influence has implications for various areas. In multi-group learning, it could help understand how training on different demographic groups affects model behavior, potentially revealing biases or areas of underperformance due to complex interactions. In understanding pretrained models, it can shed light on which pre-training tasks contribute most to generalization on downstream tasks. This capability can contribute to developing more robust, fair, and transparent AI systems.

Future work suggested by the authors, such as extending the framework to planning abilities in LLMs, addressing generalization gaps, or modeling order-sensitive effects in in-context learning, indicates the broad potential for KERNELSM to serve as a foundational tool for deeper exploration into the mechanics of advanced AI.
