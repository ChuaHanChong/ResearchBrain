# Effective Parameters, Real Behavior: Renormalization for Robotics -- From Infinite Electron Mass to Sim-to-Real Gap

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from the University of Maryland and Stanford University introduces renormalization as a method to bridge the sim-to-real gap in robotics, advocating for the use of "effective parameters" in simulators that compensate for unmodeled physical details rather than solely striving for perfect physical accuracy. This approach demonstrates that deliberately adjusted parameters can enable simplified simulations to accurately reproduce real-world robot behavior for specific tasks and resolutions.

## Problem

- The sim-to-real gap remains a central challenge in robotics, as simulators inherently simplify the physical world, leading to discrepancies between simulated and real robot behavior.
- Traditional methods focus on increasing simulator accuracy through complex models, which often incurs high computational costs and may still fail to capture all physical nuances.
- Conventional practice often involves directly transferring physically measured parameters to simulators, which may not be optimal given the inherent approximations and finite resolutions of simulation environments.

## Method

- The paper proposes adopting **renormalization**, a concept from theoretical physics, to robotics, where simulators utilize ==effective parameters== that deviate from physically measured values.
- These ==effective parameters== are designed to absorb the effects of omitted physical details and finite simulation resolution, ensuring the simulator's observable behavior matches that of the real system.
- A ==four-step practical procedure== is outlined, guiding researchers to choose task-relevant observables, identify omitted physics, select minimal effective parameters, and validate them against real-world data.

## Results

- Analytical derivation for PD control at finite simulation frequency demonstrated that effective derivative gain and inertia parameters in the simulator must be adjusted upwards (e.g., KDsim=KDreal+δtKPrealK^{sim}_D = K^{real}_D + δ t K^{real}_PKDsim=KDreal+δtKPreal, Jsim=Jmeasured+δtKDrealJ_{sim} = J_{measured} + δ t K^{real}_DJsim=Jmeasured+δtKDreal).
- A simplified rope model successfully enabled a robot to tie a flying knot by focusing on reproducing the rope's shape and velocity at the critical self-collision moment, validating task-critical state fidelity.
- **Five** fitted hydrodynamic coefficients derived from **two** real swimming trajectories accurately predicted the forward-swimming behavior of an underwater robot at additional, unfitted actuation frequencies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Finite simulation frequencies in PD control cause a mixing of control gains and inertia, meaning simulator inertia and derivative gains need to be deliberately adjusted from physical measurements.
> - Achieving behavioral correctness for complex tasks, like dynamic rope manipulation, may not require full fidelity of the entire system, but rather accurate reproduction of task-critical states with simplified models and adjusted parameters.
> - Simplified, stateless models with a few fitted coefficients can effectively represent complex continuous physics, such as fluid dynamics for underwater robots, while maintaining predictive accuracy for observable behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Bridging the sim-to-real gap constitutes a central challenge in contemporary robotics research. The prevailing approach to address this gap involves the development of simulators that aim for increasing levels of physical accuracy, often by incorporating more complex models and higher computational fidelity. This paper introduces an alternative perspective, proposing that the sim-to-real gap can be addressed not solely by enhancing simulator accuracy but also by employing "renormalization."

The concept of renormalization originates from theoretical physics, notably Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), where it enables accurate predictions despite the presence of divergent "bare" parameters by absorbing the effects of unresolved details into effective, resolution-dependent parameters. The paper draws a parallel to computational fluid dynamics (CFD), specifically Large-Eddy Simulation (LES), where the effects of unresolvable small-scale turbulent eddies are represented by an effective viscosity and model coefficients, which vary with simulation resolution.

In robotics, simulators inherently simplify the physical world, using finite time steps and approximated models for phenomena such as actuation, material deformation, contact mechanics, and fluid interaction. The traditional practice involves measuring physical properties (e.g., inertia, damping, stiffness, friction) from real systems and directly transferring these values to the simulator. This paper challenges this conventional approach by suggesting that a simulator may more accurately reproduce real robot behavior when some parameters are deliberately adjusted away from their measured physical values. These "effective parameters" are posited to compensate for the physical details omitted by the simulator, thereby achieving behavioral correspondence even if the underlying parameters are not physically exact. This work positions itself as a complementary strategy to the ongoing efforts in high-fidelity simulation.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and demonstrate the concept of renormalization as a strategy for bridging the sim-to-real gap in robotics. The authors aim to illustrate that using effective, resolution-dependent parameters in a simulator can reproduce real robot behavior, even when these parameters deviate from physically measured values.

The motivation for this approach stems from the observed limitations and computational costs associated with striving for perfectly accurate simulators:
- **Inherent Simplification of Simulators:** Robot simulators, by design, cannot capture every detail of the physical world. They operate with finite computational resources, employing discretized time steps and simplified models for complex physical phenomena (e.g., actuators, deformable objects, contact, fluid dynamics). These simplifications inevitably lead to discrepancies between simulated and real-world behavior, contributing to the sim-to-real gap.
- **Lessons from Physics and Engineering:** The paper draws motivation from the successful application of renormalization in fields like QED and CFD. In QED, the theory accurately predicts experimental outcomes despite containing seemingly "infinite" parameters by absorbing unmodeled quantum effects into measurable effective parameters. Similarly, LES in CFD uses effective viscosity to account for the impact of unresolved small-scale turbulence, allowing coarser simulations to accurately predict large-scale fluid behavior. These examples highlight a precedent where effective parameters are used to compensate for omitted details.
- **Challenging the "Measured Parameters" Paradigm:** The conventional instinct in robotics is to populate simulators with parameters measured directly from the physical robot. The authors motivate their work by questioning whether this practice is always optimal. They propose that parameters chosen to reproduce observable behavior, rather than physical exactitude, might be more effective in bridging the sim-to-real gap, especially given the inherent approximations of simulators.
- **Computational Efficiency and Practicality:** Developing and running high-fidelity simulators can be computationally expensive. For example, reducing simulation frequency can accelerate reinforcement learning (RL) training, but it also alters the simulated dynamics. Renormalization offers a path to use computationally less demanding, coarser simulators effectively by adjusting parameters to compensate for the reduced resolution, thereby providing an alternative to simply increasing simulation accuracy.
- **Addressing Behavioral Discrepancies:** The core motivation is to achieve "behavioral correctness" in simulation. This means the simulator should reproduce the observable behavior of the real robot, even if the internal parameters or underlying physics model within the simulator are not a perfect representation of reality. The paper suggests that deliberately "wrong-looking" parameters can lead to "right measurable behavior" in the context of a simplified model.

Through these motivations, the paper aims to shift the focus from solely improving simulator accuracy to strategically adjusting simulator parameters to effectively represent the unmodeled complexities of the real world.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The paper introduces its renormalization-based approach through a conceptual argument and three distinct examples, followed by a generalized practical procedure. The core idea is that simulators, which inherently omit physical details, can absorb the effects of these omissions into a small number of effective, resolution-dependent parameters.

**Conceptual Framework:**
The approach starts from the premise that simulators operate at a finite resolution (denoted by 'a', e.g., physics time step, segment length in a rope model). Instead of using physically measured parameters ($\theta_{\text{measured}}$), the simulator employs effective parameters ($\theta^*_{\text{sim}}(a)$) that may differ from their measured counterparts. These effective parameters are chosen such that the observable behavior in simulation ($O_{\text{sim}}(a, \theta^*_{\text{sim}}(a))$) closely matches the observable behavior of the real system ($O_{\text{real}}$).

**Illustrative Examples:**

1. **PD Control at Finite Simulation Frequency:**
 - **Problem:** Robot low-level PD controllers, often used with policies derived from reinforcement learning (RL), are evaluated in simulators at a finite frequency ($f_{\text{sim}}$). This finite frequency introduces a delay compared to ideal continuous-time dynamics.
 - **Approach:** The authors analytically model this delay as an effective time delay ($\delta t \propto 1/f_{\text{sim}}$). They compare the ideal continuous-time dynamics ($J \ddot{q} = K_P(q_d - q) + K_D(\dot{q}_d - \dot{q})$) with the delayed simulated dynamics ($J \ddot{q} = K_P(q_d - q(t-\delta t)) + K_D(\dot{q}_d - \dot{q}(t-\delta t))$).
 - **Derivation:** By expanding the delayed position and velocity terms using Taylor series approximation (up to $O(\delta t^2)$), they demonstrate how the finite simulation frequency modifies the effective proportional, derivative, and inertial terms.
 - **Multi-joint extension (Appendix A):** For a multi-joint robot, an effective-action derivation is provided. This involves introducing a history field $y(s,t)$ to represent the delayed state, along with response fields and Lagrange multipliers. Integrating out these fields results in an effective action that explicitly shows the first-order corrections to the inertia matrix and gain matrices due to the delay.
 - **Goal:** To determine how the simulator's gains ($K_{P,\text{sim}}, K_{D,\text{sim}}$) and inertia ($J_{\text{sim}}$) should be adjusted from the real robot's gains ($K_{P,\text{real}}, K_{D,\text{real}}$) and measured inertia ($J_{\text{measured}}$) to compensate for the finite simulation frequency and match real-world behavior.

2. **Dynamic Rope Manipulation with a Simplified Model:**
 - **Problem:** Simulating the full deformation and self-contact of a continuous rope is computationally intensive.
 - **Approach:** The study considers a robot task of tying a flying knot with a rope. Instead of a high-fidelity continuous rope model, the simulator uses a simplified representation: a short chain of point masses connected by simple constraints.
 - **Observable Focus:** The core insight is that accurate reproduction of the *entire* rope trajectory is not necessary. Instead, the focus is on reproducing the "task-critical state"—specifically, the rope's shape and velocity at the moment of the first rope-rope self-collision, which is crucial for knot success.
 - **Renormalization Aspect:** The simplified chain model, which omits the continuous degrees of freedom and complex physics of the real rope, is used. Its parameters (or, implicitly, the robot's commands derived from this model) are adjusted to ensure the task-critical observable matches reality, thereby absorbing the effects of the omitted details into the model's effective behavior.

3. **Underwater Swimming with a Five-Parameter Fluid Model:**
 - **Problem:** Simulating the continuous fluid field surrounding an underwater robot is computationally expensive (requiring full CFD).
 - **Approach:** A tendon-driven robotic fish is modeled using a simplified, stateless hydrodynamic model. This model directly computes fluid forces based on the robot's current motion, without explicitly simulating the velocity and pressure fields of the surrounding water.
 - **Effective Parameters:** The model employs five hydrodynamic coefficients.
 - **Procedure:** These five coefficients are fitted using data from two real swimming trajectories of the robot.
 - **Renormalization Aspect:** The five fitted coefficients act as renormalized parameters, collectively representing the complex and continuous fluid dynamics that are not explicitly simulated. The goal is to preserve observable swimming behavior (e.g., trajectory, forward speed) without the computational burden of a full fluid simulation.

**Practical Procedure (Generalization):**
Based on these examples, the authors propose a four-step procedure for applying renormalization in robotics:
1. **Choose the observable behavior:** Define the specific, task-relevant measurements to be matched between simulation and reality (e.g., joint response, a critical event state, a swimming speed).
2. **Identify omitted physics:** Determine which physical details the chosen simulator resolution or model simplifications explicitly exclude.
3. **Select effective parameters:** Identify a minimal set of simulator parameters capable of absorbing the effects of the identified omitted details. These parameters are initialized from physical measurements but are allowed to deviate during the matching process.
4. **Determine and validate parameters:** Fit these effective parameters using real-world measurements and then validate their generalizability and robustness under new operating conditions. If validation fails, either the parameter set or the simulator model may need refinement.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research paper presents its findings primarily through analytical derivations and conceptual validations across three distinct robotics scenarios, culminating in a generalized practical procedure.

**Analytical Findings for PD Control at Finite Simulation Frequency:**
- **Effective Parameter Mixing:** The analytical derivation for a PD controller operating at a finite simulation frequency ($f_{\text{sim}}$), modeled by an effective delay ($\delta t$), revealed that this delay causes a mixing of control gains and inertia.
 - The proportional gain ($K_P$) influences the effective derivative gain, such that the effective derivative gain becomes $K^{\text{eff}}_D = K_D - \delta t K_P$.
 - The derivative gain ($K_D$) influences the effective inertia, resulting in an effective inertia $J^{\text{eff}} = J - \delta t K_D$.
 - Additionally, a velocity-feedforward term, $\delta t K_P \dot{q}_d$, arises when the desired position is moving.
- **Renormalized Simulator Parameters:** To ensure the finite-frequency simulator matches the real robot's dynamics, the simulator parameters must be adjusted. Specifically:
 - The simulator's proportional gain ($K^{\text{sim}}_P$) should equal the real gain ($K^{\text{real}}_P$).
 - The simulator's derivative gain ($K^{\text{sim}}_D$) should be set to $K^{\text{real}}_D + \delta t K^{\text{real}}_P + O(\delta t^2)$. This implies that $K^{\text{sim}}_D$ should be higher than $K^{\text{real}}_D$.
 - The simulator's inertia ($J_{\text{sim}}$) should be set to $J_{\text{measured}} + \delta t K^{\text{real}}_D + O(\delta t^2)$. This is a key finding, indicating that the inertia parameter used in the simulator ($J_{\text{sim}}$) should be deliberately different from the physically measured inertia ($J_{\text{measured}}$) to compensate for the effects of finite simulation frequency.
- **Multi-joint Generalization:** The derivation in Appendix A extends these findings to multi-joint robots, showing similar corrections to the joint-space inertia matrix and gain matrices, reinforcing the idea that simulator parameters need to deviate from physical measurements.

**Conceptual Validation for Dynamic Rope Manipulation:**
- **Task-Critical State Fidelity over Full Trajectory Fidelity:** The rope manipulation example illustrates that a highly simplified simulator (using a short chain of point masses instead of a continuous rope) can successfully enable a complex task (tying a flying knot) without accurately reproducing the complete real rope trajectory.
- **Observable-Driven Parameter Adjustment:** Success was achieved by focusing parameter correction on a specific "task-critical state"—the rope's shape and velocity at the first self-collision point. This suggests that for certain tasks, it is sufficient for the simplified model's behavior at critical junctures to match reality, rather than requiring global fidelity. The simplified model, through its adjusted parameters or derived robot commands, implicitly absorbs the effects of the continuous rope's complex dynamics.

**Empirical Validation for Underwater Swimming:**
- **Effective Representation of Complex Physics:** The study of an underwater robot demonstrated that a simplified, stateless hydrodynamic model, employing only five fitted hydrodynamic coefficients, could effectively represent the complex interaction between the robot and its surrounding continuous fluid field. This avoids the computational cost of full CFD simulation.
- **Generalizability of Renormalized Parameters:** These five coefficients were determined by fitting two real swimming trajectories. A notable finding was that these same coefficients were then able to predict the robot's forward-swimming behavior accurately at additional actuation frequencies that were not used during the initial fitting process. This indicates that the fitted coefficients captured a consistent and repeatable aspect of the fluid dynamics, effectively acting as renormalized parameters that absorb the influence of the unsimulated fluid field while preserving observable swimming behavior.

**Synthesis into a Practical Procedure:**
The paper synthesizes these specific examples into a four-step practical procedure that guides researchers in applying renormalization. This procedure emphasizes choosing task-relevant observables, identifying omitted physics, selecting a minimal set of effective parameters, and validating these parameters against real-world data. This represents a methodological finding, translating the theoretical concept into an actionable research strategy.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that deliberately adjusting simulator parameters away from their physically measured values can systematically compensate for omitted physical details, leading to simulations that more accurately reflect real-world robot behavior for specific tasks and resolutions. This approach offers a means to achieve behavioral fidelity in simulation without necessarily requiring full physical fidelity.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The work introduces renormalization as a principled and complementary approach to bridging the sim-to-real gap in robotics, offering several potential impacts:

- **Paradigm Shift in Sim-to-Real:** The paper challenges the prevailing methodology of continuously striving for increased simulator accuracy. By proposing that "physically incorrect but behaviorally correct" parameters can yield accurate observable robot behavior, it provides a theoretical justification for a different strategy. This could lead to a shift in how researchers approach simulator development and parameter tuning.
- **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** Renormalization allows for the effective use of simpler, computationally less expensive simulators. For instance, the analytical results on PD control at finite simulation frequency suggest that instead of increasing simulation frequency (and thus computational cost), effective parameters can be adjusted to maintain fidelity. This has significant implications for accelerating simulation-based robot learning, such as reinforcement learning, where numerous interactions with a physics engine are required.
- **Improved Simulation Fidelity for Task-Specific Behavior:** By focusing on matching specific, task-relevant observables, the renormalization approach can produce simulations that are more accurate for the intended application, even if they do not perfectly model every physical detail. This task-centric fidelity can be more valuable than universal physical accuracy for many robotics applications.
- **Systematization of Existing Practices:** The paper points out that several successful robotics studies already implicitly utilize the principles of renormalization without explicitly labeling them as such. By formally introducing the concept and providing a structured procedure, this work offers a unifying framework that can help turn individual insights into a systematic line of research, encouraging more deliberate application of these principles.
- **Practical Guidance for Researchers:** The outlined four-step practical procedure provides concrete methodological guidance for robotics researchers. It prompts questions about omitted physics, relevant observables, and the selection and validation of effective parameters, offering a structured approach to addressing sim-to-real challenges. This can make the process of transferring policies from simulation to reality more systematic and less reliant on ad-hoc tuning.
- **Rethinking "Physical Parameters":** The paper encourages a re-evaluation of the role of physical parameters in simulators. It demonstrates that parameters might not need to strictly correspond to their real-world measured values if they effectively absorb the complexity of unmodeled phenomena. This perspective could broaden the scope of parameter optimization in robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By establishing renormalization as a formal concept in robotics, the paper lays a foundation for future research into developing systematic methods for identifying omitted physics, selecting optimal effective parameter sets, and robustly determining these parameters through limited real-world experimentation.

In essence, this research suggests that a strategically simplified simulation, augmented by effective parameters that compensate for omitted details, can offer a more efficient and practical path to bridging the sim-to-real gap compared to solely pursuing ever-higher levels of physical accuracy.
