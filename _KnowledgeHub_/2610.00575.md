# Token-World: World Modeling in Vision-Language Model Token Space for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Token-World is an action-conditioned world model that simulates robot futures directly in the visual-token space a VLA policy consumes, skipping RGB generation. A semantic VAE compresses Qwen3-VL tokens from 2560 to 16 channels per token, and a flow-matching spatiotemporal Transformer rolls dynamics forward in that space. It beats RGB simulators on fidelity, matches real policy success better (r=0.794), and runs 5.3x faster than Ctrl-World.

## Problem

- RGB world-model simulators predict future pixels that must be re-encoded into VLA visual tokens, an indirect interface that adds latency between simulation and policy execution.
- Pixel reconstruction spends model capacity on appearance details the downstream policy may ignore, instead of targeting the representation actually used to generate actions.
- Raw policy-facing VLM tokens are high-dimensional and built for perception rather than generative modeling, so predicting their dynamics directly is costly and inaccurate.

## Method

- ==Semantic VAE (S-VAE)== codec: compresses frozen ==Qwen3-VL== visual tokens from 108x2560 to 108x16 along the feature dimension only, keeping spatial layout; trained with ==MSE, cosine and KL losses==, then frozen.
- Action-conditioned ==spatiotemporal Transformer== with ==factorized spatial and causal temporal attention==, plus a ==GRU== recurrent state carrying context beyond the bounded attention window, over compact tokens and proprioception.
- ==x0-parameterized flow-matching objective== with ==shortcut forcing== for 16-step generation, using ==sliding-window autoregressive rollout== fully in compact space and decoding to VLM tokens only for the policy.

## Results

- On RoboTwin **50** tasks, VLM-feature cosine is **0.7714** (NMSE **0.4892**) versus **0.7372**/**0.5685** for IRASim; policy-action cosine is **0.9439** versus **0.9265** for Ctrl-World.
- Closed-loop success estimates correlate with reference success at r=0.794 versus **0.583** for Ctrl-World; step time of **0.359** s gives **2.0x**, **5.3x** and **6.1x** speedups over IRASim, Ctrl-World and WorldGym.
- Under the same backbone, S-VAE cuts feature NMSE to **0.4892** from **0.7851** for raw VLM features and **0.8164** for SDXL-VAE; d=16 beats 8, **32** and **48**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A world model coupled to a policy can simulate in the policy's own input representation, removing pixel round-trips and making policy evaluation both faster and more faithful.
> - Better reconstruction does not mean better dynamics: larger latent dimensions reconstruct more accurately but get less diffusion-friendly, so latent size should be tuned for predictability.
> - Compressing along the channel dimension while keeping spatial token layout preserves the local structure that makes dynamics learnable, unlike pooling a frame into a few tokens.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Evaluating a robot-manipulation policy through repeated real-world trials is slow and costly, so researchers can instead run imagined rollouts in a learned simulator. The usual simulator first paints a future picture and then translates that picture back into the input the policy actually uses. Token-World skips that round trip and predicts in a compact form of the policy's own input. In the authors' closed-loop test, its simulated policy outcomes tracked reference outcomes more closely than those from Ctrl-World, and its measured simulation latency was lower. This is evidence from one particular evaluation. It does not show that every task, policy, or simulator will benefit.

(a) World-model versus reference success rate. Each point is a policy–task pair, the gray dotted line is the oracle y = x, and the black dashed line is the fitted regression. Token-World: r = 0.794, y = 0.78x + 0.19. Ctrl-World: r = 0.583, y = 0.50x + 0.40. (b) Average step time in seconds: IRASim 0.719, Ctrl-World 1.897, WorldGym 2.200, Token-World 0.359. Lower is better.

### 2. Predicting in the policy's language

The policy is a vision-language-action (VLA) controller, which uses visual input and instructions to choose actions. Existing pipelines often generate future RGB images and re-encode them into the visual tokens the policy consumes. The authors frame that mismatch as both wasted computation and a risk of modeling details irrelevant to action.

Token-World starts from frozen Qwen3-VL features. In the main setup, these are 108 spatial tokens with 2,560 channels each. A separately trained semantic VAE (S-VAE) compresses this to 16 channels per token. The compression keeps the number and spatial arrangement of tokens, with no spatial pooling or token fusion.

During a rollout, a spatiotemporal Transformer takes in the compact visual state, robot proprioception, action history, and the next action. It then predicts future compact states autoregressively. Temporal attention has a finite context window, so a recurrent GRU state carries earlier context into later windows. When the policy needs an input, the S-VAE decoder restores policy-facing features. RGB is not reconstructed in the rollout or policy-feedback loop. A separate frozen RGB decoder is used only for visualization and manual success assessment.

Token-World overview. RGB enters frozen Qwen3-VL. The S-VAE compresses its 108×2560 visual tokens to 108×16, and the decoder feeds a downstream action expert. Rollout runs across sliding windows, with bounded KV caches and GRU states carried between windows.

A controlled comparison tests whether this representation helps. It holds the dynamics backbone and training protocol fixed and changes the state representation along with its input/output projections. Against raw VLM features, S-VAE lowers feature NMSE from 0.7851 to 0.4892 and raises feature cosine similarity from 0.6375 to 0.7714. It lowers action NMSE from 0.2180 to 0.1145 and raises action cosine similarity from 0.8948 to 0.9439. SDXL-VAE latents also do worse than S-VAE. This ablation supports the claim that representation design matters. It does not isolate representation as the only cause of the full system's advantage over Ctrl-World.

### 3. Closed-loop outcomes and latency

In the closed-loop test, predicted states are fed back to the policy to generate later actions. The evaluated policies were three StarVLA variants: OFT, PIv3, and GR00T. All share a Qwen3-VL backbone and differ in their action heads. Reference and simulated rollouts started from the same recorded states, and success was manually assessed from RGB-decoded rollouts. Across policy–task pairs, the Pearson correlation between simulated and reference success rates was 0.794 for Token-World and 0.583 for Ctrl-World. Correlation measures how outcomes vary together. It is not a success rate, and it does not establish perfect calibration or accurate prediction for every task.

Token-World took 0.359 seconds per predicted observation, 5.3× faster than Ctrl-World in the paper's measured setup. This is batch-1 latency on PPU-ZW810E accelerators. RGB baselines use 50 denoising steps, while Token-World uses 16 steps with shortcut forcing. For RGB baselines, the timing includes RGB decoding plus VLM re-encoding. For Token-World, it includes compact-state prediction plus direct decoding to policy-facing VLM features.

### 4. The interface is the contribution

Token-World's shift is to predict in a compact form of the representation a policy actually consumes, rather than generating pixels only to translate them back. The controlled representation comparison and the closed-loop correlation both support this design. The tests cover manipulation, and the evaluated policies share a Qwen3-VL backbone. Whether the representation generalizes across policy backbones remains open.
