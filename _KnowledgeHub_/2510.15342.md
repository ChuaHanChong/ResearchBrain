# SHARE: Scene-Human Aligned Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> SHARE reconstructs both human motion and the surrounding 3D scene in metric scale from a single stationary-camera RGB video. It initializes SMPL meshes and masks with TRAM and keyframe point maps with MoGe-2, merges keyframe point maps into one background scene, then optimizes only human translations with a keyframe Chamfer body loss plus a relative root-joint loss. It cuts root position error to 0.44 m, beating prior methods.

## Problem

- Human motion generation for scene interaction needs paired human-scene data, which is costly to capture at scale and synthetically limited in interaction diversity.
- Reinforcement learning alternatives sidestep paired data but hinge on carefully hand-crafted objective functions, making plausible interaction behavior hard to obtain reliably.
- Joint human-plus-scene reconstruction stays underexplored: one prior method stacks many losses, another needs a separate contact-prediction model to ground humans.

## Method

- Initialization stage: **TRAM** supplies per-frame human masks and initial ==SMPL== local poses, shape, and translations; **MoGe-2** supplies metric-scale keyframe depth maps and camera intrinsics.
- Scene reconstruction: rescale the second keyframe depth by a mean-depth ratio over non-human pixels, then merge ==inverse-projected point maps==, averaging shared background and filling each keyframe's human-occluded pixels from the other.
- Human optimization: freeze poses and shape, optimize translations under a keyframe ==symmetric Chamfer body loss== anchoring mesh vertices to masked human points, plus a ==relative root-joint MSE loss== propagating keyframe shifts.

## Results

- On a **3.3k**-frame RICH test subset, SHARE reaches **0.44 m** MRPE and **0.44 m** V2V, versus **0.61**/**0.62** for BEV, **0.98**/**0.98** for TRAM, and **1.19**/**1.20** for the scene-aware baseline.
- A **20**-participant user study on Toyota Smarthome videos rated SHARE **3.22** realism versus **2.07** for the competing scene-aware method, with an **85%** versus **15%** pairwise preference rate.
- Optimization needs only **600** Adam iterations at learning rate **0.01** on one **48 GB** GPU, using just the first and last frames as keyframes for short clips without extra keyframe cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scene point maps carry enough metric depth signal to ground body placement directly, so two simple losses can replace multi-term objectives or learned contact predictors.
> - Freezing pose and shape while optimizing only translation isolates the actual failure mode: regressors capture articulation well but place bodies poorly in depth.
> - Anchoring keyframes and propagating their correction through relative root offsets smooths trajectories, but mesh-size mismatch still causes floor penetration and keyframe jitter.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of realistic motion for virtual characters and autonomous agents is a critical area within computer graphics, augmented/virtual reality (AR/VR), gaming, and robotics. A significant challenge in this field is accurately animating characters that interact plausibly with their surrounding environments. Human motion rarely occurs in isolation; characters navigate spaces, sit on furniture, and engage with objects. Therefore, incorporating Human-Scene Interaction (HSI) into motion generation is essential for achieving higher levels of immersion and naturalness.

Current research approaches to HSI often fall into two categories: data-driven methods and reinforcement learning (RL) approaches. Data-driven methods learn motion patterns from paired human-scene datasets, but such data is inherently difficult and expensive to acquire at scale, leading to limited diversity. RL methods can leverage unpaired data but require meticulous crafting of objective functions to guide learning effectively.

A related and foundational task is human motion reconstruction from monocular videos, which offers a pathway toward joint human-scene understanding. However, existing methods that aim to recover both human motion and the surrounding environment from single-view video face specific limitations. For instance, MHMocap [Luvizon et al. 2023] relies on multiple complex loss functions, and JOSH [Liu et al. 2025] necessitates a separate model for contact prediction. Many other methods focus solely on human motion without explicitly accounting for scene geometry, leading to inaccuracies in 3D human placement.

This work addresses the gap in accurately grounding human motion in 3D space by explicitly leveraging scene geometry. It proposes a unified framework that reconstructs both human motion and the surrounding scene from a monocular RGB video, aiming to achieve superior 3D human positioning without the complexities of multi-loss systems or dedicated contact prediction models found in some prior joint reconstruction efforts. The research positions itself as an advancement in robustly extracting metric-scale human and scene data, which can then serve as a foundation for data-driven HSI applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method, named Scene-Human Aligned REconstruction (SHARE), capable of accurately reconstructing 3D human motion and the surrounding scene from a monocular RGB video captured by a stationary camera. A central focus is on improving the accuracy of 3D human placement within the reconstructed environment.

The motivation behind SHARE stems from several key challenges and requirements in related fields:

1. **Enhancing Realism in Virtual Environments:** Realistic motion for virtual characters is crucial for increasing user immersion in gaming, AR/VR, and for enabling more natural human-robot interactions. Generating plausible motion necessitates grounding characters accurately within their surrounding environment, as human actions are intrinsically tied to spatial contexts (e.g., navigating a room, sitting on a sofa).
2. **Addressing Limitations of Current HSI Approaches:** Existing methods for Human-Scene Interaction (HSI) generation face significant hurdles. Data-driven methods struggle with the scarcity and cost of obtaining diverse, high-quality paired human-scene datasets. Reinforcement learning methods, while flexible, depend on carefully designed objective functions, which can be difficult to define comprehensively for complex interactions.
3. **Improving Monocular Human Motion Reconstruction:** While human motion reconstruction from monocular videos is a well-established task, approaches that jointly recover both human motion and the environment remain less explored and often exhibit limitations. Specifically, previous methods frequently struggle with precisely placing humans in 3D space relative to the scene. The paper highlights that methods like MHMocap [Luvizon et al. 2023] employ multiple loss functions, and JOSH [Liu et al. 2025] requires an additional model for contact prediction, increasing complexity.
4. **Leveraging Scene Geometry for Spatial Grounding:** The research is motivated by the hypothesis that robust depth cues derived from scene geometry can provide strong spatial constraints to accurately ground human motion reconstruction. By directly exploiting these cues, the authors aim to achieve better 3D human positioning compared to methods that do not explicitly incorporate scene context or rely on less robust depth estimation.
5. **Developing a Unified and Simplified Framework:** The objective is to create a streamlined framework that effectively reconstructs both human motion (represented by SMPL parameters) and a metric-scale scene point map using only two specific loss functions. This aims to simplify the reconstruction process while maintaining or improving accuracy compared to more complex multi-loss systems.
6. **Enabling Downstream Applications:** By providing accurate and metrically scaled reconstructions of both humans and scenes from common video sources, SHARE seeks to facilitate various use cases. This includes enabling data-driven motion generation for HSI by offering a reliable source of interaction data, applicable to both curated datasets and unstructured "in-the-wild" web videos.

In summary, the core motivation is to overcome the challenge of accurate 3D human placement within reconstructed scenes from monocular video by intelligently integrating scene geometry, thereby contributing to more realistic virtual character interactions and providing valuable data for HSI research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SHARE framework takes a monocular RGB video as input, assuming a stationary camera and a single person in view. Its goal is to reconstruct a 3D mesh of the person for each frame, represented by Skinned Multi-Person Linear Model (SMPL) parameters (local pose $\theta$, body shape $\beta$, and translation $\pi$), and a metric-scale background scene point map $S$. The process is divided into three main stages: Initialization, Scene Reconstruction, and Human Optimization.

#### 3.1. Initialization

The first stage involves independently initializing human meshes and segmentation masks, along with keyframe scene point maps, using pre-trained models.

- **Human Meshes and Masks:** The TRAM [Wang et al. 2024] method is employed to obtain initial SMPL parameters ({$\theta_t$}, $\beta$, {$\pi_t$}) for all frames $t$, and human segmentation masks ($H_t$). Although TRAM includes SLAM capabilities, these are not utilized in the stationary camera setting. TRAM's robust human masking component is leveraged to produce binary masks for each frame.
- **Keyframe Scene Point Maps:** MoGe-2 [Wang et et al. 2025] is used to estimate 3D point maps, 2D depth maps ($D_t$), and camera intrinsics ($I_t$) from selected keyframes. For computational efficiency, typically the first and last frames of a video are chosen as keyframes (frame 1 and frame $T$). MoGe-2 is selected for its enhanced metric-scale prediction and improved architecture over its predecessor. The model's estimated 2D depth map is projected using its predicted camera intrinsics to generate a 3D point map, with each point inheriting color from the original image. MoGe-2's built-in mask is applied to filter out noisy points from the depth map.

#### 3.2. Scene Reconstruction

This stage aims to reconstruct a single, consistent metric-scale scene point map by integrating information from the keyframes.

- **Depth Map Alignment:** MoGe-2 can produce slightly different depth estimations for the same scene across different keyframes. To align these, a scale factor $\alpha$ is calculated. This factor is the ratio of the mean depth of $D_1$ to the mean depth of $D_T$, computed only for pixels outside the human masks in both keyframes (i.e., for $i \in \Omega \setminus \{H_1 \cup H_T\}$).
- **3D Point Map Generation:** Using the inverse projection function ($\Pi^{-1}$), the initial 3D point map $S_1$ is obtained from ($D_1, I_1, f_1$), and the scaled 3D point map $S_T$ is derived from ($\alpha \cdot D_T, I_T, f_T$).
- **Combining Point Maps:** The final scene point map $S$ is constructed by combining $S_1$ and $S_T$. The method assumes that human masks in the keyframes do not overlap, enabling a complete background reconstruction. For common background points (pixels not covered by human masks in either keyframe), the average of their corresponding 3D points from $S_1$ and $S_T$ is taken. Points that were occluded by the human in one keyframe but visible in the other are directly incorporated from the complementary keyframe (e.g., points $S_{i,1}$ where $i \in H_T$ and points $S_{i,T}$ where $i \in H_1$). This process leverages the stationary camera assumption to merge keyframe points effectively.

#### 3.3. Human Optimization

The final stage refines the human’s 3D translations, grounding them accurately within the reconstructed scene. This optimization focuses on translations ($\pi_t$) while freezing the initial local poses ({$\theta_t$}) and body shape ($\beta$) provided by TRAM, as MoGe-2 provides metric scale for position but not fine body shape details. The optimization minimizes a combined loss function $L = L_{body} + L_{root}$.

- **Keyframe Body Loss ($L_{body}$):** This loss anchors the human mesh to the human point map at the keyframes. It is computed as the symmetric Chamfer Distance (CD) between the human mesh vertices ($V_t$) and the subset of the scene point map corresponding to the human pixels ($S_{i,t} : i \in H_t$) for both keyframes.
 $L_{body} = CD(V_1, \{S_{i,1} : i \in H_1\}) + CD(V_T, \{S_{i,T} : i \in H_T\})$
 This encourages the keyframe translations ($\pi_1$, $\pi_T$) to place the human meshes robustly within the densest parts of the (potentially noisy) human point maps.
- **Relative Root Joint Loss ($L_{root}$):** This loss aims to smooth the human motion trajectory and ensure that adjustments to keyframe positions by $L_{body}$ are consistently propagated throughout the entire motion.
 1. **Smoothed Initial Root Joints:** A Gaussian filter G is applied to the initial root joint trajectory from TRAM ($J^{init}_{root}$) to obtain a smoothed trajectory ($\tilde{J}^{init}_{root}$).
 2. **Relative Position Calculation:** For each keyframe $k$ (e.g., frame 1 or $T$), the relative root joint positions ($d^{init,k}$ and $d^k$) are calculated. $d^{init,k}$ represents the smoothed initial root joint positions relative to the smoothed keyframe root joint $k$, for all frames except the complementary keyframe $k'$. Similarly, $d^k$ represents the current root joint positions relative to the current keyframe root joint $k$.
 3. **Mean Squared Error:** The $L_{root}$ loss is defined as the mean squared error between the initial smoothed relative root joint positions and the current relative root joint positions, for both keyframes.
 $L_{root} = \frac{1}{T-1} (||d^{init,1} - d^1||^2_2 + ||d^{init,T} - d^T||^2_2)$
 This loss guides the current motion to follow the initial smoothed trajectory, ensuring consistency and stability across the sequence.

The optimization is performed using an Adam optimizer for 600 iterations with a learning rate of 0.01 on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative evaluations on human motion reconstruction and qualitative demonstrations of joint human motion and scene reconstruction, supported by a user study.

#### 4.1. Human Motion Reconstruction (Quantitative)

- **Dataset:** Evaluation was conducted on a subset of the RICH dataset's test split [Huang et al. 2022]. This subset comprised 33 sequences, each 100 frames long, derived from 8 videos across 3 distinct scenes, totaling 3.3k frames. All sequences featured a single person and a static camera, chosen to ensure compatibility with all compared models.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Two standard metrics were used:
 - **Mean Root Position Error (MRPE):** Measures the accuracy of the pelvis (root joint) placement.
 - **Vertex-to-Vertex Error (V2V):** Quantifies the overall fidelity of the reconstructed human mesh.
 Both metrics report error in meters (m), with lower values indicating better performance.
- **Comparative Analysis (Table 1):** SHARE was compared against three existing methods:
 - **MHMocap [Luvizon et al. 2023]:** Another SMPL-based method that also reconstructs the 3D scene from an RGB video with a stationary camera.
 - **TRAM [Wang et al. 2024]:** A method for human motion reconstruction from any video, which does not reconstruct the scene.
 - **BEV [Sun et al. 2022]:** A method for monocular regression of 3D people in depth, without scene reconstruction.

 The quantitative results are summarized as follows:
 - **SHARE (ours):** MRPE 0.44 ± 0.37 m, V2V 0.44 ± 0.36 m
 - **BEV:** MRPE 0.61 ± 0.35 m, V2V 0.62 ± 0.33 m
 - **TRAM:** MRPE 0.98 ± 0.37 m, V2V 0.98 ± 0.36 m
 - **MHMocap:** MRPE 1.19 ± 0.67 m, V2V 1.20 ± 0.67 m

- **Conclusion:** SHARE demonstrated superior performance, outperforming all prior works on both MRPE and V2V metrics by a notable margin. The authors attribute this improvement to SHARE's enhanced accuracy in predicting human body translations in 3D space. The performance of MHMocap, the only other scene-reconstructing method, was noted to be limited by inaccuracies stemming from its depth estimation model, DPT [Ranftl et al. 2021]. TRAM and BEV, which do not account for the scene, also yielded higher errors.
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 2):** Visualizations on selected RICH dataset videos illustrate that SHARE's reconstructions align more closely with the ground truth motion when placed within ground truth scene scans compared to the other methods.

#### 4.2. Human Motion + Scene Reconstruction (Qualitative and User Study)

Beyond quantitative evaluation, the paper emphasizes SHARE's generalization capabilities across various scenarios.

- **In-the-Wild Web Videos (Figure 1):** SHARE demonstrated strong qualitative performance on an unstructured "in-the-wild" web video, showcasing its potential for broader applicability.
- **Curated Datasets Lacking Ground Truth (Figure 3):** The method was applied to the Toyota Smarthome dataset [Das et al. 2019], which, despite providing clean RGB videos, suffers from noisy depth and skeletal data. SHARE successfully reconstructed human motion and the scene, producing more reliable data modalities from this dataset. An example of an older adult walking around a table from this dataset is provided, illustrating the reconstruction.
- **User Study (Table 2):** A user study involving 20 participants was conducted to evaluate the perceived realism of reconstructions.
 - **Methodology:** Participants rated the realism of human and scene reconstructions (on a 1 to 5 scale) from 3 randomly selected video pairs from the Toyota Smarthome dataset, processed by MHMocap and SHARE. They also indicated their preferred reconstruction in each pair.
 - **Results:**
 - **SHARE (ours):** Average Realism Score 3.22, Selection Rate 85%
 - **MHMocap:** Average Realism Score 2.07, Selection Rate 15%
 - **Conclusion:** The user study indicated a clear preference for SHARE, suggesting higher perceived realism of its combined human motion and scene reconstructions. This finding supports the argument that SHARE can be particularly useful for researchers seeking to extract HSI motions from diverse video sources without relying on existing or accurate annotations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SHARE framework represents an advancement in the joint reconstruction of 3D human motion and surrounding environments from monocular video, with particular emphasis on accurate human placement.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to produce highly accurate 3D positional estimates for human motion within a reconstructed scene. This is achieved through a methodology that explicitly leverages scene geometry from estimated point maps to ground human mesh reconstructions, utilizing only two loss functions for optimization. The quantitative results on the RICH dataset indicate a substantial improvement in Mean Root Position Error (MRPE) and Vertex-to-Vertex Error (V2V) compared to existing state-of-the-art methods, including those that also attempt scene reconstruction.

The potential impacts of SHARE are multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Realism in Virtual Applications:** The improved accuracy in 3D human placement directly contributes to creating more realistic and immersive character interactions in applications such as gaming, AR/VR, and virtual try-on. By allowing virtual characters to be precisely grounded within their digital environments, the illusion of presence and interaction is strengthened.
2. **Advancement in Human-Robot Interaction:** For autonomous agents and robotics, accurately understanding and predicting human motion in relation to the environment is crucial for natural and safe collaboration. SHARE's ability to reconstruct both human motion and scene geometry in metric scale can provide valuable input for developing more sophisticated human-robot interaction systems.
3. **Facilitating Data-Driven HSI Research:** The framework offers a robust tool for researchers working on Human-Scene Interaction (HSI). By reliably extracting human motion and scene data from various video sources, including "in-the-wild" web videos and curated datasets with noisy or absent annotations (as demonstrated with the Toyota Smarthome dataset), SHARE can alleviate the challenges associated with data acquisition for HSI. This can enable the creation of richer, more diverse datasets for training data-driven HSI models, potentially leading to breakthroughs in motion generation and behavior prediction.
4. **Broader Applicability:** The method's effectiveness on unstructured web videos and its ability to process datasets with imperfect annotations suggest its utility across a wide range of real-world video data, extending its impact beyond highly controlled laboratory settings.
5. **Simplified Joint Reconstruction:** By achieving competitive, and in many cases superior, performance with a relatively straightforward loss function setup, SHARE offers a more accessible and potentially more robust approach to joint human-scene reconstruction compared to methods requiring complex multi-loss formulations or auxiliary models for specific cues like contact.

Looking ahead, the authors envision extensions towards physically plausible motion reconstruction for human-scene interaction, indicating that SHARE could serve as a foundational step for developing more sophisticated models that not only accurately place humans but also simulate realistic physical interactions with their environment.

**Limitations** acknowledged by the authors include:
- The current method assumes a stationary camera and a single human in the video, limiting its applicability to scenarios involving camera movement or multiple individuals.
- The accuracy of human reconstruction is dependent on the quality of initialization provided by pre-trained models like TRAM and MoGe-2.
- Potential for artifacts such as floor penetration due to mismatches between the human mesh size and the height information in the human point map.
- Discrepancies between the initial smoothed keyframe meshes and the currently optimized ones may introduce minor jitter at keyframes during the optimization process.
