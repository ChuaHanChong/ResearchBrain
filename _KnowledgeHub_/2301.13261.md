# Emergence of Maps in the Memories of Blind Navigation Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> AI agents trained to navigate using only egomotion sensing spontaneously construct internal spatial representations of their environment. These "blind" agents achieved 95.1% success and 62.9% SPL, demonstrating memory utilization over long horizons (1,000 steps) and enabling behaviors like shortcut taking and allocentric map decoding with 32.5% IoU, significantly outperforming untrained baselines.

## Problem

- The mechanisms behind the strong navigation performance of "map-free" neural network models remain unclear.
- It is unknown if artificial intelligent agents can spontaneously develop internal spatial representations, or cognitive maps, solely through navigation training.
- Understanding if mapping is a fundamental strategy for navigation in intelligent embodied agents, akin to biological systems, needed further investigation under controlled conditions.

## Method

- Researchers trained "blind" AI agents in a simulated 3D environment using ==reinforcement learning== for a point-goal navigation task.
- Agents were restricted to only egomotion sensing (relative position and heading changes) and utilized a generic LSTM-based recurrent neural network.
- The experimental setup deliberately excluded explicit mapping modules, direct mapping supervision, or architectural biases for spatial representation.

## Results

- "Blind" agents achieved a **95.1%** success rate and **62.9%** SPL in novel environments, demonstrating comparable performance to sighted agents while surpassing a clairvoyant baseline.
- Memory utilization was critical, with performance gains observed up to 1,000 steps of temporal context, equivalent to remembering information over approximately 89 meters.
- Probes with trained memory navigated more efficiently (**85.0%** vs **62.9%** SPL), taking shortcuts and free-space inference, and could predict allocentric occupancy maps at **32.5%** IoU (vs **12.5%** untrained), showing selective forgetting of detours.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective navigation is possible with only egomotion sensing, indicating that agents must rely on internal memory and representations.
> - The internal memory developed by trained agents encodes rich spatial information, enabling them to infer free space, take shortcuts, and navigate bidirectionally.
> - Challenging agents with limited sensory input (blindness) can compel the spontaneous emergence of decodable, allocentric spatial maps within generic recurrent neural network architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The study addresses a fundamental question at the intersection of animal cognition, robotics, and artificial intelligence: whether intelligent agents, particularly AI systems, spontaneously develop internal spatial representations, or maps, of their environment as a consequence of learning to navigate.

In animal navigation research, the hypothesis that organisms build and maintain internal spatial representations (cognitive maps) to facilitate navigation, path planning, and shortcut taking has been a central tenet for decades, supported by observations across various species. Similarly, in traditional robotics, explicit mapping and localization techniques (e.g., SLAM) have been fundamental for autonomous navigation.

More recently, the field of AI navigation has witnessed the emergence of neural network models that achieve high performance in navigation tasks without incorporating explicit mapping modules. These "map-free" or "pixels-to-actions" architectures, often comprising convolutional and recurrent neural networks, have demonstrated capabilities in novel environments that are not fully understood. The mechanisms underlying their performance remain an open question of both scientific and practical relevance.

This work positions itself as an investigation into these mechanisms. By exploring the emergence of implicit maps in AI agents, the paper aims to:
1. Provide an explanation for the observed high performance of ostensibly map-free neural networks.
2. Reinforce the theoretical perspective that mapping is a fundamental mechanism for navigation in intelligent embodied agents, whether biological or artificial.

The authors note that the controlled experimental environment of AI agents allows for isolating specific mechanisms, unlike animal studies. By designing an agent with deliberately impoverished sensing capabilities, the study seeks to eliminate alternative navigation strategies (such as reliance on visual landmarks or olfactory cues), thereby isolating the role of memory and internal spatial representation in navigation. The concept of "convergent evolution" is invoked to suggest that if mapping emerges independently in different intelligent systems (biological and artificial), it may indicate a natural and fundamental solution to the problem of navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether artificial intelligence (AI) agents learn to construct internal spatial representations, or "mental maps," of their environment as a natural outcome of being trained to navigate. This central question is motivated by two main points:

1. **Explaining "Map-Free" Neural Network Performance:** Recent literature has shown neural network models achieving strong navigation performance without explicit mapping modules. The mechanisms behind this capability are not well understood. The authors aim to determine if these "map-free" systems are, in fact, implicitly building and utilizing map-like representations. A positive answer would offer an explanation for this phenomenon.
2. **Strengthening Evidence for Mapping as a Fundamental Mechanism:** By demonstrating map emergence in artificial agents under controlled conditions, the work seeks to provide additional evidence that mapping is a fundamental cognitive process for intelligent embodied agents, applicable across biological and artificial domains.

To address this, the researchers adopted a specific experimental design to isolate mapping behavior from other potential navigation strategies. Key aspects of this design include:

- **"Blind" Agents:** The agents are equipped with an intentionally minimal perceptual system, limited solely to egomotion sensing (changes in their own location and orientation, akin to a GPS+Compass). This excludes any other sensory inputs such as vision, audio, olfaction, haptic, or magnetic fields. This isolation aims to prevent reliance on landmarks or other external cues and compel the agent to depend on memory and internal representations. This design is inspired by studies of blind animals like mole rats that use path integration.
- **Navigation-Agnostic Architecture:** The agents are composed of generic, ubiquitous architectural components (fully-connected layers and LSTM-based recurrent neural networks). This means no specialized "map-like" or "spatial structural components" are pre-built into the agent's design.
- **Absence of Inductive Bias towards Mapping:** The experimental setup provides no explicit supervisory signals for mapping, no auxiliary tasks designed to encourage map-building, and no inherent architectural biases that would directly promote the formation of spatial representations. The only learning signal is a reward for making progress towards a goal in a reinforcement learning paradigm.

Under these "harsh conditions," the paper aims to answer four specific research questions:

1. **Is effective navigation possible with only egomotion sensing?** This assesses the baseline performance of these minimally-equipped agents in novel environments.
2. **What mechanism accounts for strong performance in "blind" agents?** This investigates the role and extent of memory utilization by the agents.
3. **What information does the memory encode about the environment?** This is the core question regarding the emergence of implicit maps, explored through behaviors like shortcut taking and the explicit decoding of spatial information.
4. **Are emergent maps task-dependent?** This examines whether the agents selectively remember environmental features based on their current navigation goal, potentially "forgetting" irrelevant information like exploratory detours.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers employed a reinforcement learning framework within a simulated 3D environment to train and evaluate "blind" navigation agents.

**Agent Design and Sensing:**
- **Embodiment:** Agents are modeled as a cylinder (0.2m diameter, 1.5m height) within virtualized 3D replicas of real houses using the AI Habitat simulator and datasets (Gibson, Matterport3D).
- **Perceptual System:** The agents are "blind," equipped with an egomotion sensor (referred to as "GPS+Compass"). This sensor provides relative position (Δx, Δy, Δz) and relative heading (Δθ) between successive steps. This information is integrated to track the agent's current location and orientation relative to its starting point in an episodic coordinate system. No other sensory inputs (e.g., vision, audio, haptic) are provided.
- **Actions:** The agent can perform four discrete actions: move forward (0.25 meters), turn left (10°), turn right (10°), and stop (to signal goal achievement).
- **Architecture:** The agent's policy is parameterized by a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent neural network. It uses a 3-layer LSTM with a 512-dimensional hidden state. At each time step, observations (relative goal coordinates, current relative position, current relative heading, and a proximity-to-goal indicator) are projected via fully-connected layers, concatenated with a learned embedding of the previous action, and fed into the LSTM. The LSTM's output (internal memory representation) is then used by a fully-connected layer to produce action probabilities and a value function estimate.

**Task and Training:**
- **Task:** PointGoal navigation. The agent is initialized at a random location in a new environment and tasked with navigating to a specified relative 3D coordinate (x, y, z) within a maximum of 2,000 steps.
- **Reinforcement Learning:** Agents are trained using the Decentralized Distributed Proximal Policy Optimization (DD-PPO) algorithm.
- **Reward Function:** The reward structure encourages progress towards the goal and penalizes long episodes. Specifically, a positive reward is given for successfully stopping within 0.2 meters of the target. Otherwise, the agent receives a reward based on the negative change in geodesic distance to the goal, plus a small slack penalty per step.
- **Generalization:** Training is conducted across 411 Gibson and 72 Matterport3D scenes. Performance is evaluated in 18 previously unseen Matterport3D test scenes to assess generalization.
- **No Inductive Bias:** Neither the training procedure nor the agent's architecture contains explicit biases towards mapping or planning.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Success:** Binary metric indicating whether the agent successfully stopped within 0.2 meters of the target.
- **Success weighted by inverse Path Length (SPL):** Measures path efficiency by dividing the shortest possible path length by the agent's actual path length, weighted by success.

**Specific Experimental Analyses:**

1. **Memory Length Analysis:** To determine the temporal horizon of memory utilization, agents are evaluated with a restricted memory capacity (k steps). The LSTM's hidden state is reset or recomputed based only on the last `k` observations, starting from an all-zero state for observations older than `k`.
2. **Probe Experiments (Shortcut Taking):**
 - **Setup:** A primary "agent" navigates from a source (S) to a target (T). After the agent completes the task, a "probe" agent, structurally identical to the primary agent but with its initial memory initialized by the primary agent's final hidden state (h_T, c_T), is tasked with navigating the same S→T path (SecondNav(S→T)) or the reverse T→S path (SecondNav(T→S)).
 - **Controls:** Control probes include `AllZeroMemory` (empty initial memory) and `UntrainedAgentMemory` (memory generated by an untrained agent following the primary agent's path).
 - **Metrics:** SPL to quantify efficiency, and a Chamfer distance-based metric (`PathDiff`) to quantify "Excursion Removal" (how much of the agent's detours are skipped by the probe) and "Free Space Inference" (how much the probe travels through unexplored but navigable space).
3. **Occupancy Map Decoding:**
 - **Setup:** A separate decoding network is trained to predict an allocentric (top-down) occupancy grid (free-space vs. non-navigable) of the environment, using only the agent's final internal memory representation (h_T, c_T).
 - **Decoder Architecture:** The decoder uses a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) followed by Coordinate Convolution (CoordConv) and Up-Convolution layers to generate a 2D occupancy map.
 - **Evaluation:** Accuracy is measured using Intersection over Union (IoU) with the true occupancy grid. Predictions are limited to within 2.5 meters of locations the agent visited.
 - **Comparison:** Performance is compared between decoders trained on `TrainedAgentMemory` and `UntrainedAgentMemory`.
4. **Past Position Prediction and Excursion Forgetting:**
 - **Setup:** A decoder network is trained to predict the agent's location 'k' steps in the past from its current internal state (h_t, c_t).
 - **Evaluation:** Relative L2 error between predicted and true past locations.
 - **Excursion Analysis:** Episodes are manually labeled for "excursions" (sub-paths forming loops). The accuracy of past position prediction is analyzed in relation to whether the remembered position was part of an excursion.
5. **Collision Detection Neurons Analysis:**
 - **Setup:** A linear classifier is trained on the frozen internal representation (h_t, c_t) to predict whether the previous action (a_{t-1}) resulted in a collision.
 - **Analysis:** L1-regularization is used to identify sparse sets of neurons most predictive of collisions. t-SNE is then used to visualize the clustering of these neurons based on action type and collision status.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings concerning the capabilities and internal representations of "blind" navigation agents:

1. **Blind Agents are Effective Navigators:**
 - Despite their minimal egomotion-only sensing, the blind agents achieved a high success rate of 95.1% ± 1.3% in navigating to a point goal in novel environments.
 - Their paths were moderately efficient, with an SPL of 62.9% ± 1.6%. This performance significantly surpassed a "clairvoyant Bug" algorithm (46% SPL), which had privileged information about optimal wall-following direction. The success rate was comparable to sighted agents equipped with depth cameras.
 - **Emergent Behaviors:** Agents consistently exhibited wall-following behavior, a strategy hypothesized to emerge due to the lack of visual information about obstacles and the unknown nature of the environment (episodic coordinate system preventing memorization of global maps).
 - **Collision Detection:** The agent's internal memory contained sufficient information to detect collisions. A linear classifier trained on the agent's internal representation achieved 98% accuracy in predicting collisions. Analysis using t-SNE on sparse sets of collision-predictive neurons revealed four distinct clusters corresponding to the previous action taken and whether a collision occurred.

2. **Memory is Utilized Over Long Horizons:**
 - Memory was found to be critical for navigation. Agents with no memory (k=1) failed almost entirely (0% success).
 - Navigation performance, measured by both success and SPL, did not saturate until the memory capacity was around 1,000 steps. This corresponds to the agent remembering information over an average distance of 89.1 ± 0.66 meters, indicating long-term temporal and spatial information storage.

3. **Memory Enables Shortcuts and Supports Bidirectional Navigation:**
 - **Probe Experiments:** When a "probe" agent was initialized with the final memory state of a trained agent, it navigated the same S→T task significantly more efficiently (85.0% ± 0.16% SPL) compared to the original agent (62.9% ± 1.6% SPL). This gain in efficiency was attributed to the probe taking shortcuts and eliminating exploratory excursions made by the initial agent.
 - **Learned Behavior:** Control experiments demonstrated that this improved performance was a result of learned representations. Probes initialized with either empty memory or memory from an untrained agent showed no significant improvement over the baseline, suggesting that the ability to construct useful spatial representations from a trajectory is a learned phenomenon.
 - **Bidirectional Utility:** The emergent memory representations were equally effective for navigating the reverse path (T→S), achieving similar SPL (84.8% ± 0.22%), indicating that the spatial information is not directionally constrained.
 - **Free Space Inference:** Analysis showed that probes with inherited memory not only removed excursions but also traveled through previously unexplored parts of the environment (e.g., cutting across open spaces), suggesting the internal representations include anticipatory inferences about navigable free space.

4. **Learning Navigation Improves Metric Map Decoding:**
 - A separate decoding network trained to predict an allocentric top-down occupancy grid from the agent's final memory state revealed that the `TrainedAgentMemory` enabled significantly more accurate map predictions (32.5% average IoU) compared to `UntrainedAgentMemory` (12.5% average IoU).
 - The decoder was capable of making predictions about unvisited locations, for example, inferring the presence of a parallel wall when the agent traveled alongside one, suggesting a corridor.
 - *Supplementary finding:* In contrast, similar analyses on sighted agents (equipped with depth cameras) did not provide conclusive evidence for metric map decoding. The authors conjecture that the challenging nature of the "blind" navigation problem, with fewer "trivial solutions," may have induced the emergence of map-building strategies.

5. **Mapping is Task-Dependent: Agent Forgets Excursions:**
 - Analysis of past position prediction showed that the agent's memory selectively preserves information. Excursions (exploratory detours that form loops) were more difficult to decode from memory than non-excursion paths, suggesting the agent "forgets" irrelevant detours.
 - Interestingly, the "exit" points of excursions were easier to decode, potentially serving as salient landmarks within the agent's memory.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable finding regarding the capabilities of artificial intelligence systems in embodied navigation. Its significance and potential impact span theoretical understanding and practical applications:

**Scientific Contribution:**
- **Existence Proof for Emergent Mapping:** The study provides an "existence proof" or an ontogenetic developmental account for the emergence of sophisticated internal spatial representations (maps) in AI agents. This occurs solely through goal-driven reinforcement learning, without any pre-existing architectural biases towards mapping or direct supervision for map construction. This finding suggests that mapping may be a natural and fundamental solution strategy for intelligent embodied agents facing navigation challenges, whether biological or artificial, analogous to convergent evolution in biological systems (e.g., the independent evolution of eyes).
- **Bridging Biological and Artificial Intelligence:** The work establishes a conceptual link between decades of research in animal navigation (positing cognitive maps) and modern deep reinforcement learning. By showing that artificial agents, much like certain biological organisms (e.g., blind mole rats), can build and leverage maps from egomotion alone, it reinforces the universality of mapping as a mechanism for intelligent navigation.

**Explanation of "Map-Free" AI Navigation:**
- A key impact is providing an explanation for the high performance observed in "map-free" neural network navigation agents. The research suggests that these ostensibly map-free systems do not lack spatial representations but rather learn to implicitly construct and maintain map-like internal representations within their generic memory modules (LSTMs). This understanding clarifies how these agents achieve generalization and intelligent behaviors in novel environments.

**Implications for Future AI Navigation Research:**
- **Informed Architectural Design:** The findings suggest that future AI navigation research could benefit from designing agents with explicit or implicit priors that encourage or facilitate the learning of internal representations similar to the map-like features identified here. This might involve architectural modifications or specific training regimes/auxiliary objectives.
- **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** A deeper understanding of these emergent maps could lead to more robust, efficient, and generalizable navigation agents capable of tackling more complex embodied AI tasks, such as object rearrangement in novel environments.
- **Understanding Learning Mechanisms:** The observation that "blind" agents develop maps while "sighted" agents (in supplementary experiments) did not show conclusive evidence of metric map decoding, points to an interesting hypothesis: that creating a sufficiently challenging learning problem (e.g., by limiting sensory input) might be critical for inducing the emergence of higher-level strategies like map-building, preventing the model from relying on simpler, "trivial" solutions.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point to future research avenues:
- **Generalization to Complex Environments:** The current work focuses on 2D manifold navigation in indoor environments. Generalizing these findings to 3D navigation (e.g., drones in forests) or more complex, dynamic, and unstructured environments is an open question.
- **Sensor Noise and Actuation Noise:** The current setup assumes idealized sensors and deterministic actuations. Incorporating noise, a crucial aspect of real-world robotics and biological path integration, would be a valuable extension.
- **Alternative Mapping Mechanisms:** The study primarily explores implicit mapping via LSTMs. Future work could investigate agents with differentiable read/write map architectures without direct mapping supervision.
- **Long-Term Memory and Exploration:** The agents' memory is reset per episode. Investigating map formation and utilization over much longer deployment periods, akin to animal and robot experiences, could reveal different or more sophisticated mapping strategies.
- **Mechanistic Account:** While showing *what* emerges, the paper does not provide a complete mechanistic account of *how* these map-like representations are specifically encoded and processed within the neural network. Further work could explore connections to biological concepts like grid cells.
