# Gated Memory Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed the Gated Memory Policy (GMP), a Transformer-based Diffusion Policy enhanced with a learned memory gate, cross-attention with cached tokens, and diffusion noise augmentation for historical actions. This policy significantly improves performance on non-Markovian robotic manipulation tasks, achieving a 30.1% average success rate improvement over long-history baselines, while maintaining efficiency and robustness on Markovian tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation policies struggle with non-Markovian tasks requiring long-term memory, either within a single interaction trial or across multiple trials.
- Naively extending observation histories in visuomotor policies often leads to performance degradation due to overfitting, increased input dimensionality, distribution shifts, and high computational costs (quadratic scaling of self-attention).
- Existing memory-based approaches are often task-dependent, require extensive manual engineering, or lack efficient and fine-grained action-observation alignment within visuomotor policies.

## Method

- Introduced the **Gated Memory Policy** (GMP), which incorporates a learned binary memory gate to adaptively activate history conditioning only when required, improving efficiency and robustness.
- Implemented a ==cross-attention== module with cached tokens and Multi-Head Attention Pooling (MAP) for visual features, enabling efficient conditioning on long history sequences with linear scaling of computation.
- Augmented training by injecting ==diffusion==-scheduled noise into historical actions, both during training and inference, to enhance robustness against noisy or inaccurate histories.

## Results

- GMP achieved an average **30.1%** success rate improvement over long-history baselines on the proposed MemMimic benchmark for non-Markovian tasks, with high success rates up to **98.7%** on cross-trial memory tasks.
- The policy maintained competitive performance on Markovian tasks (RoboMimic), demonstrating that the memory mechanisms do not degrade performance when memory is unnecessary.
- Showcased low computational cost, with inference scaling linearly with history length when memory is active, and constant time when the gate is off, leading to faster execution compared to quadratic scaling baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An adaptively learned memory gate can prevent over-reliance on irrelevant historical information, improving generalization and computational efficiency by engaging memory only when truly beneficial for a task.
> - Self-supervised calibration of the memory gate, based on comparing action prediction errors of policies with and without memory, is effective for training the gate to identify critical memory-dependent timesteps without overfitting.
> - Injecting diffusion noise into historical actions consistently during training and inference significantly improves policy robustness against noisy real-world observations and accumulated errors over time.
