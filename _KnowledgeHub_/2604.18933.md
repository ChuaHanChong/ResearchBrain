# Gated Memory Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed the Gated Memory Policy (GMP), a Transformer-based Diffusion Policy enhanced with a learned memory gate, cross-attention with cached tokens, and diffusion noise augmentation for historical actions. This policy significantly improves performance on non-Markovian robotic manipulation tasks, achieving a 30.1% average success rate improvement over long-history baselines, while maintaining efficiency and robustness on Markovian tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation policies struggle with non-Markovian tasks requiring long-term memory, either within a single interaction trial or across multiple trials.
- Naively extending observation histories in visuomotor policies often leads to performance degradation due to overfitting, increased input dimensionality, distribution shifts, and high computational costs (quadratic scaling of self-attention).
- Existing memory-based approaches are often task-dependent, require extensive manual engineering, or lack efficient and fine-grained action-observation alignment within visuomotor policies.

## Method

- Introduced the **Gated Memory Policy** (GMP), which incorporates a learned binary memory gate to adaptively activate history conditioning only when required, improving efficiency and robustness.
- Implemented a ==cross-attention== module with cached tokens and Multi-Head Attention Pooling (MAP) for visual features, enabling efficient conditioning on long history sequences with linear scaling of computation.
- Augmented training by injecting ==diffusion==-scheduled noise into historical actions, both during training and inference, to enhance robustness against noisy or inaccurate histories.

## Results

- GMP achieved an average **30.1%** success rate improvement over long-history baselines on the proposed MemMimic benchmark for non-Markovian tasks, with high success rates up to **98.7%** on cross-trial memory tasks.
- The policy maintained competitive performance on Markovian tasks (RoboMimic), demonstrating that the memory mechanisms do not degrade performance when memory is unnecessary.
- Showcased low computational cost, with inference scaling linearly with history length when memory is active, and constant time when the gate is off, leading to faster execution compared to quadratic scaling baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An adaptively learned memory gate can prevent over-reliance on irrelevant historical information, improving generalization and computational efficiency by engaging memory only when truly beneficial for a task.
> - Self-supervised calibration of the memory gate, based on comparing action prediction errors of policies with and without memory, is effective for training the gate to identify critical memory-dependent timesteps without overfitting.
> - Injecting diffusion noise into historical actions consistently during training and inference significantly improves policy robustness against noisy real-world observations and accumulated errors over time.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks exhibit a spectrum of memory requirements, ranging from tasks that are largely Markovian and require minimal historical information to complex non-Markovian tasks that necessitate recalling information over a single interaction trial (in-trial memory) or across multiple trials (cross-trial memory). Previous efforts to enable memory in visuomotor policies have explored various strategies, each with limitations that this work seeks to address.

**Existing Approaches and Their Limitations:**
Prior memory-based policies often fall into two categories: structured memory and generalizable long-context policies.
- **Structured memory approaches** typically involve manual design, such as storing keyframe heatmaps, referencing object trajectories, or utilizing visual trace overlays. More advanced methods leverage semantic or latent representations to estimate environment dynamics, generate textual plans via Large Language Models (LLMs), or retrieve embeddings from vector databases. While successful in specific contexts, these methods are often task-dependent, require significant manual engineering, and can be time-consuming to deploy.
- **Generalizable long-context visuomotor policies** have been a subject of ongoing research. Early attempts used Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), but more recent studies suggest that shorter or no history often performs better on Markovian tasks. For non-Markovian tasks, including in-context learning and adaptation, longer context is essential. Modern approaches to scale context length involve external memory banks, keyframe selection, attention aggregation, noise injection into history tokens, and auxiliary training objectives like predicting past actions or future videos. However, these methods frequently present trade-offs:
 - Including memory only in high-level Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can lack fine-grained action-observation alignment, hindering the inference of complex object dynamics and iterative action refinement.
 - Additional generation objectives can slow down policy inference, thereby impairing reactivity.
 - Naive extension of observation histories often leads to overfitting due to increased input dimensionality and exacerbates distribution shift when encountering noisy or unfamiliar observations at inference time. This also incurs significant computational overhead, as self-attention mechanisms scale quadratically with history length.

**Gated Networks for Temporal Sequence Modeling:**
The concept of gated architectures is fundamental in temporal sequence modeling, seen in classical recurrent models like LSTMs and GRUs, which regulate information flow across time. Extensions to the Transformer paradigm, such as GTrXL and more recent state-space models like Mamba, also incorporate gating mechanisms for efficient and robust sequence modeling. Gated attention mechanisms have been proposed to dynamically modulate attention weights for long-context tasks. This research builds upon this foundation by integrating a learned gating mechanism into a visuomotor policy to trigger memory retrieval selectively.

**Contribution of GMP:**
The Gated Memory Policy (GMP) aims to synthesize the benefits of long-context understanding with the efficiency and robustness required for practical robotic manipulation. It addresses the critical questions of *when* to recall memory and *what* information to recall, striving to overcome the limitations of prior work by offering a task-agnostic, efficient, and robust approach to memory-enabled policy learning. The introduction of the MemMimic benchmark further contributes to the landscape by providing a dedicated suite for evaluating long-term memory in robotics, particularly focusing on in-trial and cross-trial memory tasks, which are often overlooked.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a visuomotor policy capable of effectively handling diverse memory requirements in robotic manipulation tasks, ranging from simple Markovian scenarios to complex non-Markovian ones requiring prolonged in-trial or cross-trial memory. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current robotic manipulation policies:

1. **Performance Degradation with Naive History Extension:** While extending observation histories might seem a direct way to enable non-Markovian behaviors, it frequently leads to a significant performance drop, even on simpler Markovian tasks. This is primarily due to the substantial increase in input dimensionality, which heightens the risk of overfitting during training.
2. **Distribution Shift and Overfitting:** Longer observation histories amplify the problem of distribution shift between training and deployment. The presence of minor noise or unfamiliar observations in a few historical frames can shift the entire input sequence out of distribution, making the policy unreliable.
3. **High Computational Overhead:** Attending to long histories, especially cross-trial memory, incurs considerable computational costs. In Transformer-based architectures, self-attention scales quadratically with history length, making both training and inference inefficient and expensive.
4. **Inefficient Memory Recall:** For many tasks, memory is only critical at specific moments. Conditioning on history constantly can distract the policy from the current observation, slow down inference, and reduce reactivity. There is a need for a mechanism to selectively recall memory only when necessary.
5. **Difficulty in Identifying Relevant Information:** In complex manipulation tasks, it is challenging to explicitly specify what information from history should be stored and retrieved. Policies need to learn to identify and leverage relevant visual and action history efficiently without explicit supervision.

To address these challenges, the Gated Memory Policy (GMP) was developed with three specific objectives:

- **To learn *when* to recall memory:** GMP aims to adaptively regulate information flow between historical context and current observations using a learned memory gate. This gate selectively activates history context only when needed, enhancing robustness and reactivity by preventing over-reliance on past observations and reducing unnecessary computation.
- **To learn *what* to recall efficiently:** GMP incorporates a lightweight cross-attention module designed to focus on the most relevant history without requiring additional supervision. This module reduces redundant computation inherent in processing entire history sequences.
- **To further enhance robustness to history noise:** GMP aims to mitigate sensitivity to noisy or inaccurate historical information by injecting diffusion noise into historical actions during training. This strategy makes the policy more robust to accumulated errors during inference.

By achieving these objectives, the researchers aim to create a practical, effective, and memory-focused imitation learning framework that generalizes across tasks with varying memory requirements while maintaining high performance and computational efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Gated Memory Policy (GMP) is built upon a Transformer-based Diffusion Policy backbone and introduces specific architectural and training modifications to enable efficient and robust memory recall.

**A. Background: Transformer-based Diffusion Policy**
The foundation is a Diffusion Policy, which takes the current image observation ($I_t$) and robot proprioception ($P_t$) to predict a trajectory of future actions ($A_{t:t+h}$). During training, Gaussian noise is progressively added to ground-truth actions, and a denoising network ($\phi_\theta$) is trained to predict the original action. Actions are denoised using a DDIM scheduler at test time. Standard Diffusion Policies typically condition on very short histories, limiting their ability to handle memory-dependent tasks. Naively extending this history leads to overfitting and high computational costs (quadratic scaling of self-attention).

**B. Gated Memory Policy (GMP)**
GMP addresses the limitations of naive history extension through three core components:

1. **Cross-Attention with Cached Tokens for History Conditioning:**
 - To manage the computational cost of long histories, GMP avoids concatenating all historical tokens into a single sequence for self-attention. Instead, it employs a separate cross-attention module for history conditioning.
 - Historical actions are encoded as sequences of `h` actions per chunk: {A_{t-nh:t-(n-1)h}..., A_{t-h:t}}.
 - Historical images are sparsely sampled (e.g., one image per `h` action steps): {I_{t-nh:h:t}...}. These images are processed by a pretrained ViT encoder, and Multi-Head Attention Pooling (MAP) aggregates image patches into single tokens, significantly reducing the number of visual tokens.
 - Inspired by KV caches in causal Transformers, GMP caches a sliding window of `n` history trajectories (each comprising an aggregated image feature and `h` action tokens). This allows retrieving history tokens without additional re-computation, contributing to efficiency.

2. **Diffusion Noise Augmentation to Reduce Overfitting:**
 - To prevent overfitting to clean training histories and improve generalization, GMP injects scheduled diffusion noise into historical actions during training.
 - At a given diffusion step `k`, the model predicts future actions ($A^k_{t:t+h}$) conditioned on history actions that are "one step cleaner" ($A^{k-1}_{t-nh:t}$). This creates a noise schedule where the noise level decreases as denoising progresses.
 - Strong noise is applied in early diffusion steps to provide substantial augmentation, preventing overfitting. Minimal noise is applied in later steps to allow the policy to capture fine-grained historical information.
 - Crucially, this noise injection is applied consistently during both training and inference, enhancing robustness to noisy or inaccurate histories encountered during deployment.

3. **Memory Gating for On-Demand Memory Recall:**
 - Many tasks do not require constant memory access; always conditioning on history can be distracting and computationally wasteful. GMP introduces a learned binary memory gate ($\mu_t$) to adaptively modulate the contribution of history.
 - The gate is produced by an MLP ($\phi$) with a sigmoid activation, conditioned on the current image ($I_t$) and proprioception ($P_t$): $\mu_t = 1\{\sigma(\phi(I_t, P_t)) > 0.5\}$.
 - The gate directly controls whether the output of the history cross-attention module ($h_{t:t+h}$) is added to the residual connection ($z_{t:t+h}$) before being passed to the feed-forward layer.
 - **Memory Gate Calibration:** To train the gate effectively without pitfalls like overfitting or underutilization, a self-supervised calibration process is employed:
 - The dataset is split into training ($D_{train}$) and validation ($D_{val}$).
 - Two policies are trained on $D_{train}$: $\pi$ (memory gate always off) and $\pi_{mem}$ (memory gate always on).
 - These policies are evaluated on $D_{val}$ to compute action prediction errors ($\delta_t$ for $\pi$, $\delta^t_{mem}$ for $\pi_{mem}$).
 - A binary memory gate label ($\mu_t=1$) is assigned if $\delta_t$ is significantly larger (by a ratio threshold $\theta$) than $\delta^t_{mem}$, indicating a timestep where memory is beneficial. Otherwise, $\mu_t=0$.
 - The memory gate's MLP ($\phi$) is then trained separately using Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss on these generated labels.
 - Finally, the memory gate weights are frozen, and the complete GMP ($\pi_{gated}$) is retrained on the full dataset (D_{train} and D_{val}). This ensures the policy leverages historical information only when deemed necessary by the calibrated gate.

**C. Benchmark Tasks:**
To rigorously evaluate GMP, the authors propose **MemMimic**, a new benchmark suite featuring non-Markovian tasks requiring both in-trial (e.g., Match Color, Discrete/Continuous Place Back) and cross-trial memory (e.g., Iterative Pushing, Flinging, Casting) in both simulation and real-world settings. GMP is also evaluated on **RoboMimic** (Markovian tasks like Square, Transport, Tool Hang) to verify that memory mechanisms do not degrade performance where memory is not required, and on **MIKASA-Robo** (existing memory-intensive benchmarks) for comparison with VLA baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Gated Memory Policy (GMP) against several baselines across Markovian and non-Markovian tasks in both simulation and real-world environments yielded several key findings:

1. **Performance Degradation with Naive History Extension:** The research confirmed that simply increasing the history length of standard Diffusion Policy (DP) or Past-Token-Prediction (PTP) policies generally degrades performance on Markovian tasks (e.g., RoboMimic benchmarks). For instance, "Long-hist DP" (120 history steps) performed significantly worse than "Mid-hist DP" (16 history steps) and "No-hist DP" on RoboMimic tasks. This suggests that expanding the input space without selective processing leads to overfitting and generalization issues.

2. **Superior Performance on Non-Markovian Tasks:** GMP achieved substantial improvements on non-Markovian tasks, demonstrating a 30.1% average success rate improvement over long-history baselines on the proposed MemMimic benchmark.
 - **In-trial Memory Tasks:** On tasks like "Match Color," "Discrete Place Back," and "Continuous Place Back," GMP consistently outperformed baselines. For "Match Color," GMP achieved 100% success where naive long-history policies also succeeded, but crucially, it maintained this performance efficiently. For complex real-world tasks like "Continuous Place Back" and "In-the-wild Flip and Place Back," GMP showed robustness, achieving a 95% success rate for "Continuous Place Back" compared to 0% for long-history baselines due to stability issues.
 - **Cross-trial Memory Tasks:** GMP demonstrated strong in-context adaptation capabilities. On "Iterative Pushing," "Iterative Flinging," and "Iterative Casting," GMP achieved success rates of 98.7%, 97.3%, and 95%, respectively, significantly outperforming long-history DP (31.7%, 43.3%, 20%) and long-history PTP (33.3%, 42.7%). This indicates GMP's ability to learn from past trial outcomes and iteratively refine actions.
 - **MIKASA-Robo Tasks:** GMP outperformed existing VLA baselines on MIKASA-Robo benchmarks by an average of 26.6%, further validating its effectiveness on memory-intensive tasks.

3. **Competitive Performance on Markovian Tasks:** Despite incorporating memory mechanisms, GMP maintained competitive performance on Markovian tasks from the RoboMimic benchmark (Fig. 11). This was attributed to the learned memory gate mechanism, which correctly identifies when memory recall is unnecessary.

4. **Selective Memory Recall and Modality Attention:** Visualizations of cross-attention weights demonstrated that GMP selectively attends to task-relevant history at critical moments. For example, in "Match Color," attention focused on the specific timestep when bin colors were first observed. In "Iterative Pushing," attention in later trials focused on outcomes (overshoot/undershoot) from previous trials, enabling adaptive adjustments (Fig. 4, 8). The memory gate was found to be off for a significant portion of the task (e.g., 73% in "Match Color," 58% in "Iterative Pushing"), indicating efficient, on-demand activation.

5. **Effectiveness of Memory Gate Calibration:** The self-supervised calibration process for the binary memory gate was identified as crucial. Jointly training a binary gate without regularization led to overfitting (gate always on), degrading Markovian performance. With regularization, a trade-off emerged, making it difficult to find a universally effective weight. The independent calibration on a validation set allowed GMP to accurately identify and label critical moments requiring historical information, avoiding these issues (Fig. 13).

6. **Computational Efficiency:** GMP demonstrated low computational cost. Its use of a cross-attention module scales linearly with history length when the memory gate is active, contrasting with the quadratic scaling of self-attention baselines. More significantly, when the memory gate is off, GMP entirely skips history attention, leading to a minimal and constant inference time, resulting in faster execution (Fig. 14).

7. **Robustness from Diffusion Noise Augmentation:** The proposed strategy of injecting diffusion-scheduled noise into history actions during both training and testing significantly improved policy robustness. Comparisons on "Iterative Pushing" showed that "Diffusion Noising" outperformed alternatives like "No Noise," "Random Level," or "Diffusion Forcing," which either led to over-reliance on clean histories or inconsistency between training and testing (Fig. 15).

8. **Scalability of Cross-Attention for Long Histories:** GMP's cross-attention mechanism proved scalable for long history lengths. On "Match Color with Random Delay," it achieved a 99.0% success rate with a memory buffer of 6000 image frames and 6000 actions, completing inference in 0.16s, indicating its capability for extensive short-term memory tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Gated Memory Policy (GMP) represents a notable advancement in visuomotor policy learning for robotic manipulation, addressing several fundamental challenges associated with integrating long-term memory. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging Memory Requirements:** GMP offers a unified policy framework that can effectively operate across a wide spectrum of memory requirements, from Markovian tasks needing no memory to complex non-Markovian tasks demanding extensive in-trial or cross-trial memory. This capability reduces the need for task-specific policy designs, simplifying the development and deployment of robotic systems.

2. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** By employing a learned memory gate and diffusion noise augmentation for historical actions, GMP significantly improves policy robustness. The memory gate prevents over-reliance on potentially irrelevant past information, mitigating overfitting and improving generalization, particularly on tasks where naive long-history policies struggle with distribution shifts. The noise injection strategy further contributes to robustness against real-world noise and inaccuracies in historical data.

3. **Improved Computational Efficiency:** The design of GMP, particularly its cross-attention module with cached tokens and the conditional activation by the memory gate, leads to substantial computational efficiency. The linear scaling of computation with history length (when active) and the ability to completely bypass history processing (when inactive) result in faster inference times and reduced resource consumption, making GMP more practical for real-time robotic control.

4. **Enabling Advanced Manipulation Behaviors:** GMP's demonstrated ability to handle cross-trial memory tasks, such as iterative pushing, flinging, and casting, is critical for tasks requiring adaptation and trial-and-error learning. This capability allows robots to infer unobservable physical properties of objects and environments and refine their actions over multiple interactions, paving the way for more sophisticated and intelligent robotic behaviors in unstructured environments.

5. **New Benchmark for Memory-Intensive Tasks:** The introduction of the MemMimic benchmark addresses a gap in the evaluation of visuomotor policies, providing a rigorous suite specifically designed to test in-trial and cross-trial memory capabilities. This benchmark will likely serve as a valuable resource for the research community, driving further advancements in memory-enabled robot learning.

6. **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The empirical studies conducted in both simulation and real-world settings, including "in-the-wild" scenarios, underscore GMP's practical utility. Its ability to perform consistently in diverse and challenging real-world conditions suggests its potential for deployment in various applications, from industrial automation to service robotics.

7. **Foundational for Future Research:** The concepts introduced by GMP, such as self-supervised memory gate calibration and selective cross-attention, provide a foundation for future research. Potential avenues include exploring more sophisticated selective caching strategies to handle "infinite" memory contexts within fixed attention budgets, and incorporating additional modalities (e.g., task semantics) to refine memory retrieval criteria beyond just action prediction error, especially for ambiguous or uncertain tasks. This work opens doors for developing even more adaptive, autonomous, and context-aware robotic systems.
