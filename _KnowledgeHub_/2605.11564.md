# RIO: Flexible Real-Time Robot I/O for Cross-Embodiment Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> RIO is an open-source, Python-based framework providing flexible, real-time robot input/output for cross-embodiment robot learning, using a modular architecture for hardware-agnostic control. It achieved a significantly reduced observation-to-action latency of 130.3 ms, compared to 581.2 ms for an existing framework, and successfully deployed various learning policies on diverse robot platforms.

## Problem

- Fragmented infrastructure in robotics creates high engineering overhead for deploying modern robot learning models, like Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, on new and diverse hardware.
- Existing robot control stacks often lack flexible abstractions, leading to platform-specific code that hinders reusability and sharing of policies and datasets across different robot embodiments.
- The process of collecting standardized, high-quality teleoperated demonstration data across multiple robot platforms is often complex and inconsistent.

## Method

- **RIO** provides a Python-based, open-source framework built around a flexible ==Node and Middleware architecture==, allowing users to interchange robot hardware, sensors, teleoperation interfaces, and policies with minimal configuration changes.
- It implements ==asynchronous policy inference== and utilizes high-performance middlewares (e.g., ==Shared Memory==) for low-latency, real-time robot control, distributing blocking operations to dedicated nodes.
- The framework facilitates consistent data collection by enforcing standardized units and an ==RLDS-style format==, supporting ==morphologies== for abstract state descriptions across diverse robots.

## Results

- RIO demonstrated an end-to-end observation-to-action latency of **130.3 ms** during policy rollouts, substantially lower than LeRobot's **581.2 ms**, contributing to a higher effective control frequency.
- Successfully deployed diverse policies (VLAs, Diffusion Policy, RL) on single-arm (xArm7), bimanual (SO-100), and humanoid (Unitree G1, Booster T1) robots, achieving task success rates **above 90%** for VLA tasks on single-arm manipulation.
- A new, previously unsupported robot arm (AgileX PiPER) was integrated into RIO in under an hour with the aid of a coding agent, demonstrating the framework's flexibility and ease of extending hardware support.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A modular 'Node' and 'Middleware' architecture enables hardware-agnostic application logic, allowing the same control code to function across various robot stations by solely modifying configuration.
> - Achieving low observation-to-action latency and smooth real-time robot control is critical and can be realized through asynchronous policy inference and efficient middleware selection for communication.
> - Standardizing data formats and state representations across diverse robot morphologies is essential for streamlining data collection, fine-tuning, and deployment in cross-embodiment learning scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge within the field of robotics, specifically concerning the deployment and scalability of modern robot learning techniques, such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Recent advancements in VLA models have shown promise for developing generalist robot policies by leveraging large-scale, cross-embodiment datasets. These models aim to adapt pre-trained vision-language models to predict robot actions through imitation learning, building on foundational work in visuomotor policy learning.

Despite the theoretical potential of VLAs to learn broadly capable behaviors, their practical deployment on new robot morphologies and platforms remains challenging. Current VLA model checkpoints are often fine-tuned for specific robot setups, necessitating either a precise replication of the original hardware or substantial engineering effort to adapt them to different platforms. This limits their out-of-the-box usability and hinders the dissemination of learned policies across the community.

The paper identifies a broader issue of fragmented infrastructure within robotics. Diverse robot morphologies, sensor configurations, hardware platforms, and manufacturer-specific driver code result in highly specialized infrastructure. This specialization creates significant overhead when researchers attempt to reuse code, share datasets, or build upon existing work. While initiatives like Open X-Embodiment aggregate large datasets from multiple embodiments, the heterogeneity of collection techniques and sensor configurations still requires extensive curation.

Existing robot control stacks often aim for cross-embodiment control, but many, such as ROS, introduce complexity with distributed communication and high barriers to entry. Other frameworks like LeRobot offer more accessible Python-based implementations, yet the broader landscape often lacks flexible abstractions that allow for easy interchangeability of components across the entire stack. This fragmentation stems from roboticists often working with single platforms, leading to vendor-specific code over abstract solutions, and a lack of frameworks offering comprehensive flexibility at every layer.

RIO aims to provide the missing foundational components for hardware and control, analogous to specialized GPU kernels in high-level auto-differentiation frameworks. It seeks to offer reusable building blocks with flexible abstractions, enabling researchers to deploy, benchmark, and iterate on robot learning approaches across diverse hardware without extensive re-engineering.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing RIO is to address the fragmented infrastructure prevalent in the field of robotics, which is identified as a significant impediment to advancing cumulative progress and collaboration, particularly in the context of generalist robot learning. The researchers observe that despite efforts to develop multi-task, multi-embodiment datasets and VLA models, the practical deployment and adaptation of these models on diverse robot platforms remain difficult and resource-intensive.

The paper outlines several key objectives to counter this fragmentation:

- **Develop a Flexible Real-Time Robot I/O Framework:** The core objective is to create RIO as an open-source, Python-based framework designed for flexible, real-time input/output operations on robots. This framework is intended to be lightweight and composed of reusable components.
- **Provide Reusable Building Blocks:** RIO aims to offer modular infrastructure for fundamental robot operations, including control, teleoperation, data formatting, sensor configuration, and policy deployment. The emphasis is on building blocks that can be quickly combined and modified.
- **Enable Cross-Embodiment Flexibility:** A critical goal is to allow users to make choices at every layer of the robot learning stack—covering robot morphologies (e.g., single-arm, bimanual, humanoid), specific robot hardware (arms, grippers), teleoperation interfaces, cameras, middlewares, data formats, and policies—and to switch between these options with minimal reconfiguration effort. This aims to abstract away hardware specifics from control logic.
- **Facilitate Data Collection for Policy Fine-tuning:** The framework is designed to streamline the collection of high-quality teleoperated demonstration data across multiple embodiments. This data is crucial for fine-tuning state-of-the-art VLAs and other robot learning policies.
- **Support Effective Policy Deployment:** RIO seeks to provide a robust and performant environment for deploying trained policies on real-world robot hardware, ensuring smooth robot trajectories and efficient utilization of computational resources.
- **Promote Open Science and Community Collaboration:** By open-sourcing RIO, the authors intend to lower the barrier to entry for robotics practitioners, thereby accelerating the pace of research, benchmarking, and iteration on modern robot learning approaches.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several observed limitations in current robotics practices:
1. **High Engineering Overhead:** Deploying VLA models or any robot learning policy on a new robot setup typically demands substantial engineering effort, often requiring the recreation of an entire control stack.
2. **Platform-Specific Code:** Most existing robot code is highly specific to the exact hardware setup, making it difficult to reuse, share, or adapt across different platforms without significant rewriting.
3. **Lack of Abstractions:** Existing frameworks are often criticized for lacking flexible abstractions at every layer of the stack, which perpetuates the cycle of writing platform-specific code.
4. **Growing Cost of Fragmentation:** As robot hardware becomes more diverse and accessible, the specialized nature of infrastructure translates into increasing integration overhead for each new platform.

By addressing these issues, RIO aims to provide a standardized, yet flexible, foundation that allows research to focus more on policy development and less on infrastructure engineering.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RIO is presented as a Python-based framework designed for flexible, real-time Robot I/O. Its architecture and implementation are guided by five core design tenets: flexibility, reusability, accessibility, performance, and consistency.

**Design Philosophy:**
- **Flexible:** RIO is designed to be agnostic to specific components, allowing users to choose and switch between different robot hardware, sensors, teleoperation interfaces, middlewares, data formats, and policies with minimal effort.
- **Reusable:** The framework is built from a set of lightweight, modular building blocks that can be combined and modified for various configurations.
- **Accessible:** It aims for ease of installation and use, featuring Python-based hardware modules and a command-line interface controlled by a single configuration file.
- **Performant:** RIO is developed to support high-frequency real-time robot control, incorporating asynchronous policy inference for smooth robot trajectories.
- **Consistent:** The framework prioritizes consistent, scalable, and reproducible data collection and robot learning workflows.

This philosophy supports the overarching goal that abstract user logic should be written once and be reusable across any robot station, with hardware changes managed through configuration rather than code modification.

**Nodes and Middlewares:**
RIO's architecture is centered around the `Node` abstraction. Nodes serve as a template for implementing components such as teleoperation interfaces, sensors, robots, and policies. Each Node dynamically inherits from a specified `Middleware` class, which handles message passing transparently.

- **Node Execution Patterns:** Nodes support three primary execution patterns: `pub` for publishing data, `req` for handling requests, and `pubreq` for a combined approach. Complementary operations can be run in separate worker threads.
- **Data Flow:** Published data streams through a ring buffer, providing time-synchronized access to states (e.g., sensor readings, robot poses) at a fixed frequency. Requests are managed by a queue, enabling asynchronous, timestamped command communication from multiple clients.
- **API and Synchronization:** Each Node defines a public API (`__api__`), whose methods are automatically wrapped for serialization and deserialization, facilitating transparent bidirectional communication. Process synchronization is managed via `ready` and `exit` events, ensuring proper initialization and cleanup.
- **Middlewares:** Middlewares provide the underlying transport mechanism for Node communication. They abstract away transport-level details, allowing Nodes to remain middleware-agnostic.
 - **Shared Memory:** Used for high-throughput local communication, allocating ring buffers and request queues in shared memory for zero-copy data exchange.
 - **Zenoh/ZeroRpc:** Network-based middlewares for distributed communication over TCP or IPC.
 - **Thread:** A debugging middleware for running all components within a single process, simplifying stack traces and exception handling.
 - Middlewares can be interchanged based on performance, distribution, or debugging requirements.

**Robot Stations:**
A robot station is defined by a composable dataclass configuration that specifies the hardware topology (e.g., `arm`, `gripper`, `wrist_camera`). This configuration simplifies robot control loops by aggregating all necessary nodes for an environment. A context manager initializes server Nodes with the chosen middleware backend, while client Nodes provide proxy objects for transparent interaction. This approach allows the same application logic to operate over different hardware configurations without modification. Table II lists currently supported hardware, including various humanoid robots, robot arms, grippers, teleoperation interfaces, cameras, and middlewares.

**Teleoperation and Data Collection:**
RIO provides specific functionalities for human-robot interaction and data logging:
- **Teleoperation Scripts:** Three types of teleoperation scripts are offered:
 1. Control of relative end-effector poses (e.g., via Keyboard, Spacemouse).
 2. Mapping absolute joint positions using leader-follower setups (e.g., ALOHA, GELLO).
 3. Wrist pose retargeting from VR headsets (e.g., Apple Vision Pro) for humanoid upper-body control.
 These scripts are designed to be hardware-agnostic. Interpolators and signal-processing filters are included for smooth control and noise mitigation.
- **Data Collection:** A recorder logs robot demonstrations, enforcing standardized units (meters for world coordinates, radians for angular measurements). Data is structured using an RLDS-style format, aggregating multiple data streams into a unified state representation. The concept of "morphologies" provides abstract descriptions of robot structures, each defining its own set of state keys, standardizing state reporting across diverse platforms. For efficient storage and loading, RIO integrates with RoboDM, which uses flexible compression schemes. Users can write minimal converters to export data to their preferred training formats.

**Policy Inference:**
RIO aims to simplify policy integration and deployment:
- **High-Level API:** A lightweight API is provided for policies, requiring minimal implementation for instantiation, observation conversion, and inference.
- **Asynchronous Inference:** A policy wrapper node directly instantiates policies and handles inference requests asynchronously via the middleware, avoiding the overhead of a dedicated policy server.
- **Configurable Inference Script:** A policy-agnostic and hardware-agnostic script continuously obtains observations, queries the policy wrapper, post-processes actions for smoothness, and sends commands to hardware. This enables seamless switching between different policies and hardware.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RIO focused on its ability to support the robot learning workflow—from teleoperated data collection to policy fine-tuning and deployment—across diverse embodiments and policy classes. The experiments addressed RIO's performance for real-time deployment, its capacity to support data collection across various morphologies and teleoperation interfaces, and its effectiveness in deploying different policy types (VLAs, Diffusion Policy, RL) across multiple embodiments.

**Experimental Setup:**
All evaluations were conducted on a desktop equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, an AMD Ryzen 7 5700X CPU, and 64 GB of RAM.

**Policy Fine-tuning and Deployment:**
The researchers adopted a "bring-your-own-training-stack" approach, where RIO facilitates demonstration collection, data export, and subsequent policy deployment, maintaining consistency between collection and rollout.
- **Policies and Embodiments:** The workflow was validated using VLAs (π
 0.5, GR00T N1.5), Diffusion Policy (DP), and RL (PPO). These policies were deployed across three morphologies (single-arm, bimanual, humanoid) and four robot platforms (UFactory xArm7, SO-100, Unitree G1, Booster T1).
- **Training Details:** For π
 0.5, fine-tuning involved 20,000 steps from DROID/ALOHA checkpoints. GR00T N1.5 used 150 demonstrations for humanoid manipulation. DP policies were trained with 50 demonstrations per task. Locomotion policies used PPO trained in simulation. All demonstrations were collected at 50–80 Hz and stored in a compressed, exportable format (e.g., 150 three-camera episodes occupied 1.31 GB).
- **Metrics:** Success rates were reported, and average task completion times were compared to average demonstration times.

**Specific Results:**
- **Single-Arm Tabletop Manipulation (UFactory xArm7):**
 - **π0.5 (Pick-and-place, Fold Shirt):** Achieved success rates above 90%, with task completion times within 2 seconds of the demonstration average. Teleoperation with a Spacemouse yielded cleaner demonstrations for tasks with less rotation. Trajectory interpolation and low-pass filtering were applied to smooth gripper actions.
 - **Diffusion Policy (Tortilla Flipping, Ball Throwing):** For dynamic tasks, DP reached 66.7% success on tortilla flipping and 100% on ball throwing over 20 trials. Execution times were within a few seconds of the demonstration average.
- **Bimanual Tabletop Manipulation (SO-100):**
 - **π0.5 (Cloth Folding, Bowl Scrubbing):** All bimanual tasks achieved at least a 60% success rate. Policy completion times were longer relative to demonstrations (an average difference of -8.87 seconds), attributed to the policy reattempting tasks when not succeeding on the first try, rather than system overhead.
- **Humanoids (Unitree G1, Booster T1):**
 - **GR00T N1.5 (Pick Box on Unitree G1):** Demonstrated 95% success, with policy rollouts completing faster on average than human demonstrations.
 - **RL-PPO (Locomotion on Unitree G1 and Booster T1):** Policies trained in simulation successfully controlled locomotion on both platforms, achieving 100% success on navigation tasks.
 These results collectively indicate RIO's compatibility with both RL and imitation policies, vendor-specific hardware drivers, and the high-frequency control demanded by humanoid robots.

**Performance Analysis:**
- **Middleware Latency:** Benchmarking revealed low communication overhead. Zenoh and Shared Memory middlewares achieved sub-millisecond round-trip latency. Thread and ZeroRpc provided approximately 1 ms latency, while Portal had about 2 ms. This demonstrates the ability to select middlewares based on desired performance-distribution trade-offs for high-frequency control or debugging.
- **End-to-end Profiling (Observation-to-Action Latency):** During π
 0.5
 rollouts with three Intel RealSense cameras, RIO maintained a non-blocking main loop by distributing blocking operations (camera streaming, policy inference, robot control) to dedicated nodes. Asynchronous inference enabled preemptive action requests, allowing for continuous control despite an approximate 85.8 ms model forward pass time.
 - **Comparison with LeRobot:** RIO achieved an end-to-end observation-to-action latency of 130.3 ms, significantly lower than LeRobot's 581.2 ms. This efficiency is attributed to RIO's direct use of middleware for asynchronous inference, contrasting with LeRobot's approach of threading observations before network transmission to an asynchronous policy server. Lower pipeline latency directly contributes to a higher effective control frequency, supporting various policy classes.

**Cross-Embodiment Fine-Tuning (Appendix V-B):**
RIO facilitated fine-tuning π
0.5
on a mixed dataset combining unimanual (xArm7) and bimanual (SO-100) demonstrations. This demonstrated RIO's support for the complete cross-embodiment learning workflow—data collection, mixing, and policy rollout—without requiring additional tooling beyond standard scripts. The observed performance drop relative to single-embodiment fine-tuning was noted as an open research challenge, not a framework limitation.

**Onboarding a New Robot with a Coding Agent (Appendix V-C):**
The integration of an unsupported AgileX PiPER arm into RIO using a coding agent (Claude Code, Opus 4.6) was completed in under an hour. This included approximately 40 minutes for physical hardware setup and 9.2 minutes for software implementation (420 lines of code across 3 files) with no human intervention in code generation. The integration was validated by successfully teleoperating a cup-stacking task using the existing teleoperation script, with only the station configuration modified.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RIO represents a contribution to addressing the challenges of fragmented infrastructure and limited reusability in real-world robotics research, particularly for the deployment of advanced robot learning models. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

- **Enabling Generalist Robot Learning:** By providing flexible and performant infrastructure, RIO directly supports the development and deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and other generalist robot policies. It aims to bridge the gap between theoretical advancements in robot learning and their practical application on diverse robot hardware, potentially accelerating the progress towards more capable and adaptive robotic systems.
- **Reducing Engineering Overhead:** The framework's design, emphasizing modularity, flexible abstractions, and standardized interfaces, can substantially reduce the engineering effort required to integrate new hardware or adapt existing policies. This allows researchers to allocate more resources to core research problems rather than infrastructure development.
- **Fostering Reusability and Collaboration:** RIO's reusable building blocks and consistent approach to data collection and policy deployment facilitate the sharing of code, datasets, and learned policies across different research groups and hardware setups. This can promote collaboration and cumulative progress within the robotics community, moving away from platform-specific solutions.
- **Enhancing Accessibility:** The Python-based nature, ease of installation, and simplified configuration through a single file contribute to lowering the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners new to robotics or those working with limited resources. This can broaden participation in robot learning research.
- **Improving Performance and Consistency:** RIO's focus on real-time control, low observation-to-action latency (demonstrated through comparisons with existing frameworks), and asynchronous processing ensures that policies can be executed efficiently and smoothly. Standardized data formats and consistent state reporting across morphologies contribute to more reproducible and scalable experimentation.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The open-sourcing of RIO is a direct contribution to the public good in robotics. It provides a foundational tool that can be adopted, extended, and improved by the broader community, potentially accelerating innovation by democratizing access to robust robot learning infrastructure.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations and outline future directions for RIO.
- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** The current work primarily focused on single-embodiment fine-tuning. While RIO supports the workflow for cross-embodiment data mixing, effectively leveraging such data to improve policy generalization remains an open research problem. Future work includes exploring systematic benchmarking of distribution shift across embodiments.
- **Dynamic Task Performance of VLAs:** While RIO demonstrated dynamic tasks through teleoperation and Diffusion Policy, reliable dynamic task performance by VLAs is identified as an open research question requiring further investigation.
- **Hardware Expansion:** Future work aims to extend RIO's support to include additional robot hardware, such as mobile manipulators and multi-fingered dexterous robot hands, further increasing its versatility and applicability.

Overall, RIO seeks to provide a necessary infrastructure layer that can help unlock the potential of advanced robot learning models by making them more accessible, deployable, and scalable across the diverse landscape of robotic hardware.
