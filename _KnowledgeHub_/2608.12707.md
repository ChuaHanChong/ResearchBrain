# SAP-Nav: Spatial Semantic Representation Meets Active Perception for Hierarchical Open-Vocabulary Object Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> SAP-Nav is a fully online, zero-shot navigation framework for hierarchical open-vocabulary object navigation. It builds a Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation from actively acquired room snapshots plus a sub-room probability field, and adds Active Viewpoint Verification that repositions the agent to informative views before checking category and attribute constraints. It reaches state-of-the-art success across LangMap granularities and 49.7% SR on HM3D-OVON.

## Problem

- Standard object-navigation benchmarks score any instance of a category, ignoring how people phrase goals with room context and discriminative instance attributes.
- Methods needing complete 3D scans assume prior environment access unavailable online, while passive online mapping yields incomplete, viewpoint-biased coverage and no explicit room semantics.
- Candidates first appear distant, occluded, or badly angled; prior agents pick actions only for goal progress, treating observation quality as an incidental byproduct.

## Method

- **Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation** fuses online ==door-accumulated room segmentation==, VLM-labeled holistic room snapshots taken at entry and expansion events, and a ==Places-encoder sub-room probability field== over BEV cells.
- **Active Viewpoint Verification** scores visibility and perspective from candidate crop plus full egocentric image, triggering repositioning when the ==sufficiency score== falls below threshold, then verifying category and parsed attributes.
- Viewpoint selection samples ==concentric-ring poses==, filters by navigability and an adaptive minimum-distance bound from camera vertical field of view, and ranks by ==raycast visibility== on a ==2.5D height map==.

## Results

- On LangMap, SAP-Nav reaches **47.7%** room-level and **43.8%** region-level SR, a **12.2%** region-level SR gain over the best training-based baseline PlaNaVid at **31.6%**.
- On HM3D-OVON val unseen, SAP-Nav attains **49.7%** SR, above MetaNav at **46.1%** and NavFoM at **45.2%**, though SPL of **23.1** trails NavFoM's **31.9**.
- Ablating the representation drops room SR from **47.7%** to **26.9%**; removing all verification components drops instance-level SR from **26.8%** to **3.5%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Grounding and verification demand different visual evidence: holistic multi-view context for room semantics, close discriminative views for attributes; one passive stream serves neither well.
> - Making observation quality an explicit action objective, gated by a cheap sufficiency check, buys large reliability gains while limiting the detour cost to when it matters.
> - Deliberate repositioning trades path efficiency for success; SPL penalizes active perception, so efficiency metrics can undervalue methods that deploy more reliably.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on Open-Vocabulary Object Navigation (OVON). Traditionally, OVON research has concentrated on scenarios where an agent is tasked with finding any instance of a specified object category in an unseen environment using only onboard observations. This problem formulation has enabled standardized evaluation but often does not fully encompass the complexity of human-expressed navigation goals.

The broader research landscape is evolving to address more nuanced and multi-granularity instructions. The LangMap benchmark (Miao et al. 2026) introduced a significant extension to OVON, formalizing "hierarchical OVON." This setting requires agents to follow free-form instructions that can specify targets at various semantic granularities, including scene-level (e.g., "find a chair"), room-level (e.g., "find the chair in the living room"), region-level (e.g., "find the chair in the first living room"), and instance-level (e.g., "find the red chair with four legs in the living room"). Such instructions better reflect how humans articulate navigation goals, necessitating a more sophisticated understanding of spatial context and object attributes.

Current approaches to object navigation exhibit several limitations when confronted with hierarchical OVON tasks. Methods relying on complete 3D scans (e.g., Werby et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2026) assume environmental information that is not available in fully online, zero-shot deployment scenarios. Approaches that passively accumulate observations (e.g., Yin et al. 2024; Zhong et al. 2026) may acquire incomplete or viewpoint-biased scene coverage, making it difficult to establish persistent spatial grounding or obtain clear, discriminative views for target verification. Furthermore, many existing methods require extensive task-specific navigation training (e.g., Hong et al. 2021; Mousavian et al. 2019), which can limit their generalizability to novel environments or tasks without retraining.

SAP-Nav positions itself as a fully online, zero-shot framework that addresses these challenges by integrating active perception. It aims to overcome the limitations of passive observation and reliance on pre-built maps or extensive training, thus enabling more robust and generalized object navigation in complex, previously unseen environments under multi-granularity instructions. The work contributes to advancing embodied agents toward more human-like capabilities in understanding and executing complex spatial commands.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for SAP-Nav stems from the inherent difficulties of reliably solving hierarchical OVON under conditions of partial observation in unseen environments. The paper identifies two primary challenges that traditional passive observation methods struggle to overcome:

1. **Spatial Grounding**: This refers to the agent's ability to infer and understand room context and distinguish specific room instances (e.g., "the first living room" versus "the second living room") based on visual evidence. To achieve this, agents require holistic observations that reveal the surrounding spatial layout and semantics. However, during typical egocentric navigation, the agent's limited field of view provides only fragmented and potentially biased information, making it challenging to build a consistent and queryable understanding of the environment's semantic structure.

2. **Target Verification**: This involves confirming whether a detected candidate object precisely matches the category and attribute constraints specified in the instruction. Instance-level constraints, such as color or material, demand clear, discriminative views of the candidate object. Passive observation may present candidates that are distant, occluded, or viewed from an uninformative angle, rendering direct and reliable verification difficult. Incorrect verification can lead to navigation failures, approaching the wrong object, or overlooking the correct one.

Motivated by these challenges, the paper proposes that active perception is necessary to address the shortcomings of passive observation. An agent capable of deliberately controlling its sensing behavior—deciding what, how, when, and where to perceive—can actively acquire the specific types of visual evidence required for both robust spatial grounding and precise target verification.

The key objectives of this research are:
- To develop a fully online and zero-shot framework, named SAP-Nav, capable of executing hierarchical open-vocabulary object navigation instructions.
- To construct a **Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation (QSSR)** incrementally and online. This representation aims to provide persistent, environment-level semantic evidence by combining online room segmentation with actively acquired holistic room observations, enabling spatial queries from any explored location.
- To implement an **Active Viewpoint Verification (AVV)** module. This module is designed to assess the sufficiency of current visual evidence for target verification and, when necessary, guide the agent to a more informative viewpoint before attempting to verify candidate objects against category and attribute constraints.
- To ensure that the developed framework supports both hierarchical and standard category-level OVON tasks without requiring task-specific training or precomputed scene maps.
- To validate the practical feasibility of the proposed framework through real-world robotic deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SAP-Nav is presented as a fully online, zero-shot framework that employs active perception at two complementary stages: Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation (QSSR) and Active Viewpoint Verification (AVV).

**Overall Framework Overview:**
The agent begins navigation in an unknown environment, receiving a natural-language goal `G` that can range from a general object category to specific instance-level constraints. At each timestep, it receives egocentric RGB-D observations, estimates its pose, and executes an action. The agent performs vision-language-guided frontier exploration to search for the target. During this exploration, the QSSR is progressively built. When a candidate object is detected, SAP-Nav queries QSSR to check if it satisfies any room- or region-level constraints in `G`. Only spatially consistent candidates then trigger AVV. AVV assesses the current viewpoint's sufficiency for verification, repositions the agent if needed, and finally verifies the candidate against the instruction's category and attribute constraints. Accepted candidates are approached, while rejected ones are blacklisted and exploration resumes.

**Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation (QSSR):**
QSSR is a hybrid representation designed to support querying room types and distinguishing among multiple room instances of the same type using holistic visual evidence. It comprises a spatial semantic Bird's-Eye View (BEV) map, where each grid cell stores room type semantics, and a visual record of actively acquired room snapshots. Its construction involves three progressive stages:

1. **Online Room Segmentation (S1):** This initial stage establishes a geometric structure by partitioning the explored free space into distinct room instances. It adapts an online room segmentation method that utilizes door projections over a temporal window. Detected door boundaries signal entry into new rooms and provide an inward direction for capturing holistic room snapshots. Each explored BEV location is mapped to a unique room instance `R_k`.

2. **Active Room Perception (S2):** Building on the geometric structure from S1, this stage associates each room instance `R_k` with a set of semantic types `C_k` and a visual record `V_k`.
 - **Holistic Room Snapshot Acquisition:** Snapshots are actively acquired under three conditions to provide comprehensive visual evidence:
 - **Initialization:** The initial panorama captured at the episode's start is used for the starting room.
 - **First Entry:** When entering a new room `R_k`, the agent rotates in place, facing inward from the entry door, to capture and stitch `N_v` frames into a holistic snapshot `v_k^0`.
 - **Expansion Update:** If a previously classified room `R_k` substantially increases its explored area, a new stitched view `v_k^n` is captured, aimed at the centroid of the newly explored portion, to reflect the room's full extent.
 - **Semantic Type Inference:** Each acquired snapshot `v_k^n` is processed by a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to predict a semantic type `c_k^n`. These predictions are accumulated in `C_k`, and `V_k` retains the holistic visual evidence.

3. **Sub-Room Semantic Refinement (S3):** While S2 assigns room-level semantics, open areas or under-segmented spaces may contain multiple functional areas within a single geometrically defined room. S3 refines QSSR with a sub-room semantic probability field `Φ` over the BEV map. For each image patch, a pretrained scene encoder produces room type logits, which are then restricted to an indoor room-type vocabulary. These per-observation room probabilities are back-projected to their corresponding BEV grid cells. Evidence from patches observed across timesteps and viewpoints is accumulated in `Φ_t(u)` for each cell `u`, providing a normalized room type distribution that can distinguish functional areas.

**Spatial Semantic Query:** When a candidate object `o` is detected, SAP-Nav queries QSSR using the BEV cells covered by its projected segmentation footprint. The room type `ˆc(o)` is determined either directly from `C_k` if it contains a single type, or by aggregating `Φ_t(u)` values if multiple types are present. For region-level goals, `V_k` provides visual evidence for Visual Question Answering (VQA) to match the region description.

**Active Viewpoint Verification (AVV):**
AVV addresses the challenge of unreliable candidate verification from insufficient viewpoints. It consists of three stages:

1. **Sufficiency Evaluation:** Using the current egocentric observation `I_t`, a VLM assesses the candidate `o` along two criteria: (1) visibility (clarity) and (2) perspective (informativeness of the angle for details). Each criterion is scored (1-5), and their sum `s` determines sufficiency. If `s < τ` (a predefined threshold), the view is deemed insufficient, triggering viewpoint selection.

2. **Viewpoint Selection:** If the current view is insufficient, a more informative viewpoint `v*` is identified:
 - **Geometric Sampling:** Potential viewpoints `V` are sampled along concentric rings around the candidate's segmentation footprint centroid `c`, with varying radii (0.8-2.4 m) and uniformly spaced angles (24 angles).
 - **Constraint Filtering:** Sampled viewpoints are filtered based on navigability within the explored region and an adaptive minimum distance from the object. This minimum distance accounts for camera height, estimated object top height, and camera's vertical field of view to prevent the object from falling out of view.
 - **Visibility Scoring:** Each valid viewpoint `v ∈ V` is evaluated based on its visibility to the target. An online 2.5D height map helps determine if rays from the potential camera pose to the object's footprint are obstructed. The viewpoint maximizing visibility to the target's footprint is selected as `v*`.
 - **Repositioning:** The agent navigates to `v*`. If sufficiency remains below `τ` after three repositioning attempts, the best observation acquired during these attempts is used for verification, assuming further viewpoint changes are unlikely to improve sufficiency significantly.

3. **Target Verification:** Once the sufficiency score `s ≥ τ`:
 - The VLM is presented with the highlighted candidate region within its surrounding context and prompted to verify if it matches the target category from the instruction.
 - If `G` includes attribute constraints, an LLM first parses `G` into structured attribute descriptions. The VLM then verifies if the candidate's visual attributes align with these parsed constraints.
 - The candidate is accepted only if all category and attribute constraints are satisfied. Rejected candidates are added to a blacklist to avoid redundant re-evaluation, and exploration resumes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of SAP-Nav was evaluated through extensive experiments on two OVON benchmarks and real-world robotic deployments.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Datasets:**
 - **LangMap (Miao et al. 2026):** A large-scale, multi-granularity benchmark based on HM3D scans, comprising approximately 15,000 tasks across scene-, room-, region-, and instance-level instructions.
 - **HM3D-OVON (Yokoyama et al. 2024b):** A widely used scene-level object navigation benchmark with 379 open-vocabulary categories, evaluated on its Val Unseen split (over 3,000 episodes).
- **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL). Success is defined as stopping within 1 meter of a target viewpoint within 500 steps.
- **Implementation Details:** The agent's action space included MOVE\_FORWARD (0.25m), TURN\_LEFT/TURN\_RIGHT (30 degrees), and STOP. Various open-source (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-thinking) and closed-source (GPT-4o) Vision-Language Models (VLMs) were utilized for reasoning.

**Benchmark Results:**

- **Hierarchical OVON on LangMap (Table 1):**
 - SAP-Nav, particularly when combined with Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-thinking, achieved the highest reported performance across all four granularity levels (Scene, Room, Region, Instance) in terms of both SR and SPL, without requiring task-specific navigation training.
 - With Qwen3.5-9B, SAP-Nav exceeded the performance of strong training-based baselines such as PlaNaVid (Miao et al. 2026).
 - Even using the same Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone, SAP-Nav demonstrated superior performance compared to PlaNaVid, especially on the Region level.
 - Compared to other training-free methods like adapted VLFM (Yokoyama et al. 2024a) and 3D-Mem (Yang et al. 2025), SAP-Nav showed substantial improvements across all granularity levels, with notable gains on room- and region-level tasks. The abstract specifically notes a 12.2% improvement in SR over training-based methods on region-level navigation.

- **Standard OVON on HM3D-OVON (Table 2):**
 - Despite being designed for multi-granularity instructions, SAP-Nav achieved the highest SR among all compared methods on the standard scene-level OVON task, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities to conventional object-goal navigation without navigation training.
 - SAP-Nav's SPL was observed to be lower than some training-based methods (e.g., NavFoM, MetaNav). This reduction in path efficiency is attributed to the AVV module's deliberate repositioning actions, which extend path length in pursuit of more reliable target verification. The paper posits this as a reasonable trade-off, prioritizing successful and accurate target identification over path minimization in real-world applications.

**Real-world Deployment (Figure 3):**
- SAP-Nav was validated on a Deep Robotics Jueying Lite3 quadruped robot in diverse indoor environments (offices, meeting rooms, living rooms, kitchens).
- Qualitative examples illustrated the system's ability to execute hierarchical OVON instructions on a physical robot. This included cases where QSSR successfully rejected spatially inconsistent candidates based on online room semantics and where AVV improved the viewpoint before performing target verification, such as identifying a potted plant with slender leaves that was initially occluded. These demonstrations supported the practical feasibility of the approach.

**Ablation Study:**

- **Effect of Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation (QSSR) (Table 3):**
 - Removing QSSR entirely resulted in significant performance degradation, highlighting the necessity of spatial semantic awareness for handling room- or region-level constraints.
 - The incremental inclusion of QSSR components (S1+S2, S1+S2+S3) progressively improved performance. Relying solely on scene predictions from passive observations (implied by removing active room perception) led to lower performance, indicating that individual views provide limited context and noisy room-type evidence.
 - The S3 component (Sub-Room Semantic Refinement) further improved performance, especially in open or under-segmented rooms where geometric room boundaries do not precisely align with functional areas.

- **Effect of Active Viewpoint Verification (AVV) (Table 4):**
 - The ablation study demonstrated that removing AVV resulted in a clear performance drop across all granularities, indicating that passive observations are often insufficient for reliable task completion.
 - Each component of AVV—Sufficiency Evaluation (SE), Viewpoint Selection (VS), and Target Verification (TV)—contributed to performance gains. TV, which explicitly checks candidates against instructions, yielded substantial improvements.
 - While VS improved verification reliability by acquiring better viewpoints, it could lead to slightly reduced SPL due to additional movements. SE mitigated this by adaptively triggering VS only when the current observation was genuinely insufficient, balancing verification reliability with navigation efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SAP-Nav framework represents a notable advancement in the field of embodied AI, particularly for open-vocabulary object navigation. Its primary significance lies in the introduction of a fully online, zero-shot approach that effectively integrates active perception to address complex, hierarchical language-based navigation tasks.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

1. **Bridging the Gap for Hierarchical OVON:** SAP-Nav is specifically designed to tackle the limitations of traditional OVON in handling multi-granularity, free-form instructions. By actively building and querying a spatial semantic representation and actively seeking informative views for verification, it enables agents to understand and execute goals ranging from broad category searches to precise instance-level disambiguation, mirroring how humans naturally interact with and instruct robots.

2. **Robustness in Unseen Environments:** The zero-shot nature of SAP-Nav, coupled with its online construction of scene representations, means it does not rely on pre-built 3D maps or extensive task-specific training data for each new environment. This significantly enhances its applicability and robustness in novel and unexplored settings, which is crucial for real-world robotic deployment.

3. **Demonstrated Performance:** The system establishes new performance benchmarks for hierarchical OVON on the LangMap dataset, outperforming both training-based and other training-free methods. Its strong performance on standard scene-level OVON (HM3D-OVON) further attests to its generality and effectiveness beyond its primary design for hierarchical tasks. This suggests that the principles of active perception for spatial semantic understanding are broadly beneficial across various navigation scenarios.

4. **Practical Feasibility:** The successful deployment and qualitative demonstrations on a real-world quadruped robot underscore the practical viability of SAP-Nav. This move from simulation to physical hardware is a critical step towards developing deployable intelligent agents for domestic, industrial, or service robotics applications, where agents must follow human instructions in dynamic and unknown environments.

5. **Addressing Core Challenges with Active Perception:** By explicitly tackling the issues of partial observation, spatial grounding, and reliable target verification through its Queryable Spatial-Semantic Representation (QSSR) and Active Viewpoint Verification (AVV) modules, SAP-Nav provides a principled approach to overcoming long-standing challenges in embodied navigation. This paradigm of active perception offers a blueprint for future research in creating more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The paper acknowledges two primary limitations, which also outline avenues for future research:

1. **Optimizing Active Viewpoint Selection:** Currently, AVV's viewpoint selection is primarily based on geometric visibility. Future work could integrate semantic utility and motion cost into the viewpoint selection process. This could lead to more efficient navigation by choosing viewpoints that are not only geometrically clear but also semantically informative for verification, while minimizing unnecessary movements.

2. **Persistent Memory Integration:** SAP-Nav's QSSR is built online for each task and not retained across successive tasks. Integrating QSSR with a persistent memory system could enable the agent to accumulate and reuse spatial semantic knowledge across multiple tasks and over extended periods. This would facilitate more efficient lifelong navigation, where the agent continuously learns about its environment, reducing exploration time and improving performance on recurring tasks.

Overall, SAP-Nav contributes to pushing the boundaries of embodied AI by demonstrating how active perception can enable more intelligent, adaptable, and robust navigation capabilities in response to complex human language instructions.
