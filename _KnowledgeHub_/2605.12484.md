# Learning, Fast and Slow: Towards LLMs That Adapt Continually

> [!summary] Summary
> The Fast-Slow Training (FST) framework enables large language models (LLMs) to adapt continually by jointly optimizing persistent model parameters (slow weights) and mutable textual prompts (fast weights). This method achieves higher performance, greater data efficiency, and improved plasticity across reasoning tasks while reducing catastrophic forgetting, demonstrating superior continual learning capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing LLM adaptation methods (SFT, RL) encode all task-specific information into a single set of model weights, leading to catastrophic forgetting, reduced plasticity, and decreased out-of-distribution generalization.
- In-context learning via prompts offers rapid adaptation but often cannot match the peak performance of parameter-based updates.
- Traditional parameter-only updates are computationally expensive and data-intensive, struggling in continual learning scenarios where models need to acquire new skills sequentially without performance degradation.

## Method

- Introduces **Fast-Slow Training (FST)**, a framework that integrates ==slow weights== (model parameters) and ==fast weights== (optimized textual prompts) for joint optimization.
- Slow weights are updated using ==Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)== with a ==cispo== objective, targeting general, persistent behaviors.
- Fast weights are optimized through ==Generative Evolutionary Prompt Optimization (GEPA)==, a reflective evolutionary process that generates and refines natural language prompts for rapid, task-specific adjustments.

## Results

- Achieved peak performance of RL with substantially fewer training samples (e.g., **3.0x fewer** on CodeIO and HoVer-hard), demonstrating superior data efficiency without OOD generalization decline.
- Consistently reached a higher performance asymptote than RL alone (e.g., **+4.4 percentage points** on CodeIO, **+7.7 pp** on HoVer-hard), indicating enhanced maximum accuracy.
- Maintained greater plasticity and reduced catastrophic forgetting, enabling acquisition of multiple tasks sequentially in continual learning scenarios where RL-only methods struggled or stalled.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distributing adaptation across both textual and parametric channels allows the system to converge to a higher overall performance ceiling and remain closer to the base model's capabilities.
> - The fast weight channel rapidly extracts and injects task structure early in training, providing effective gradient signals that accelerate the learning process for slow weights.
> - Separating transient task-specific knowledge into mutable textual contexts prevents over-specialization of core model parameters, preserving general reasoning behaviors and plasticity for future tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language model (LLM) adaptation predominantly relies on modifying model parameters through methods such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL). While effective for specialized domains, this approach presents a fundamental challenge: all task-specific information, regardless of its transience or reusability, must be encoded into the same persistent set of model weights. This can lead to issues such as catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses previously acquired knowledge, reduced plasticity, which diminishes the model's ability to adapt to new or future tasks, and decreased out-of-distribution generalization. These phenomena are often observed as the model drifts from its base behavior, impacting its overall utility for continual learning.

Concurrently, LLM systems employ in-context learning through prompts, instructions, and contextual information. These textual components offer a flexible and economical adaptation mechanism, capable of rapid, task-specific adjustments without altering model parameters. Prompt optimization methods have demonstrated the capacity for substantial behavioral improvements. However, in-context learning alone typically does not achieve the same performance gains as parameter-based updates.

The presented work addresses this dichotomy by proposing a unified framework that integrates both adaptation channels. It draws inspiration from the long-standing concept of "fast and slow weights" in neural networks, which is rooted in the neuroscience theory of complementary learning systems. This theory posits that biological brains utilize different learning mechanisms operating on distinct timescales – a fast, temporary system for new, specific experiences and a slow, cumulative system for general, persistent knowledge. By translating this concept to LLMs, the research seeks to move beyond the existing limitations where learning is restricted to either in-context or in-weights, and instead enable a co-evolution of these two forms of adaptation. This positions the work as an effort to develop more robust, adaptable, and efficient LLM post-training methodologies capable of continuous learning without the severe performance degradation seen in current approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel framework, termed Fast-Slow Training (FST), that enables Large Language Models (LLMs) to adapt continually to new tasks and domains while mitigating the fundamental limitations observed in existing adaptation paradigms. The motivations for introducing FST are detailed below:

1. **Address Catastrophic Forgetting and Plasticity Loss:** Current LLM adaptation methods, particularly supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL), modify the model's parameters to absorb task-specific information. This process often causes the model to "forget" previously learned general reasoning behaviors or common-sense knowledge. Furthermore, it can lead to a loss of plasticity, meaning the model's capacity to learn new, subsequent tasks is degraded. FST aims to counteract this by distributing the learning burden, allowing model parameters to maintain a closer resemblance to the base model's general capabilities.

2. **Enhance Data Efficiency:** Relying solely on slow, parameter-based updates for adaptation can be computationally expensive and data-intensive. The research is motivated by the need for more efficient learning processes, especially in contexts where large volumes of labeled data or extensive reinforcement learning rollouts are costly to generate. FST aims to leverage the rapid feedback mechanisms of fast, textual adaptation to achieve performance gains with fewer training samples.

3. **Achieve Higher Performance Asymptotes:** While in-context learning through prompt optimization offers rapid adaptation, it often cannot match the peak performance achievable through parameter updates. Conversely, parameter updates can sometimes struggle to capture highly specific, transient task heuristics efficiently. The motivation is to combine the strengths of both, allowing the system to converge to a higher overall performance ceiling by letting each component contribute to the aspect of adaptation it handles best.

4. **Enable Robust Continual Learning:** In dynamic real-world scenarios, LLMs need to continuously acquire new skills and adapt to changing task distributions without requiring full retraining or experiencing significant performance degradation on prior tasks. The current methods often struggle in such continual learning settings. FST is motivated by the goal of creating LLMs that can effectively acquire multiple new tasks sequentially, demonstrating sustained adaptability over time.

5. **Leverage Complementary Learning Mechanisms:** Drawing inspiration from cognitive science, which suggests humans learn at different timescales (e.g., System 1 fast, intuitive learning vs. System 2 slow, deliberate learning), the research is motivated by the hypothesis that LLMs could similarly benefit from distinct, complementary learning systems. FST proposes to instantiate this by treating model parameters as "slow weights" for persistent, general behaviors and optimized textual contexts (prompts) as "fast weights" for rapid, task-specific adjustments. This division of labor is hypothesized to lead to a more effective and flexible learning architecture.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to pave the way for LLMs that are not only more performant on specialized tasks but also more stable, adaptable, and capable of sustained learning in evolving environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the Fast-Slow Training (FST) framework, which proposes a joint optimization of two complementary adaptation components: "slow weights" (model parameters) and "fast weights" (optimized textual context or prompts). The methodology intertwines these two channels to facilitate continuous learning.

#### 3.1. Components of Fast-Slow Training (FST)

- **Slow Weights (θ):** These refer to the intrinsic parameters of the Large Language Model. Their updates are generally expensive, persist across tasks, and are intended to encode long-lived, general reasoning behaviors.
 - **Optimization Method:** Slow weights are updated using **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**. Specifically, the authors follow the ScaleRL recipe, which employs the `cispo` (clipped importance-sampling policy-gradient) objective.
 - **Reward Function:** A reward `r(x, y) ∈` is provided by an automatic verifier for a given query `x` and model response `y`. This is commonly used in reasoning tasks like math and code.
 - **Advantage Calculation:** For each query, the current policy generates a group of `G` rollouts. Group-relative advantages are computed to normalize rewards based on the mean and standard deviation of rewards within that group. This helps in comparing variations induced by both prompts and sampling.
 - **Update Mechanism:** The policy is updated using the `L_cispo` loss, which incorporates a truncated importance-sampling ratio and the computed advantages, aggregated at the prompt level.

- **Fast Weights (Φ):** These refer to textual scaffolds such as prompts, instructions, and contextual information. They are relatively cheap to modify, can be updated frequently, and immediately influence model behavior. They are designed to capture task-level adaptation without permanently altering the model's parameters.
 - **Optimization Method:** Fast weights are optimized using **Generative Evolutionary Prompt Optimization (GEPA)**. GEPA is a reflective evolutionary procedure that operates over a discrete text space (Σ*).
 - **Fitness Function:** For a fixed policy `π_θ`, the fitness of a prompt `ϕ` on an instance `x` is defined as its expected reward, `s(ϕ; x) = E_{y~π_θ(·|x,ϕ)}[r(x, y)]`.
 - **Evolutionary Process:** GEPA maintains a population of candidate prompts. It uses rollouts from the current `π_θ` to elicit natural-language critiques from a separate, frozen reflection LM (π_ref, specifically OpenAI gpt-5.2 in this work). Based on these critiques, `π_ref` proposes textual mutations to improve prompts on an anchor set of problems.
 - **Population Management:** Instead of returning a single "best" prompt, GEPA retains a Pareto frontier of complementary prompts. This diverse population ensures that different prompts can specialize to different problem subsets, providing rich conditioning signals during subsequent slow-weight updates.

#### 3.2. Interleaving Mechanism

FST jointly optimizes `θ` and `Φ` by interleaving their respective update processes:

1. **Cycle-based Training:** Training is structured into cycles, with each cycle comprising `T` slow-weight updates.
2. **Lookahead Batch:** At the beginning of cycle `c`, a lookahead batch `L_c` (union of the next `T` RL minibatches) is pre-fetched from the data stream `D`.
3. **Fast Weight Update (GEPA Phase):** GEPA is executed with the current policy `π_θ_c` as the rollout model, `π_ref` as the proposer, `L_c` (or a subset) as the anchor set, and the previous prompt population `Φ_c` as the seed. GEPA returns the top `K` candidates from its Pareto frontier, forming the new fast-weight population `Φ_c+1`.
4. **Slow Weight Update (RL Phase):** For the subsequent `T` steps, `θ` is updated on minibatches from `L_c`, while `Φ_c+1` is held fixed.
 - **Rollout Group Formation:** For each problem `p` in a minibatch, a rollout group of size `G` is formed. Each prompt `ϕ^(k)` from `Φ_c+1` is sampled `G/K` times. These `K` mini-groups are cumulatively treated as one group for `p`, and rewards are normalized using the per-problem statistics. This setup mixes prompt-induced and sampling-induced variation within the same advantage computation.
 - **Policy Update:** The `cispo` update (Eq. 4 in the paper) is applied to update `θ`.
5. **Iteration:** After `T` updates, the procedure repeats with a new GEPA phase and an updated policy.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup and Baselines

- **LLM:** Qwen3-8B (or an SFT-version for Math tasks).
- **Tasks:** Code-output prediction (CodeIO), math (Polaris), and multi-hop fact verification (HoVer-hard). A synthetic star-graph reasoning task was also used for early-training behavior analysis.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Validation accuracy, KL divergence from the base policy, plasticity (measured by performance on a subsequent task after initial training), and performance in continual learning settings.
- **Baselines:** Standard RL (slow weights only) and GEPA alone (fast weights only).
- **Rollout Reuse:** An optimization implemented to cache GEPA evaluation rollouts, reducing redundant generation during subsequent RL steps, thus improving computational efficiency without compromising accuracy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates several advantages of Fast-Slow Training (FST) over traditional parameter-only Reinforcement Learning (RL) or prompt optimization alone across various reasoning tasks.

#### 4.1. Data Efficiency

FST exhibits superior data efficiency compared to RL. On CodeIO, Math (Polaris), and HoVer-hard tasks, FST reached the peak performance of RL with substantially fewer training samples (3.0x fewer on CodeIO, 1.4x on Math, and 3.0x on HoVer-hard). This efficiency was observed without a measurable decline in out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. For instance, on CodeIO-trained OOD benchmarks, FST achieved 49.7% accuracy versus RL’s 51.1%, despite using significantly fewer training steps to reach its in-distribution peak. This suggests that the textual fast weights efficiently incorporate task-level signals, accelerating the learning process.

#### 4.2. Performance Asymptote

FST consistently achieved a higher performance asymptote than RL alone. By fitting sigmoid curves to validation accuracy trajectories, FST’s upper asymptote (A) exceeded RL’s across all three tasks: +4.4 percentage points (pp) on CodeIO (47.4% vs 43.0%), +2.9 pp on Math (Polaris) (49.2% vs 46.4%), and +7.7 pp on HoVer-hard (25.0% vs 17.3%). This indicates that distributing adaptation across both textual and parametric channels enables the overall method to converge to a higher accuracy ceiling.

#### 4.3. Proximity to Base Model and Reduced Drift

FST-trained models remained closer to the base LLM policy, as measured by lower KL divergence (KL(π_train || π_base)), while achieving higher performance. On all tested tasks (CodeIO, HoVer, Physics), FST achieved higher validation rewards at significantly lower KL divergences compared to RL. For example, at matched reward, FST models exhibited up to 70% less KL divergence to the base policy than RL-only baselines. This reduced displacement from the initial policy is associated with preserving general capabilities and preventing plasticity loss.

#### 4.4. Preserved Plasticity

FST-trained models demonstrated greater plasticity, retaining the capacity to learn subsequent tasks more effectively. In a two-phase experiment, where models were first trained on task X (Math or Physics) and then continued with RL on a different task Y (HoVer-hard), FST-initialized models significantly outperformed RL-initialized models. For instance, after Math training, the RL-trained checkpoint showed near-zero learnability on HoVer-hard, while the FST-trained checkpoint performed close to a base-model initialization. This suggests FST prevents over-specialization of slow weights to the initial task.

#### 4.5. Improved Continual Learning

In a continual learning scenario involving sequential task changes (HoVer → CodeIO → Physics every 200 steps), FST successfully acquired each new task, reaching near-peak performance in every stage. In contrast, parameter-only RL acquired the first task but stalled significantly on the second (CodeIO) and only partially recovered on the third (Physics). FST's ability to route task-level adaptation through both fast textual and slow parametric weights enabled it to remain capable of acquiring later tasks under continued optimization.

#### 4.6. Insights into FST's Efficacy

Two key observations explain FST's performance:
1. **Fast Weights Accelerate Early Task Signal Acquisition:** On a synthetic star-graph reasoning task where the base model yielded near-zero rewards, parameter-only RL showed a delay of approximately 300 steps before rewards began to rise. FST, however, achieved measurable rewards around step 50, primarily driven by early GEPA cycles. This highlights the fast channel's ability to rapidly extract and inject task structure, providing gradient signals for the slow weights.
2. **Combined Optimization Elevates Performance Ceiling:** A decomposition of in-distribution gains on CodeIO and HoVer-hard revealed that both slow (parametric) and fast (textual) channels contributed to performance. On CodeIO, the joint contribution of FST-trained weights and FST-evolved prompts (43.3%) surpassed gains from slow-only (25.1%) or fast-only (34.5%) channels. This indicates that both components jointly optimizing for reward are necessary to reach the higher performance ceiling.

#### 4.7. Rollout Reuse

An implemented rollout reuse mechanism, which caches GEPA evaluation rollouts, reduced the wall-time per RL step by approximately 29% on HoVer-hard without any observed loss in accuracy. This enhanced computational efficiency by serving a significant fraction of RL trajectories from the cache.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Fast-Slow Training (FST) framework introduces a significant paradigm shift in how large language models (LLMs) are adapted and continually learn. By jointly optimizing model parameters (slow weights) and textual contexts/prompts (fast weights) through an interleaved process, the research presents a methodology that addresses several critical limitations of current LLM post-training.

One of the primary significances of FST is its **mitigation of catastrophic forgetting and preservation of plasticity**. Conventional parameter-only adaptation often forces LLMs to over-specialize, leading to a loss of general reasoning capabilities and a reduced capacity to learn new tasks. FST's approach, which routes task-specific information into mutable textual contexts, allows the core model parameters to remain closer to their base configuration. This property is crucial for the development of LLMs that can continuously acquire new skills and adapt to evolving environments without needing frequent, costly full retraining or experiencing performance degradation on previously learned tasks.

The observed **improvements in data efficiency and higher performance asymptotes** are also highly impactful. By leveraging the rapid feedback mechanisms inherent in prompt optimization, FST can achieve comparable or superior performance with substantially fewer training samples than parameter-only RL. This has direct implications for reducing the computational resources and time required for LLM adaptation, making advanced LLM capabilities more accessible and sustainable. The ability to reach a higher performance ceiling further suggests that this multi-channel optimization approach can unlock capabilities not fully realized by either method in isolation.

Furthermore, FST's demonstrated capability in **continual learning scenarios** is critical for real-world deployment of LLMs. As models are expected to operate in dynamic environments where new tasks and data streams constantly emerge, the ability to adapt to new objectives on the fly without stalling or forgetting is paramount. FST provides a robust mechanism for LLMs to progressively learn multiple tasks sequentially, maintaining a high level of adaptability throughout.

The conceptual foundation of FST, drawing from the neuroscience of complementary learning systems, offers a novel perspective on LLM intelligence. By separating declarative knowledge (in prompts) from procedural knowledge (in weights), it suggests a more biologically plausible and, empirically, more effective architecture for general-purpose reasoners.

The potential impact extends to various applications where LLMs need to operate robustly over time and adapt to diverse, changing requirements, such as personalized assistants, evolving code generation systems, or dynamic problem-solving agents. Future work can explore variations in prompt and weight optimizers, improve computational efficiency, and delve deeper into distillation-based approaches, further extending the applicability and performance of this fast-slow learning paradigm.
