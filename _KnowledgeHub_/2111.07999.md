# Adversarial Skill Chaining for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation via Terminal State Regularization

> [!summary] Summary
> T-STAR chains independently-trained subtask policies for long-horizon manipulation by adversarially regularizing each policy's terminal-state distribution toward the next subtask's initiation set, jointly training all subtask policies with GAIL and a terminal-state-regularization reward, achieving the first model-free RL success on IKEA furniture assembly tasks.

## Problem

- Naive sequential execution of independently trained subtask policies fails when a policy's terminal state falls outside the next policy's initiation set.
- Prior skill-chaining methods widen initiation sets to cover terminal states, but this cascades into ever-larger termination sets down the chain.
- This cascading effect makes fine-tuning infeasible for long skill sequences, limiting prior approaches to short chains only.

## Method

- ==Initiation set discriminator==: an adversarially trained classifier distinguishing a subtask's initiation-set states from the preceding policy's terminal states, per subtask.
- ==Terminal state regularization (TSR)== reward: augments the policy reward with the discriminator's output to pull terminal states toward the next policy's initiation set.
- Joint alternating training algorithm (**T-STAR**): iteratively updates ==GAIL== discriminators, initiation-set discriminators, and all subtask policies together using environment, GAIL, and TSR reward terms.

## Results

- T-STAR reaches **0.90** and **0.89** average task progress on TABLE LACK and CHAIR INGOLF versus **0.63** and **0.77** for the Policy Sequencing baseline.
- Success rate improves from **0%** to **56%** on CHAIR INGOLF and from **59%** to **87%** on TABLE LACK compared to policy sequencing.
- PCA visualization over **36M-45M** training steps shows the baseline's terminal-state distribution spreading horizontally and vertically while T-STAR's stays bounded and even shrinks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Regularizing where a policy ends up, not just widening where the next policy can start, prevents runaway state-distribution growth across long skill chains.
> - Unconstrained initiation-set widening is a self-reinforcing failure mode: each fix enlarges the next policy's burden, capping feasible chain length.
> - Adversarial discriminators can shape terminal-state distributions the same way GAIL shapes action distributions, generalizing discriminator-guided rewards beyond imitation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Robot manipulation of complex objects in the real world remains one of the most challenging problems in robotics. While robots excel at repeating simple, precise movements in controlled environments, they struggle with long-horizon tasks that require multiple sequential steps and adaptability. Furniture assembly represents a perfect example of such challenges, requiring a sequence of precise manipulations in a specific order.

In "Adversarial Skill Chaining for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation via Terminal State Regularization," researchers from the University of Southern California, NVIDIA, Caltech, and UT Austin present a novel approach to addressing this challenge. Their work introduces Terminal State Regularization (T-STAR), which enables robots to successfully complete complex sequential tasks by ensuring smooth transitions between subtasks.

### 2. The Challenge of Long-Horizon Tasks

The central challenge in training robots to perform complex tasks is the exponential growth in complexity as the task horizon increases. Traditional reinforcement learning approaches struggle with this "curse of dimensionality" because the state and action spaces become prohibitively large, making exploration inefficient and learning slow.

A common solution is to break down complex tasks into simpler subtasks, training individual policies for each subtask and then chaining them together. However, this approach faces a critical problem: the terminal state of one subtask may not align with the initiation set (the set of states from which a policy can successfully complete its assigned subtask) of the next subtask in the sequence.

When a policy completes a subtask, it produces a terminal state that serves as the initial state for the next subtask. If this terminal state falls outside the initiation set of the next policy, the chain breaks, and the overall task fails. This misalignment between terminal states and initiation sets becomes increasingly problematic as the number of subtasks increases, creating a cascading "state explosion" that makes long-horizon tasks virtually impossible to complete.

### 3. Terminal State Regularization (T-STAR)

To address this challenge, the authors introduce Terminal State Regularization (T-STAR), a novel approach that regularizes the terminal state distribution of each subtask policy to ensure compatibility with the following subtask's initiation set.

The core insight of T-STAR is to both expand the initiation sets of policies and to constrain the terminal state distributions. This two-pronged approach creates a more robust skill chaining mechanism:

1. **Widen Initiation Sets**: Each policy is trained to succeed from a wider range of initial states, increasing its robustness to variations in the terminal states of the previous policy.

2. **Constrain Terminal States**: Each policy is encouraged to terminate in states that align well with the initiation set of the subsequent policy, facilitating smooth transitions.

This approach differs from previous skill chaining methods that focus solely on expanding initiation sets, which can lead to computational inefficiency as the state space grows exponentially with each additional subtask.

### 4. Adversarial Learning Framework

The authors implement T-STAR using an adversarial learning framework. For each pair of consecutive subtasks, they train a discriminator that differentiates between the ideal initiation states of a subtask and the terminal states produced by the preceding subtask. This discriminator provides a reward signal that guides the preceding policy to produce terminal states that are similar to the ideal initiation states of the next policy.

The overall reward function for training the subtask policies combines three components:

$$R = \lambda_1 R_{ENV} + \lambda_2 R_{GAIL} + \lambda_3 R_{TSR}$$

Where:
- $R_{ENV}$ is the environmental reward for completing the subtask
- $R_{GAIL}$ is the reward from Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning, which helps the policy mimic expert demonstrations
- $R_{TSR}$ is the terminal state regularization reward, which encourages the policy to reach terminal states that align with the next policy's initiation set

The initiation set discriminator learns to distinguish between the desirable initiation states and the actual terminal states produced by the preceding policy. The output of this discriminator is used to compute $R_{TSR}$, providing a learning signal that encourages alignment between consecutive policies.

### 5. Implementation Details

The researchers implement their approach using the following steps:

1. **Task Decomposition**: Break down complex tasks like furniture assembly into manageable subtasks (e.g., picking up parts, aligning components, attaching pieces).

2. **Initial Policy Training**: Pre-train individual policies for each subtask using expert demonstrations and Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL).

3. **Initiation Set Modeling**: For each subtask, collect a buffer of successful initial states through policy rollouts.

4. **Terminal State Collection**: For each subtask policy, collect terminal states reached after successful completion.

5. **Discriminator Training**: Train discriminators to distinguish between ideal initiation states and terminal states from the preceding policy.

6. **Policy Fine-tuning**: Update each policy with the combined reward function that includes the terminal state regularization component.

The process is applied iteratively, allowing policies to adapt to each other and ensuring increasingly smooth transitions between subtasks.

For policy representation, the authors use neural networks with parameters optimized through Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a state-of-the-art reinforcement learning algorithm. The state space includes both proprioceptive information (joint positions, velocities) and visual features extracted from simulated camera images.

### 6. Experimental Results

The researchers evaluated T-STAR on two furniture assembly tasks in simulation:

1. **CHAIRINGOLF**: A chair assembly task requiring the robot to attach chair legs to a seat.
2. **TABLELACK**: A table assembly task requiring the attachment of table legs to a tabletop.

The results show that T-STAR significantly outperforms baseline methods:

- For CHAIRINGOLF, T-STAR improves the success rate from 0% (with naive policy sequencing) to 56%.
- For TABLELACK, T-STAR improves the success rate from 59% to 87%.

These improvements demonstrate that terminal state regularization effectively addresses the key challenge in skill chaining for long-horizon tasks.

### 7. Applications and Significance

The T-STAR approach has several important implications for robotics and artificial intelligence:

1. **Complex Task Automation**: By enabling robots to reliably perform long-horizon tasks, T-STAR opens the door to applications like furniture assembly, cooking, household chores, and manufacturing processes that require complex sequences of actions.

2. **Reduced Learning Complexity**: Breaking down complex tasks into subtasks and ensuring smooth transitions between them significantly reduces the complexity of learning, making it feasible to train robots for tasks that would be intractable with end-to-end reinforcement learning.

3. **Skill Reuse**: Individual subtask policies can be reused across different complex tasks, enabling more efficient learning and generalization to new scenarios.

4. **Robustness to Variations**: By training policies with wider initiation sets, T-STAR creates more robust behaviors that can handle variations in the environment and object properties.

### 8. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its promising results, T-STAR has several limitations that provide opportunities for future research:

1. **Task Decomposition**: The current approach assumes that the task decomposition is provided manually. Automating the decomposition of complex tasks into appropriate subtasks remains an open challenge.

2. **Simulation-to-Reality Gap**: While the results are impressive in simulation, deploying T-STAR on physical robots would require addressing the sim-to-real gap, particularly for contact-rich manipulation tasks like furniture assembly.

3. **Scalability**: As the number of subtasks increases further, additional techniques may be needed to prevent error accumulation and maintain high success rates.

4. **Dynamic Environments**: The current implementation assumes a relatively static environment. Adapting T-STAR to dynamic environments with moving objects or human collaborators presents additional challenges.

Future work could explore integrating T-STAR with meta-learning approaches to enable faster adaptation to new tasks, developing more sophisticated discriminator architectures to better model initiation sets, and combining T-STAR with hierarchical reinforcement learning to handle even more complex task structures.

### 9. Conclusion

Terminal State Regularization (T-STAR) represents a significant advancement in enabling robots to perform complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks. By addressing the critical challenge of state space explosion in skill chaining through an adversarial learning framework, T-STAR makes it feasible to train robots for tasks that were previously intractable.

The demonstrated success on furniture assembly tasks showcases the potential of this approach for practical applications. As robots become increasingly integrated into our homes, workplaces, and manufacturing facilities, the ability to perform complex sequential tasks will be essential. T-STAR provides a promising framework for developing these capabilities, bringing us closer to robots that can reliably assist with a wide range of human activities.
