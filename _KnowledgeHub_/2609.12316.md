# DATAFARM: Distribution-Aligned Task and Motion Planning for Fine-Tuning Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> DATAFARM makes task and motion planning (TAMP) a useful data source for fine-tuning pretrained VLAs by aligning planner trajectories with the VLA's pretraining distribution. A Gaussian-mixture joint-configuration prior selects IK endpoints, and a frozen trajectory encoder's Mahalanobis cost steers path optimization and retiming. Fine-tuning pi0.5-DROID on its demonstrations lifts average success from 8.3% with raw TAMP to 56.7%, near teleoperation's 61.7%.

## Problem

- Prior TAMP-based demonstration generation trained visuomotor policies from scratch, but successful planner trajectories barely help pretrained VLAs: raw TiPToP data lifts pi0.5-DROID only from 1.7% to 8.3%.
- Planner outputs differ from teleoperated pretraining data in arm postures, motion style and execution speed, a behavioral mismatch that standard planners never optimize against.
- Synthetic-data methods for VLA adaptation, such as the concurrent DREAM, target the deployment workspace or visual diversity and do not use the pretraining distribution of robot behavior as a planning objective.

## Method

- Joint-space endpoint selection: a 16-component ==Gaussian mixture== fit on DROID joint configurations ranks ==IK== solutions, including the rotated parallel-jaw grasp, and picks the arm posture most typical of the reference data.
- Style-aligned path optimization: a ==variational trajectory encoder==, trained to predict 113 motion descriptors from joint positions through jerks, gives a ==Mahalanobis latent cost== added to ==cuRobo=='s objective and optimized by gradients.
- Timing-aligned retiming: a tanh-bounded ==softmax time allocation== over 64 arc-length intervals is optimized with ==Adam== against the same latent cost plus a boundary-speed penalty matching speeds around gripper events.

## Results

- Across three tabletop tasks with 20 demonstrations each, DATAFARM reaches **65%**, **35%** and **70%** success versus **5%**, **5%** and **15%** for raw TAMP, and beats teleoperation on Geometric Constraints (**65%** vs **50%**).
- Ablations: without timing alignment success falls to **0%**, without style alignment to **15.0%**, and without joint-space alignment to **23.3%**. Trajectory-latent overlap with DROID is **96%**, versus **0%** for raw TAMP.
- Cloth folding, excluded from fine-tuning and beyond the planner's ability, keeps **85%** average success versus **90%** for the pretrained policy. On Geometric Constraints, success grows from **65%** with 20 demonstrations to **85%** with 80.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task-valid demonstrations are not automatically good fine-tuning data. Matching the pretrained model's behavioral distribution can matter more than whether the trajectories solve the task.
> - Execution timing is a first-class property of demonstrations for VLAs: the same geometric path run at a mismatched speed can make the data useless to a pretrained action expert.
> - Distribution-overlap metrics alone can mislead: the style ablation kept 89% latent overlap yet reached only 15% success, so alignment should be judged by downstream policy performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses the data bottleneck in robot learning, particularly the difficulty of collecting demonstrations for vision-language-action (VLA) models. VLAs are trained on large collections of robot demonstrations, often obtained through human teleoperation. These models can acquire broad capabilities, including behaviors that are difficult to specify analytically, but collecting additional task-specific demonstrations is costly in human time.

Task and motion planning (TAMP) offers a contrasting source of data. A TAMP system can reason over symbolic task sequences and generate collision-free robot trajectories without requiring a human operator for every demonstration. It is particularly suitable for tasks involving explicit geometric, kinematic, and ordering constraints. However, planner-generated trajectories can differ substantially from human demonstrations and from the trajectories used to pretrain a VLA. They may use unusual robot postures, different movement smoothness, or atypical timing. The paper argues that such behavioral differences can make otherwise successful planner trajectories ineffective as fine-tuning data.

DATAFARM is positioned at the intersection of three research areas. First, it extends planning-based demonstration generation, in which TAMP solutions are used to train visuomotor policies. Second, it relates to demonstration augmentation systems such as MimicGen and DemoGen, although it does not require human demonstrations of the target task. Third, it contributes to synthetic-data methods for VLA adaptation, including approaches based on rendering, simulation, video generation, or workspace reconstruction.

The main distinction is that DATAFARM uses the pretrained model’s original demonstration distribution as a behavioral prior during planning. Rather than treating task validity as the only criterion for a demonstration, it attempts to generate trajectories that are both task-valid and statistically similar to the pretraining data. The planner remains responsible for solving the new task, while the reference data guide how the robot moves.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Central Objective

The central objective is to develop a low-human-effort procedure for generating demonstrations that improve a pretrained VLA on new tasks. The procedure must satisfy two requirements:

1. The generated demonstrations must solve the target task under the robot’s geometric, kinematic, and collision constraints.
2. The demonstrations must resemble the behavioral distribution on which the VLA was pretrained.

The authors evaluate whether this alignment improves the usefulness of TAMP-generated data for fine-tuning.

#### 2.2. Motivation From the Planner–Policy Mismatch

The paper begins with an empirical observation: raw TAMP demonstrations can be successful when executed by the planner but produce little improvement when used to fine-tune a pretrained VLA. In the authors’ initial comparison, fine-tuning the π^0.5-DROID model on successful TiPToP demonstrations increased average success on three tabletop tasks from 1.7% to only 8.3%.

The paper attributes this limited transfer to behavioral distribution mismatch rather than to task failure in the planner. Several forms of mismatch are considered:

- **Joint-space mismatch:** TAMP may select inverse-kinematics solutions or arm postures that are uncommon in the pretraining data.
- **Motion-style mismatch:** Planner trajectories may have different smoothness, path geometry, velocity profiles, or high-frequency corrections than human-operated trajectories.
- **Temporal mismatch:** Planner execution may be too fast, too slow, or timed differently around actions such as grasping and releasing.

The broader goal is not merely to improve performance on planner-compatible tasks. The authors also investigate whether fine-tuning on these new demonstrations preserves capabilities that TAMP cannot perform, such as folding a deformable cloth.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Planning Framework

DATAFARM is integrated into TiPToP, a modular robot manipulation system. TiPToP uses perception models to construct a 3D scene representation, identify object geometries and candidate grasps, and formulate symbolic task goals. Its cuTAMP planner enumerates candidate action sequences, or plan skeletons, such as moving an obstruction before placing another object.

Continuous parameters include grasps, placements, robot configurations, and connecting motions. Inverse kinematics generates multiple candidate configurations for a desired end-effector pose. These candidates may reach the same pose while placing the arm in different postures. Motion segments between endpoints are generated using cuRobo, which optimizes waypoint configurations subject to smoothness, collision, and robot-limit constraints.

DATAFARM adds reference-distribution objectives at three stages: endpoint selection, path optimization, and retiming.

#### 3.2. Joint-Space Alignment

The system first fits a 16-component, full-covariance Gaussian mixture model to robot joint configurations from the DROID pretraining dataset. The model uses 987,631 moving-arm frames from 4,000 reference episodes. Stationary frames are excluded so that long pauses do not dominate the estimated distribution.

For each inverse-kinematics candidate \(q\), DATAFARM computes a configuration cost equal to the negative log probability under this mixture model. The planner selects the candidate with the lowest cost, favoring joint configurations that are common in the reference demonstrations. For parallel-jaw grasps, it also considers an equivalent orientation rotated by 180 degrees around the gripper’s approach axis. This allows the planner to choose among additional kinematically equivalent configurations.

Joint-space alignment does not determine whether a configuration achieves the task. Task validity continues to be enforced by the planner’s inverse-kinematics, collision, placement, and feasibility checks.

#### 3.3. Motion-Style and Temporal Alignment

For whole trajectory segments, the authors train a learned encoder that maps a timed joint trajectory into a 16-dimensional latent representation. The input trajectory is resampled at 15 Hz and includes joint positions, velocities, accelerations, and jerks. The physical sampling interval is retained, so changing the execution speed changes the representation.

The encoder is trained using an auxiliary prediction task. It must predict 113 motion descriptors, including per-joint velocity, acceleration, jerk, range of motion, direction reversals, frequency-band content, path length, straightness, speed statistics, and smoothness measures. A variational regularization term encourages a structured latent space. Random temporal crops of reference episodes are included during training so that the encoder can evaluate segments of varying lengths, similar to those produced by the planner.

After training, the encoder is frozen, and a Gaussian distribution is fitted to embeddings of the reference trajectories and their crops. DATAFARM defines a trajectory cost as the squared Mahalanobis distance between a candidate trajectory’s embedding and this reference Gaussian. This cost captures deviations in motion style and execution timing.

#### 3.4. Path Optimization

With endpoints fixed, the trajectory cost is added to the ordinary TAMP motion objective. The planner optimizes interior waypoints while retaining collision penalties, smoothness costs, and robot-limit constraints. Gradients are propagated through the frozen trajectory encoder, trajectory resampling, standardization, and finite-difference computations.

This stage changes the geometric path and the waypoint sequence. As a result, it can influence the trajectory’s velocities, accelerations, and higher-order motion properties while preserving the task-valid endpoints.

#### 3.5. Retiming

DATAFARM separately optimizes the temporal execution profile while keeping the geometric path fixed. The path is divided into 64 equal arc-length intervals. A parameterized time-scaling function determines how much time is allocated to each interval, while a separate scalar controls total duration.

The same latent trajectory cost is used to optimize timing. The authors also add a boundary-speed penalty because trajectory crops do not reliably represent the slower motions commonly observed immediately before and after gripper events. The target boundary speed is set from reference demonstrations to 0.07 radians per second. Candidate retimings are rejected if they violate velocity or acceleration limits.

The resulting trajectories are executed on a Franka Panda robot with a Robotiq gripper and three stereo cameras. Human assistance is permitted for scene setup and resets, but the demonstration motions are generated and executed autonomously.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Comparison of Demonstration Sources

The evaluation uses three target tasks, each requiring three object moves:

- **Geometric Constraints:** placing three toys on a plate without collisions.
- **Multi-Step Reasoning:** removing an obstructing banana from a tray before placing two toys.
- **Semantic Reasoning:** sorting toys and food items into separate bowls.

For each method and target task, a separate VLA checkpoint is fine-tuned on 20 successful demonstrations and evaluated over 20 fresh trials.

The pretrained π^0.5-DROID model achieves an average target-task success rate of 1.7%. Fine-tuning on raw TiPToP demonstrations increases this to 8.3%, with task-specific success rates of 5%, 5%, and 15%.

DATAFARM raises target-task success to 65% on Geometric Constraints, 35% on Multi-Step Reasoning, and 70% on Semantic Reasoning. The average is 56.7%, while average task progress reaches 84.7%. Human teleoperation demonstrations produce 50%, 45%, and 90% success, respectively, for an average of 61.7%. Thus, DATAFARM performs close to the human-demonstration condition in this evaluation, although it is lower on two of the three tasks and higher on Geometric Constraints.

#### 4.2. Evidence of Distribution Alignment

The authors compare planner-generated trajectories with DROID data in joint-space and learned trajectory-latent projections. In the displayed joint-space contour, 74% of DATAFARM samples fall inside the DROID contour, compared with 66% for raw TAMP and 60% for the method without joint-space alignment.

In trajectory-latent space, 96% of DATAFARM samples fall inside the displayed DROID contour. Raw TAMP has 0% overlap in this projection, while removing style alignment gives 89% and removing timing alignment gives 19%. The authors caution that these are two-dimensional projections and do not establish equality with the full pretraining distribution.

#### 4.3. Ablation Results

All three alignment components contribute to performance, although their effects differ. Removing joint-space alignment reduces average target-task success from 56.7% to 23.3%. Removing style alignment reduces it to 15.0%. Removing timing alignment results in 0% success across all three target tasks.

The failure analysis supports these findings. Without joint-space alignment, unusual arm postures account for a substantial share of failures. Without timing alignment, many trials become stuck or fail to finish within the evaluation horizon. Without style alignment, grasping failures become common, suggesting that motion characteristics influence the policy’s ability to reproduce reliable manipulation behavior.

The ablations also show that high overlap in one representation is not sufficient. The style ablation retains relatively high latent overlap but performs poorly, indicating that alignment metrics alone do not fully capture the task-specific usefulness of demonstrations.

#### 4.4. Retention of Existing Capabilities

The authors evaluate cloth folding as an out-of-distribution capability. The TAMP system cannot perform this deformable-object task, and no cloth-folding examples are included in fine-tuning.

The pretrained model achieves 90% success. After DATAFARM fine-tuning, success is 80% for the Geometric Constraints checkpoint, 85% for Multi-Step Reasoning, and 90% for Semantic Reasoning. The average retained success is therefore 85%. This indicates some performance loss in one setting but substantial preservation overall.

#### 4.5. Demonstration-Budget Scaling

On Geometric Constraints, the pretrained model starts with 0% success and 18% task progress. Twenty DATAFARM demonstrations increase success to 65%. Success reaches 75% with 40 and 60 demonstrations and 85% with 80 demonstrations. Task progress rises from 84% with 20 demonstrations to 97% with 80. These results suggest that additional aligned data can continue to improve performance, although the experiment covers only one task and a limited set of budgets.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper’s main significance is its identification of behavioral compatibility as a factor in using planner-generated demonstrations for VLA fine-tuning. A trajectory can be valid for a task while still being poorly matched to the policy’s learned representation of robot behavior. DATAFARM provides a way to incorporate this compatibility into planning without abandoning the explicit constraints that make TAMP useful.

If the approach generalizes, it could reduce the need for task-specific teleoperation when a new task falls within a planner’s capabilities. It may also provide a mechanism for extending VLAs while preserving capabilities learned during pretraining. The method is relevant to systems in which planners and foundation models have complementary strengths: planning can handle geometry and long-horizon ordering, whereas the VLA can retain broader semantic and deformable-object skills.

The limitations are substantial. DATAFARM inherits the coverage and failure modes of TiPToP, which in this implementation focuses mainly on quasi-static pick-and-place manipulation. It does not yet address contact-rich, non-prehensile, or highly deformable interactions. The method also requires access to representative pretraining demonstrations or a suitable substitute distribution. Its evaluation includes one VLA checkpoint, one robot embodiment, three target tasks, and one retained capability. The experiments use small evaluation sets, and the paper does not report confidence intervals or broader cross-embodiment comparisons. Consequently, the results support the proposed mechanism in this setting but do not yet establish its generality across models, robots, or task families.
