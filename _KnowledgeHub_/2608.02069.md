# Open-DiffLoco: Open-Source Differentiable Learning for Deployable Blind Quadruped Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Open-DiffLoco is an open-source framework that trains deployable blind quadruped locomotion policies with differentiable simulation, implementing Short-Horizon Actor-Critic in MuJoCo XLA plus a Jacobian-Augmented Value Estimation extension that supervises critic Jacobians. Using a minimal differentiable reward set, no reference trajectories, and proprioception-only actor observations, the policy transfers to a Unitree Go2, tracking omnidirectional commands with sub-0.2 m/s RMSE after roughly 20-60 minutes of single-GPU training.

## Problem

- Model-free RL treats the simulator as a black box, relying on high-variance zeroth-order gradients, so locomotion training is sample-inefficient and demands long runs and heavy compute.
- Prior differentiable-simulation locomotion results rely on closed-source or custom-built simulators, blocking community adoption and reproducible end-to-end transfer of such policies to physical hardware.
- Existing differentiable gait rewards impose fixed step-frequency parameters and kinematic references, adding hyperparameters that cap the motion range and suppress emergent high-speed behaviour.

## Method

- ==Short-Horizon Actor-Critic== implemented in ==MuJoCo XLA== with a modular YAML-configured pipeline: interchangeable algorithm and MLP network modules, plus a C++/ROS2 onboard deployment stack exporting policy weights.
- **Jacobian-Augmented Value Estimation (JAVE)** adds a ==gradient-Bellman loss== supervising the critic's observation-space Jacobian, targeted via a learned residual dynamics model and target critic, alongside the standard ==TD-lambda value loss==.
- Minimal differentiable reward set (velocity/yaw tracking, height, upright, joint deviation, action rate) with implicit terrain: per-episode tilted gravity for slopes and contact-scaled ==Ornstein-Uhlenbeck== foot forces for bumps.

## Results

- On a Unitree Go2, the proprioception-only policy tracks omnidirectional velocity commands with root-mean-square error below **0.2 m/s**, exceeds **1 m/s**, and resists lateral pushes and uneven terrain.
- First-order-gradient methods are roughly **100x** more sample efficient than PPO per environment step; JAVE with horizon **32** leads all first-order variants on per-iteration reward too.
- Training consumes under **6 GB** VRAM of **16 GB** on one RTX 5080 and finishes in about **20-60 minutes**, using only **64-256** parallel environments and **8-16M** environment steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense temporal signal from backpropagating through dynamics substitutes for auxiliary shaping rewards, so gait-pattern terms that densify model-free learning become unnecessary rather than merely differentiable.
> - A pointwise-accurate critic can still bias short-horizon policy updates, because the actor gradient depends on the critic's local Jacobian at the truncation boundary, not just its value.
> - Sample efficiency does not imply wall-clock advantage: per-step cost of backpropagating through the simulator keeps first-order training slower than optimised model-free libraries.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) and, more specifically, legged robotics, is an area of increasing research interest due to the versatility of legged platforms in navigating complex and challenging environments. Unlike wheeled systems that are largely confined to flat terrain, legged robots can traverse obstacles, inclines, and stairs, making them suitable for diverse applications in agriculture, search-and-rescue, and extraterrestrial exploration.

Robot locomotion policies are frequently developed using reinforcement learning (RL). Within RL, model-free approaches, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), have been widely adopted. These methods treat the simulation environment as a black box, learning through trial-and-error interactions and relying on zeroth-order gradients (ZoG). While robust and flexible, model-free RL typically suffers from low sample efficiency, requiring extensive training times and significant computational resources. A common practice in model-free RL for complex tasks like locomotion is the use of elaborate reward engineering, which often includes auxiliary rewards and kinematic reference trajectories to guide policy discovery and improve convergence stability.

Differentiable simulation presents an alternative approach by treating the simulator as a glass box, allowing for the computation of highly informative first-order gradients (FoG) through backpropagation through the environment’s dynamics. This significantly improves sample efficiency compared to model-free methods. However, differentiable simulation introduces its own set of challenges: dynamics and reward functions must be strictly differentiable, and the presence of discontinuities, particularly from contact-rich interactions, can lead to numerical instability during gradient calculations. Consequently, existing literature using differentiable simulation has primarily demonstrated policies for low-speed locomotion, and the frameworks employed are often closed-source or custom-built, limiting broader community adoption. Algorithms like Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) have emerged to mitigate the instability of long-horizon backpropagation by truncating differentiable rollouts.

This work addresses the existing limitations by developing an open-source framework that leverages the efficiency of differentiable simulation within a mainstream, accessible simulation ecosystem (MuJoCo XLA – MJX). It aims to enable the training of robust, deployable quadrupedal locomotion policies that overcome the need for complex reward engineering and privileged observations, while providing an accessible platform for researchers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce Open-DiffLoco, an open-source framework designed for training deployable blind quadruped locomotion policies using differentiable simulation. The motivation behind this framework stems from several critical challenges and gaps in current robot locomotion learning:

1. **Overcoming Sample Inefficiency of Model-Free RL**: Traditional model-free RL algorithms, while effective, demand substantial computational resources and time due to their low sample efficiency, making policy development costly and time-consuming. The paper is motivated by the potential of differentiable simulation to provide highly informative first-order gradients, thereby significantly improving sample efficiency.

2. **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Deployment**: While differentiable simulation offers efficiency benefits, the state of the art often relies on closed-source or custom frameworks, hindering the transferability and reproducibility of policies to physical hardware. A key objective is to provide an end-to-end open-source solution that facilitates the deployment of learned policies on real robots, demonstrating the practical applicability of differentiable simulation.

3. **Simplifying Reward Engineering**: Model-free RL for complex locomotion tasks frequently necessitates intricate and often hand-engineered reward functions, including auxiliary rewards (e.g., gait pattern rewards) and reference trajectories. This process can be labor-intensive, introduce additional hyperparameters, and potentially constrain the robot's ability to discover emergent, high-performance behaviors. A motivation is to demonstrate that analytical gradients in differentiable simulation can eliminate the need for such complex reward engineering and reference trajectories, allowing policies to discover stable gaits with a minimal, simplified reward structure.

4. **Enhancing Robustness through Minimalist Observations**: Policies trained with privileged information (e.g., base linear velocity that requires an onboard estimator) can struggle in real-world deployment where such information might be noisy or unavailable. The objective is to train policies that rely solely on onboard proprioceptive observations, making them "blind" to external terrain sensing and more robust for real-world scenarios.

5. **Improving Numerical Stability in Differentiable Simulation**: Differentiable simulation can suffer from numerical instability, especially when dealing with long-horizon backpropagation through contact-rich dynamics, which can amplify perturbations. The work aims to leverage and extend methods like Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) to mitigate this instability, and specifically to propose algorithmic improvements (JAVE) to enhance early policy-gradient training and allow for longer analytical gradient horizons.

In summary, the motivation is to democratize advanced robot locomotion learning by making differentiable simulation accessible, efficient, robust, and deployable on physical hardware, while reducing the complexity of policy design and training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Open-DiffLoco framework is designed with modularity to facilitate adaptation and extensibility. Its core components include:

- **Framework Modularity**: The system utilizes a YAML configuration file as an entry point, allowing users to define embodiment-specific parameters such as the reward function and physical structure, making it adaptable to various robotic platforms. The core learning pipeline supports interchangeable modules for the training algorithm (e.g., SHAC, JAVE) and network architectures (currently multilayer perceptrons, MLPs). Policies can be exported and deployed to real hardware using a custom C++ and ROS2 inference stack.

- **Core Algorithm and Extension**: The framework implements the Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) algorithm in MuJoCo XLA (MJX). SHAC reduces numerical instability by truncating differentiable rollouts to a short horizon (h) and replacing the remaining return with a learned value function.
 - **Jacobian-Augmented Value Estimation (JAVE)**: As an algorithmic extension to SHAC, JAVE is proposed to address potential biases in policy updates even with accurate value estimates, if their local Jacobians are inaccurate. This is motivated by the fact that actor gradients depend not only on the value estimate but also on its gradient at the truncation boundary. JAVE directly supervises the value-function Jacobian using a Bellman-gradient relation (Equation 9). The JAVE critic objective augments the standard TD-λ value loss with a gradient Bellman loss, balancing these components with scalar weights (α_TD, α_GB). The Bellman-gradient target is constructed in critic-observation space, and an estimated one-step observation map (learned dynamics model Fψ) is used to predict the critic-observation residual. This approach allows for asymmetric actor-critic observations, where the actor may receive a smaller, deployment-compatible observation.

- **Reward Functions**: A key aspect of the methodology is the use of a simplified, strictly differentiable reward set. Unlike model-free methods that often rely on statistical smoothing of discontinuities and complex auxiliary rewards, differentiable simulation demands strictly differentiable rewards. The paper argues that analytical gradients provide a dense temporal signal "for free," negating the need for auxiliary shaping rewards like gait patterns (e.g., airtime rewards), which are common in PPO. The reward formulation (Table I) primarily consists of quadratic terms for velocity tracking (x, y, yaw-rate), height, vertical velocity, upright posture, joint deviation from a reference pose, action rate, action magnitude, and roll/pitch angular velocity.

- **Training Cost and Efficiency**: The computational time complexity is O(I·N·H) and VRAM complexity is O(N·H). Training is conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080 GPU, consuming under 6 GB of VRAM and completing in approximately 20–60 minutes, demonstrating high efficiency and accessibility.

- **Observation Space**: The deployed policy removes privileged information, such as base linear velocity, from the actor's observations. The policy relies entirely on onboard proprioception, enhancing its robustness and real-world applicability without requiring external sensors or complex state estimators.

- **Simulation Environment**: Policies are evaluated in MJX using a simulated Unitree Go2 quadruped. The control policy operates at 50 Hz, with the physics engine stepping at 250 Hz (5 substeps per action).
 - **Implicit Terrain Randomization (Appendix III)**: The environment uses implicit terrain models to expose the policy to uneven surfaces without explicit mesh height fields. This includes a per-episode tilted gravity vector for slopes and contact-scaled foot forces based on a differentiated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process for local terrain "bumps."

- **Hardware Deployment**: A custom C++ deployment code, utilizing the `unitree_sdk2` package and optionally ROS2, is provided. This code runs fully onboard a Unitree Go2 EDU robot equipped with a Jetson AGX Orin, allowing for real-world validation of the trained policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings derived from both simulation and real-world experiments:

- **Sample Efficiency and Training Performance**:
 - **Comparison with PPO**: In simulation, all three first-order gradient (FoG) methods (SHAC, JAVE with horizon 16, and JAVE with horizon 32) demonstrated substantially higher sample efficiency (reward gained per environment step) compared to PPO. While PPO (using its native reward set) showed comparable performance in terms of reward per iteration, the FoG methods achieved a similar reward level with significantly fewer environment steps.
 - **JAVE Performance**: Among the FoG methods, JAVE with a horizon length of 32 performed best in terms of sample efficiency. JAVE showed improved early training stability and increased gradient stability compared to SHAC, which permitted the use of longer analytical gradient horizons. This improvement was particularly visible in early training phases.

- **Simplified Reward Structure and Motion Autonomy**: Policies trained with Open-DiffLoco, utilizing a minimalist set of differentiable rewards and without kinematic reference trajectories, successfully discovered stable gait patterns. This finding suggests that analytical gradients provide a sufficiently dense learning signal, reducing or eliminating the need for complex auxiliary rewards typically required by model-free RL algorithms like PPO for reliable convergence.

- **Minimalist Observation Space and Robustness**: The framework enabled the training of a robust policy that relies entirely on onboard proprioception, successfully removing privileged information such as base linear velocity from the actor observations. This contributes to the policy's real-world applicability without reliance on potentially noisy or unavailable external state estimators.

- **Computational Resource Efficiency**: The training process exhibited low computational requirements, completing in approximately 20–60 minutes on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080 GPU and consuming under 6 GB of VRAM. This demonstrates the accessibility of high-performance differentiable policy training without the need for extensive compute clusters.

- **Simulation Velocity Tracking Accuracy**:
 - The trained policy demonstrated accurate omnidirectional velocity tracking in simulation. For slow and 1 m/s profiles, forward velocity and yaw-rate commands were tracked effectively.
 - Lateral velocity tracking accuracy was observed to be traded for precision in other components, especially at higher speeds.
 - The policy achieved a maximum forward speed of 2 m/s, with performance plateauing beyond this limit.

- **Real-World Validation (Unitree Go2)**:
 - **Velocity Tracking**: When deployed on a physical Unitree Go2 quadruped, the policy successfully tracked omnidirectional velocity commands. The root-mean-square error for velocity tracking remained below 0.2 m/s. While not exact, the policy consistently followed the commanded velocities.
 - **Speed and Robustness**: The deployed policy reached speeds above 1 m/s. It also demonstrated robustness to uneven terrain (simulated implicitly during training) and external physical disturbances, such as lateral pushes, allowing the robot to maintain balance and recover.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Open-DiffLoco framework represents a contribution to the field of legged robot locomotion by addressing several limitations of current training methodologies.

- **Enhanced Accessibility and Reproducibility**: As the first reported open-source framework for training deployable locomotion policies using differentiable simulation, Open-DiffLoco increases the accessibility of advanced robot learning techniques. By providing a transparent and modifiable platform built on mainstream tools like MuJoCo XLA, it facilitates research, collaboration, and the reproducibility of results in the robotics community.

- **Reduced Development Complexity**: The demonstration that robust, deployable policies can be learned with a significantly simplified reward structure and without kinematic reference trajectories has implications for robot learning development. This reduces the engineering effort typically associated with complex reward function design, which can be a bottleneck in model-free RL.

- **Improved Efficiency and Resource Utilization**: The framework's ability to achieve high sample efficiency and rapid training times (20–60 minutes) on a single consumer-grade GPU makes advanced robot locomotion learning more attainable for researchers and institutions with limited computational resources. This contrasts with the often prohibitively long training durations and large compute clusters required by traditional model-free methods.

- **Robustness and Practical Deployability**: The successful transfer and validation of policies to physical hardware, capable of omnidirectional velocity tracking, reaching speeds above 1 m/s, and withstanding external disturbances and uneven terrain, underscores the practical utility and robustness of policies trained with differentiable simulation. This indicates a viable pathway for deploying simulation-trained policies in real-world applications where adaptability and resilience are critical.

- **Algorithmic Advancement**: The introduction of Jacobian-Augmented Value Estimation (JAVE) provides an algorithmic extension that enhances the stability of early training and gradient accuracy in first-order policy gradient methods. This improvement could enable the use of longer analytical gradient horizons and contribute to more stable and efficient learning in contact-rich differentiable simulations.

- **Future Research Directions**: The work also highlights areas for future investigation, such as exploring explicit gait transitions, achieving higher locomotion speeds beyond 2 m/s, further optimizing wall-clock training times to compete with highly optimized model-free frameworks, and scaling the number of parallel environments for broader domain randomization. Further theoretical exploration of JAVE's properties and practical implementation details (e.g., annealing schedules for actor dependence) are also identified, suggesting avenues for continued algorithmic development.

In summary, Open-DiffLoco offers a comprehensive, accessible, and efficient solution for developing deployable legged robot locomotion policies, contributing to the broader adoption and advancement of differentiable simulation in robotics research.
