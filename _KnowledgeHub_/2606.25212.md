# RigPI: Dynamic Parameter Identification of Rigid Body via VLM-Seeded Differentiable Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> RigPI identifies dynamic parameters (mass, center of mass, inertia tensor, friction) of unconstrained and multi-link rigid bodies from robot-object interaction. A vision-language model seeds an informed initialization and bounded search space from RGB-D images; NVIDIA Newton differentiable simulation supplies gradients that refine parameters under a pose loss plus bound-penalty. Real-world tests give 1.5-7.2% mass error and stable, predictive trajectory reproduction.

## Problem

- Inverse-dynamics least-squares identification needs designed excitation trajectories and corrupts both regressor and observations under sensing noise, yielding physically inconsistent or unstable estimates.
- Differentiable simulators are computation backbones only: performance stays sensitive to initialization and parameter bounds, with no mechanism for posing well-formed problems from unstructured real data.
- VLM-based physical reasoning operates open-loop or inside planning-oriented action pipelines, giving coarse zero-shot guesses rather than precise quantitative system identification.

## Method

- ==VLM seeding==: Intern-S1 or Gemini-3 Pro reads RGB-D images to infer geometry, volume, internal structure, density and friction, producing initial vector and feasible bounds under uniform-density assumption.
- Differentiable identification loop in ==NVIDIA Newton==: measured wrenches drive forward simulation (==semi-implicit integrator== for free bodies, ==Featherstone== for articulated), ==autodiff== gradients update parameters.
- ==Augmented objective==: pose loss over position and quaternion plus quadratic out-of-bounds penalty weighted 0.1, optimized with a dynamic learning rate scaled two orders below parameter magnitude.

## Results

- Mass relative error drops to **1.50-7.21%** across cube, sphere, apple, drawer, cabinet and oven doors, versus **11-196%** for least-squares and **5.5-53%** for the GradSim baseline.
- Stability across **five** runs: coefficient of variation **5.6-12.5%** for mass, **8.3-25.7%** for center of mass, **11.4-22.4%** for inertia, confirming repeatable convergence.
- Predictive validity under unseen wrenches yields pose losses **0.0998-0.441** and endpoint distances **3.79e-2 to 8.25e-2 m**; ablations show random init diverges to invalid minima while VLM init stays low-loss.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treat foundation-model physical estimates as priors and bounds, not answers: coarse semantic guesses become accurate when a differentiable physics gradient refines them.
> - Bound penalties derived from perception act as implicit constraints, steering optimization away from physically invalid local minima without specialized constrained solvers.
> - Constrained motion limits observability: joints that cannot excite all rotational directions leave some inertia components poorly identified regardless of optimizer quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate dynamic parameter identification of manipulated objects is a foundational requirement for developing advanced robotic manipulation capabilities, such as predictive control, and for constructing digital twins that faithfully simulate real-world physics. This research area traditionally grapples with challenges stemming from sensing noise, modeling errors, and the inherent difficulty of obtaining comprehensive prior knowledge about object properties.

Traditional approaches to dynamic parameter identification often rely on inverse-dynamics formulations. These methods estimate inertial parameters by modeling joint torques as linear functions, typically using least-squares or robust variants. While effective in structured environments, these techniques are sensitive to measurement noise and necessitate carefully designed excitation trajectories to ensure the regression problem is well-posed. Furthermore, their underlying linear assumptions and simplified contact/friction models frequently prove insufficient in complex, contact-rich manipulation scenarios, potentially leading to physically inconsistent or ill-posed parameter estimates.

Recent advancements have shifted focus toward differentiable simulation-based identification schemes. These methods evaluate candidate parameters by performing forward simulations of the system's time evolution, leveraging analytic gradients from physics engines. Modern differentiable physics simulators, such as DiffTaichi, Dojo, and NVIDIA Newton, provide GPU-accelerated computation for efficient forward and backward passes, supporting gradient-based optimization in environments featuring complex geometry and collisions. However, these simulators primarily serve as computational backbones; their performance in parameter optimization remains sensitive to initialization quality and the definition of parameter bounds. They often lack systematic mechanisms for constructing well-posed identification problems from unstructured real-world sensor data. Previous works in differentiable physics for robotics have largely concentrated on inertial parameters of single rigid bodies or have employed data-driven models that may lack physical interpretability.

Concurrently, progress in foundation models, particularly Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has enabled inference of physical properties from visual data. Early deep learning approaches regressed properties like mass from simulated interactions but often faced generalization limitations. VLMs offer capabilities for zero-shot, common-sense physical reasoning. However, current VLM-based systems are not typically designed for precise real-world system identification; they tend to operate in open-loop settings or primarily support task planning rather than quantitative parameter estimation. Nevertheless, studies indicate that visual priors can be integrated with physical inference beyond semantic reasoning.

RigPI addresses the gap between VLM-derived high-level semantic priors and high-fidelity differentiable simulation. It aims to integrate these diverse information sources—visual priors, force-torque measurements, and motion observations—within a gradient-based optimization framework. By leveraging VLMs for informed initialization and constraining the search space, and then refining these parameters using gradients from a differentiable physics simulator, RigPI seeks to overcome the limitations of methods relying solely on either semantic priors or differentiable simulation. The work extends the scope of differentiable system identification to both unconstrained and multi-link rigid bodies, preserving the physical interpretability of the identified parameters.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a systematic framework, named RigPI, for the accurate and stable identification of dynamic parameters for external objects, encompassing both unconstrained rigid bodies and multi-link rigid bodies, during robot-object interaction. This framework aims to integrate diverse data sources and prior knowledge within a gradient-based optimization pipeline.

The motivation behind RigPI stems from several challenges inherent in dynamic parameter identification:

- **Necessity for Advanced Robotic Manipulation**: Accurate knowledge of an object's mass, inertia, and frictional properties is fundamental for advanced robotic tasks such as predictive control, compliant manipulation, and the creation of high-fidelity digital twins. Without precise parameters, robots cannot reliably predict object behavior, limiting their ability to perform complex interactions.
- **Limitations of Traditional Identification Methods**: Existing inverse-dynamics based methods are highly susceptible to measurement noise and modeling inaccuracies. They require specific, often labor-intensive, excitation trajectories and frequently yield physically inconsistent or unstable parameter estimates, particularly in contact-rich or complex environments.
- **Challenges in Real-World Data Acquisition**: Acquiring physically consistent inertial and frictional properties from real-world robot-object interactions is challenging due to inherent sensing noise, cumulative modeling errors, and the often-limited prior knowledge available about novel objects.
- **Bridging the Gap in Differentiable Simulation**: While differentiable physics simulators offer powerful tools for gradient-based optimization in complex scenarios, they are not complete identification solutions. Their performance is often sensitive to the initial parameter guess and the bounds of the search space. There is a need for mechanisms to construct well-posed optimization problems from unstructured real-world data and to effectively guide the optimization process.
- **Leveraging High-Level Priors**: Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can infer common-sense physical properties from visual data. However, their outputs are typically qualitative or coarse and lack the precision required for robotic control. The motivation is to integrate these high-level, visually-derived priors as informed initializations and search space constraints, rather than terminal estimates, to guide a more precise, physics-grounded optimization.
- **Ensuring Physical Plausibility and Stability**: A key objective is to ensure that identified parameters are not only accurate but also physically consistent and stable across multiple identification runs. This requires a robust optimization strategy that can mitigate noise sensitivity and avoid convergence to physically implausible solutions.

By addressing these motivations, RigPI aims to provide a reliable, automatic, and systematic framework that enhances the robot's understanding of its environment, thereby enabling more adaptive and intelligent manipulation behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RigPI is structured as a real-to-sim-to-real framework for identifying the physical parameters of rigid objects. Its approach combines data acquisition from real-world robot-object interactions with VLM-seeded differentiable simulation for parameter optimization.

**A. Object State Observation**
The initial stage involves gathering experimental data from a robotic manipulator interacting with the object of interest. This interaction is performed on either an unconstrained rigid body or a rigid part of a multi-link body. For each interaction, the robot records:
- **Wrench Data (f, τ)**: A 3D force vector ($f \in \mathbb{R}^3$) and a 3D torque vector ($\tau \in \mathbb{R}^3$) applied to the object. These are typically measured at the gripper base and transformed to the object's center of mass.
- **Object Pose (x, q)**: The position ($x \in \mathbb{R}^3$) of the object's center of mass and its orientation, represented by a unit quaternion ($q \in SO(3)$). For multi-link bodies, the focus is on the interacting rigid part.

The collected dataset, denoted $D = \{(f_k, \tau_k, x_k, q_k)\}_{k=1}^N$, provides a time-series record of the object's dynamic behavior under controlled manipulation.

**B. Parameter Initialization and Optimization**
Following data collection, the framework identifies the object's dynamic parameters by minimizing a loss function that quantifies the discrepancy between simulated and observed behavior. This process involves:

1. **Dynamic Parameters**: The parameters identified include:
 - **Mass (m)**: Scalar translational inertia.
 - **Center of Mass (com)**: 3D vector for mass distribution.
 - **Inertia Tensor (I)**: 3x3 symmetric positive-definite matrix for rotational inertia.
 - **Coefficient of Surface Friction (μ)**: Scalar modeling Coulomb friction.
 For multi-link bodies, this vector is extended to cover parameters for all constituent links and joints. All identifiable parameters are vectorized into $\theta = (m, \text{com}, I, \mu)$.

2. **VLM-Seeded Parameter Initialization and Search Space Definition**:
 - To address the sensitivity of optimization to initial guesses, RigPI utilizes Vision-Language Models (VLMs), such as Intern-S1 and Gemini-3 Pro.
 - RGB-D images of the target object are input to the VLM, which infers geometric properties (pose, scale, volume, internal structure) and material characteristics (density, friction coefficient).
 - Assuming a uniform mass distribution, these inferences yield an informed initial parameter vector $\theta^{(0)} = (\hat{m}_0, [\text{com}_0], \hat{I}_0, \hat{\mu}_0)$ and define a plausible search space $C = [\theta_{\min}, \theta_{\max}]$ for each parameter. This two-stage strategy, where VLM provides the first "stage" of informed guesses, helps guide the subsequent gradient-based refinement.

3. **Differentiable Simulation**:
 - The NVIDIA Newton physics engine is used for differentiable simulation.
 - The collected wrench data $(f_k, \tau_k)$ are applied in the simulator to propagate the object's state over time, given the current parameter guess $\theta$.
 - A semi-implicit integrator is used for unconstrained rigid bodies, while a Featherstone integrator is employed for multi-link rigid bodies. The differentiable nature of the engine allows for the computation of gradients with respect to the parameters.

4. **Augmented Loss Function**:
 - An augmented loss function is defined to guide the optimization process.
 - **Pose Discrepancy Term ($L_{\text{pose}}(\theta)$)**: Measures the difference between the simulated poses ($x_k(\theta), q_k(\theta)$) and the empirically observed poses ($x_k, q_k$):
 $L_{\text{pose}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N (\|x_k(\theta) - x_k\|^2 + \|q_k(\theta) - q_k\|^2)$.
 - **Penalty Term ($P(\theta, C)$)**: Enforces the parameter bounds defined by $C = [\theta_{\min}, \theta_{\max}]$. It imposes a quadratic penalty if any parameter $\theta_i$ falls outside its valid range:
 $P(\theta, C) = \sum_{i=1}^d (\max\{0, \theta_i - \theta_{\max,i}\}^2 + \max\{0, \theta_{\min,i} - \theta_i\}^2)$.
 - **Total Loss ($L_{\text{total}}(\theta)$)**: The final objective function to be minimized is a weighted sum of the pose discrepancy and penalty terms:
 $L_{\text{total}}(\theta) = L_{\text{pose}}(\theta) + \lambda P(\theta, C)$, where $\lambda$ is a weighting hyperparameter (empirically set to 0.1).

5. **Gradient-Based Optimization**:
 - An iterative gradient descent algorithm is employed to find the optimal parameters $\theta^*$.
 - The gradient $\nabla_\theta L_{\text{total}}(\theta)$ is computed efficiently through the automatic differentiation capabilities of the NVIDIA Newton engine.
 - The parameter update rule is: $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} - \alpha^{(t)} \nabla_\theta L_{\text{total}}(\theta^{(t)})$, where $\alpha^{(t)}$ is the learning rate.
 - A **dynamic learning rate** strategy is used, where $\alpha^{(t)}$ is scaled such that the update magnitude is approximately two orders of magnitude smaller than the current parameter scale. This approach aims to balance convergence speed and stability, ensuring that updates are neither too aggressive nor too slow. The penalty term generates steep gradients that implicitly steer the optimization away from invalid parameter regions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental results demonstrating RigPI's performance in accuracy, stability, and predictive validity, along with ablation studies to support key design choices.

**A. Accuracy and Stability of the Identification Process**
- **Parameter Accuracy**: RigPI's identified parameters for mass, center of mass (com), principal moments of inertia ($I_{xx}, I_{yy}, I_{zz}$), and selected joint parameters exhibited improvements in accuracy compared to VLM initial guesses, the traditional Least-Square baseline, and the GradSim baseline. For instance, in the Cube object, RigPI achieved a mass error of 1.50%, significantly lower than 14.00% for Least-Square and 9.00% for GradSim. Similar trends were observed across unconstrained rigid bodies (Cube, Sphere, Apple) and multi-link rigid bodies (Drawer, Cabinet Door, Oven Door).
- **Limitations for Multi-Link Bodies**: The paper notes that some moments of inertia for multi-link rigid bodies showed comparatively lower accuracy. This is attributed to the limited range of joint motion during manipulation, which may not sufficiently excite all directions, thus challenging precise estimation.
- **Stability**: Stability, quantified by the Coefficient of Variation (CoV) across five independent runs, was assessed for parameters such as friction coefficients and com locations. RigPI achieved low CoV values across various objects, indicating consistent and reliable parameter convergence. For example, for the Apple object, RigPI with Gemini-3 Pro initialization showed CoV values of 7.3% for mass, 11.0% for com, 11.4% for inertia, and 7.3% for friction.

**B. Predictive Validity with Identified Parameters**
- To evaluate the practical utility of the identified parameters ($\theta^*$), they were used in simulation to predict object motion under novel, unseen robot-applied forces and torques.
- **Trajectory Reproduction**: The predicted trajectories were compared against real-world robot measurements. The quantitative assessment, using the $L_{pose}$ loss (Equation 4), showed low loss values across all tested objects (e.g., Cube: 9.98e-2, Sphere: 2.93e-1, Apple: 1.30e-1). This indicates that the identified parameters effectively captured the objects' dynamics and could accurately predict their responses under new interactions.
- **Qualitative Visualization**: Visualizations of simulated trajectories demonstrated a close correspondence with the ground-truth real-world trajectories, providing qualitative evidence for the predictive capability.

**C. Ablation Study**
- **Effectiveness of VLM Priors**:
 - VLM-derived initial parameter estimates (using Gemini 2.5 Pro) were found to be closer to the ground truth compared to randomized initialization and geometry-aware initialization (based on point clouds). For instance, for the Cube, VLM mass estimation had a 170.0% relative error compared to 212.5% for point cloud.
 - VLM guidance during optimization resulted in a "low and consistent loss" curve, contributing to stable convergence. In contrast, randomized initialization, while sometimes appearing to converge well, often led to physically invalid local minima and high variability, with many runs diverging dramatically to extreme, physically incorrect values (which were excluded from the reported averages). This suggests that VLM priors are instrumental in regularizing the optimization process.
- **Effect of Dynamic Learning Rate ($\alpha$)**:
 - Comparison of fixed learning rates (0.01, 0.1) with a dynamic learning rate strategy demonstrated that the dynamic approach facilitated "fast and stable convergence."
 - A small fixed $\alpha$ resulted in slow convergence, while a large fixed $\alpha$ often led to unstable optimization and potentially physically invalid parameter solutions. The dynamic $\alpha$ adaptively balances the step magnitude, thereby promoting efficient and robust optimization convergence without necessarily altering the final accuracy of the estimate significantly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RigPI presents an approach to the dynamic parameter identification problem in robotics, integrating vision-language model (VLM) semantic priors with gradient-based optimization through differentiable physics simulation. This work has several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Addressing the Ill-Posed Problem**: The research addresses the inherent ill-posedness of dynamic parameter identification by transforming it into a constrained optimization problem. The integration of VLM-derived priors provides critical initial guidance and search space bounds, which helps to regularize the optimization and reduce sensitivity to noise and initial conditions.
- **Multimodal Information Integration**: RigPI demonstrates an effective synergy between high-level visual and linguistic understanding (via VLMs) and low-level physical feedback (force/torque and motion data). This multimodal integration allows the system to leverage common-sense knowledge for informed parameter initialization while ensuring precision through physically grounded simulation and gradient-based refinement.
- **Improved Accuracy and Stability**: The experimental results indicate that RigPI yields parameters with higher accuracy compared to traditional methods and other differentiable simulation baselines. The low coefficient of variation in identified parameters underscores the stability and repeatability of the proposed framework, which are critical for real-world robotic deployment.
- **Enhanced Predictive Validity**: The ability of the identified parameters to accurately predict object trajectories under novel interactions is a significant outcome. This predictive capability is foundational for model-predictive control, enabling robots to anticipate and adapt to object dynamics in complex manipulation tasks.
- **Applicability to Diverse Objects**: RigPI's demonstrated performance on both unconstrained rigid bodies and multi-link articulated structures (e.g., drawers, cabinet doors) expands the scope of objects for which accurate dynamic models can be automatically acquired. This increases the versatility of robotic systems in unstructured environments.
- **Advancing Digital Twin Construction**: By enabling the creation of high-fidelity physical models from real-world interaction data, RigPI contributes to the construction of more faithful digital twins. Such digital representations are valuable for simulation, testing, and teleoperation, reducing the reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world experiments.
- **Future Directions in Robotic Autonomy**: The framework provides a basis for future research in autonomous parameter discovery and continuous learning. Integrating these dynamic models into robot planning and control frameworks could lead to more intelligent, adaptive, and dexterous robotic manipulation systems, capable of handling novel objects with minimal prior configuration.
