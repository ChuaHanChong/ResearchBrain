# ASPIRE: Agentic /Skills Discovery for Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> ASPIRE, a collaborative effort by researchers from NVIDIA, UMich, UIUC, UC Berkeley, and CMU, introduces a continual learning system for robotics that autonomously discovers, refines, and accumulates reusable skills. The framework significantly improves task success rates across various simulation benchmarks, facilitates zero-shot generalization to novel tasks, and demonstrates effective sim-to-real transfer by reducing real-world robot programming and debugging effort.

## Problem

- Traditional robot programming is complex, involving orchestration of multimodal perception, management of contact dynamics, and adaptation to diverse environments.
- Existing robotic coding agents often rely on simplistic, coarse task-level feedback, hindering precise failure attribution and effective debugging strategies.
- Current systems generally lack mechanisms for accumulating experience across tasks, preventing the agent from building reusable knowledge or improving performance over time.

## Method

- **ASPIRE** integrates a ==closed-loop robot execution engine== that provides fine-grained, ==per-primitive multimodal execution traces== for detailed failure diagnosis.
- It maintains a dynamic, continually expanding ==skill library== that distills validated repair knowledge and recovery patterns into reusable, transferable forms.
- An ==evolutionary search== procedure is incorporated to broaden the exploration of executable robot programs and encourage diverse repair hypotheses, preventing local optimization traps.

## Results

- Achieved substantial improvements on LIBERO-Pro benchmarks, with gains up to **77%** on Object perturbations and **42.5%** on Spatial perturbations over strongest baselines.
- Demonstrated strong zero-shot transfer to novel, long-horizon tasks (LIBERO-Pro Long), outperforming prior methods like CaP-Agent0 and VLA models with **23%** and **38%** success rates on position and task perturbations, respectively.
- Provided initial evidence of sim-to-real transfer, reducing the token cost for generating successful real-robot programs (e.g., from **61.94M** to **6.58M** tokens for 'lift soda can') and enabling success on tasks where naive baselines failed (e.g., 'open/push drawer').

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fine-grained, per-primitive multimodal execution traces are crucial for enabling autonomous agents to accurately localize failures and synthesize targeted repairs in complex robotic tasks.
> - The systematic accumulation of validated debugging and recovery patterns into a persistent skill library significantly enhances zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks and reduces future debugging effort.
> - An evolutionary search mechanism effectively promotes the exploration of diverse program strategies, allowing agents to overcome challenging failures and discover alternative solutions beyond incremental patching.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics programming has historically presented significant challenges, encompassing the orchestration of multimodal perception, management of complex physical contact dynamics, and adaptation to diverse environment configurations and execution failures. Recent advancements in software engineering agents, particularly those leveraging large language models (LLMs), have demonstrated capabilities in autonomously inspecting execution traces, localizing failures, revising implementations, and improving through iterative interaction with execution environments. This progress has influenced robotics research, leading to the development of "code-as-policy" systems. These systems aim to compose perception modules, planning APIs, and control primitives into executable robot programs, allowing for explicit representation and potential inspection, editing, and debugging of robot behaviors.

However, existing robotic coding agents often encounter fundamental limitations. They typically rely on simplistic execution environments that provide only coarse, task-level feedback. This lack of fine-grained diagnostic information hinders an agent's ability to precisely attribute failures, distinguishing between issues related to perception, grasping, motion planning, or recovery. Consequently, effective debugging and repair strategies are difficult to formulate. Furthermore, current systems generally lack mechanisms for accumulating experience across tasks. Once a task is completed, any discovered fixes or recovery strategies are typically discarded, preventing the agent from building reusable knowledge or improving its performance over time. This approach contrasts with human robotics engineers, who progressively internalize transferable debugging experience and recovery strategies, becoming more effective with accumulated knowledge.

ASPIRE addresses these identified gaps by introducing a continual learning system designed to overcome the limitations of coarse feedback and lack of experience accumulation. It aims to bridge the gap between human debugging and robot self-improvement by enabling agents to autonomously diagnose failures using detailed execution traces, synthesize repairs, validate corrected behaviors, and consolidate successful recovery patterns into a persistent, reusable skill library. This positions ASPIRE as an advancement in agentic robot control, moving beyond fixed pipelines and single-task learning towards a more open-ended, self-improving paradigm. The work contributes to the development of more robust, adaptive, and generalizable robotic systems by focusing on the active learning and transfer of debugging knowledge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for ASPIRE stems from the inherent difficulties associated with traditional robot programming and the limitations observed in contemporary code-as-policy systems.
- **Challenges in Robot Programming:** The process of programming robots is complex, involving the coordination of diverse sensory inputs (multimodal perception), managing intricate physical interactions (contact dynamics), adapting to varying environmental conditions, and robustly handling execution failures. This complexity necessitates highly skilled human intervention for development and debugging.
- **Limitations of Existing Code-as-Policy Systems:** While language models have enabled robots to generate and execute code-based policies, these systems are often hampered by the quality of feedback they receive. They typically obtain only coarse, task-level success or failure signals. This limited feedback prevents the agent from understanding the root causes of failures, such as whether an issue lies in perception, an unstable grasp, a planning error, or a recovery failure. Without fine-grained diagnostic traces, the agent's ability to localize failures and devise targeted repair strategies is restricted.
- **Lack of Continual Learning and Knowledge Accumulation:** A significant deficiency in many current robotic agents is their inability to accumulate and leverage experience across different tasks. Discovered fixes and effective recovery strategies are often treated as ephemeral solutions for a single task and are not consolidated into reusable knowledge. Consequently, an agent addressing a new task does not benefit from prior debugging efforts, essentially starting anew each time.
- **Emulating Human Expert Behavior:** Human robotics engineers iteratively debug programs by replaying executions, inspecting detailed outputs (e.g., perception, motion trajectories), pinpointing failing subsystems, and revising implementations. Crucially, they internalize these experiences into transferable knowledge (e.g., grasp recovery heuristics, navigation strategies), which enhances their effectiveness over time. The motivation behind ASPIRE is to imbue robotic agents with a similar capacity for experience accumulation and self-improvement.

Based on these motivations, ASPIRE sets forth several key objectives:
- **Autonomous Program Generation and Refinement:** To develop a system that can autonomously write and iteratively refine robot control programs within a code-as-policy framework.
- **Discovery and Reusability of Skills:** To enable the automated discovery and distillation of validated repairs and recovery patterns into a continually expanding, reusable skill library. These skills are intended to persist and be transferable across multiple tasks, diverse simulation and real-world environments, and different robot embodiments.
- **Closed-Loop, Open-Ended Learning:** To establish an open-ended learning loop where the agent can autonomously diagnose failures using fine-grained multimodal execution traces, synthesize appropriate repairs, and validate the effectiveness of these repairs.
- **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** To improve the success rate of robots on various manipulation tasks, particularly those involving perturbations, contact-rich interactions, and long-horizon sequences, by leveraging accumulated skills for rapid adaptation.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** To demonstrate that the accumulated skill library facilitates strong zero-shot generalization to novel, unseen long-horizon tasks, reducing the need for extensive test-time reasoning and retries.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To provide initial evidence that skills discovered and refined in simulation environments can transfer to real-world robot systems, thereby reducing the manual programming and debugging effort required for physical robots, even when facing different embodiments and robot APIs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ASPIRE operates as a self-improving continual learning robotic system, structured around an open-ended learning loop. This loop integrates three primary components: a closed-loop robot execution engine, a continually expanding skill library, and an evolutionary search procedure. The system employs a coordinator-actor architecture to manage parallel learning processes. A central coordinator maintains the shared skill library and dispatches individual actor coding agents to specific tasks. Each actor then works within the robot execution engine to write, execute, diagnose, and repair robot programs.

#### 3.1. Robot Execution Engine

The robot execution engine is designed to provide fine-grained, per-primitive multimodal traces, significantly enhancing the agent’s ability to debug. Unlike traditional systems that offer coarse, task-level feedback, ASPIRE's engine records detailed information for each perception, planning, grasping, and control call. This includes:
- **API Invocation Details:** The specific API called, its input parameters, and its output values.
- **Execution Status:** The return status of each primitive call, indicating success or failure.
- **Multimodal Evidence:** Relevant visual and sensor data such as RGB keyframes (images immediately before and after the primitive call), perception overlays, candidate grasp poses, detected object poses, and motion planning results.
This detailed tracing enables the agent to selectively inspect specific primitive logs, systematically localize the source of a failure, and validate its repairs through re-execution. For instance, if a navigation command results in a `PLANNING_ERROR`, the engine's traces allow the agent to pinpoint the issue to collision avoidance triggered by an infeasible target pose rather than misperceived object locations.

#### 3.2. Skill Library

ASPIRE maintains a dynamic skill library that accumulates validated repair knowledge from the agent's debugging experiences. This library does not store entire task programs but rather heterogeneous repair knowledge, distilled into reusable, transferable forms. Examples of stored skills include:
- **Localization Heuristics:** Strategies for disambiguating objects or improving perception prompts.
- **Grasping Constraints:** Adaptive grasp strategies based on object geometry or context.
- **Navigation Recovery Strategies:** Methods to overcome motion planning failures, such as multi-angle approach routines to navigate around obstacles.
- **Motion Primitives:** Reusable low-level action patterns, like a linear push for flat objects.
- **Debugging Workflows:** Generalizable patterns for diagnosing specific failure signatures.
Skills are generated through a process where the coding agent diagnoses a failure from execution traces, patches the program, validates the fix on debugging configurations, and then the coordinator admits reusable patterns into the shared library. Each skill is represented as compact in-context guidance, detailing the failure signature, the conditions under which it should be applied, the repair strategy, and, when beneficial, a representative code sketch. This structured representation allows future actors to retrieve and apply validated repairs, promoting zero-shot transfer and enabling cross-embodiment generalization from simulation to real robots. The coordinator audits actor-reported findings to ensure compliance with API policies and reusability before admitting new skills.

#### 3.3. Evolutionary Search

To prevent the agent from becoming stuck in local repair loops (i.e., repeatedly patching the same failing strategy), ASPIRE incorporates an evolutionary search procedure. This mechanism broadens the exploration of executable robot programs and encourages diverse repair hypotheses. The process involves:
- **Candidate Program Generation:** In each round, the coding agent proposes a population of $K$ candidate programs. These candidates are conditioned on the current state of the skill library, the best-performing programs from previous rounds, and residual failure traces.
- **Parallel Execution and Evaluation:** Each candidate program is executed within the robot execution engine, yielding task outcomes and new diagnostic traces.
- **Selection and Iteration:** The next generation of programs is conditioned on the best-performing programs from the current round and their associated failure modes. This iterative selection process promotes the exploration of distinct strategies rather than solely refining existing ones.
The evolutionary search continues until a candidate program successfully solves the debugging configurations or a predefined search budget is exhausted. Upon conclusion of the search, validated repairs from successful candidates are admitted into the skill library, provided they demonstrate generalization across environment variations and tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ASPIRE was evaluated across a range of simulation benchmarks and demonstrated initial evidence of real-robot transfer. The primary coding agent used for simulation benchmarks was Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.6, operating within the CaP-X framework. For real-robot transfer, OpenAI Codex GPT-5.5 was employed on a bimanual YAM manipulation station. The evaluation compared ASPIRE against prior coding agents (CaP-Agent0) and end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies (OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$).

#### 4.1. Main Evaluation Results on Simulation Benchmarks

ASPIRE exhibited improved performance across three benchmark families:
- **LIBERO-Pro (Short-Horizon Robustness):** On tasks involving object, goal, and spatial perturbations, ASPIRE showed substantial improvements. Averaging the Position and Task perturbation axes, ASPIRE achieved gains of up to 77% on Object perturbations, 41.5% on Goal perturbations, and 42.5% on Spatial perturbations over the strongest baselines in each suite. Specifically, ASPIRE reached 98% success on Object-Pos, 95% on Object-Task, 81% on Goal-Pos, 45% on Goal-Task, 51% on Spatial-Pos, and 60% on Spatial-Task, surpassing both CaP-Agent0 and VLA models.
- **Robosuite (Contact-Rich Manipulation):** For single- and dual-arm manipulation tasks, ASPIRE maintained near-saturated performance on simpler tasks and significantly improved complex bimanual tasks. On the bimanual handover task, ASPIRE's success rate increased from 20% to 92%, indicating improved handling of contact-rich interactions.
- **BEHAVIOR-1K (Long-Horizon Household Tasks):** In long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks like "navigate-and-pick-up-soda-can" and "navigate-and-pick-up-radio," ASPIRE outperformed human experts and CaP-Agent0. For "navigate-and-pick-up-radio," ASPIRE increased task success from 56% (CaP-Agent0) to 88%.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Transfer to Unseen Tasks

The research investigated the zero-shot transfer capabilities of skills accumulated on LIBERO-90 to novel, held-out LIBERO-Pro Long tasks.
- ASPIRE, leveraging its skill library (N=90 tasks), achieved 23% success on position perturbations and 38% on task perturbations in LIBERO-Pro Long. This significantly outperformed prior methods like CaP-Agent0 (overall 4% success) and $\pi_{0.5}$ (overall 5% success), which rely on test-time reasoning and retries.
- A consistent trend was observed where zero-shot success rates increased with the size of the accumulated skill library, demonstrating that validated repairs from shorter-horizon tasks contribute to solving more complex, longer-horizon compositions.

#### 4.3. Real-Robot Skill Transfer Across Embodiments

To assess the practical utility of simulation-discovered skills, a study was conducted on a real bimanual robot with a different embodiment and API. The goal was to determine if retrieving simulation-learned skills could reduce real-world debugging effort.
- For tasks such as "put bowl on plate," "lift soda can," and "open/push drawer," providing simulation-discovered skills as in-context guidance consistently reduced the token cost for generating a successful real-robot program.
- For "lift soda can," total tokens decreased from 61.94M to 6.58M, and success improved from 13/20 to 19/20.
- For "open/push drawer," the no-skill baseline exhausted a budget of 334.917M tokens without success (0/20), whereas with skill guidance, ASPIRE achieved 11/20 success with 81.67M total tokens.
These results indicate that selected failure-derived skills can guide real-robot program synthesis, reducing debugging effort and enabling success across different robot embodiments and APIs.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies on LIBERO-Pro elucidated the contributions of ASPIRE's core components:
- **Robot Execution Engine:** This component provided the largest average improvement, increasing the macro-average success rate from 14% (base system without engine or evolutionary search) to 62% on LIBERO-Pro. This highlights the importance of fine-grained execution traces for effective debugging.
- **Evolutionary Search:** After the gains from the robot execution engine, evolutionary search further improved performance, raising the macro-average success to 72% when both components were active. This demonstrates its role in exploring diverse repair hypotheses and tackling harder residual tasks.
- **Evolutionary Search Iterations:** Performance on low-performing tasks steadily improved over the first few rounds of evolutionary search, indicating that exploring multiple repair hypotheses effectively recovers alternatives. Subsequent rounds showed diminishing but still positive returns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ASPIRE represents a methodological advancement in the field of robotic control and learning, addressing several long-standing challenges in programming and deploying robots. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Continual Learning for Robots:** The primary significance of ASPIRE is its establishment of a continual learning system for robotics. By autonomously discovering, refining, and accumulating reusable skills into a growing library, ASPIRE equips robots with the capacity to learn from their own failures and improve over time. This mimics human expert behavior in engineering and debugging, moving beyond static, pre-programmed behaviors towards more adaptive and experience-driven robotic intelligence.

- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The system's ability to self-diagnose failures using fine-grained multimodal traces and synthesize targeted repairs significantly enhances the robustness of robot programs. This is particularly evident in its improved performance on tasks with perturbations, complex contact dynamics, and long-horizon requirements, where traditional methods often struggle. The learned skills contribute to more reliable operation in varied and unpredictable environments.

- **Accelerated Development and Generalization:** The reusable skill library facilitates both within-task and cross-task generalization. The demonstrated zero-shot transfer to unseen long-horizon tasks, where ASPIRE substantially outperformed prior methods without additional debugging, indicates a reduction in the need for extensive, task-specific engineering. This capacity for generalization could accelerate the deployment of robots into new scenarios by leveraging previously acquired knowledge.

- **Reduced Sim-to-Real Gap:** The initial evidence of sim-to-real transfer, where skills discovered in simulation reduced real-robot programming effort and enabled success on tasks where naive debugging failed, is a crucial step towards practical robot deployment. By providing a mechanism for simulation-learned knowledge to guide real-world robot code generation and debugging, ASPIRE offers a pathway to mitigate the costs and complexities associated with real-robot experimentation and fine-tuning, even when facing different embodiments and APIs.

- **Advancement in Agentic AI for Robotics:** ASPIRE contributes to the broader field of agentic AI by demonstrating a sophisticated write-execute-debug loop in an embodied setting. The system's capacity for autonomous failure attribution, repair synthesis, and validation, coupled with mechanisms for exploration (evolutionary search) and knowledge retention (skill library), represents progress in developing more autonomous and intelligent robotic agents.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** While demonstrating substantial capabilities, ASPIRE also highlights avenues for future research. These include scaling autonomous real-world lifelong learning (requiring robust success detection, safe reset, and calibration), validating the approach with smaller or weaker LLMs, enabling agents to propose and incorporate new robot primitives, and developing more sophisticated long-term memory management for the skill library (e.g., retrieval, pruning, ranking, re-validation). Addressing the computational intensity of the debug and evolutionary search loop will also be important for widespread scalability.

In conclusion, ASPIRE represents a significant step towards creating more autonomous, robust, and continually improving robotic systems by integrating sophisticated debugging capabilities with a scalable mechanism for knowledge accumulation and transfer.
