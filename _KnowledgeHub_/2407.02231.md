# Safety-Driven Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Cobots: A Sim2Real Approach

> [!summary] Summary
> Safety-Driven DRL (SD-DRL) embeds ISO 10218 safety requirements, notably the 250mm/s safety-rated reduced collision speed, directly into the reward function of a TQC agent trained in Gazebo for UR5 grasping, then zero-shot transfers to a real cobot. It adds penalties for collisions, obstacle contact, speed limits, and IK failures, raising success by 16.5% in simulation and 2.5% on hardware, certified at SIL 2.

## Problem

- Sparse or purely goal-driven reward functions in robotic DRL optimize task achievement alone and never penalize unsafe actions needed for real industrial deployment.
- Prior safe-RL work uses fixed rule-driven shields or external safety controllers rather than making regulatory speed and collision limits part of the learning objective itself.
- Learned robot policies are rarely assessed against functional safety standards, so no safety integrity level is assigned before transferring them onto physical collaborative hardware.

## Method

- ==Safety-Driven reward function== extends the baseline distance-plus-grasp reward with penalties for joint speed excess, workspace and cube and obstacle collisions, IK failure, and collision velocity above **250mm/s**.
- ==Sim2Real== environment framework built on ROS, Gazebo with the ODE physics engine, and Gymnasium-Robotics, exposing **UR5GraspEnv-v0** with end-effector position actions and inverse-kinematics safety checks as soft constraints.
- Functional safety assessment pipeline computing ==Mean Time To Failure==, ==Probability of Failure on Demand==, and ==Risk Reduction Factor== from operational data to assign IEC 61508 Safety Integrity Levels, plus a new safety-driven success rate metric.

## Results

- Simulation success rate rose from **0.29** to **0.36** without obstacles, and from **0.00** to **0.30** with a static bar obstacle; real testbed rose **0.15** to **0.25** and **0.00** to **0.16**.
- Obstacle-scenario violations dropped sharply: simulated obstacle collisions **17.54** to **14.24** and velocity violations **14.95** to **5.51**; real-world obstacle collisions fell from **0.316** to **0.053**.
- Real-world Probability of Failure on Demand improved from **0.0023** to **0.0013** and MTTF from **440** to **742** steps, both models certified **SIL 2**; collision force rose **24.12** to **31.98N**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Encoding a regulatory numeric threshold directly as a reward penalty produces anticipatory behavior, visible as smoother pre-collision velocity profiles rather than abrupt uncorrected impacts.
> - Safety penalties can raise interaction counts, so per-event violation rates rise even as overall safety improves; normalize violation metrics by successful attempts.
> - Physics-engine fidelity bounds Sim2Real transfer of physical quantities: velocity penalties learned under ODE did not reliably reduce measured real collision forces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) into Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) holds potential for dynamic and adaptable robotic systems. However, its adoption in real-world industrial settings is constrained by safety concerns arising from direct interaction with human operators. While traditional industrial robotics relies on safety-rated control systems (e.g., PLCs, relays, e-stops, safety scanners) to meet standards, these are often rule-driven and may lack the flexibility required for unobserved or unexpected situations in DRL-driven systems. A significant challenge in this field is the reliable transfer of learned policies from simulation environments to real-world robots, termed the Sim2Real approach, while maintaining robust and safe behavior across diverse conditions.

The existing research landscape addresses robotic safety through established guidelines such as ISO 10218, which defines safety requirements for industrial robots, and ISO/IEC 61508, which governs the functional safety of electrical, electronic, and programmable electronic safety-related systems. These standards provide a framework for designing and managing safe robotic operations. Incorporating safety into DRL algorithms typically involves modifying either the optimization goals or the learning processes. This includes developing agents capable of avoiding dangerous states, navigating obstacles, performing error recovery through reversible behaviors, and ensuring secure exploration within constrained action spaces. Techniques such as control barrier functions, safety shielding, and reward function shaping are employed to integrate safety constraints into DRL.

Current DRL approaches for robotics often utilize sparse reward functions to facilitate unbiased learning. However, these functions may not adequately account for critical safety measures required in real-world applications. While some studies have focused on multi-goal reward functions using hindsight experience replay, they often prioritize goal achievement without explicitly penalizing unsafe actions. Benchmarks like Safety-Gymnasium aim to standardize evaluation for safe DRL, and simulators such as Gazebo integrated with Robot Operating System (ROS) are widely used for training and testing robot models before real-world deployment. The choice of physics engine within these simulators (e.g., Open Dynamics Engine (ODE) used in this study) has been shown to impact simulation fidelity, particularly concerning physical interactions like force and velocity during collisions.

This study builds upon previous work in simulation environments and benchmarks for industrial robotics, proposing a novel safety-driven DRL reward function. It positions itself within the ongoing effort to bridge the gap between DRL's adaptive capabilities and the stringent safety requirements of industrial collaborative robotics, moving towards learning-based safety protocols rather than solely fixed-rule ones.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent tension between the adaptive capabilities of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) and the critical need for safety in industrial applications. While DRL offers dynamic and flexible solutions for complex, unpredictable tasks, its data-driven nature can lead to behaviors that compromise safety when interacting with human operators or the environment. Existing safety protocols, often based on fixed rules and hardware-based safety systems, may not be sufficiently adaptable for the emergent behaviors of DRL agents.

The key objectives of this study are outlined as follows:

1. **Develop a Novel Safety-Driven DRL (SD-DRL) Framework:** The central objective is to design and implement a DRL framework that integrates specific industrial safety requirements directly into the robot's learning algorithm. This involves incorporating velocity constraints, derived from standards such as ISO 10218, into the DRL model's reward function. The aim is to enable the robot to inherently prioritize safety while learning operational tasks.
2. **Validate the Framework Across Diverse Scenarios:** The research seeks to evaluate the efficiency and robustness of the proposed SD-DRL model by subjecting it to various operational scenarios. These scenarios include standard grasping tasks and more complex tasks requiring obstacle avoidance, both within simulation and on a physical robot.
3. **Implement a Sim2Real Approach for Deployment:** A crucial objective is to facilitate the transfer of learned policies from a simulated environment to a real robotic cell. This Sim2Real approach aims for zero-shot transfer, meaning the trained policy should operate effectively on the physical robot without requiring further fine-tuning or extensive real-world training, thereby reducing development time and costs.
4. **Conduct a Functional Safety Assessment:** The study aims to perform a comprehensive functional safety assessment of the DRL-driven robotic system according to the ISO/IEC 61508 standard. This includes determining Safety Integrity Levels (SILs) for the DRL application, which involves hazard analysis, risk assessment, and calculating metrics such as Probability of Failure on Demand (PFD) and Risk Reduction Factor (RRF).
5. **Compare Performance Against Traditional DRL:** A comparative analysis is a key objective to demonstrate the advantages of the SD-DRL framework. The performance of the SD-DRL model is to be benchmarked against a conventional DRL model across various metrics, including average number of safety violations, obstacle avoidance capabilities, and success rate of grasping tasks, both in simulation and real-world environments. This comparison is intended to quantify the improvements in safety performance while maintaining operational efficiency.

The overarching motivation is to advance the state-of-the-art in safe human-robot collaboration by developing DRL systems that are not only efficient but also compliant with stringent industrial safety standards, thereby fostering their wider deployment in manufacturing and other safety-critical sectors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study involved the development, evaluation, and validation of a Safety-Driven Deep Reinforcement Learning (SD-DRL) framework using a Sim2Real approach.

#### 3.1. A. Environment Framework

The experimental setup comprised both simulated and real-world environments.
- **Robotic Workspace:** The "Universal Robot Grasp Task Space (UR5GraspEnv-v0)" was utilized. This environment models a UR5 collaborative robotic arm designed for grasping tasks, where episodes terminate upon successful grasping or collisions with the environment.
- **Software Stack:** The Robot Operating System (ROS) served as the middleware for communication and control. Gazebo was used as the simulator for developing and testing algorithms. The Gymnasium-Robotics environment provided a platform for evaluating and benchmarking reinforcement learning algorithms for robotic tasks.
- **Sim2Real Approach:** A zero-shot Sim2Real transfer strategy was implemented, where policies trained in the simulated environment were directly deployed to a physical UR5 robot testbed without additional fine-tuning.

#### 3.2. B. Deep Reinforcement Learning

- **Algorithm:** The Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) algorithm was selected due to its capabilities in addressing overestimation bias in DRL.
- **State (S_t):** The state vector included various observations related to the end-effector's position, the object's position, and other environmental parameters within the UR5GraspEnv.
- **Action (a_t):** A 4-dimensional action vector controlled the end-effector's movement and gripper state in the UR5GraspEnv.
- **Reward Function:**
 - **Traditional DRL Reward (R):** This function primarily incentivized goal achievement, defined as a combination of the negative distance between the end-effector and the cube, a reward for successful grasping, and a penalty for failed grasp attempts.
 `R = -d + g - gc`
 - **Safety-Driven DRL Reward (R_SD-DRL):** This novel reward function extended the traditional reward by incorporating several safety-related penalty terms:
 `R_SD-DRL = -d + g - sc - cc - ccc - gc - cv - bcc - ikc`
 Where `d` is distance, `g` is grasp reward, `gc` is failed grasp penalty. Additional penalties include `sc` (exceeding joint speed limit), `ikc` (inverse kinematic solution failure), `cc` (collisions with environment), `ccc` (collisions with the cube), `bcc` (collisions with obstacles), and `cv` (exceeding a safety-rated reduced collision velocity of < 250mm/s, derived from ISO 10218).

#### 3.3. C. Evaluation Metrics

The performance of the DRL agents was assessed using the following metrics:
- **Average Episode Returns:** The cumulative sum of rewards per episode, normalized by the average steps per failed episode.
- **Average Number of Violations per Episode:** A count of safety compliance breaches, including collisions, speed limit exceedances, and crossing the velocity threshold for collision safety.
- **Success Rate:** The proportion of episodes where the agent successfully achieved its task goal (grasping) from random initial states.
- **Safety-Driven Success Rate:** A novel metric measuring the agent's ability to accomplish tasks while adhering to predefined safety protocols.

#### 3.4. D. Safety Standard Compliance

- **ISO 10218 Integration:** Safety-rated speed controls and collision velocity thresholds were directly integrated into the DRL reward function, influencing the learning objectives to prioritize safe behaviors.
- **ISO/IEC 61508 Functional Safety Assessment:** This standard guided the evaluation of the system's functional safety. A risk assessment process was conducted to identify hazards within the DRL's operational environment, assessing their probability and severity.
 - **SIL Determination:** Safety Integrity Levels (SILs) were assigned based on the assessment. This involved calculating the Probability of Failure on Demand (PFD) and the Risk Reduction Factor (RRF) using operational data from both simulated and real-world experiments. PFD was approximated considering the probability of safe states and Mean Time To (dangerous) Failure (MTTF). An industrial functional safety expert validated the SIL assignments.

#### 3.5. E. Design of Experiments

- **Task Control:** The UR5GraspEnv-v0 focused on end-effector position control, with coordinates converted to joint states via inverse kinematics. Safety checks ensured valid solutions within soft speed limits (ignored commands if exceeded). Hard constraints included episode termination for workcell collisions or forces exceeding 100N.
- **Collision Avoidance:** Penalties for collisions with the workspace, objects, or obstacles were incorporated into the reward function.
- **Speed Reduction at Collision:** A cost was applied for exceeding a safety-rated velocity threshold during collisions.
- **Joint Limits:** Commands were ignored if joint speeds exceeded safety-standard limits (2.97 rad/s per joint) or for invalid inverse kinematic solutions.
- **Training and Testing:** Policies were trained in simulation for approximately 2.2 × 10^6 steps for normal scenarios and 6.5 × 10^6 steps for static obstacle scenarios. Validation included 450-1300 simulation tests and 20 real-world tests for each scenario, involving random cube/obstacle positions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the SD-DRL framework against a traditional DRL model across various metrics in both simulated and real-world environments, for grasping tasks with and without static obstacles.

#### 4.1. A. Violations During Testing

- **General Trend:** SD-DRL generally exhibited fewer safety violations compared to conventional DRL methods.
- **Exceptions:** In certain scenarios, particularly those involving object collisions and velocity violations, SD-DRL showed a higher incidence. The authors suggested this trend might be attributed to SD-DRL making more successful attempts, which resulted in increased interaction with the object. They also noted that physics parameters like speed violation or force during collisions were sometimes better for conventional DRL, suggesting that the fidelity of the simulation's physics engine (ODE) could impact the correlation between simulated and real-world physics-based safety parameters.
- **Real-World vs. Simulation:** For real-world validation, velocity during collision in simulation experiments was replaced with force during collision on the testbed.
 - In the normal scenario, SD-DRL had fewer collisions in simulation (0.043 vs 0.048 for DRL) but slightly more in the real world (0.400 vs 0.200).
 - In the static obstacle scenario, SD-DRL significantly reduced collisions in simulation (0.162 vs 1.548 for DRL) and also in the real world (0.421 vs 0.737).
 - SD-DRL consistently showed lower velocity during collision in simulation. In the real world, SD-DRL generally resulted in higher forces during collision (37.11N vs 34.89N for DRL in normal scenario, 31.98N vs 24.12N for DRL in static obstacle scenario). This counter-intuitive result reinforced the hypothesis regarding physics engine limitations impacting Sim2Real transfer for physics-based parameters.

#### 4.2. B. Velocity Profiles During Collision

- **Conventional DRL:** Velocity profiles for conventional DRL showed abrupt changes or no significant alteration upon collision, indicating a lack of anticipatory behavior to mitigate impact forces.
- **SD-DRL:** In contrast, SD-DRL demonstrated smoother velocity transitions leading up to a collision. This suggested that the SD-DRL model developed an ability to anticipate collisions and adjust its movement to minimize potential impact forces, thereby balancing task completion with safety.

#### 4.3. C. Success Rate and Safety-Driven Success Rate

- **Overall Performance:** SD-DRL demonstrated superior performance over conventional DRL in terms of both success rate and safety-driven success rate across most scenarios.
- **Quantitative Improvements (Abstract Claim):** The proposed approach outperformed the conventional method by a 16.5% average success rate in simulations and 2.5% in the testbed, specifically noting these achievements occurred *without safety violations*.
- **Detailed Metrics:**
 - **Simulation (Normal Scenario):** SD-DRL achieved a success rate of 0.36 (vs 0.29 for DRL) and a safety-driven success rate of 0.34 (vs 0.28 for DRL). Average episode returns were also higher for SD-DRL (0.89 vs 0.53).
 - **Simulation (Static Obstacle Scenario):** SD-DRL showed substantial improvement, achieving a success rate of 0.30 (vs 0.00 for DRL) and a safety-driven success rate of 0.27 (vs 0.00 for DRL). This indicates DRL failed completely in the obstacle scenario.
 - **Real-World (Normal Scenario):** SD-DRL had a success rate of 0.25 (vs 0.15 for DRL) and a safety-driven success rate of 0.15 (equal to DRL).
 - **Real-World (Static Obstacle Scenario):** SD-DRL achieved a success rate of 0.16 (vs 0.00 for DRL) and a safety-driven success rate of 0.05 (vs 0.00 for DRL).

#### 4.4. D. Functional Safety Assessment (SILs)

- **SIL Determination:** Both DRL and SD-DRL models, when assessed using the ISO/IEC 61508 standard, achieved a Safety Integrity Level (SIL) 2. This determination was based on calculated PFD (Probability of Failure on Demand) values and confirmed by an industrial safety expert.
- **Improved Metrics for SD-DRL:** While both achieved SIL 2, the SD-DRL model demonstrated improved functional safety metrics in the real-world environment compared to DRL.
 - For SD-DRL, the real-world MTTF (Mean Time To Failure) was 742.34 steps (vs 440.30 for DRL), and PFD was 0.0013 (vs 0.0023 for DRL). This indicates better reliability and a lower probability of dangerous failure for SD-DRL.
- **Compliance:** The DRL-based system exhibited substantial compliance with required safety standards, achieving a consistent SIL 2 across different testing environments, underscoring its potential for deployment in manufacturing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for integrating safety compliance directly into the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithm for collaborative robots. The proposed Safety-Driven DRL (SD-DRL) framework represents a step toward developing inherently safe and adaptable robotic systems, addressing a critical barrier to the broader adoption of DRL in industrial human-robot collaboration (HRC).

The significance of this work lies in several areas:

- **Enhanced Safety in DRL:** By incorporating industrial safety standards (e.g., ISO 10218 velocity constraints) directly into the DRL reward function, the framework enables robots to learn behaviors that inherently prioritize safety, rather than relying solely on external, rule-based safety systems. This capability is crucial for dynamic and unpredictable environments where DRL's adaptive nature can lead to emergent, potentially unsafe, actions.
- **Compliance with Industrial Standards:** The successful functional safety assessment, including the determination of Safety Integrity Levels (SILs) according to ISO/IEC 61508, indicates the practical viability of the SD-DRL framework for deployment in safety-critical industrial settings. Achieving SIL 2 demonstrates a quantifiable level of safety reliability, which is essential for certification and acceptance in manufacturing and other regulated sectors.
- **Effective Sim2Real Transfer:** The study demonstrates a zero-shot Sim2Real approach, allowing policies trained in simulation to be directly deployed on physical robots without extensive real-world fine-tuning. This accelerates the development and deployment cycle for DRL-driven robotic applications, reducing costs and risks associated with real-world training.
- **Improved Task Performance with Safety:** The quantitative results show that SD-DRL not only reduces safety violations but also outperforms traditional DRL in terms of task success rates, particularly in complex scenarios involving obstacle avoidance. This indicates that safety integration can be achieved without significantly compromising operational efficiency.
- **Foundation for Future Safe HRC:** The framework provides a foundational methodology for developing more robust and trustworthy collaborative robots. By explicitly embedding safety into the learning process, it opens avenues for advanced HRC scenarios where robots can interact more intimately and safely with human operators.

The potential impact of this research includes:

- **Broader Adoption of DRL in Industry:** By demonstrating a pathway for DRL algorithms to meet industrial safety standards, the work contributes to increasing confidence in DRL technology, potentially accelerating its integration into various manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics applications.
- **Development of Smarter Collaborative Robots:** Future collaborative robots could exhibit more intelligent and context-aware safety behaviors, dynamically adjusting their actions based on learned safety protocols rather than rigid pre-programmed rules.
- **Guidance for Simulation Design:** The identified limitations concerning the physics engine's impact on Sim2Real transfer for physics-based safety parameters highlight the importance of high-fidelity simulation and physics-informed learning approaches, influencing future simulator development and DRL training strategies.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** As safety concerns are mitigated, robots can be designed for closer and more seamless interaction with humans, potentially leading to more efficient and ergonomic human-robot workspaces.

Future research directions identified, such as the use of physics-informed neural networks and addressing dynamic human collision avoidance, indicate further advancements towards highly reliable and safe DRL systems in complex HRC environments.
