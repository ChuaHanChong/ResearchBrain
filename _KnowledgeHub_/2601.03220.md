# From Entropy to Epiplexity: Rethinking Information for Computationally Bounded Intelligence

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and New York University introduce epiplexity, a new information measure that quantifies structural content learnable by computationally bounded observers. This framework resolves paradoxes in classical information theory, demonstrating how computation can create information, how data ordering affects learnability, and how models extract emergent structures, showing a correlation between higher epiplexity and improved out-of-distribution generalization.

## Problem

- Classical information theory (Shannon entropy, Kolmogorov complexity) assumes infinite computational capacity, making it inadequate for explaining phenomena in modern computationally bounded AI.
- The inability of classical theory to explain how deterministic processes can yield new information, why factorization order matters, or how likelihood modeling uncovers emergent structures in AI systems.
- A lack of a theoretical framework to quantify the 'value of data' for broad generalization and guide data selection, rather than just model selection, in large-scale AI.

## Method

- Define ==epiplexity== (structural information) and ==time-bounded entropy== (random information) using a time-bounded ==Minimum Description Length (MDL)== principle relative to a ==computationally bounded observer== (probabilistic model).
- Empirically estimate these quantities using ==neural networks== (specifically ==transformers==) as the bounded observers, leveraging ==prequential and requential coding== to approximate model description length.
- Optimize hyperparameters and training dynamics within given computational budgets (FLOPs) to find the ==compute-optimal two-part code==, analyzing the ==Pareto frontier== of MDL versus compute.

## Results

- Elementary Cellular Automata (ECA) Rule 54 generates high epiplexity due to complex emergent patterns, while Rule 30 produces high time-bounded entropy (randomness) for a bounded observer.
- Experiments with chess data and ECA Rule 30 showed that reversing the data factorization order leads to both higher time-bounded entropy (harder to predict) and higher epiplexity (more complex structures learned).
- Models trained on data yielding higher epiplexity (e.g., 'reverse' chess order, OpenWebText) consistently demonstrated improved out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization and downstream task performance, with language models exhibiting higher epiplexity than vision models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Computationally bounded observers can 'create' new and useful structural information (epiplexity) from deterministic processes, such as complex cellular automata rules, resolving the paradox of information increase.
> - The order of data factorization significantly impacts the learnable information and epiplexity for bounded observers, as demonstrated by one-way functions and chess game sequences, challenging classical information symmetry.
> - Likelihood modeling in computationally limited systems can necessitate learning inductive strategies and discovering emergent patterns not explicitly present in the data-generating process, leading to higher epiplexity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a perceived gap in classical information theory when applied to the context of modern artificial intelligence, particularly large-scale machine learning. Traditional frameworks like Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity are founded on the assumption of observers with unlimited computational capacity. This assumption, while mathematically convenient, often leads to conclusions that are at variance with empirical observations in machine learning, especially regarding the value of data, the impact of computational processes, and the transferability of learned representations.

The paper argues that as AI systems evolve towards more general-purpose intelligence, the success of models increasingly depends on the quality and structure of the data they are trained on, rather than solely on architectural choices or optimization for in-distribution performance. This shift highlights a need for a theoretical framework that can quantify the "usable" or "transferable" information content of data, guiding "data selection" rather than just "model selection." Existing information theory, which often suggests that deterministic transformations cannot increase information, struggles to account for phenomena like the utility of synthetic data, the emergence of complex behaviors from simple rules (e.g., AlphaZero learning chess strategies), or the dependence of learning on data ordering.

The work builds upon concepts from algorithmic information theory, such as sophistication and logical depth, which attempt to distinguish between random and structural information. However, it critically observes that even these concepts typically assume unbounded computation, rendering them insufficient for practical machine learning scenarios where observers are inherently computationally bounded. Simultaneously, the paper draws parallels with cryptographic randomness, which explicitly introduces computational constraints to define pseudorandomness, thereby acknowledging the observer's limitations. By integrating these perspectives, the authors aim to introduce a novel information measure that aligns more closely with the operational realities and observed complexities of contemporary AI development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and formalize "epiplexity," a new measure of information specifically designed for computationally bounded observers. This formalization aims to address critical limitations of classical information theory that become apparent in the context of modern AI research.

The motivations for introducing epiplexity are rooted in three apparent paradoxes, which highlight the tension between theoretical predictions from classical information theory and empirical observations in machine learning:

1. **Paradox 1: Information cannot be increased by deterministic transformations.**
 - **Classical View**: Both Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity imply that applying a deterministic function to data cannot increase its information content. The data processing inequality suggests that synthetic data derived from existing data adds no additional value beyond the original data and the generating model.
 - **Motivation for Rethink**: This contradicts observed phenomena such as the effectiveness of pseudorandom number generators in producing apparent randomness, the utility of synthetic data for improving model capabilities, the derivation of new mathematical knowledge from axioms, the emergence of phenomena in dynamical systems, and the acquisition of complex strategies by AI systems like AlphaZero through self-play, despite being initialized with simple rules. The paper argues that classical theory fails to capture the "new and useful information" generated through computation by computationally bounded agents.

2. **Paradox 2: Information is independent of the order of data (factorization).**
 - **Classical View**: Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity posit that the total information content of a joint distribution or a string is invariant to how its components are factorized (e.g., H(X,Y) = H(X|Y) + H(Y)).
 - **Motivation for Rethink**: Empirical evidence suggests that the order of data presentation significantly impacts learning. For instance, large language models (LLMs) learn more effectively from text ordered left-to-right than in reverse. Cryptographic primitives also exploit directional computational hardness, implying that predicting in one direction is easy while the inverse is hard for limited observers. These observations indicate that the *learnable* or *extractable* information is sensitive to factorization order, which classical theory does not account for.

3. **Paradox 3: Likelihood modeling is merely distribution matching.**
 - **Classical View**: Maximizing likelihood is often equated with matching the true data-generating process. It is assumed that a model cannot extract more structure or learn features not explicitly present or used in generating the data. The true data-generating process is considered the "perfect" model, setting an upper bound on what can be learned.
 - **Motivation for Rethink**: This perspective conflicts with the observation that computationally limited observers can uncover significantly more structure than is overtly present in the data-generating process. Examples include the emergence of complex patterns (e.g., gliders) in Conway's Game of Life from simple local rules, or AI models performing "induction" (inferring latent variables or underlying rules) to explain observed data. The paper argues that for a computationally bounded observer, the model required to predict or explain data generated by a simple process can be far more complex and contain more "structure" than the generator itself.

By introducing epiplexity, the authors aim to provide a quantitative measure that can formalize these intuitions, enabling the evaluation of data's intrinsic value independently of a specific downstream task and guiding strategies for data selection, generation, and transformation in AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodological contribution is the formal definition and practical estimation of epiplexity and time-bounded entropy within a computationally constrained framework.

**Conceptual Framework:**
The paper defines information as observer-dependent, explicitly incorporating computational resources. It decomposes the total information content of a random variable (e.g., a dataset) into two components for a computationally bounded observer:
- **Epiplexity (S_T(X))**: The amount of "structural information" and regularity extractable from data. This is quantified by the length of the program (`|P|`) that describes the optimal probabilistic model.
- **Time-Bounded Entropy (H_T(X))**: The "random, unpredictable content" that remains after the structural information has been extracted. This is quantified by the expected negative log-likelihood of the data given the optimal model (`E[log 1/P(X)]`).

These definitions are anchored in the **Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle**, but critically, they introduce a **time constraint (T)** on the computational resources available to the observer (T-bounded probabilistic model). An optimal program `P*` is sought that minimizes the sum of its own length and the expected entropy coding length of the data, subject to a time bound `T(n)` for both sampling and probability evaluation on a prefix-free universal Turing machine (UTM).

**Key Properties of Epiplexity:**
- **Observer Dependence**: The same object can appear random or structured depending on the observer's computational resources.
- **Asymmetry**: Unlike Kolmogorov complexity, the complexity of a function and its inverse can differ significantly under computational constraints, especially if one direction is computationally "hard."
- **Distinction from Classical Measures**: The framework is shown to characterize cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generators (CSPRNGs) as having high time-bounded entropy but negligible epiplexity for polynomial-time observers, aligning with intuition.

**Practical Estimation using Neural Networks:**
Recognizing the intractability of directly searching the space of all programs, the authors propose practical procedures to estimate epiplexity and time-bounded entropy using neural networks as probabilistic models. The decoding time of a trained model is estimated as `6ND + 2ND` (for N parameters and D tokens), comprising training and inference.

1. **Prequential Coding (Heuristic)**:
 - **Approach**: This method approximates the model's description length (`|P_preq|`) as the area under the loss curve during training, above the final loss value. It's based on the idea that the reduction in loss over time signifies the information absorbed by the model from the data.
 - **Estimation**: `|P_preq| ≈ Σ_i (log 1/P_i(Z_i) - log 1/P_M(Z_i))`, where `P_i` is the model at step `i`, `Z_i` is the `i`-th training token, and `P_M` is the final model. The expected data encoding length is the final model's loss on unseen data.
 - **Advantages**: Conceptually simple, easy to evaluate from standard training runs.
 - **Limitations**: Not rigorously an upper bound for Kolmogorov complexity or time-bounded complexity, as it relies on an information symmetry argument that doesn't fully extend to time-bounded contexts.

2. **Requential Coding (Rigorous)**:
 - **Approach**: This method provides a more rigorous estimate of the model's description length (`|P_req|`). It encodes the training process of a student model by compressing the synthetic tokens sampled from a teacher model. The teacher model typically consists of checkpoints from training on real data.
 - **Estimation**: `|P_req| ≈ Σ_i KL(P_t,i || P_s,i)`, where `P_t,i` is the teacher and `P_s,i` is the student at step `i`. The data encoding length is the final student model's loss.
 - **Advantages**: Provides an explicit code and guarantees a known runtime for decoding, making it formally sound within the time-bounded MDL framework.
 - **Limitations**: Computationally more expensive than prequential coding (2-10x slower).
 - **Empirical Comparison**: Both methods show good correlation in ranking datasets by epiplexity, with prequential estimates typically being higher, suggesting prequential coding can serve as a useful proxy.

**Optimization Procedure**:
For both methods, the goal is to find the optimal hyperparameters (model size N, training data D, learning rate, architecture, etc.) that minimize `|P| + E[log 1/P(X)]` under a given compute budget T. This involves:
- Using techniques like Maximal Update Parameterization (μP) and CompleteP for stable hyperparameter scaling across model sizes.
- Training models of various sizes and durations.
- Estimating the Pareto frontier of the two-part code length versus compute, often smoothed using the lower convex hull and selecting median points to account for finite sweep artifacts.

**Theoretical Scaling Analysis**:
The paper also analyzes how epiplexity and time-bounded entropy are expected to scale with compute and dataset size under general assumptions (e.g., diminishing returns for increased N or D). This analysis predicts that epiplexity generally grows with compute and dataset size, while time-bounded entropy decreases.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical arguments and empirical evidence to support the concept of epiplexity and resolve the three apparent paradoxes of information theory for computationally bounded observers.

**Resolution of Paradox 1: Information Can Be Created by Deterministic Transformations**
- **Theoretical Proof**: The paper formally proves that a cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator (CSPRNG) can dramatically increase the time-bounded entropy of its input. For a polynomial-time observer, the output of a CSPRNG appears nearly maximally random, despite being generated deterministically from a short, low-entropy seed. This demonstrates that information (specifically time-bounded entropy) can be "created" through computation for a bounded observer, unlike in classical information theory (Theorem 12).
- **Empirical Evidence (Elementary Cellular Automata - ECA)**: Experiments with LLMs trained to predict the next state of ECAs (Rule 15, 30, 54) show varied outcomes for information creation. Rule 15 (simple patterns) yields low epiplexity and time-bounded entropy. Rule 30 (chaotic) produces high time-bounded entropy but low epiplexity, meaning it generates randomness but little learnable structure. Rule 54 (complex, emergent patterns) generates both significant time-bounded entropy and high epiplexity, demonstrating that deterministic computations can indeed create substantial structural information learnable by an LLM.

**Resolution of Paradox 2: Information Content Depends on Factorization Order**
- **Theoretical Proof**: The paper proves that if one-way permutations exist, then the time-bounded entropy of a system can be asymmetric with respect to factorization order (Theorem 13). Specifically, predicting X given Y (where Y = f(X) and f is a one-way permutation) can be significantly harder (higher time-bounded entropy) than predicting Y given X.
- **Empirical Evidence (Chess Games)**: LLMs were trained on the Lichess dataset, presented in two orders: "moves-then-board" (forward) and "board-then-moves" (reverse). The "reverse" order (inferring moves from a final board state) results in higher time-bounded entropy and higher epiplexity. This indicates that a more computationally challenging factorization forces the model to extract more structural information (higher epiplexity) from the data, which is also reflected in increased residual randomness (higher time-bounded entropy).

**Resolution of Paradox 3: Likelihood Modeling Can Be More Than Distribution Matching**
- **Induction**: The paper demonstrates that computationally bounded models, when trained via likelihood maximization, can learn to "induct" on missing information or latent variables that were not explicitly part of the data-generating process.
 - **"Induction Hard" (ECA Rule 30 with hidden bits)**: An LLM trained to predict the output of Rule 30 ECA given a masked initial state (hidden bits) learns to infer the missing bits, paying an exponentially increasing computational cost with the number of hidden bits. This inductive capability leads to increased epiplexity, despite the simple underlying data-generating process.
 - **"Induction Easy" (Markov Chains with hidden transitions)**: A model trained on sequences from a Markov chain with partially hidden transition probabilities learns to combine direct use of given probabilities with induction on the missing ones, resulting in higher epiplexity than scenarios where all probabilities are either given or entirely hidden. The learned inductive strategy is not present in the data generation.
- **Emergence**: The paper formalizes "epiplexity emergence" (Definition 14), where simple rules generate patterns that, for a compute-limited observer, require a more complex, high-epiplexity program to predict than the brute-force rule itself.
 - **Empirical Evidence (ECA Rule 54)**: A compute-constrained non-looped transformer model trained to predict future states of ECA Rule 54 initially learns increasingly sophisticated emergent patterns (higher epiplexity) to shortcut the infeasible exact simulation. However, if sufficient compute is allocated to a "looped" model (which learns to iteratively simulate the underlying rules), epiplexity abruptly drops, revealing a simpler underlying structure when brute-force becomes feasible. This demonstrates how perceived structural complexity is tied to computational bounds.

**Epiplexity, Pre-Training, and OOD Generalization**
- **Correlation with OOD Performance**: The chess experiments show that pre-training on the "board-then-moves" ordering, which induces higher epiplexity, leads to better performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks like centipawn evaluation, compared to the lower-epiplexity "moves-then-board" ordering. This suggests that epiplexity measures the amount of reusable structural information a model extracts.
- **Structural Information in Natural Data**:
 - **Modality Comparison**: Empirical measurements on 5B tokens (OpenWebText, Lichess Chess, CIFAR-5M) at 6e18 FLOPs reveal that OpenWebText has the highest epiplexity, followed by chess, and then CIFAR-5M. This finding aligns with the observation that pre-training on language data often yields broader OOD generalization than image data, despite image data having a high total (but largely random) information content.
 - **Scaling Laws**: Extrapolations using scaling laws for 1T tokens at 10e25 FLOPs further support that language data generally exhibits higher epiplexity than image or video data. VQ tokenization for images increases epiplexity by allowing models to focus on higher-level semantic structures.
- **Data Selection for LLMs**: The Adaptive Data Optimization (ADO) strategy, which dynamically adjusts data distribution to favor subsets with faster-decreasing training losses, is shown to inadvertently select data that leads to higher epiplexity. This higher epiplexity correlates with improved downstream performance and OOD perplexity, providing an intrinsic metric for data quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces epiplexity as a novel theoretical construct that offers a computationally informed perspective on information, addressing long-standing discrepancies between classical information theory and the empirical realities of modern machine learning. Its significance and potential impact span both theoretical and practical domains.

**Theoretical Significance:**
- **Unified Framework**: Epiplexity provides a unifying language to connect ideas from algorithmic information theory (sophistication), cryptography (pseudorandomness), complexity science (emergence), and learning theory, all centered on the crucial role of computational constraints.
- **Resolution of Paradoxes**: By explicitly incorporating computational bounds, the framework resolves several fundamental tensions, demonstrating how information can be created through computation, how data ordering impacts learnable content, and how models can extract more structure than explicitly present in data-generating processes.
- **Foundation for Data-Centric AI**: It offers a theoretical foundation for "data selection," a burgeoning field in AI, by providing a principled way to quantify the intrinsic value of data, rather than solely relying on model selection. This can lead to new insights into the effectiveness of synthetic data, data augmentation, and curriculum learning.
- **New Hardness Notions**: The concept of epiplexity could lead to new notions of hardness for learning and transfer, beyond traditional sample complexity. It highlights that the "hardness" of a task for a bounded learner might relate to the size of the structural program it needs to construct, even if the underlying generative process is simple. This may explain why certain tasks require disproportionately large models or extensive training.
- **Explanation for Scaling Laws**: The framework suggests new avenues for explaining the observed universality in neural network scaling laws and why exponents might depend weakly on specific architectural or optimizer details, as these factors may converge on optimal strategies for extracting epiplexity.

**Practical Impact and Implications for AI Development:**
- **Guidance for Data Curation and Generation**: Epiplexity can serve as a metric to guide the selection, curation, and even generation of training data. It suggests that data should be evaluated not just by held-out perplexity (which captures time-bounded entropy) but by its capacity to induce reusable structural information (epiplexity) in models. This could inform the design of more effective synthetic data generation techniques and targeted data augmentation strategies.
- **Understanding OOD Generalization**: The correlation between epiplexity and OOD generalization observed in experiments provides a mechanistic explanation for why certain data sources (e.g., natural language) or data formatting strategies are more conducive to broad transferability across tasks and domains. Models trained on high-epiplexity data are hypothesized to accumulate richer, more reusable internal programs.
- **Curriculum Learning and Data Ordering**: The empirical findings on factorization order and adaptive data optimization (ADO) indicate that dynamically adjusting data presentation to maximize epiplexity can lead to better learning outcomes. This offers a principled approach to curriculum design and data ordering.
- **Representation Learning Insights**: Representation learning can be reinterpreted as the gradual accumulation of epiplexity, where models construct increasingly sophisticated internal programs to efficiently approximate and predict data distributions within their computational budget.
- **Beyond In-Distribution Performance**: The work advocates for a shift in evaluation metrics, emphasizing that the "goodness" of pre-training data should not solely be judged by in-distribution performance but by the structural complexity it enables the model to learn, especially for general-purpose AI.
