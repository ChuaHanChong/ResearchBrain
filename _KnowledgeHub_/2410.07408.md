# Automated Creation of Digital Cousins for Robust Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed "digital cousins," virtual environments automatically generated from a single real-world image, enabling robust zero-shot sim-to-real policy transfer. The Automated Creation of Digital Cousins (ACDC) pipeline leverages foundation models to produce diverse simulated scenes, leading to improved robot policy generalization compared to training on single digital twins.

## Problem

- Training robot policies for real-world deployment is unsafe, costly, and difficult to scale due to the inherent "sim-to-real gap."
- Existing solutions like broad domain randomization often lack quality or relevance, while high-fidelity digital twins are expensive, labor-intensive, and limit policy generalization to specific instances.
- Achieving sufficient environment diversity for robust and generalizable robot policy learning remains a significant challenge.

## Method

- Introduced "digital cousins" as virtual assets or scenes that preserve geometric and semantic affordances of real-world counterparts without requiring an exact, high-fidelity replica.
- Developed the Automated Creation of Digital Cousins (ACDC) pipeline to automatically generate interactive simulated scenes from a single real-world RGB image with zero human input.
- Utilized large language and vision foundation models (GPT-4, GroundedSAM-v2, DINOv2, Depth-Anything-v2, CLIP) for real-world object extraction, 3D reconstruction, semantic matching with virtual assets, and scene generation.

## Results

- The ACDC pipeline successfully generated high-quality digital cousin scenes, accurately reproducing object categories and models with high geometric fidelity.
- Policies trained on digital cousins exhibited significantly superior robustness in out-of-distribution (unseen) setups while maintaining comparable performance on original setups, outperforming digital twin policies.
- Digital cousins enabled robust zero-shot sim-to-real policy transfer, achieving a 90% success rate for a door-opening task on a real-world cabinet, compared to 25% for a digital twin policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Relaxing the requirement for an exact virtual replica enables fully automated generation of diverse, yet semantically relevant, training data that is critical for robustness.
> - A distribution of "digital cousins" provides a more effective and 'guided' form of domain randomization, leading to better policy generalization compared to arbitrary randomization or training on single digital twins.
> - Integrating cutting-edge foundation models facilitates an end-to-end, automated pipeline from a single real-world image to interactive simulated environments for robot policy learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: safely, cost-effectively, and scalably training robot policies for real-world deployment. Traditionally, this process is plagued by the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in highly controlled simulations fail when transferred to the unpredictable physics and semantics of the real world.

The existing landscape of solutions typically falls into two broad categories:

- **Domain Randomization (DR):** This approach aims to make policies robust by augmenting synthetic data with variations in visual, physical, or scene-level parameters. While effective to a degree, broad or "naive" domain randomization can sometimes lack the necessary quality or scale, or may generate distributions of environments that are not sufficiently grounded in real-world relevance. Policies trained on overly diverse, yet semantically disconnected, scenes might not achieve optimal performance on specific real-world tasks.
- **Digital Twins:** These are high-fidelity, interactive virtual replicas of specific real-world environments. Digital twins can capture nuanced details crucial for certain tasks. However, their creation is labor-intensive, expensive, and often requires manual human input. Crucially, policies trained in a single digital twin are optimized for that specific instance and struggle to generalize to even slight variations in the original scene, thus limiting their broader applicability.

The concept of "digital cousins" proposed by Dai et al. offers an innovative middle ground that seeks to overcome the limitations of both domain randomization and digital twins. A digital cousin is defined as a virtual asset or scene that, unlike a digital twin, does not explicitly model a real-world counterpart but still exhibits similar *geometric and semantic affordances*. For instance, a digital cousin of a real-world cabinet would share a similar handle layout and drawer configuration, even if its material or specific detailing differs.

This work fits into the research landscape by:
1. **Reducing Cost and Labor:** By relaxing the requirement for an exact replica, digital cousins enable fully automated creation, bypassing the significant human labor and expense associated with digital twins.
2. **Enhancing Robustness and Generalization:** Instead of training on a single, exact digital twin, digital cousins provide a *distribution* of similar training scenes. This augmented set of environments facilitates better robustness to variations in the real world, addressing the generalization challenge inherent in digital twin approaches.
3. **Providing Guided Domain Randomization:** Digital cousins offer a more targeted and "grounded" form of randomization compared to arbitrary procedural generation. They are fundamentally related to a real-world scene, preserving high-level scene properties like spatial object layouts and key semantic/physical affordances, ensuring that the generated diversity remains relevant to the target task.

In essence, digital cousins aim to bridge the sim-to-real gap by providing abundant, diverse, yet semantically meaningful synthetic training data that is both inexpensive to generate and highly effective for developing generalizable robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the inherent difficulties and limitations in training robotic manipulation policies for real-world applications. These challenges can be summarized as:

1. **Safety and Cost of Real-World Training:** Directly training robots in physical environments can be unsafe (e.g., robots colliding with objects or humans), prohibitively expensive due to hardware wear and tear, and time-consuming.
2. **Scalability Issues:** Achieving sufficient environment diversity for robust policy learning is difficult in the real world, hindering the development of policies that can generalize beyond a few specific scenarios.
3. **Sim-to-Real Disparity:** While simulation offers an inexpensive and scalable alternative, policies trained solely in virtual environments often fail when deployed in the real world. This "sim-to-real gap" arises from discrepancies in physics, visual semantics, and sensory perception between simulated and real environments.
4. **Limitations of Current Sim-to-Real Solutions:**
 - **Domain randomization:** While useful, it can struggle to generate high-quality interactive data at the necessary scale and relevance for complex tasks.
 - **Digital twins:** Although highly detailed, they are costly and labor-intensive to create and inherently limit policy generalization to a single instance, making them impractical for varied real-world deployment.

To address these critical limitations, the authors set forth several key objectives, which also encapsulate their main contributions:

1. **Introduce the concept of "digital cousins":** Define a new paradigm for virtual assets and scenes that focuses on preserving geometric and semantic affordances without requiring an explicit, exact replica of a real-world counterpart.
2. **Develop a fully automated pipeline for digital cousin creation (ACDC):** Design and implement a novel method, Automated Creation of Digital Cousins (ACDC), capable of generating interactive simulated scenes from a single real-world RGB image with zero human input.
3. **Establish an automated recipe for policy learning in digital cousins:** Create a system to automatically collect demonstrations and train robot policies within these generated digital cousin environments.
4. **Demonstrate superior policy robustness and zero-shot sim-to-real transfer:** Empirically show that robot manipulation policies trained using digital cousins achieve comparable performance to digital twin-trained policies within the original environment, significantly outperform them in out-of-distribution (unseen) setups, and enable robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to physical robots.

Ultimately, the overarching objective is to accelerate and democratize robot learning by providing an accessible, automated, and effective means to generate diverse and realistic training data, thereby mitigating the sim-to-real gap and fostering the development of truly generalist robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the "Automated Creation of Digital Cousins (ACDC)" pipeline, which is fully automated and integrated into a real-to-sim-to-real framework. This pipeline transforms a single real-world RGB image into a fully interactive simulated scene, which is then used to train robot policies for zero-shot deployment in the original real-world environment.

The ACDC pipeline consists of three sequential steps:

1. **Real-World Extraction:**
 - **Input:** A single RGB image (X) taken by a calibrated camera.
 - **Object Masking and Labeling:** GPT-4 is first prompted to generate captions for all objects in X. These captions are then fed into GroundedSAM-v2 to produce precise object masks (m_i). GPT-4 is subsequently used to re-synchronize and select accurate labels (l_i) for each mask from the initial caption list.
 - **Depth Estimation and Point Cloud Generation:** To reconstruct 3D information, the state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation model, Depth-Anything-v2, is used to infer a depth map (D) from the RGB image. A 3D point cloud (P) is then generated using D and the camera's intrinsic matrix (K). Object-specific point clouds (p_i) are extracted using the object masks (m_i), resulting in a set of object representations {o_i = (l_i, m_i, p_i, x_i)}.
 - **Mounting Type Inference:** GPT-4 is leveraged to classify each object's mounting type (e.g., "Wall Mounted," "On Floor or On Another Object," "Mixture"). This semantic information is crucial for accurately positioning and orienting objects within the simulated scene, especially for partially observed objects (Appendix A.2).

2. **Digital Cousin Matching:**
 - **Virtual Asset Dataset:** The method utilizes a large-scale virtual asset dataset, BEHAVIOR-1K, which contains over 10,000 diverse 3D object models. Each asset is pre-annotated with a semantic category and has multiple 2D snapshots taken from various orientations, including a representative snapshot (Appendix A.1).
 - **Hierarchical Matching:** For each extracted real-world object (o_i):
 - **Category Matching:** CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) is used to compute semantic similarity scores between the object's label (l_i) and all asset category names in BEHAVIOR-1K. The top-k closest categories are selected.
 - **Model Candidate Selection:** Within these selected categories, DINOv2 (self-supervised visual features) is used to compute embedding distances between the masked object's RGB image (x_i) and the representative snapshots (I_j) of candidate asset models. Top-k candidates are chosen.
 - **Orientation Selection:** DINOv2 distances are re-computed for each candidate model against its individual snapshots (i_js). The virtual asset (A_c) and its corresponding orientation (q_c) with the lowest distance are selected as the digital cousin. An optional GPT-based orientation refinement is also explored (Appendix B.1).
 - **Articulated Object Heuristics:** For articulated objects (e.g., cabinets with doors/drawers), the matching process prioritizes articulated assets from the dataset and can apply a threshold for the number of joints to ensure affordance preservation (Appendix A.7).

3. **Simulated Scene Generation:**
 - **Object Placement and Scaling:** For each matched digital cousin (A_c, q_c), its bounding box center is placed at the centroid of the corresponding real-world object's point cloud (p_i) and then rescaled to match p_i's extents.
 - **Plane Fitting:** Floor and wall planes are fitted from the extracted point clouds. GPT-4 determines whether objects should be mounted on these planes based on their inferred mounting types.
 - **Post-Processing:** All objects are de-penetrated to ensure physical plausibility and stability. Collision checks are performed, and object positions are adjusted in the x-y plane to avoid overlap (Appendix A.3).

**Policy Learning Approach:**
Once the digital cousin scenes are generated, the authors employ an automated recipe for training robot policies:

- **Imitation Learning from Scripted Demonstrations:** Rather than human demonstrations or reinforcement learning, the system uses programmatic, sample-based skills (Open, Close, Pick, Place) leveraging motion planning (CuRobo) and ground-truth simulation data (Grasp Pose Generator, GPG) to collect successful demonstrations within the generated scenes (Appendix A.4, A.5).
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** To enhance robustness, broad domain randomization (visual, physics, kinematic, instance-level) is applied to the digital cousin scenes during demonstration collection. This ensures diverse training data without losing the underlying scene-level semantics.
- **Policy Architecture:** The policies are trained using Behavioral Cloning with an RNN to handle temporal sequences and a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) to capture action distributions. Observations consist of robot proprioception (end-effector pose, gripper state) and a unified point cloud (processed by a PointNet encoder), which includes both scene and gripper points (Appendix B.5).
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The ultimate goal is to deploy these policies directly to the real world without any additional fine-tuning, leveraging the diversity and relevance provided by the digital cousins.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper meticulously evaluates the effectiveness of digital cousins through a series of experiments designed to answer four key research questions:

**Q1. Can ACDC produce high-quality digital cousin scenes?**
- **Findings:** The experiments, conducted in both sim-to-sim and real-to-sim settings, confirm that ACDC successfully generates high-quality digital cousin scenes. In sim-to-sim evaluations (where ground truth is available), ACDC accurately reproduces original per-object categories and models in most cases. Geometric fidelity is also high, with reconstructed objects' scales and positions closely matching their original counterparts (e.g., average L2 distance errors were typically less than 10 cm, with the largest being 17 cm for a 10-meter scene). Qualitatively, side-by-side comparisons demonstrate strong visual similarity and preservation of scene-level semantic and spatial details.
- **Ablation Study (Appendix B.1):** An ablation study comparing DINOv2 and CLIP for object matching, with and without GPT-based refinement, revealed that DINO-based methods, especially DINO+GPT for model selection, yielded superior geometric similarity. DINO+GPT was found to act as a "denser sampler" for finding highly similar cousins and generally produced more accurate and robust scene reconstructions.

**Q2. Can policies trained on digital cousins match the performance of policies trained on a digital twin when evaluated on the original setup?**
- **Findings:** Yes, policies trained exclusively on a distribution of digital cousins often perform comparably to, or even slightly better than, policies trained on a single, exact digital twin when tested on the original target setup. For instance, in the "Door Opening" task, digital cousin policies achieved ~90% success rates, close to the digital twin's ~97%. This suggests that the broader data distribution provided by cousins helps generalize effectively even to the specific instance the digital twin represents. Notably, policies trained on *all feasible assets* (naive domain randomization) performed significantly worse, emphasizing that digital cousins provide a more beneficial, *conditional* form of randomization.

**Q3. Do policies trained on digital cousins exhibit better robustness compared to policies trained on a digital twin when evaluated on out-of-distribution setups?**
- **Findings:** This is a major strength of digital cousins. Policies trained on digital cousins demonstrated significantly superior robustness when evaluated on held-out, unseen setups with increasing dissimilarity. While digital twin policies experienced sharp performance degradation (e.g., dropping to ~30% for "Door Opening" on an OOD asset), digital cousin policies maintained much higher success rates (e.g., ~70% on the same OOD asset). The study also observed that DINOv2 embedding distance could serve as a proxy for out-of-distribution performance, with further distances correlating with greater policy degradation. Furthermore, training on digital cousins led to more stable policy training (lower standard deviation in success rates) compared to training on digital twins or all assets. An ablation showed that merely increasing domain randomization for twin policies was insufficient; digital cousins provided a more effective pathway to robustness.

**Q4. Do policies trained on digital cousins enable zero-shot sim-to-real policy transfer?**
- **Findings:** Crucially, the research demonstrates that digital cousins enable robust zero-shot sim-to-real policy transfer. In a real-world evaluation involving an IKEA cabinet, a policy trained on digital cousins achieved a 90% success rate for the "Door Opening" task. In stark contrast, a policy trained on the exact digital twin of the cabinet (in simulation) only achieved a 25% success rate in the real world. Even a digital twin policy with increased domain randomization only reached 55%. This outcome strongly suggests that the broader, yet semantically relevant, distribution of training data provided by digital cousins is instrumental in overcoming the sim-to-real domain gap, which often arises from subtle modeling inaccuracies and sensor differences.
- **Full Pipeline Demonstration:** The paper successfully showcased the entire real-to-sim-to-real pipeline by training a policy on digital cousins generated from a single RGB image of a real kitchen scene, and then deploying that policy zero-shot to successfully open a cabinet in the original real kitchen.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of "digital cousins" and the fully automated ACDC pipeline represents a significant advancement in robot learning, with profound implications for the development and deployment of intelligent robotic systems.

1. **Democratization and Acceleration of Robot Learning:** By automating the creation of diverse, interactive training environments from single images, ACDC drastically reduces the expertise, labor, and cost barriers traditionally associated with simulation-based robot learning. This can accelerate research and development by enabling rapid iteration and large-scale data generation, potentially from "in-the-wild" images, thus democratizing access to high-quality synthetic data.
2. **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Generalization:** The primary impact is the demonstrated ability to train robot policies that are significantly more robust to real-world variations and generalize better to unseen environments. Unlike digital twins, which optimize for a single instance, digital cousins provide a distribution of similar scenes, equipping policies with the flexibility needed for real-world unpredictability. This is a critical step towards robots that can function reliably outside of highly structured, predefined settings.
3. **Enabling Practical Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The ability to deploy policies trained in simulation directly to physical robots without any real-world fine-tuning is a "holy grail" for robotics. Digital cousins effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap, offering a powerful method to overcome discrepancies in physics, visual semantics, and sensor characteristics, which have historically hindered such transfers. This opens doors for faster deployment of new robotic skills.
4. **Novel Approach to Domain Randomization:** Digital cousins offer a refined, "guided" approach to domain randomization. Instead of arbitrary randomization that might create irrelevant training data, cousins generate variations that are semantically and geometrically grounded in a real-world scene, ensuring that diversity is meaningful and beneficial for the target task.
5. **Leveraging Foundation Models for Robotics:** The work masterfully integrates cutting-edge large language models (GPT-4) and vision foundation models (GroundedSAM-v2, DINOv2, Depth-Anything-v2, CLIP) into a coherent and effective robotics pipeline. This demonstrates the immense potential of pre-trained, general-purpose AI models to revolutionize complex tasks like scene reconstruction and asset matching in specialized domains.
6. **Future Research Directions:** While transformative, the paper acknowledges limitations, such as reliance on the diversity of the underlying asset dataset (BEHAVIOR-1K) and inheriting limitations from the foundation models used. These point towards exciting future work, including expanding asset databases, improving foundation model robustness, and integrating digital cousins with more advanced robot learning paradigms like diffusion policies or more complex object relationship modeling.

In conclusion, "Automated Creation of Digital Cousins for Robust Policy Learning" offers a groundbreaking solution to the long-standing challenges of robot policy training. By enabling the automated generation of diverse, realistic, and interactive simulated environments, this research significantly pushes the frontier of generalizable and deployable robotics.
