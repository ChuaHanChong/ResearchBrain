# TAPIOCA: Why Task- Aware Pruning Improves OOD model Capability

> [!summary] Summary
> TAPIOCA is an analysis of why task-aware layer pruning works, studying the TALE method across controlled in-context regression transformers and fine-tuned 8B and 120B language models. It shows gains are exclusively out-of-distribution, and explains them geometrically: each task induces an adapted representation geometry, OOD inputs distort it, and pruning layers that amplify the distortion realigns them, lifting MMLU by 7.4 points.

## Problem

- Prior task-aware pruning work reports that deleting layers at inference can raise task accuracy, but never establishes when such gains appear or what mechanism produces them.
- Classical layer pruning targets compression, selecting blocks by representation similarity, perplexity or weight magnitude, so its task-agnostic redundancy criteria cannot explain accuracy improvements above the unpruned baseline.
- Layer-importance studies disagree over whether early or late layers matter, treating importance as a fixed positional property rather than something conditioned on the evaluation distribution.

## Method

- A controlled in-context regression testbed: a 12-layer, 8-head transformer trained from scratch on coefficients from U(-1,1), giving an explicit ID/OOD split, paired with ==LoRA==-finetuned math and code specialists at 8B and 120B.
- A ==layerwise discrepancy measure== comparing tuples of representation statistics - mean hidden-state norm, median last-token pairwise distance, variance and spectral summary - between ID and OOD inputs to quantify geometric mismatch per layer.
- Per-layer ==local linear surrogates== fit to residual-stream input-output pairs, diagnosed by ==stable rank== of W-I and on-data norm gain, plus inverse-surrogate hooks and residual-scaling interventions for causal tests.

## Results

- The 8B math expert stays at **87.5%** on MATH500 with no layer removable, yet pruning lifts MMLU from **36.7%** to **44.1%** and BoolQ by **3.0**; the 120B model gains **7.0** points.
- In regression, OOD distances inflate to **~385** and pruning contracts them toward ID; with the shift direction reversed pruning instead expands distances, so the effect is alignment, not norm suppression.
- Causal checks: an inverse surrogate at layer 23 recovers **37.5%** versus **36.0%** full and **37.8%** pruned, and attenuating one layer's residual update raises MMLU from **0.367** to **0.389**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Layer usefulness is a joint property of weights and input distribution: one layer gives a diffuse near-identity update near-ID but near-rank-one, order-of-magnitude amplification far-OOD.
> - Pruning aligns geometry rather than shrinking activations, so heuristics that simply delete high-norm layers will misfire when the shift demands expansion instead of contraction.
> - Pruned models are per-distribution experts, not better models: one traded roughly 1500x worse in-distribution MSE for its OOD gain, so keep distribution-specific checkpoints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the field of neural network pruning, particularly focusing on transformer models and large language models (LLMs). Traditionally, pruning has been employed as a method for model compression, aiming to reduce computational costs while maintaining general-purpose quality, often with some degradation in accuracy. Existing methods in this category are largely task-agnostic, removing blocks based on representation similarity, perplexity, dimensionality reduction, or unstructured pruning criteria like reconstruction error, weight magnitude, or activations.

More recently, a shift towards "task-aware pruning" has emerged, where the objective is to improve performance on specific tasks by removing layers, even without retraining the model. This is a less explored area compared to traditional compression-focused pruning. Previous work by Peer et al. and Naim et al., particularly the TALE method (Task-Aware Layer Elimination), have demonstrated that such pruning can lead to accuracy gains.

This paper specifically investigates *when* and *why* these task-aware pruning improvements occur. It diverges from previous work that might assume layer importance is purely positional (e.g., early layers being critical vs. later layers holding task-relevant information) or fixed. Instead, it proposes that layer utility is dependent on both the task and, crucially, the input data distribution. The work builds on geometric analyses of neural representations, drawing inspiration from studies that explore how LLMs learn to structure internal representations (e.g., token trajectories, matrix entropy). It extends the idea that pretrained models encode multiple task-specific geometries by suggesting that each layer's contribution to these geometries is distribution-dependent.

The paper aims to provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed OOD performance improvements, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of how distribution shifts impact internal model representations and how targeted architectural modifications, like pruning, can mitigate these effects.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the underlying mechanisms behind the phenomenon where task-aware layer pruning, particularly the TALE method, improves model performance. The authors aim to understand the conditions under which such improvements manifest and the representational reasons for their occurrence.

Specifically, the key objectives include:

1. **Characterizing the performance impact of task-aware pruning:** The first objective is to determine precisely *when* task-aware pruning yields benefits. The authors hypothesize that improvements might be specific to certain types of data rather than a general enhancement. This involves rigorously differentiating between in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) data evaluations.
2. **Empirically demonstrating ID/OOD asymmetry:** A core motivation is the surprising and seemingly counterintuitive observation that pruning can *improve* task performance without retraining. The authors seek to empirically confirm their finding that these gains are not uniform but are consistently observed on OOD inputs, while no benefits, or even degradation, are seen on ID data. This pattern is investigated across models of different scales and architectures.
3. **Uncovering the geometric changes induced by OOD inputs:** The paper aims to establish an empirical link between distribution shift and internal model representations. It hypothesizes that OOD inputs induce layerwise representation geometries (echaracterized by hidden-state norms and pairwise distances) that deviate significantly from those induced by ID inputs.
4. **Developing a geometric explanation for pruning benefits:** Based on the observed geometric changes, a central objective is to propose a mechanistic explanation: task-aware pruning improves OOD performance by identifying and removing layers that either create or amplify the distortion in representational geometry caused by OOD inputs. By removing these layers, OOD representations are shifted closer to the model’s task-adapted ID geometry, thereby improving performance.
5. **Providing causal evidence for the geometric hypothesis:** To move beyond correlation, the authors aim to provide causal evidence that correcting geometric distortions in the internal representations is indeed responsible for the observed performance gains. This involves direct interventions on the residual stream of specific layers.
6. **Demonstrating consistency across model scales and tasks:** A final objective is to show that the identified phenomena—OOD-specific gains and the geometric realignment mechanism—are not artifacts of small-scale or specific models but are consistent across controlled synthetic tasks, smaller transformers, and large, fine-tuned LLMs.

The motivation for this work stems from the counterintuitive nature of "inference-time pruning improving performance" and the need for a deeper, mechanistic understanding beyond mere empirical observation. By elucidating the representational dynamics at play, the research seeks to advance the understanding of OOD generalization in neural networks and inform future strategies for building more robust and adaptable models.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach combining controlled experimental settings with large-scale language model evaluations and detailed representational analyses. The core method under investigation is Task-Aware Layer Elimination (TALE), as introduced by Naim et al..

#### 3.1. TALE (Task-Aware Layer Elimination)

TALE is a training-free, test-time pruning method. Given a base model and a task-specific validation distribution, it operates greedily: at each step, it removes the layer whose deletion yields the largest improvement in the target metric on the validation data. The process terminates when no further deletion improves the metric.

#### 3.2. Controlled Experimental Setting: In-context Linear Regression

To establish a clear distinction between in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) data, the authors first use a controlled setting based on the in-context learning framework of Garg et al..
- **Model:** A 12-layer, 8-head transformer with a hidden dimension of 256, trained from scratch.
- **Task:** In-context linear regression, where the model learns to predict labels from context pairs generated by a linear function `f(x) = ax + b`.
- **ID/OOD Definition:** A base model (`Ba1`) is trained on coefficients `a, b` sampled from `U(-1, 1)`. ID evaluation uses functions sampled from the same `U(-1, 1)` range. OOD evaluation uses functions sampled from wider intervals `U(-σ, σ)` where `σ > 1`.
- **Pruning Application:** TALE is applied using validation data from both ID (`U(-1, 1)`) and OOD (`U(-σ, σ)`) distributions, producing "best-pruned" models (`Beσ`) for each validation distribution.
- **Evaluation:** Mean Squared Error (MSE) is used to assess performance.

#### 3.3. Large-Model Setting: Fine-tuned LLMs

To assess the generality of findings, the analysis is extended to pretrained LLMs:
- **Models:** Llama 3.1 8B (dense transformer) and GPT-OSS 120B (sparse Mixture-of-Experts model).
- **Fine-tuning:** Models are fine-tuned on two task families: mathematical reasoning (using NuminaMath-CoT, MATH500) and code (using Code Alpaca).
- **ID/OOD Definition:** The fine-tuning task (e.g., mathematical reasoning for math specialists) is treated as the ID task. Other benchmarks (e.g., full MMLU, BoolQ for math specialists; MMLU Math, MMLU CS for code specialists) are treated as OOD tasks relative to the model's specialization.
- **Evaluation:** Accuracy on standard NLP benchmarks (MATH500, MMLU, BoolQ, Code Alpaca).

#### 3.4. Geometric Analysis of Internal Representations

To understand *why* pruning helps, the authors analyze the internal representation geometry:
- **Hidden State Extraction:** Hidden states are extracted at every transformer layer for the final query token.
- **Representational Statistics:**
 - **Layerwise Distance Profiles:** The `L2` (and `L1` in appendix) distance from the final query token's representation to preceding token representations within the same prompt is computed and averaged across prompts. This summarizes how the model separates token representations across layers.
 - **Norms:** Mean last-token `L2` norm (and pairwise `L2` distance between calibration examples) are computed to capture typical activation magnitudes and spread.
- **Local Linear Surrogate Analysis:** For each layer `ℓ` and dataset `D`, a best linear surrogate `WDℓ` is fitted to the layer's residual-stream input-output pairs. This approximates the layer’s local action on the evaluated data distribution.
 - **Singular-Value Spectrum:** The singular-value spectrum of `WDℓ - I` is analyzed to measure how much the layer departs from a passthrough map and whether this departure is spread or concentrated.
 - **On-Data Norm Gain:** The ratio `||y_i|| / ||x_i||` (where `x_i` is input to layer `ℓ`, `y_i` is output) is used to quantify how much the layer expands or contracts representations from a given distribution.

#### 3.5. Causal Evidence through Interventions

To establish causality for the geometric explanation:
- **Inverse Surrogate Intervention:** An inverse surrogate `W⁻¹ℓ` is inserted after a TALE-selected layer `ℓ`. This map approximately reverses the distortion induced by the layer. If performance recovers, it suggests geometric correction is causal.
- **Residual Scaling Intervention:** For a TALE-selected layer `ℓ`, the residual update `Δℓ(hℓ)` is rescaled by `α ∈`, such that `hℓ+1 = hℓ + αΔℓ(hℓ)`. Varying `α` allows for continuous modulation of the layer's contribution without topological changes, testing if the magnitude of the residual update is causally involved in performance changes.

#### 3.6. Ruling Out Alternative Explanations (Appendix C)

The authors also systematically test and rule out alternative hypotheses for pruning gains, such as regularization effects (using noisy vs. clean OOD data like Runge and Weierstrass functions), optimizer artifacts (comparing Adam and Muon optimizers), and information-theoretic bottlenecks (mutual information analysis).

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several key findings, supported by empirical evidence across controlled regression tasks and large language models, alongside detailed geometric analyses and causal interventions.

#### 4.1. Task-Aware Pruning Gains Are OOD-Specific

- **No ID Benefit:** The TALE method consistently did not improve performance when models were evaluated on the in-distribution (ID) data to which they were adapted. In the controlled linear regression setting, applying TALE with ID validation data resulted in no layers being removed without a loss of accuracy. Similarly, for fine-tuned LLMs specializing in math or code, TALE did not remove any layers without degrading performance on their respective ID tasks (e.g., MATH500 for math specialists, Code Alpaca for code specialists).
- **Consistent OOD Improvement:** In contrast, TALE consistently improved performance under distribution shift (OOD inputs).
 - In the controlled regression setting, as the coefficient range of linear functions widened beyond the training distribution (e.g., `U(-2, 2)` vs. `U(-1, 1)`), TALE identified removable layers, leading to significant reductions in Mean Squared Error (MSE) on OOD samples (e.g., MSE dropped from 0.040535 to 0.034397 on `U(-2, 2)`). This improvement was observed to be selective, benefiting functions outside the training geometry.
 - For LLMs, an 8B math-specialized model saw a +7.4 absolute gain (20% relative gain) on MMLU (OOD) and +3.0 on BoolQ (OOD), while its ID performance on MATH500 remained unchanged. A 120B math-specialized model similarly gained +7.0 points on MMLU (OOD) with no change on its ID task. Similar patterns were observed for code-specialized LLMs across both scales.
- **Conclusion:** The layers removed by TALE are not globally redundant. They are beneficial for ID prediction but can become liabilities under distribution shift.

#### 4.2. OOD Inputs Induce a Mismatch in Representation Geometry

- **Distorted Distance Profiles:** For an adapted task, ID inputs induce characteristic layerwise hidden-state norms and pairwise distances, defining the task’s adapted representation geometry. OOD inputs cause hidden states to deviate from this geometry, producing norm and distance profiles that differ from ID data.
 - In the controlled regression task, OOD inputs (e.g., from `U(1, 2)`) produced larger hidden-state distances that amplified across intermediate layers, correlating with performance degradation.
 - In the 8B Llama model, the MMLU (OOD) distance profile diverged from the MATH500 (ID) profile in middle and late layers, showing larger pairwise distances for OOD representations.
- **Pruning Reduces Mismatch:** Pruning layers selected by TALE reduced this mismatch by shifting OOD representation profiles toward the ID profile.
 - When the model was trained on `U(-1, 1)` and tested on `U(1, 2)` (inflated distances), pruning contracted OOD distances toward the ID trajectory.
 - Conversely, when the model was trained on `U(1, 2)` and tested on `U(-1, 1)` (contracted distances), pruning *expanded* OOD distances toward the ID baseline.
- **Conclusion:** TALE's effect is geometric alignment, not merely norm suppression. It adjusts OOD representations to better fit the model's task-adapted geometry, regardless of whether that requires contraction or expansion.

#### 4.3. Some Layers Act as Distribution-Dependent Amplifiers

- **Context-Dependent Layer Behavior:** The same layer can exhibit different behaviors depending on the input distribution. Local linear surrogate analyses of layers revealed that certain layers, identical in their weights, act as benign refinements on ID inputs but become high-gain, low-rank amplifiers on far-OOD inputs.
- **Anisotropic Amplification:** For example, layer 3 in an LLM had moderate gain and a diffuse update spectrum on a near-ID MMLU slice, but exhibited much larger gain and a highly concentrated (near rank-one) spectrum on a far-OOD MMLU slice, leading to order-of-magnitude amplification.
- **Conclusion:** Harmful layer behavior is not an intrinsic property of the layer's weights alone but a joint property of the layer and the distribution of representations entering it. TALE improves performance by removing layers that amplify geometric mismatch under distribution shift.

#### 4.4. Causal Evidence for Geometric Correction

- **Inverse Surrogate Recovery:** Inserting an inverse surrogate (`W⁻¹ℓ`) after a TALE-selected layer, which approximately undoes its geometric distortion, largely recovered the pruned model’s performance on OOD data. For instance, removing layer 23 from a Llama 8B math expert improved MMLU accuracy from 36.0% to 37.8%; inserting `W⁻¹23` yielded 37.5%, nearly matching the pruned model.
- **Residual Scaling:** Continuously scaling down the residual contribution of a TALE-selected layer monotonically improved OOD accuracy. This showed that improvement was attributable to the magnitude of the layer’s residual contribution, not discrete topological changes or side-effects.
- **Conclusion:** Geometric information plays a causal role in pruning's effect on model performance. Correcting OOD geometric distortions is sufficient to recover much of the performance gain.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The findings of this research offer a novel and mechanistic explanation for the observed benefits of task-aware layer pruning, particularly regarding out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. This work extends the understanding of neural network behavior beyond traditional views of pruning as mere compression or regularization.

#### 5.1. Reconceptualization of Layer Utility

The paper challenges the notion of fixed layer importance in transformer architectures. It demonstrates that the utility of a given layer is not solely task-dependent but also critically distribution-dependent. Layers that are essential for optimal performance on in-distribution (ID) data can become detrimental "amplifiers" under distribution shift. This suggests a more dynamic and contextual understanding of how information propagates and is transformed within deep networks.

#### 5.2. Mechanistic Explanation for OOD Robustness

By revealing the "geometric mismatch" between OOD and ID representations and showing how pruning realigns these representations, the research provides a concrete, interpretable mechanism for improved OOD robustness. This moves beyond simply observing performance gains to explaining the underlying representational dynamics. This mechanistic insight can inform the design of more robust models by focusing on how to maintain consistent representational geometries under varying input conditions, rather than solely on increasing model capacity or refining training procedures.

#### 5.3. Implications for Pruning Strategies

The findings suggest that for tasks requiring OOD generalization, pruning methods should explicitly consider the representational effects of layers under distribution shift, rather than solely relying on ID validation or task-agnostic metrics. Future pruning algorithms could potentially incorporate metrics of geometric distortion or "amplification" to identify and selectively remove harmful layers for specific OOD scenarios.

#### 5.4. Guidance for Model Development and Deployment

The observation that existing layers can be reactivated or repurposed through pruning for OOD tasks, without retraining, has practical implications.
- **Test-time Adaptation:** It suggests that models could be dynamically adapted at inference time to perform better on encountered OOD data by removing or attenuating specific layers, offering a flexible strategy for deployment in unpredictable environments.
- **Resource Efficiency:** Achieving OOD robustness through inference-time pruning, rather than extensive retraining or fine-tuning for every potential distribution shift, could lead to more resource-efficient model deployment and maintenance.
- **Interpretability and Debugging:** The geometric analysis provides a framework for diagnosing why models might fail on OOD data – by identifying which layers distort representations. This could aid in debugging and understanding model limitations.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Research

This work opens several avenues for future investigation:
- Developing pruning methods that explicitly optimize for geometric alignment.
- Exploring whether other OOD mitigation strategies implicitly achieve similar geometric realignment.
- Investigating the properties of layers that act as "amplifiers" for OOD inputs to predict such behavior a priori.
- Broadening the analysis to other model architectures, diverse distribution shifts, and longer context scenarios to further validate the generality of the proposed mechanism.

In summary, TAPIOCA significantly advances the understanding of task-aware pruning, reframing it not as a method for compression but as a tool for "geometric recalibration" that selectively enhances OOD performance by addressing representational distortions. This contributes to the broader goals of building more robust, adaptable, and interpretable artificial intelligence systems.
