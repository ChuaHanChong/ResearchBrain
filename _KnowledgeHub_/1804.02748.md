# Scaling Egocentric Vision: The EPIC-KITCHENS Dataset

> [!summary] Summary
> The EPIC-KITCHENS dataset introduces 55 hours of unscripted egocentric video from 32 participants across diverse kitchens, providing 39.6K action segments and 454.3K object bounding boxes. Baseline evaluations demonstrate that current methods for fine-grained action recognition and anticipation exhibit limited performance and struggle to generalize to unseen environments.

## Problem

- Existing egocentric vision datasets were limited in scale, often featuring short, scripted videos and lacking diversity, which hindered model generalization to real-world, unscripted activities.
- A scarcity of extensively annotated video datasets led to slower progress in video understanding, particularly for first-person perspectives.
- Previous datasets struggled to capture natural multi-tasking, spontaneous decisions, and genuine human intentions due to their scripted nature.

## Method

- Researchers collected 55 hours of unscripted egocentric video from 32 participants across 32 unique kitchen environments in four cities, capturing natural daily routines.
- They implemented a participant-driven narration strategy for post-recording annotation, yielding 39,596 intent-driven action segments and 454,255 bounding box annotations for active objects.
- The dataset establishes benchmarks for object detection, action recognition, and action anticipation, with distinct evaluation splits for 'seen' and 'unseen' kitchens to assess generalization.

## Results

- Object detection baselines (Faster R-CNN) achieved mean Average Precision (mAP) below 40% (e.g., 35.41% on S1, 33.11% on S2 at IoU > 0.5), indicating high challenge and varying performance across object classes.
- Action recognition baselines (TSN Fusion) yielded low Top-1 action accuracy (20.54% for S1, 10.89% for S2), demonstrating difficulty in jointly classifying verbs and nouns.
- Action anticipation (TSN Fusion) was found to be the most challenging, with Top-1 action accuracy dropping significantly to 4.62% for S1 and 1.74% for S2, often exhibiting a bias towards common next actions like 'put'.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Participant-driven narration provides richer, more accurate annotations grounded in true human intention and context, which is difficult for external observers to capture.
> - Understanding unscripted, multi-tasking human activities in diverse, real-world egocentric videos poses a considerably harder challenge for current AI models than controlled or third-person settings.
> - Generalization to entirely unseen environments is a key limitation for current action recognition and anticipation methods, while object detection shows comparatively better adaptability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision has experienced substantial advancements in domains such as image classification, object detection, and visual question answering, largely attributable to progress in deep learning and the availability of large-scale image benchmarks. However, progress in video understanding has been comparatively slower due to a scarcity of extensively annotated datasets. While recent benchmarks have addressed action classification, many of these datasets feature short, scripted videos focused on single actions. For instance, datasets like Charades have attempted activity recognition with daily actions, but these are often enacted under scripted instructions, resulting in less natural interactions and a lack of real-life multi-tasking.

This research specifically addresses egocentric or first-person vision, which offers a unique perspective on human activities, providing insights into interactions with objects, attention mechanisms, and intentions. Egocentric data is considered valuable for tasks such as human-to-robot imitation learning and various Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) applications. Historically, egocentric datasets have been considerably smaller than their third-person counterparts, frequently captured in single, controlled environments, and characterized by scripted activities. This limits the ability of models trained on such data to generalize to diverse, unscripted real-world scenarios that involve multi-tasking, searching, and spontaneous decisions.

EPIC-KITCHENS aims to overcome these limitations by introducing a large-scale, diverse, and unscripted egocentric dataset. It builds upon previous egocentric datasets like ADL, which also captured activities in native environments, but substantially expands the scale and diversity. By focusing on non-scripted daily activities in various real-world kitchen environments, the dataset positions itself to drive advancements in fine-grained video understanding, moving beyond simplified, constrained scenarios to address the complexities of natural human behavior. It contributes to the broader effort of developing robust computational models for understanding human actions and intentions from a first-person perspective, which is crucial for applications requiring contextual awareness and proactive assistance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce EPIC-KITCHENS, a large-scale egocentric video benchmark dataset designed to facilitate progress in first-person vision. This objective is motivated by several identified limitations within the existing research landscape:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity and Scale:** A significant motivation is the observed slow progress in egocentric vision due to a lack of sufficiently large datasets. Prior egocentric datasets are noted as being considerably smaller than third-person benchmarks, hindering the development and evaluation of robust deep learning models that typically benefit from vast amounts of training data. EPIC-KITCHENS aims to provide a dataset of unprecedented scale in this domain, featuring 55 hours of video and 11.5 million frames.

2. **Capturing Natural, Unscripted Activities:** Existing egocentric datasets often rely on scripted activities, where participants are instructed on what actions to perform. This approach can lead to unnatural interactions, sequential rather than multi-tasking behaviors, and a lack of genuine spontaneity. The authors seek to capture authentic daily activities by asking participants to record their natural kitchen routines without specific instructions, thereby reflecting true human intention, multi-tasking, and the variability of real-world tasks (e.g., washing dishes amidst cooking).

3. **Enhancing Diversity and Generalizability:** Many previous egocentric datasets were collected in single environments or by a limited number of participants, which can restrict the generalizability of trained models. The motivation behind EPIC-KITCHENS is to provide a highly diverse dataset, recorded by 32 participants of 10 different nationalities in their native kitchen environments across four cities. This diversity in participants, cultural backgrounds, and physical environments aims to expose algorithms to a wider range of cooking styles, kitchen layouts, and object appearances, promoting models that can generalize to novel, unseen scenarios.

4. **Providing Rich and Intent-Driven Annotations:** The authors motivate the use of a unique annotation strategy where participants narrate their own recorded videos *post-recording*. The rationale is that participants, as the performers of the actions, are the most qualified to describe their true intentions and the specific details of their activities, which might not be evident to an external observer. This approach yields dense annotations for action segments and object bounding boxes that are grounded in the participant's direct experience and intent.

5. **Establishing Comprehensive Benchmarks:** The paper aims to establish clear benchmarks and challenges for the egocentric vision community, specifically focusing on object detection, action recognition, and action anticipation. By providing standardized test splits (seen and unseen kitchens) and baseline results, the authors intend to accelerate research and enable consistent comparison of new methods, particularly emphasizing the critical aspect of generalizability to novel environments.

In summary, the core motivation is to provide a foundational, large-scale, diverse, and realistically annotated dataset that addresses the current limitations in egocentric vision research, thereby stimulating the development of more capable and generalizable artificial intelligence systems for understanding complex human-object interactions from a first-person perspective.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed for creating the EPIC-KITCHENS dataset involved a multi-stage process of data collection, participant-driven narration, and crowd-sourced temporal and spatial annotation, followed by categorization and quality assurance.

#### 3.1. Data Collection

Thirty-two individuals across four cities (Bristol/UK, Toronto/Canada, Catania/Italy, Seattle/USA) voluntarily participated in the data collection between May and November 2017. Participants were asked to record all their kitchen visits for three consecutive days using a head-mounted GoPro camera. The recording started immediately before entering the kitchen and stopped upon leaving, ensuring the capture of complete, natural activity sequences without scripting. Participants were instructed to be alone during recordings and to remove any identity-disclosing items. The GoPro cameras were typically set to a linear field of view, 59.94 frames per second (fps), and Full HD resolution (1920x1080), though minor variations in settings occurred. The dataset comprises 55 hours of video, equivalent to 11.5 million frames. On average, each participant recorded 1.7 hours over 13.6 sequences.

#### 3.2. Narration Collection

To obtain initial, intent-driven annotations, participants watched their own recorded videos after completing data collection and narrated their actions using a hand-held recording device. This post-recording narration approach was chosen to capture true intentions without disturbing natural activities. Participants narrated in their native language or English, resulting in five languages (English, Italian, Spanish, Greek, Chinese). Instructions for narrations emphasized verb-object pairs, present tense, and the optional omission of articles and pronouns. This process yielded 39,596 action narrations, averaging 4.9 seconds per narration and 2.8 words per phrase. The authors acknowledged inherent challenges in narrations, such as incompleteness (e.g., narrating 'open' more than 'close'), temporal belatedness, and the use of free vocabulary.

#### 3.3. Action Segment Annotations

Manual transcriptions of the audio narrations were collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). YouTube's automatic closed caption alignment tool was then used to produce precise timings for these transcriptions. For non-English narrations, AMT workers also provided translations. Speech chunks were segmented to manage the length of tasks for AMT workers.
For each narrated sentence, AMT workers were tasked with adjusting the start and end times of the action segments (A_i = [t_s, t_e]). Annotators were trained using clips explaining temporal action bounds. Constraints included a minimum action length of 0.5 seconds and ensuring an action did not start before the preceding one (though overlaps were permitted). Four annotators (K_a = 4) were assigned to each task (HIT). A consensus-based method was used to derive the final ground-truth action segment: the two annotations with the highest Intersection-over-Union (IoU) agreement were combined if their IoU exceeded 0.5; otherwise, the single best annotation was selected. This process resulted in 39,564 action segments.

#### 3.4. Active Object Bounding Box Annotations

Bounding box annotations were collected for nouns identified in the narrated phrases, representing objects relevant to the action. For each object within an action segment A_i, frames within a window of [t_s - 2s, t_e + 2s] were considered for annotation. An existing AMT interface was adapted for this task. Each HIT targeted one object over a maximum duration of 25 seconds (50 frames at 2fps). Annotators were asked to label consecutive frames to maintain consistency and were assessed for quality based on an IoU ≥ 0.7 on "golden" annotations. Three workers (K_o = 3) were assigned to each HIT, and the annotation with the maximum agreement (β) was chosen, with ties broken by selecting tighter bounding boxes. A total of 454,255 bounding boxes were collected.

#### 3.5. Verb and Noun Classes

Given the free-text and multi-lingual nature of narrations, verbs and nouns were grouped into minimally overlapping classes. Part-of-Speech tagging was performed using SpaCy. Compound nouns were preprocessed (e.g., 'pizza cutter' as 'cutter'), and then verbs and nouns were manually clustered, with some semi-automatic assistance for nouns. This resulted in 125 verb classes (C_V) and 331 noun classes (C_N), which were further categorized into 19 super categories.

#### 3.6. Annotation Quality Assurance

A manual quality assessment was performed on 300 random samples. Error rates were reported for action segment boundaries (5.7%), object bounding boxes (6.3%), verb classes (3.3%), and noun classes (6.0%), which are stated to be comparable to other recently published datasets.

#### 3.7. Benchmarks and Baseline Results

The dataset establishes three challenges: Object Detection, Action Recognition, and Action Anticipation. For evaluation, 27% of the data was held out, structured into two test splits:
- **Seen Kitchens (S1):** Each kitchen appears in both training and testing sets (80% train, 20% test).
- **Unseen Kitchens (S2):** Complete sequences from four participants/kitchens are held out entirely for testing, assessing generalization to novel environments.
Baseline results were provided using established deep learning architectures: Faster R-CNN for object detection, and Temporal Segment Networks (TSN) and 2SCNN for action recognition and anticipation, often with joint prediction of verb and noun classes and fusion of spatial (RGB) and temporal (optical flow) streams. Evaluation metrics included mean average precision (mAP) for object detection and Top-1/Top-5 accuracy, as well as average class precision/recall for action recognition and anticipation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The EPIC-KITCHENS dataset provides a comprehensive benchmark for egocentric vision, and the baseline evaluations reveal several insights into the current capabilities of established methods.

#### 4.1. Dataset Scale and Diversity

The dataset is presented as the largest and most varied egocentric dataset to date, featuring 55 hours of video (11.5M frames) recorded by 32 participants across 32 distinct native kitchen environments, representing 10 nationalities and diverse cooking styles. It contains 39,596 action segments and 454,255 object bounding boxes, significantly exceeding previous egocentric datasets in scale and annotation density. The unscripted nature of the recordings captures natural multi-tasking and real-life interactions.

#### 4.2. Object Detection Benchmark

- **Performance:** Baselines using Faster R-CNN with a ResNet-101 backbone achieved mean Average Precision (mAP) below 40% at an IoU threshold of 0.5. For example, on the S1 test set, mAP at IoU > 0.5 was 35.41%, and on S2, it was 33.11%. This indicates that detecting objects in egocentric kitchen videos is a more challenging task compared to other existing datasets.
- **Class Difficulty:** Performance varied significantly across object classes. Frequently occurring objects like "pan" and "plate" showed higher AP, while "meat," "knife," and "spoon" were notably harder to detect, despite being common.
- **Few-Shot vs. Many-Shot Learning:** Detection performance for "few-shot" classes (10-100 bounding boxes in training) was substantially lower than for "many-shot" classes (≥100 bounding boxes). This highlights a challenge in quickly learning novel objects with limited examples.
- **Generalization to Unseen Environments:** The object detection performance on the Seen Kitchens (S1) split was comparable to that on the Unseen Kitchens (S2) split (e.g., mAP at IoU > 0.5 was 35.41% for S1 vs. 33.11% for S2). This suggests that baseline object detectors exhibit a degree of generalization capability across different kitchen environments.

#### 4.3. Action Recognition Benchmark

- **Overall Challenge:** Action recognition, defined as jointly classifying verb and noun, demonstrated significant room for improvement. The best baseline (TSN Fusion) achieved a Top-1 action accuracy of 20.54% for S1 and 10.89% for S2. This indicates that existing methods struggle to correctly predict both components of an action simultaneously.
- **Component Prediction:** While individual verb and noun predictions were higher (e.g., TSN Fusion Top-1 verb accuracy 48.23% for S1, 39.40% for S2; Top-1 noun accuracy 36.71% for S1, 22.70% for S2), their joint correctness remained low. This implies that methods often correctly identify one component (verb or noun) but fail on the other.
- **Generalization to Unseen Environments:** Generalizing action recognition to unseen environments (S2) proved to be more challenging than for object detection. The Top-1 action accuracy dropped by nearly half from S1 to S2, highlighting a limitation in current models' ability to adapt action understanding to novel settings.
- **Stream Contribution:** For action recognition, the fused spatial (RGB) and temporal (optical flow) streams generally performed best or comparably to the best individual stream.

#### 4.4. Action Anticipation Benchmark

- **Increased Difficulty:** Action anticipation, predicting an action before it happens based on a preceding video segment, was found to be more challenging than action recognition. The best baseline (TSN Fusion) achieved a Top-1 action accuracy of 4.62% for S1 and 1.74% for S2, showing a substantial drop from recognition performance.
- **Stream Contribution (Anticipation):** Unlike recognition, the optical flow stream and the fusion of streams did not consistently improve performance over the RGB stream for anticipation.
- **Behavioral Tendencies:** Qualitative results indicated a tendency for baseline methods to over-predict "put" as the next action once an object has been picked up. This suggests that current methods may lack sufficient long-term goal understanding to circumvent such common learned associations.

In summary, the baseline results underscore the complexity and the significant challenges posed by the EPIC-KITCHENS dataset across object detection, action recognition, and anticipation tasks, particularly when aiming for fine-grained understanding and generalization to unseen environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The EPIC-KITCHENS dataset represents a significant contribution to the field of computer vision, particularly egocentric perception and video understanding, with several potential impacts:

1. **Advancing Egocentric Vision Research:** By providing the largest and most diverse egocentric dataset to date, EPIC-KITCHENS directly addresses the long-standing limitation of data scarcity that has hindered progress in this domain. Its scale (55 hours, 11.5M frames) and rich annotations (39.6K action segments, 454.3K object bounding boxes) offer an unprecedented resource for training and evaluating deep learning models, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in first-person vision.

2. **Enabling Naturalistic Activity Understanding:** The dataset's focus on unscripted, daily activities captured in native kitchen environments (32 unique kitchens, 10 nationalities) offers a realistic representation of human behavior. This moves beyond the artificiality of scripted datasets, allowing researchers to develop models capable of understanding spontaneous actions, multi-tasking, and natural variations in task execution, which are crucial for real-world applications.

3. **Promoting Generalizability to Novel Environments:** The inclusion of both "seen" and "unseen" kitchen splits in the benchmark is critical. It explicitly challenges models to generalize their understanding of objects and actions to entirely new environments, a key requirement for robust AI systems. The baseline results demonstrate that while object detection shows some generalization, action recognition and anticipation are significantly harder in unseen settings, highlighting a major area for future research.

4. **Facilitating Intent and Context-Aware AI:** The unique participant-narrated annotation scheme provides ground truth that reflects true human intention and context. This data can drive the development of AI systems that are not only capable of recognizing "what" is happening but also inferring "why" it is happening, leading to more intelligent and context-aware agents.

5. **Impacting Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and Robotics:** The insights gained from EPIC-KITCHENS have direct implications for HCI and human-to-robot imitation learning. Anticipating a wearer's next action from a first-person view could enable proactive smart home appliances (e.g., a kettle boiling water as someone reaches for a mug) or allow robots to better understand and mimic human tasks. The challenges set forth by the dataset directly contribute to building more assistive and intuitive technologies.

6. **Setting New Benchmarks for Fine-Grained Video Understanding:** The detailed annotation of verb-noun action classes and active object bounding boxes allows for fine-grained analysis of human-object interactions. The baseline results, which show significant room for improvement across object detection, action recognition, and anticipation, establish clear new targets for the research community. This will foster innovation in areas such as temporal localization, multi-modal reasoning (combining video and audio), and long-term goal understanding.

7. **Inspiring Diverse Research Directions:** Beyond the initial challenges, the dataset naturally lends itself to a variety of less-explored tasks such as action localization, video parsing, visual dialogue, goal completion, and even skill determination. By providing a rich foundation, EPIC-KITCHENS is poised to stimulate interdisciplinary research and new problem formulations within computer vision. The stated intention to incorporate real-time performance metrics into leaderboards further encourages the development of efficient solutions suitable for practical deployment.
