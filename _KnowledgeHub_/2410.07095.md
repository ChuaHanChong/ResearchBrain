# MLE-bench: Evaluating Machine Learning Agents on Machine Learning Engineering

> [!summary] Summary
> MLE-bench is an offline benchmark of 75 curated Kaggle competitions that tests whether AI agents can do end-to-end machine learning engineering. Each task supplies a description, dataset, local grader and human leaderboard, and medals are the headline metric. The best setup, o1-preview with the AIDE scaffold, earns a medal in 16.9% of competitions.

## Problem

- Existing coding benchmarks measure isolated code generation or bug fixing, so few benchmarks holistically measure autonomous end-to-end ML engineering such as data preparation, training, debugging and iteration.
- MLAgentBench uses only 13 tasks with simple baselines and asks for a 10% gain over a baseline, not building solutions from scratch, so it is small and easy to saturate.
- Kaggle-derived data science benchmarks such as DSBench filter competitions to fit an automation template, excluding non-standard formats, and offer no direct comparison to human leaderboards on hard, diverse tasks.

## Method

- Dataset curation: manually screens 586 Kaggle competitions down to 75 (plus 7 development), annotating each with problem type and complexity, and rebuilding ==train-test splits== and ==local grading code== where test sets are unreleased.
- Human-anchored metric: agent submissions are scored against each competition's ==private leaderboard== using Kaggle's team-count-dependent ==medal thresholds==, and the headline metric is the percentage of attempts earning any medal.
- Integrity tooling: a ==validation server== checks submission format without scoring, a GPT-4o ==log inspector== detects rule-breaking, and the ==Dolos plagiarism detector== disqualifies code over 60% similar to top public notebooks.

## Results

- With AIDE scaffolding, o1-preview earns any medal in **16.9%** of competitions versus **8.7%** for GPT-4o, **7.6%** for Claude 3.5 Sonnet and **3.0%** for Llama 3.1 405B. On GPT-4o, AIDE beats MLAB at **0.8%** and OpenHands at **4.4%**.
- More attempts help: o1-preview's medal rate roughly doubles from **16.9%** at pass@1 to **34.1%** at pass@8. GPT-4o (AIDE) rises from **8.7%** at 24 hours to **11.8%** at 100 hours.
- Contamination shows no measurable effect: obfuscated descriptions give **8.4%** versus **8.5%** original, and familiarity correlates slightly negatively with performance (**-0.24**). CPU-only, one GPU and two GPUs give similar results (**9.1%**, **8.7%**, **10.2%**), as agents ignore the extra hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scaffold design matters as much as the base model: a purpose-built search scaffold that keeps iterating beat general-purpose agents that stopped early or filled their context, despite similar submission counts.
> - Current agents handle well-known approaches but fail to debug, recover from missteps, or reason about compute and time budgets, so extra hardware alone does not help.
> - Sampling multiple attempts and allowing more time raise scores substantially, so reported agent results should specify attempts, runtime and resources, and can understate what a stronger selection procedure achieves.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly large language models, has seen rapid advancements, leading to impressive performance on various cognitive and coding tasks. This paper positions MLE-bench within this dynamic landscape, addressing a crucial gap in the evaluation of AI agents.

**Current State of AI Agent Evaluation:**
Existing benchmarks have primarily focused on specific aspects of AI capabilities:
- **Code Generation:** Benchmarks like HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021), and Austin et al. (2021) assess models' ability to produce code from natural language descriptions. While frontier models are rapidly saturating these benchmarks, they often fall short of automating complex software engineering workflows.
- **Software Engineering:** SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2024) tasks models with resolving real-world GitHub pull requests, representing a step towards more holistic software development. However, these are often well-defined, bug-fixing tasks rather than open-ended problem-solving.
- **General Agentic Behavior:** AgentBench (Liu et al., 2023) and GAIA (Mialon et al., 2023) evaluate multi-turn challenges and real-world interactions, but typically do not specialize in the domain of machine learning development.

**Gap Identified by the Authors:**
Despite progress in model capabilities and agent scaffolding, there remains a significant lack of benchmarks that "holistically measure autonomous end-to-end ML engineering." Current approaches often:
- **Lack Diversity and Complexity:** Previous Kaggle-based benchmarks, such as MLAgentBench (Huang et al., 2024b), use a limited number of tasks or provide simpler baselines. Weco AI's AIDE (Schmidt et al., 2024) and DSBench (Jing et al., 2024) also focus on data science tasks, with filtering criteria that exclude many non-standard or more challenging competitions.
- **Focus on Specific Sub-tasks:** ML-Bench (Tang et al., 2024) evaluates interaction with ML repositories and code generation but not the full cycle of developing a novel ML solution.
- **Are Not Representative of Real-World Challenges:** Many existing coding benchmarks are too constrained or too easy to reflect the open-ended, iterative, and often ambiguous nature of real-world ML engineering.

**MLE-bench's Contribution to the Landscape:**
MLE-bench directly addresses this gap by creating a benchmark that is:
- **Holistic and End-to-End:** It requires agents to perform the entire ML engineering workflow, from understanding a problem description and preparing datasets to training models, running experiments, debugging, and generating final submissions.
- **Challenging and Representative:** By curating 75 diverse Kaggle competitions, including contemporary and real-world challenges (e.g., COVID-19 mRNA vaccine degradation, Vesuvius Challenge), MLE-bench captures the complexity and variety of modern ML engineering.
- **Human-Comparable:** Leveraging Kaggle's public leaderboards and medal system allows for a direct comparison of agent performance against human experts, providing a meaningful measure of progress.
- **Offline and Controlled:** The environment provides a controlled, reproducible setting for evaluation while maintaining the essence of the real competition experience.

The paper highlights the "exciting prospect" of AI agents accelerating scientific progress and the parallel need for "careful understanding of model progress in order to deploy advancements in a safe and controlled manner." By providing a rigorous evaluation tool, MLE-bench serves as a critical measure for model autonomy within safety frameworks of leading AI labs, including OpenAI's own preparedness efforts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **MLE-bench**, a novel benchmark designed to rigorously measure the ability of AI agents to perform machine learning engineering (MLE) tasks autonomously. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and specific design choices:

**Core Objectives:**
1. **Develop a Robust ML Engineering Benchmark:** To create a standardized, diverse, and challenging benchmark that accurately reflects real-world ML engineering skills, encompassing tasks like dataset preparation, model training, and experimental execution.
2. **Enable Human-Level Comparison:** To establish a mechanism for comparing AI agent performance directly against human experts, leveraging the established competitive environment of Kaggle.
3. **Facilitate Research on Autonomous ML Agents:** To provide an open-source tool that allows researchers to evaluate and improve AI agents' capabilities in solving complex, open-ended ML problems.

**Underlying Motivations:**
1. **Address the Gap in Holistic Evaluation:** As identified in the broader research landscape, there was a need for an end-to-end benchmark that moves beyond isolated coding tasks or limited data science problems to assess comprehensive ML engineering skills.
2. **Measure Real-World Progress:** The authors sought to create a measure that indicates genuine progress in autonomous ML engineering agents, going beyond simplified academic scenarios to practical, complex challenges.
3. **Accelerate Scientific Progress:** The potential for AI agents to autonomously solve ML challenges could significantly accelerate scientific discovery and development in fields such as healthcare, climate science, and fundamental AI research (including safety and alignment research itself).
4. **Inform AI Safety and Preparedness:** Understanding the current and future capabilities of AI agents in performing sophisticated ML tasks is crucial for the safe development and deployment of increasingly powerful AI models. MLE-bench serves as a tool to measure autonomy in the context of "Preparedness Frameworks" adopted by leading AI organizations, allowing for better risk assessment and governance.
5. **Understand Limits and Prospects:** The benchmark aims to reveal the strengths and weaknesses of current frontier language models and agent frameworks when applied to demanding ML engineering problems, identifying areas for future improvement.

In essence, the motivation is dual: to push the boundaries of AI agent capabilities by providing a hard, realistic test, and simultaneously to provide a critical measurement tool for understanding and safely managing the risks associated with rapid advancements in AI autonomy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in developing MLE-bench and evaluating AI agents on it is rigorous and multi-faceted, focusing on creating a realistic and challenging environment for ML engineering tasks.

#### 3.1. MLE-bench Design and Curation

- **Offline Kaggle Environment:** MLE-bench is designed as an offline simulation of Kaggle competitions. Each competition includes a description, a dataset, and grading code, allowing for local evaluation of agent submissions. Submissions are compared against real human leaderboards.
- **Competition Sourcing:** The benchmark comprises 75 machine learning engineering tasks, manually sourced from 5673 completed Kaggle competitions available on the Meta Kaggle dataset. This manual curation is critical to ensure relevance to "modern-day ML engineering" and task quality.
- **Rigorous Screening Criteria:** Competitions were selected based on nine specific criteria (detailed in Appendix A.1), including:
 - Requirement for ML engineering capabilities to achieve a medal.
 - Well-specified and unambiguous problem descriptions.
 - Local computability of evaluation metrics.
 - Competition completion (unlikely to change).
 - Use of non-widely-used datasets (to mitigate contamination).
 - Feasibility of creating new train-test splits from public data with similar distributions.
 - Requirement for a CSV submission file.
 - No external data downloads beyond Kaggle.
 - Permissive dataset licenses.
- **Dataset Handling:** For competitions where the original test set was unavailable (common in Kaggle), new train and test splits were manually created from the publicly available training data. Great care was taken to ensure the new splits maintained similar data distributions to the original and that sample/gold submissions yielded consistent results. The new test set was typically 10% of the original training data.
- **Task Annotation:** Each competition was manually annotated with its problem type (e.g., text classification, image segmentation) and complexity level (Low, Medium, High) based on estimated time for an experienced ML engineer to produce a sensible solution (Low: <2 hours, Medium: 2-10 hours, High: >10 hours). This provides crucial meta-information for analyzing agent performance across different task characteristics.
- **Local Grading Logic:** The evaluation logic for each competition was re-implemented based on its described metric, enabling local grading of agent submissions.

#### 3.2. Metrics

- **Leaderboard Comparison:** Agent performance is contextualized using snapshots of the original Kaggle competition's *Private* leaderboard. The private leaderboard is preferred over the public one to mitigate overfitting concerns.
- **Medal System:** The standard Kaggle medal system (Bronze, Silver, Gold) is applied to agent submissions by comparing their scores against human thresholds. These thresholds vary by the number of participants, ensuring consistent achievement levels.
- **Headline Metric:** The primary metric is the "percentage of attempts that are awarded any medal (bronze and above)," designed as a challenging, high-level measure of success.
- **Raw Scores:** Raw scores for each competition are also reported to track granular progress, though they are not aggregated due to differing metrics.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Execution Environment:** Agents are run in an isolated Ubuntu 20.04 Docker container within a secure cluster environment (Microsoft Azure Standard_NV36ads_A10_v5 VM), providing consistent hardware: 36 vCPUs, 440GB RAM, 4095 GiB SSD, and a single 24GB Nvidia A10 GPU.
- **Time Limit:** Each competition attempt has a maximum runtime of 24 hours.
- **Multiple Seeds:** Experiments are typically repeated with 3 seeds (runs per competition) to compute mean and standard error, with some key experiments using more (16 or 36 seeds).
- **Local Validation Server:** Agents have access to a local validation server to check submission file format validity, but this tool provides no score feedback.
- **Agent Prompts:** Agents are prompted with overall instructions about the benchmark, including competition-specific instructions, dataset location, submission format, validation server usage, and explicit rules against cheating.

#### 3.4. Rules and Mitigation Strategies

- **Forbidden Actions:** Agents are strictly forbidden from manually writing predictions to the submission file (ensuring ML engineering engagement) and from viewing online solutions.
- **Rule-Breaking Detection:** An internal tool uses GPT-4o mini to inspect agent logs for violations like manual submission writing, external LLM API calls, or unauthorized resource access. Human review is performed on flagged cases.
- **Plagiarism Detection:** The open-source code plagiarism detection tool Dolos (Maertens et al., 2024) is used to compare agent code against the top 50 associated Kaggle notebooks. Submissions with a similarity score >60% are flagged for review and disqualified if confirmed.
- **Contamination Investigation:**
 - **Familiarity Analysis:** Measures the correlation between GPT-4o's internal "familiarity" (mean token probability) with competition discussions and its performance.
 - **Obfuscated Descriptions:** An experiment where all 75 competition descriptions were manually rewritten to remove identifying information (e.g., Kaggle references, competition names) to test if models rely on memorizing solutions linked to specific problem identifiers.

#### 3.5. Agent Frameworks and Models Evaluated

- **Scaffolding:** Three open-source agent scaffolds were evaluated: AIDE (Schmidt et al., 2024), ResearchAgent (MLAB) from MLAgentBench (Huang et al., 2024b), and CodeActAgent (OpenHands) from the OpenHands platform (Wang et al., 2024). Minor modifications were made to each for benchmark compatibility and performance enhancement.
- **Language Models:** Four frontier models were tested with the best-performing scaffold (AIDE): OpenAI's o1-preview and GPT-4o, Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Meta's Llama 3.1 405B.

#### 3.6. Resource Scaling Experiments

- **Increasing Attempts (pass@k):** Evaluated performance when agents are allowed multiple attempts per competition.
- **Varying Compute:** Tested GPT-4o (AIDE) across different hardware configurations: CPU-only, standard (1 GPU), and extra GPU (2 GPUs).
- **Increasing Time:** Extended the time limit from 24 hours to 100 hours per competition for GPT-4o (AIDE) to observe performance changes over longer durations.

This comprehensive methodological approach ensures that MLE-bench provides a robust and fair evaluation of AI agents' ML engineering capabilities, with careful consideration for common challenges like data leakage and reproducibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using MLE-bench yielded several key findings regarding the current capabilities and limitations of state-of-the-art AI agents in machine learning engineering.

#### 4.1. Performance Across Scaffolding and Models

- **Scaffold Impact:** The choice of agent scaffolding significantly influences performance. GPT-4o, when paired with the purpose-built **AIDE** scaffold (designed for Kaggle competitions), substantially outperformed the same model with general-purpose scaffolds like MLAB and OpenHands. GPT-4o (AIDE) achieved medals in 8.7% of competitions, compared to 0.8% for MLAB and 4.4% for OpenHands. This highlights the importance of domain-specific scaffolding and optimized iterative loops for complex tasks.
- **Model Performance:** Among the models evaluated with the AIDE scaffold, **OpenAI's o1-preview** demonstrated superior performance, achieving a medal in **16.9%** of competitions. This is nearly double the medal rate of the next best model, GPT-4o (8.7%), and significantly higher than Claude 3.5 Sonnet (7.6%) and Llama 3.1 405B (3.0%). The authors note that o1-preview's average of 7 gold medals on MLE-bench is comparable to the Kaggle Grandmaster requirement of 5 gold medals, though with caveats regarding dataset modifications and agents' access to more recent technologies.
- **Failure Modes:** A common issue across all agents was the failure to consistently create valid submissions, despite access to a validation server. Agents also often did not fully utilize the available time, with MLAB and OpenHands sometimes ending runs prematurely. In contrast, AIDE's design repeatedly prompts the model to improve, better utilizing the allocated time. Critically, agents generally struggled to effectively manage compute and time limitations, sometimes overloading resources, and rarely verbalized strategic considerations for resource usage.
- **Complexity and Task Category:** Agents performed better on "Low" and "Medium" complexity tasks, and particular categories like Image Regression, Sequence-to-Sequence, Signal Processing, and Tabular tasks. They notably struggled with "High" complexity tasks and more recent competitions (post-2022).

#### 4.2. Impact of Resource Scaling

- **Number of Attempts (pass@k):** Providing agents with multiple attempts significantly improved performance. For o1-preview (AIDE), the medal rate doubled from 16.9% (pass@1) to **34.1%** (pass@8). Similarly, GPT-4o (AIDE) saw its score double from 8.7% (pass@1) to 17.0% (pass@6). This indicates that agents benefit greatly from iterative refinement and self-correction over multiple tries, suggesting current models can often identify better solutions if given more chances.
- **Compute Availability:** Surprisingly, providing GPT-4o (AIDE) with more compute resources (e.g., a second GPU) did not yield a significant performance improvement. Performance remained similar across CPU-only (9.1% medals), standard (8.7% medals), and extra GPU (10.2% medals) setups. Manual log inspection suggested that agents did not effectively utilize the additional GPU. This points to a current limitation in agent planning and resource management capabilities.
- **Time Availability:** Increasing the time limit per competition from 24 hours to 100 hours also led to performance gains for GPT-4o (AIDE), albeit at a slower pace. The medal rate increased from 8.7% (at 24 hours) to **11.8%** (at 100 hours). This indicates that longer iteration cycles can help agents accumulate more medals, reinforcing the value of extended runtime for complex problem-solving.

#### 4.3. Contamination and Plagiarism Analysis

- **No Systematic Contamination:** The authors performed experiments to investigate potential score inflation due to models memorizing solutions from their pre-training data.
 - **Familiarity with Top Solutions:** No positive correlation was found between GPT-4o's "familiarity" (measured by token probability on competition discussion posts) and its performance on a given competition. This suggests that explicit memorization of discussion-level strategies does not systematically drive performance.
 - **Obfuscated Descriptions:** When competition descriptions were manually rewritten to remove identifying details, GPT-4o (AIDE) showed **no significant difference** in performance (8.5% medals for original vs. 8.4% for obfuscated). This further supports the finding that the model is not simply regurgitating solutions based on problem recognition.
- **Plagiarism and Rule-Breaking Detection:** The automated plagiarism detection tool (Dolos, >60% similarity threshold) found no evidence of plagiarism in any medal-winning submissions. Similarly, the log analysis tool, despite initial false positives, confirmed no instances of agents breaking rules (e.g., manual submission writing, external LLM API calls).

In summary, the findings show that while frontier models paired with specialized scaffolding can achieve meaningful performance on ML engineering tasks, they still face significant challenges in debugging, resource optimization, and tackling truly novel or high-complexity problems. The investigation into contamination provides a degree of confidence that the reported capabilities reflect genuine problem-solving rather than mere memorization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MLE-bench represents a significant contribution to the field of AI, particularly in the domain of evaluating and advancing autonomous AI agents. Its potential impact spans scientific progress, AI safety, and the future of human-AI collaboration in engineering.

#### 5.1. Advancing AI Agent Evaluation

- **Fills a Critical Gap:** MLE-bench addresses the crucial need for a comprehensive, end-to-end benchmark for ML engineering capabilities. Unlike existing benchmarks that focus on isolated coding tasks or simpler data science problems, MLE-bench simulates the complex, iterative, and often open-ended nature of real-world ML development.
- **Robust and Realistic Assessment:** By curating 75 diverse Kaggle competitions, including contemporary challenges with real-world value, MLE-bench provides a more robust and realistic measure of agents' skills in data preparation, model training, experimentation, and debugging. The direct comparison to human performance via Kaggle leaderboards adds a meaningful context to agent capabilities.
- **Facilitates Future Research:** By open-sourcing the benchmark, the authors aim to catalyze further research. This allows other researchers to evaluate new models and agent frameworks rigorously, fostering faster progress in understanding and developing autonomous ML agents.

#### 5.2. Implications for AI R&D Acceleration

- **Potential for Scientific Breakthroughs:** The paper highlights that AI agents capable of autonomously solving the types of challenges in MLE-bench could lead to a "great acceleration in scientific progress." This could translate into breakthroughs in various domains, such as healthcare (e.g., drug discovery, disease prediction), climate science (e.g., climate modeling, renewable energy optimization), and fundamental AI research itself (e.g., developing better training algorithms or architectures).
- **Transformative Economic Impact:** Autonomous ML research capabilities could fundamentally change economies by rapidly innovating and developing new products and services, creating new industries, and significantly increasing productivity across sectors.

#### 5.3. Contributions to AI Safety and Governance

- **Measuring Autonomy for Safety Frameworks:** A key motivation for MLE-bench is its utility in assessing model autonomy within "Preparedness Frameworks" (OpenAI, Anthropic) and "Frontier Safety Frameworks" (Google DeepMind). By providing a concrete measure of an agent's ability to execute complex ML tasks, the benchmark helps evaluate the risks associated with increasingly powerful and autonomous AI systems. This is crucial for guiding the safe deployment of frontier models.
- **Understanding Risks of Rapid Progress:** The authors explicitly state that if innovations are produced faster than our ability to understand their impacts, there is a risk of developing models capable of catastrophic harm or misuse without parallel developments in securing, aligning, and controlling them. MLE-bench helps monitor the rate of progress in autonomous ML R&D, informing proactive safety measures.
- **Transparency:** Open-sourcing the benchmark increases transparency into the agentic capabilities being developed at leading research labs, contributing to a broader understanding and public discourse on AI safety.

#### 5.4. Insights into Current Agent Limitations

- **Debugging and Resource Management:** The findings reveal that even frontier models struggle with fundamental aspects of ML engineering, such as effective debugging and strategic management of compute and time resources. This pinpoints critical areas for future agent research, moving beyond mere code generation to more sophisticated reasoning and execution.
- **Generalization vs. Specialization:** The superior performance of purpose-built scaffolds (AIDE) over general-purpose ones (MLAB, OpenHands) suggests that while LLMs are powerful, effective agentic behavior often requires significant domain-specific engineering and iterative control loops.

#### 5.5. Acknowledged Limitations

The authors are transparent about the benchmark's limitations:
- **Contamination Risk:** Despite mitigation efforts, the use of publicly available Kaggle data raises concerns about models having memorized solutions or insights. The experiments, while reassuring, do not entirely rule out subtle forms of contamination.
- **Coverage of AI R&D:** MLE-bench focuses on well-defined Kaggle problems with clean datasets and clear metrics. Real-world AI R&D often involves ill-defined problems, ambiguous data, and the need to formulate research questions, which MLE-bench does not fully capture.
- **Reproducibility Challenges:** The high computational and token costs of running MLE-bench make full reproduction resource-intensive, potentially limiting accessibility for smaller research groups.

In conclusion, MLE-bench is a timely and critical contribution, providing a robust, realistic, and open-source platform for evaluating the rapidly evolving capabilities of AI agents in machine learning engineering. Its significance lies not only in pushing the boundaries of AI autonomy but also in serving as an essential tool for assessing and mitigating the risks associated with the accelerated development of powerful AI systems.
