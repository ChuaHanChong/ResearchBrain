# Heterogeneous Tactile Transformer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the National University of Singapore and Carnegie Mellon University developed the Heterogeneous Tactile Transformer (HTT) and a large-scale paired dataset to learn unified tactile representations across different sensor types like optical and array-based. This framework demonstrated improved performance on various perception tasks and enabled enhanced policy learning and zero-shot adaptation for contact-rich robot manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Tactile sensors are highly heterogeneous, differing in transduction mechanisms, measured properties, and data formats (e.g., optical images vs. array-based time series).
- A significant gap existed in general, self-supervised frameworks capable of learning shared, transferable representations across these disparate tactile sensor families from large-scale paired data.
- Existing tactile learning models are often sensor-specific, restricting their scalability and reusability across diverse robotic platforms and tasks.

## Method

- Proposed the **Heterogeneous Tactile Transformer (HTT)**, a self-supervised framework designed to process and align diverse tactile sensor inputs using a ==Masked Autoencoder (MAE)== design.
- Created the ==Heterogeneous Paired Tactile (HPT) dataset==, a novel large-scale collection of 1.6 million synchronized frames from paired optical and array-based tactile sensors.
- HTT pretraining combines ==MAE-style masked reconstruction== for sensor-specific feature learning and ==cross-modal alignment== to unify representations into a shared latent space.

## Results

- HTT pretraining yielded substantial improvements in tactile perception tasks, including object classification (up to **17%** higher accuracy) and physics-grounded force estimation and slip detection (e.g., **+12.0 macro-F1** for slip on TAC-02).
- The framework significantly enhanced performance in real-world contact-rich robot manipulation, increasing success rates for a toy screw task from **50%** to **95%** and for grasping tofu from **35%** to **55%**.
- HTT demonstrated adaptability to new, unseen tactile sensors through zero-shot adaptation or by serving as a reusable backbone for learning new encoders, reducing the need for extensive sensor-specific pretraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cross-sensor alignment, when combined with masked autoencoding, effectively binds complementary signals from different sensor types, improving representation quality for tasks like slip detection.
> - A phased pretraining schedule, starting with MAE-only warmup and then introducing cross-modal alignment with controlled gradient flow, prevents representation collapse and ensures robust feature learning.
> - The framework successfully integrates distinct information streams (e.g., spatial geometry from optical, high-rate force from array) into a unified representation, capitalizing on the strengths of each modality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing is recognized as a critical component for robots to perform complex manipulation tasks, particularly those involving intricate contact, such as handling delicate objects, precision assembly, or identifying material properties. However, a significant challenge in tactile learning at scale is the inherent heterogeneity of tactile sensors. Unlike general-purpose cameras, tactile sensors vary substantially in their transduction mechanisms, the physical properties they measure, and the data formats they produce.

The research landscape for tactile sensors broadly includes:
- **Optical tactile sensors:** Examples like GelSight-style devices (e.g., GelSight Mini, 9DTact) capture visual images of elastomer deformation, providing rich spatial and geometric information. Their limitations often include camera frame rates and sensor-to-sensor manufacturing variations.
- **Array-based tactile sensors:** Examples like Xela uSkin and TAC-02 provide distributed measurements through sensing elements that convert physical stimuli into electrical signals. These typically offer higher temporal rates and direct force/pressure readings but often have lower spatial resolution.

These two families of sensors offer complementary information. Optical sensors provide fine-grained spatial data, while array-based sensors excel in high-rate force-sensitive measurements. However, their raw outputs (images versus multi-dimensional time series) are structurally disparate, making it challenging to combine their data or develop reusable, sensor-agnostic representations.

Previous research in tactile representation learning has largely concentrated on optical tactile sensors. Methods like Masked Autoencoders (MAE) have demonstrated success in learning features from GelSight-style images. Nevertheless, models trained exclusively on optical data cannot directly process array-based signals and may not be optimal for tasks that heavily depend on high-rate force or slip cues. While some efforts have explored fusing heterogeneous tactile data for specific supervised tasks or learning mappings between sensor technologies, a general, self-supervised framework for learning shared, transferable representations across widely different tactile sensor families (optical and array-based) from large-scale paired data has been a notable gap.

This work directly addresses this gap by proposing a framework and dataset designed to learn unified tactile representations across these heterogeneous sensor types, aiming to improve transferability to novel tasks and previously unseen sensors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for learning shared, transferable tactile representations across inherently heterogeneous tactile sensors. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

- **Addressing Sensor Heterogeneity:** Current tactile learning models are typically sensor-specific, meaning a model trained on data from one sensor cannot be directly applied to another. This limits the scalability of tactile learning and the ability to leverage diverse tactile data sources. The authors aim to bridge this gap by enabling models to learn a common understanding of contact properties despite differences in sensor output.
- **Facilitating Contact-Rich Manipulation:** Many robotic manipulation tasks, such as precise object handling, assembly, or material identification, heavily rely on nuanced contact information. The inability to combine or generalize across different tactile modalities restricts the complexity and robustness of learned manipulation policies. A shared representation could unlock more capable robot behaviors in such scenarios.
- **Leveraging Complementary Sensor Strengths:** Optical and array-based tactile sensors offer distinct, yet complementary, information (e.g., detailed spatial geometry from optical sensors and high-rate force/pressure from array sensors). The motivation is to create a framework that can integrate these different information streams into a unified representation, thereby capitalizing on the strengths of each modality.
- **Moving Beyond Optical-Only Learning:** Much of the progress in tactile representation learning has focused on optical tactile sensors. This leaves a significant portion of tactile sensing technologies, particularly array-based sensors which are crucial for dynamic force and slip detection, underutilized in general representation learning. The authors are motivated to develop a "tactile backbone" that can align representations from both optical and array-based sensors in a shared latent space.
- **Enabling Transferability and Generalization:** A key motivation is to learn representations that are not only robust to sensor variations but also transferable to new tasks and generalize effectively to previously unseen sensor types. This would reduce the need for extensive retraining or data collection when deploying robots with different tactile hardware or encountering novel manipulation scenarios.
- **Constructing a Foundational Dataset:** Recognizing the lack of large-scale, synchronized heterogeneous tactile datasets, the authors are motivated to create such a resource. This dataset is crucial for training self-supervised models that require paired observations to learn cross-modal alignments.

In summary, the work seeks to enable more general and scalable tactile intelligence in robotics by overcoming the fragmentation caused by sensor heterogeneity, thereby fostering more capable and adaptable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology involves the **Heterogeneous Tactile Transformer (HTT)**, a self-supervised framework, trained on a novel, large-scale dataset called the **Heterogeneous Paired Tactile (HPT) dataset**.

#### 3.1. Heterogeneous Paired Tactile (HPT) Dataset

To address the lack of paired optical and array-based tactile data, the authors developed a custom data acquisition system based on a Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI). This setup enabled synchronous collection of heterogeneous tactile data during real-world sensor-object interactions.
- **Sensor Configuration:** Two primary sensor configurations were used: Pair A (Xela uSkin ↔ 9DTact) and Pair B (TAC-02 ↔ GelSight Mini). Sensors were mounted on opposing sides of gripper fingers using 3D-printed modular shells, allowing simultaneous measurement.
- **Scale and Diversity:** The HPT dataset comprises 1.6 million synchronized tactile frames across the four distinct sensors. Interactions involved unscripted manipulation of a diverse set of daily-life objects, encompassing press, twist, and slide motions.
- **Dataset Components:** The HPT dataset includes:
 - **Paired Unlabeled Interaction Data:** This forms the core for HTT's self-supervised pretraining, containing synchronized paired streams from both sensors during object interactions.
 - **Object Classification Data:** Labeled data collected using UMI on 20 distinct objects, varying in geometry, texture, and material, with actions like press, twist, and slide.
 - **Force Estimation Data:** Collected using a probing rig where an external 6-D Force/Torque (F/T) sensor provides synchronized ground-truth force labels. Four distinct probe geometries were used to elicit varied contact patterns.
 - **Slip Detection Data:** Collected using the same probing rig setup, with slip labels derived post-hoc from the 6-D force signal. A friction-coefficient time series was computed, standardized, and a two-sided Page CUSUM change-point detector was applied to classify frames into static, incipient, or gross slip. The dataset exhibits a class imbalance (85% slide, 13.6% static, 1.2% incipient).

#### 3.2. Heterogeneous Tactile Transformer (HTT) Architecture

HTT adopts a Masked Autoencoder (MAE) design, engineered to process diverse sensor inputs and align their representations.
- **Input Patching:** Input data is organized into a fixed 0.2-second temporal interaction window.
 - **Optical Sensors:** Individual frames (resized to 224x224x3) are tokenized into non-overlapping spatial patches, similar to Vision Transformers (ViT).
 - **Array-based Sensors:** High-frequency multi-dimensional time series are tokenized into non-overlapping temporal patches.
 - Background subtraction (using a non-contact reference frame) is applied to all raw tactile signals. All embeddings are projected to a uniform dimension (D=192).
- **Modular Architecture:** HTT consists of four main components:
 - **Sensor-specific Encoders (E_i):** Transforms tactile tokens into embeddings. ViT is used for optical data, while self-attention transformers are used for array-based time series.
 - **Shared Transformer Trunk (T):** A unified transformer network (depth 9) that processes token embeddings from all sensors in a common latent space.
 - **Sensor-specific Decoders (D_i):** Used during pretraining for masked reconstruction tasks.
 - **Cross-modal Predictors (P_ij):** Cross-attention transformers (depth 3) that predict masked target embeddings from source and visible target embeddings.

#### 3.3. HTT Pretraining

HTT pretraining combines two self-supervised objectives:
- **MAE Reconstruction (L_MAE):** For each modality *i*, a MAE-style objective is applied. The encoder *E_i* and shared trunk *T* process only the visible patches of input *x_i_v*. The sensor-specific decoder *D_i* then reconstructs the masked patches *x_i_m*. This forces the model to learn robust, high-fidelity features by reconstructing missing spatial (for optical) or temporal (for array-based) signals. Masking ratios are 0.75 for optical and 0.60 for taxel modalities.
- **Cross-Modal Alignment (L_Align):** For every ordered pair of sensors (i, j) in the dataset, the model is trained to predict the masked target embedding from the full source embedding and the visible target embedding. A stop-gradient (sg[·]) is applied to the regression target to prevent representation collapse. This aligns the disparate modalities within the shared latent space. Target mask ratios are 0.80 for taxel and 0.90 for optical.
- **Joint Pretraining Loss (L_HTT):** The total loss is a weighted sum: L_MAE + α_t * L_Align.
 - **Phased Schedule:** During an initial warmup period (first 20,000 steps), α_t is 0, so only L_MAE is active, allowing encoders and the trunk to develop sensor-specific features. Subsequently, α_t is linearly ramped up to a maximum value of 0.1 for the remaining steps.
 - **Gradient Flow Control:** Alignment gradients are blocked at the encoder outputs, meaning L_Align primarily updates the predictors *P_ij* and the shared trunk *T*, while encoders *E_i* are updated solely by L_MAE. This prevents the alignment task from distorting sensor-specific feature extraction.

After pretraining, the decoders *D_i* and predictors *P_ij* are discarded. Only the sensor encoder *E_i* and the shared trunk *T* are used to generate embeddings for downstream perception and manipulation tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HTT addresses five key questions across tactile perception and robot manipulation tasks:

- **Q1: Usefulness of HTT pretraining for heterogeneous sensors?**
 - **Object Classification:** HTT significantly outperformed the "Scratch" baseline (no pretraining) on all four heterogeneous sensors, confirming that HTT pretraining yields useful features. HTT also surpassed existing optical-only representation learning methods (SITR, T3) on optical sensors, with improvements of 13.5% on 9DTact and 17% on GSMini over SITR. This indicates HTT's ability to learn superior optical features in a heterogeneous context.

- **Q2: Transferability of HTT representation to distinct tasks?**
 - **Force Estimation & Slip Detection:** HTT and its variant (MAE-only pretraining) consistently and substantially outperformed baselines (Scratch, T3, SITR) across all sensors for both force estimation (lower 3D MAE) and slip detection (higher Macro-F1). This demonstrates HTT's capacity to transfer learned representations to physics-grounded perception tasks, even when there is a distribution shift from the pretraining data (UMI interactions vs. controlled probing rig). Optical-only baselines often performed poorly or worsened results on these force-sensitive tasks.

- **Q3: Benefit of cross-sensor alignment?**
 - **Object Classification:** Cross-sensor alignment (comparing HTT to MAE-only pretraining) improved accuracy on optical sensors (+4.8% on 9DTact, +2.8% on GSMini). However, it resulted in a minor decrease on the array-based Xela sensor (-4.3%). This asymmetry was attributed to a potential information imbalance, where the richer optical signals benefited more from complementary force cues, while coarser array signals might experience minor drift.
 - **Force & Slip Detection:** HTT generally outperformed MAE-only pretraining on these tasks, with the largest gain observed in slip detection (+12.0 macro-F1 on TAC-02). Slip detection relies on both force and fine-grained spatial cues, suggesting that cross-modal alignment effectively binds these complementary signals from paired optical and array sensors, leading to more robust representations, particularly beneficial for imbalanced class distributions.

- **Q4: Boost in contact-rich policy learning?**
 - **Real-World Robot Manipulation (Toy Screw & Grasp Tofu):** On a Sharpa hand mounted on a Franka arm, HTT embeddings significantly improved policy learning. A proprioception-only policy (qpos) failed (5% success). Adding 6-D wrench vectors improved success (screw: 50%, tofu: 35%). Replacing wrench with HTT embeddings further enhanced performance (screw: 95%, tofu: 55%). HTT enabled the policy to maintain grip for the cyclic regrip required in the toy screw task and reduced slip rates by one-third in the grasp tofu task, suggesting HTT provides richer spatial contact features than raw force readouts.
 - **Simulated ManiFeel Tasks (Peg Insertion & Bulb Installation):** HTT (both for tactile RGB and tactile force field data) outperformed baselines in peg insertion. In bulb installation, HTT achieved competitive performance. These results corroborated HTT's ability to boost policy learning.

- **Q5: Adaptability to new, unseen sensors?**
 - **Real-World Robot Manipulation:** The Sharpa hand's finger-tip sensors were not part of HTT's pretraining dataset. The HTT framework demonstrated zero-shot adaptation by applying the pretrained 9DTact encoder, yielding substantial performance gains.
 - **Simulated ManiFeel Tasks:** For the tactile force field (TacFF) data, a new TacFF encoder was learned by initializing its decoder and a new encoder with the pretrained shared trunk as a backbone, trained with MAE loss. This approach also resulted in improved performance. These findings indicate HTT's potential to serve as a reusable tactile backbone for new, previously unseen tactile sensors without requiring sensor-specific pretraining from scratch.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Heterogeneous Tactile Transformer (HTT) and the Heterogeneous Paired Tactile (HPT) dataset represent contributions to the field of robotic tactile sensing and manipulation.

The **HPT dataset** is a large-scale collection of 1.6 million synchronized paired frames across diverse optical and array-based tactile sensors. This dataset addresses a recognized gap in publicly available resources for heterogeneous tactile representation learning and provides a foundation for future research in this area.

The **HTT framework** demonstrates a practical and effective method for learning shared representations across inherently different tactile sensing modalities. Its self-supervised pretraining, combining masked reconstruction with cross-modal alignment, enables the model to extract robust, sensor-specific features while simultaneously aligning these features into a common latent space.

The findings indicate that HTT-derived representations offer **transferability** across distinct perception tasks (object classification, force estimation, slip detection) and **generalization** to new, unseen sensors in real-world manipulation scenarios. This capability mitigates the challenge of tactile sensor heterogeneity, which has traditionally limited the scalability and reusability of tactile learning models.

The improved performance observed in **contact-rich robot manipulation tasks** (toy screw, grasp tofu, peg insertion) suggests that HTT can enhance the intelligence and robustness of robotic systems. By providing richer and more transferable tactile embeddings than raw sensor outputs or single-modality approaches, HTT contributes to the development of policies that can execute complex interactions requiring fine-grained contact understanding.

In the broader context of robot learning, HTT offers a path toward **sensor-agnostic tactile backbones**, which could simplify the deployment of robots with varying tactile hardware and accelerate the development of generalist robot skills that rely on touch. This work lays a foundation for future research directions, including the extension of alignment to other sensing families, investigation of intra-family pairing effects, and the development of finer-grained spatial cross-modal supervision.
