# Physics-Conditioned Grasping for Stable Tool Use

> [!summary] Summary
> Purdue University researchers developed a framework that selects robotic grasps based on predicted interaction forces and torques during tool use, addressing mechanical instability. This approach achieved a 17.5% improvement in real-world task success compared to baselines and consistently reduced peak induced wrist torque by up to 17.6% in simulation.

## Problem

- Robotic tool use often fails due to mechanical instability (slip, rotation) caused by interaction forces exceeding grasp limits, rather than semantic misunderstanding.
- Current grasp selection metrics, relying on geometry or quasi-static assumptions, do not account for dynamic forces, inertial impulses, and lever-arm amplification that generate significant wrist torque.
- There is a limited coupling between grasp selection and the predicted interaction wrench induced by a specific task trajectory, meaning a grasp stable for static lifting may fail under active tool-object interaction.

## Method

- Introduced inverse Tool-use Planning (iTuP), a framework that conditions grasp selection on the predicted interaction wrench (forces and torques) derived from a task-conditioned trajectory.
- Developed a principled, physics-based method to quantify destabilizing effects of task-induced wrench using three penalties: interaction torque, slip, and alignment deviation, derived from rigid-body mechanics.
- Created the ==Stable Dynamic Grasp Network (SDG-Net)==, a learned surrogate model that approximates these trajectory-conditioned wrench costs in real-time, enabling efficient evaluation of numerous candidate grasps.

## Results

- SDG-Net consistently reduced peak induced wrist torque by up to **17.6%** and slip magnitude in simulation, with failure cases largely correlating with high torque levels above an empirical instability threshold of approximately **6.9** Nm.
- In real-world hardware experiments, iTuP achieved an overall task success rate of **77.5%**, representing a **17.5%** improvement over a compositional VLM baseline, with greater gains in dynamic and lever-arm-dominated tasks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that SDG-Net's wrench-conditioned scoring was responsible for the reduced torque-induced instabilities and improved success rates, demonstrating its contribution to mechanical feasibility independent of semantic reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mechanical robustness in robotic tool use is significantly improved when grasp selection is explicitly conditioned on the predicted dynamic wrench (forces and torques) that will be transmitted through the grasp during a task.
> - The separation of semantic reasoning (tool identification and interaction points) from mechanical feasibility (grasp stability under predicted forces) allows for more reliable systems that leverage advanced language models while ensuring physically sound actions.
> - Lever-arm amplification and misalignment between the gripper and interaction normal are critical factors in generating destabilizing torques and tangential forces, making grasps that mitigate these effects more stable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly tool use, is an active area of research aimed at extending robotic capabilities beyond direct interaction with objects. Historically, advancements in this field have progressed along several largely independent axes: semantic grounding, geometric grasp stability, and physics-aware motion planning.

**Semantic Grounding:** Recent developments in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have enabled robots to interpret natural language instructions, identify novel tools, localize contact regions, and infer interaction directions. This allows for open-vocabulary grounding and improved high-level task understanding. However, these systems typically delegate grasp selection to other modules without explicit consideration of dynamic interaction forces.

**Grasp Stability:** Traditional grasp analysis often relies on force-closure principles and wrench-space metrics, which assume static equilibrium or quasi-static conditions. Learning-based methods have emerged to predict robust 6-DoF grasps from sensory data (e.g., point clouds), improving generalization in complex scenes. However, these methods typically evaluate grasp stability in isolation, focusing on tasks like object pickup, where the primary forces are related to the object's weight. They generally do not account for the forces and torques generated during active tool-object interaction, especially those amplified by lever arms inherent in tool use.

**Physics-Aware Manipulation and Dynamic Interaction:** Some research integrates differentiable simulators, model-predictive control, or learned world models to reason about object motion under contact. These approaches are often used for trajectory planning or policy learning to manage interaction dynamics. However, in many such systems, the grasp pose is either pre-determined or selected using geometry-based metrics before dynamic reasoning is applied, rather than being an output of a dynamic stability assessment.

**The Identified Gap:** The paper identifies a structural gap at the intersection of these fields: the limited coupling between grasp selection and the predicted interaction wrench induced by a specific task trajectory. In tool use, forces are frequently applied at remote contact points, leading to significant torque amplification at the gripper. A grasp deemed stable for static lifting may fail under the dynamic, task-induced forces and moments. Existing systems, while adept at *what* tool to use and *how* to move, often overlook *whether* the chosen grasp can mechanically withstand the resulting interaction forces. This work addresses this gap by introducing a framework where grasp selection is explicitly conditioned on the predicted wrench transmitted through the grasp during a task.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from a recurring failure mode in robotic tool use: mechanical instability. Robots often correctly identify the appropriate tool and plan a semantically correct motion, but the grasp subsequently slips, twists, or rotates due to interaction forces that exceed its stability limits. This suggests that failures are frequently mechanical rather than semantic.

The specific issues that motivate this work are:
1. **Inadequacy of Current Grasp Selection Metrics:** Existing vision-language manipulation systems, while proficient at grounding tools and contact regions from language, typically select grasps using geometry-only metrics or quasi-static force-closure assumptions. These criteria do not account for the dynamic forces, inertial impulses, and lever-arm amplification that generate significant wrist torque and tangential loads during active tool interaction.
2. **The Wrench-Transfer Problem:** Tool use is fundamentally a problem of transferring wrench (forces and torques). Forces applied at remote contact points on a tool generate torque at the robot's wrist, the magnitude and tangential projection of which are critical determinants of grasp stability. A small force applied far from the grasp point can induce a large, destabilizing torque.
3. **Decoupling Semantic and Mechanical Feasibility:** The authors aim to improve mechanical robustness without interfering with the advanced semantic reasoning capabilities of VLMs. This involves creating a dedicated mechanism for evaluating grasp stability under predicted task dynamics, separate from the tool identification and interaction planning stages.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this research are:
- To introduce **inverse Tool-use Planning (iTuP)**, a framework that conditions grasp selection on predicted interaction wrench derived from a task-conditioned trajectory. This shifts from traditional grasp planning (where the interaction adapts to a fixed grasp) to a model where the grasp is selected to survive a specified interaction.
- To develop a principled, physics-based method for quantifying the destabilizing effects of task-induced wrench. This involves analytically deriving penalties for torque amplification, slip onset, and alignment deviation from rigid-body mechanics.
- To create a **Stable Dynamic Grasp Network (SDG-Net)**, a learned surrogate model that approximates these trajectory-conditioned wrench costs in real-time. This enables efficient evaluation over large sets of candidate grasps despite the complexity of exact physical calculations.
- To validate that explicitly incorporating wrench-aware grasp selection significantly reduces mechanical failures (slip, rotation) and improves overall task success across various dynamic interaction regimes, thereby demonstrating the causal link between predicted wrench suppression and enhanced stability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The iTuP framework addresses the wrench-transfer problem by conditioning grasp selection on the predicted interaction wrench induced by a task trajectory. The methodology is structured around a strict separation of semantic grounding, trajectory synthesis, and wrench-conditioned grasp evaluation.

**A. Problem Formulation: Grasping Under Predicted Interaction Wrench**
The task is defined by a scene observation, a set of candidate tools and targets, and a natural language instruction. The planner must produce a grasp `g` on a selected tool and a short-horizon end-effector trajectory `ξ(t)`. Unlike conventional grasp planning, the grasp's success is evaluated based on its ability to transmit the interaction wrench `W_int` generated by the trajectory at the tool contact point. A successful grasp must:
- Suppress destabilizing torque at the wrist.
- Minimize tangential force projection at the gripper interface.
- Maintain alignment between gripper surface normals and the interaction normal during contact.
Grasp selection is formalized as `g* = arg min(g ∈ G) C(g | ξ, Ω)`, where `G` is a set of candidate grasps, `Ω` contains task-conditioned contact parameters, and `C` is a trajectory-conditioned wrench penalty. This cost is external to the grasp geometry, being induced by the interaction dynamics.

**B. Interaction Regimes**
The paper distinguishes interaction regimes based on the relative magnitudes of inertial and grasp reaction forces:
- **Quasi-static:** Inertial forces are much smaller than grasp reaction forces.
- **Dynamic:** Inertial forces are comparable to or greater than grasp reaction forces.
- **Clustered:** Aggregates multiple contacts.
Crucially, the authors emphasize that large lever arms (`||r||`) can amplify even modest contact forces into destabilizing torque, making grasp stability dependent on wrench transmission even in nominally quasi-static scenarios.

**C. Trajectory-Conditioned Wrench Derivation**
The framework analytically derives wrench from rigid-body mechanics.
1. **Key Points:** The tool's center of mass (`c_COM`), the tool-object contact point (`c_obj`), and the lever arm (`r = c_obj - c_COM`) are defined.
2. **Relative Contact Velocity:** For end-effector linear `v` and angular `ω` velocities, the relative contact velocity is `v_c = v + ω × r`.
3. **Normal Impulse:** Under rigid-body impact with restitution `e`, mass `m`, and inertia `I`, the normal impulse magnitude `J` is calculated using `v_c`. Approximate mass and inertia are estimated from category priors and geometric properties of the segmented mesh, while restitution and friction coefficients are assigned from category-dependent priors. SDG-Net is designed to handle this modeling uncertainty.
4. **Induced Force and Torque:** The induced contact force `F` over duration `Δt` is `F = (J/Δt)n`, where `n` is the normal. The resulting torque at the grasp frame is `τ = r × F`.
5. **Force Decomposition:** Force is decomposed into components perpendicular to the lever arm (`F_⊥`) and tangential to the gripper surface (`F_t`), highlighting the joint dependence of stability on lever-arm length and tangential force projection.

**D. Physics-Conditioned Grasp Cost**
Three coupled penalties are defined to capture distinct failure mechanisms:
1. **Interaction Torque (`C_τ(g)`):** Quantifies torque projected onto wrist-sensitive axes (orthogonal to jaw closure) using `C_τ(g) = ||Π_⊥ τ(g; ξ)||²`.
2. **Slip Penalty (`C_s(g)`):** Measures the exceedance of the friction limit based on normal (`F_n`) and tangential (`F_t`) force components and friction coefficient `μ`: `C_s(g) = max(0, ||F_t|| - μ||F_n||)`.
3. **Alignment Deviation (`C_α(g)`):** Penalizes the angle between the gripper's surface normal (`n_finger(g)`) and the interaction normal (`n`), as misalignment increases tangential load: `C_α(g) = ∠(n_finger(g), n)`.

These penalties are combined into a total cost: `C(g) = w_τ C_τ(g) + w_s C_s(g) + w_α C_α(g)`. This cost function exhibits properties such as invariance under rigid transformations, noise robustness, reduction to alignment in the quasi-static limit, and direct penalization of destabilizing wrench components.

**E. SDG-Net: Learning Trajectory-Conditioned Wrench Cost**
Since exact physical parameters are often uncertain in real-world scenarios, SDG-Net is trained as a learned surrogate to approximate `C(g)`. It predicts `ˆC(g | X, ξ, Ω)` from local point-cloud features and trajectory-conditioned parameters. Training minimizes the squared error between predicted and analytically derived penalties. During inference, grasp selection involves `g* = arg min(g ∈ G) ˆC(g)`.

**F. Separation of Semantics and Physics (Full iTuP Pipeline)**
The pipeline enforces a clear separation:
1. **Object-level grounding:** A VLM identifies the tool and target objects from a natural language instruction.
2. **Part-level grounding:** The VLM refines interaction geometry by selecting contact points and direction (`Ω`).
3. **Trajectory synthesis:** A short-horizon interaction trajectory `ξ(t)` is generated from `Ω`.
4. **Grasp sampling:** A set of candidate grasps `G` is produced.
5. **Wrench-conditioned scoring:** SDG-Net predicts the cost `ˆC(g)` for each candidate grasp.
6. **Execution:** The grasp minimizing the predicted cost is executed.

This separation ensures that improvements in stability are attributed to physics-conditioned grasp evaluation rather than changes in semantic reasoning or visual architecture. Ablation studies confirm this by holding stages (1)–(3) fixed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates iTuP across both simulation and hardware, focusing on whether wrench-conditioned grasp selection reduces induced torque, if this reduction correlates with improved task success, and if gains are concentrated in torque-amplified regimes. Four tasks are used: hammering (impulse-dominant), sweeping (multi-contact), knocking (impulse and lever arm), and reaching (lever-arm-dominated).

**A. Simulation: Wrench Reduction Under Controlled Dynamics**
Experiments in Isaac Sim recorded peak wrist torque (`τ_max`), slip magnitude (`s_max`), and tool-axis deviation (`α_max`).
- **Torque Reduction:** SDG-Net consistently reduced peak induced torque by up to 17.6% compared to geometry-based baselines (GQ-CNN, GraspNet). For example, in the hammering task, peak torque dropped from 8.01 Nm (GQ-CNN) to 6.60 Nm (SDG-Net), with corresponding reductions in slip. This reduction is attributed to SDG-Net's ability to select grasps that minimize lever-arm amplification.
- **Torque–Failure Correlation:** Analysis showed that slip magnitude increased approximately monotonically with peak induced wrist torque. Failure cases (defined as visible rotation or slip) clustered in regions of high torque. A sharp transition in failure probability was observed around `τ_max ≈ 6.9 Nm`, indicating an empirical instability threshold. SDG-Net's torque suppression often shifted grasps below this critical threshold, leading to a disproportionate improvement in stability.

**B. Real-World Evaluation: Regime-Specific Success**
Hardware experiments were conducted on a UR5e + Robotiq 2F-85 platform with 10 trials per task.
- **Overall and Regime-Split Success:** iTuP achieved an overall real-world task success rate of 77.5%, representing a 17.5% improvement over a compositional VLM baseline (CoPa).
 - In **dynamic tasks** like hammering and knocking, removing SDG-Net caused success rates to drop (e.g., hammering from 50% to 30%), with failures manifesting as visible tool rotation or gripper-tool drift at peak impact.
 - In **lever-arm-dominated tasks** like reaching, SDG-Net mitigated geometric torque amplification, improving stability even in nominally quasi-static motions (80% vs 70% without SDG-Net).
 - In **multi-contact tasks** like sweeping, slip-driven failures increased without SDG-Net.
- **Structural Contribution of SDG-Net:** Ablation studies showed that removing SDG-Net (replacing wrench-conditioned scoring with static force-closure) increased torque-induced instabilities despite identical tool grounding and motion planning. This isolates SDG-Net's contribution to mechanical feasibility rather than semantic reasoning.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Physics-conditioned grasps aligned the jaw with the interaction normal and shortened the effective lever arm, suppressing induced torque, whereas baseline grasps amplified torque through misalignment and longer lever arms.
- **Correlation:** The paper demonstrates that tasks exhibiting larger torque suppression (e.g., hammering due to force magnitude, reaching due to lever arm length) also showed larger reductions in failure rate, confirming the link between wrench suppression and improved task success.

**C. Robustness in Cluttered Scenes**
In cluttered multi-tool scenes, iTuP demonstrated further improvements, with an overall success rate of 70.0% compared to CoPa's 47.5%. CoPa often selected correct tools but chose suboptimal grasp surfaces, leading to torque amplification. SDG-Net selected handle-biased grasps that shortened the effective lever arm, directly reducing induced torque. The largest improvements were seen in hammering and reaching tasks, where torque amplification is most dominant.

**D. Summary of Experimental Findings**
The experiments collectively demonstrated that explicit conditioning on lever-arm torque amplification and alignment-dependent force projection leads to regime-dependent gains. SDG-Net reduced induced torque, shifting grasps away from the empirical slip boundary in simulation, which translated to improved task success in hardware. The improvements were concentrated in dynamic and lever-arm-dominated regimes, while performance was comparable to static grasp metrics in low-wrench, quasi-static interactions. The findings consistently supported the wrench-transfer hypothesis, where torque scales with slip, high torque predicts failure, and torque suppression correlates with reduced failure rates across different interaction regimes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a significant contribution to robotic manipulation by addressing a fundamental limitation in current tool-use systems: the susceptibility of grasps to mechanical instability under task-induced wrench. By formalizing tool use as a wrench-transfer problem, the paper provides a principled framework for selecting grasps that are robust to dynamic interactions.

**Key Significance Points:**
- **Improved Robustness:** The introduction of inverse Tool-use Planning (iTuP) and the Stable Dynamic Grasp Network (SDG-Net) directly enhances the mechanical stability of robotic tool use. This is particularly crucial for tasks involving impulsive forces, long lever arms, or multi-contact interactions, where conventional geometry- or quasi-static-based grasp selection often fails.
- **Bridging Semantic and Mechanical Feasibility:** The framework effectively decouples semantic reasoning (identifying tools and interaction points) from mechanical feasibility (ensuring grasp stability under predicted forces). This allows robots to leverage advanced VLM capabilities for high-level understanding while ensuring that the chosen physical actions are mechanically sound, thereby creating a more complete and reliable robotic system.
- **Physics-Grounded Approach:** The analytical derivation of torque, slip, and alignment penalties from rigid-body mechanics provides a strong physical foundation for the grasp evaluation process. SDG-Net's role as a learned surrogate allows for real-time evaluation while maintaining the underlying physical structure of the cost function, making it practical for real-world deployment despite inherent uncertainties.
- **Quantifiable Impact:** The empirical validation in both simulation and hardware, showing consistent reductions in induced torque (up to 17.6%) and increases in real-world task success (17.5% over baselines), demonstrates the practical efficacy of the approach. The correlation between torque suppression and failure rate reduction provides a clear causal link for the observed improvements.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Robotic Capabilities:** Robots equipped with iTuP could perform a wider range of manipulation tasks, especially those requiring forceful or dynamic tool interactions, with greater reliability. This could include assembly, maintenance, or hazardous material handling, where robust tool use is critical.
- **Safer Robot Operation:** By anticipating and mitigating destabilizing torques and potential slips, the system could contribute to safer human-robot collaboration and reduce damage to tools, objects, or the robot itself.
- **Advancement in Generalizable Manipulation:** The framework represents a step towards more generalizable stable manipulation, as it focuses on fundamental physics principles rather than task-specific heuristics. This could facilitate the deployment of robots in novel environments and tasks without extensive re-training.
- **Future Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for future research, such as integrating compliance-aware wrench estimation, optimizing grasp and trajectory jointly under uncertainty, and addressing the propagation of semantic grounding errors into wrench estimation, which could further refine robotic tool use capabilities.

In summary, this research offers a methodical approach to a critical problem in robotics, demonstrating that explicit consideration of task-induced wrench during grasp selection is essential for robust and successful tool manipulation.
