# Hyperbolic Category Discovery

> [!summary] Summary
> The Hyperbolic Category Discovery (HypCD) framework from the Visual AI Lab at the University of Hong Kong addresses generalized category discovery by embedding image features into hyperbolic space. This approach, which is compatible with existing parametric and non-parametric methods, improves classification accuracy and better transfers knowledge to novel categories by naturally representing hierarchical relationships in data.

## Problem

- Classifying images in datasets that contain both labeled data from known categories and unlabeled data which may include both known and novel categories (Generalized Category Discovery, GCD).
- Traditional GCD methods operating in Euclidean or spherical spaces are limited in their ability to effectively represent and leverage hierarchical relationships between object categories.
- Existing approaches often exhibit a significant performance gap in classifying known versus novel categories, indicating challenges in transferring knowledge to unseen classes.

## Method

- Introduces the ==Hyperbolic Category Discovery (HypCD)== framework, which maps image features from a pre-trained encoder into ==hyperbolic space (Poincaré ball model)== using exponential mapping.
- Implements ==hyperbolic representation learning== via contrastive loss functions that combine hyperbolic distance-based and angle-based terms to encourage distinct clustering.
- Integrates a Hyperbolic Feed-Forward Network for parametric classification or utilizes semi-supervised k-means clustering in hyperbolic space for non-parametric category discovery, making the framework compatible with various GCD methods.

## Results

- Achieved consistent improvements in overall classification accuracy across standard GCD benchmarks including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100, and the Semi-Supervised Benchmark (SSB) when integrated with existing baseline methods.
- Reduced the accuracy gap between known and unknown categories, demonstrating better knowledge transfer and improved performance on novel classes.
- Produced **more distinct and tightly clustered** groups for categories in t-SNE visualizations compared to Euclidean approaches, indicating more effective feature separation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hyperbolic spaces, due to their exponential volume growth, are naturally well-suited for embedding hierarchical data structures like those found in object categories and their parts.
> - Representing image features in hyperbolic space enables more effective capture of hierarchical relationships, which in turn facilitates better knowledge transfer from known to unknown categories in Generalized Category Discovery (GCD).
> - Utilizing a combination of hyperbolic distance-based and angle-based losses within a contrastive learning framework enhances the learning of discriminative and clustered features in hyperbolic geometry.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) addresses a fundamental challenge in computer vision: how to classify images in datasets containing both labeled data from known categories and unlabeled data that may include both known and novel categories. This task reflects real-world scenarios where AI systems encounter objects they weren't explicitly trained to recognize.

Traditional approaches to GCD typically operate in Euclidean or spherical spaces, which have inherent limitations when representing hierarchical data structures. The paper "Hyperbolic Category Discovery" by Liu, He, and Han from the Visual AI Lab at the University of Hong Kong introduces a novel framework that leverages hyperbolic geometry to better capture the hierarchical relationships between object categories and their parts.

As shown in the figure above, the key difference between traditional spherical-based methods and the proposed hyperbolic approach lies in how they handle hierarchical relationships and scale variations. The hyperbolic approach can effectively represent hierarchical structures, allowing for better knowledge transfer from known to unknown categories.

### 2. Understanding Hyperbolic Geometry

Hyperbolic geometry differs fundamentally from Euclidean geometry in that parallel lines diverge and the sum of angles in a triangle is less than 180 degrees. More importantly for machine learning applications, hyperbolic spaces have an exponential volume growth relative to their radius, making them particularly well-suited for embedding hierarchical structures.

This property is crucial for GCD, as object categories often exhibit hierarchical relationships. For instance, as illustrated in the figure below, car categories can be organized hierarchically (e.g., vehicle → brand → model), and bird species share hierarchical relationships based on physical features.

The figure shows both inter-category hierarchical relations (left) where cars are organized by brand and model, and intra-category relations (right) where birds within the same species share similar visual features. Hyperbolic space naturally accommodates these hierarchical structures, allowing for more effective representation learning.

### 3. The HypCD Framework

The proposed Hyperbolic Category Discovery (HypCD) framework is designed to be compatible with both parametric (classifier-based) and non-parametric (clustering-based) GCD methods. The overall framework is illustrated in the following figure:

The framework consists of several key components:

1. An image encoder (pre-trained backbone like DINO or DINOv2)
2. Exponential mapping to convert Euclidean features to hyperbolic space
3. Hyperbolic representation learning with distance-based and angle-based losses
4. A hyperbolic classifier (for parametric methods) or semi-supervised k-means clustering (for non-parametric methods)

### 4. Mapping Features to Hyperbolic Space

The first step in HypCD is to map Euclidean feature embeddings from a pre-trained backbone network into hyperbolic space, specifically the Poincaré ball model. The Poincaré ball model is defined as:

$$\mathbb{B}^n_c = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : c\|x\|^2 < 1\}$$

where $c > 0$ is the curvature parameter that controls the "hyperbolicity" of the space.

The mapping from Euclidean to hyperbolic space is achieved through exponential mapping:

$$\exp^c_0(v) = \tanh(\sqrt{c}\|v\|) \frac{v}{\sqrt{c}\|v\|}$$

To address the issue of gradient vanishing during training (which occurs when features approach the boundary of the Poincaré ball), the authors introduce a feature clipping operation:

$$\tilde{f} = \min(1, \frac{\tau}{\|f\|}) \cdot f$$

where $\tau$ is a clipping threshold.

### 5. Hyperbolic Representation Learning

Once features are mapped to hyperbolic space, the framework implements contrastive learning using both distance-based and angle-based loss functions.

The hyperbolic distance between two points $x$ and $y$ in the Poincaré ball is defined as:

$$d_\mathbb{B}(x, y) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{c}} \tanh^{-1}(\sqrt{c}\|-x \oplus_c y\|)$$

where $\oplus_c$ represents Möbius addition.

The distance-based contrastive loss encourages similar samples to be close in hyperbolic space while pushing dissimilar samples apart:

$$\mathcal{L}^h_{dis} = -\log \frac{\exp(-d_\mathbb{B}(f_i, f_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-d_\mathbb{B}(f_i, f_k)/\tau)}$$

Additionally, the angle-based loss considers the angles between samples, capturing another aspect of their relationship:

$$\mathcal{L}^h_{ang} = -\log \frac{\exp(\cos^\mathbb{B}(f_i, f_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\cos^\mathbb{B}(f_i, f_k)/\tau)}$$

The total representation learning loss combines these two components:

$$\mathcal{L}^h_{rep} = \alpha_d \mathcal{L}^h_{dis} + (1-\alpha_d) \mathcal{L}^h_{ang}$$

where $\alpha_d$ is a hyperparameter controlling the weight of the distance-based loss.

### 6. Hyperbolic Classification

For parametric GCD methods, the framework replaces the standard Euclidean classifier with a Hyperbolic Feed-Forward Network (HypFFN). The HypFFN operates entirely in hyperbolic space using hyperbolic linear layers:

$$W \otimes_c x = \exp^c_0(W\log^c_0(x))$$

where $\log^c_0$ is the logarithmic map (the inverse of the exponential map) and $W$ is the weight matrix.

The classification loss is then computed using hyperbolic distances between features and class prototypes:

$$\mathcal{L}^h_{cls} = -\log \frac{\exp(-d_\mathbb{B}(f_i, w_{y_i})/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(-d_\mathbb{B}(f_i, w_j)/\tau)}$$

For non-parametric methods, the framework employs semi-supervised k-means clustering in hyperbolic space.

### 7. Experimental Results

The HypCD framework was evaluated on several standard GCD benchmarks, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100, and the Semi-Supervised Benchmark (SSB). The results show consistent improvements over baseline methods when integrated with both parametric and non-parametric approaches.

### 8. Advantages Over Conventional Approaches

The HypCD framework offers several advantages over conventional GCD approaches:

1. **Hierarchical Representation**: Hyperbolic space naturally accommodates hierarchical relationships between object categories and parts, enabling more effective knowledge transfer.

2. **Scale Invariance**: As shown in the first figure, hyperbolic geometry better handles objects at different scales, allowing for more consistent representation.

3. **Improved Clustering**: The hyperbolic approach results in more compact clusters with clearer boundaries between different categories, as demonstrated by the T-SNE visualizations.

4. **Reduced Feature Dimension**: The experiments show that HypCD can achieve satisfactory performance with a lower embedding dimension in hyperbolic space, potentially reducing computational complexity.

5. **Versatility**: The framework is compatible with both parametric and non-parametric GCD methods, making it widely applicable.

### 9. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive performance, the HypCD framework has some limitations:

1. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: The performance is sensitive to hyperparameters such as manifold curvature, clipping value, and loss weight, which need to be carefully tuned for different datasets.

2. **Computational Complexity**: Operations in hyperbolic space are generally more computationally intensive than their Euclidean counterparts, potentially increasing training time.

3. **Backbone Dependency**: The framework relies on pre-trained backbones like DINO or DINOv2, and its performance is influenced by the quality of these pre-trained features.

Future work could explore:

- Adaptive curvature learning to automatically determine the optimal hyperbolicity for different datasets
- Extensions to other non-Euclidean geometries that might be suitable for specific types of data
- Integration with other computer vision tasks beyond category discovery

### 10. Conclusion

The Hyperbolic Category Discovery framework represents a significant advancement in the field of Generalized Category Discovery by leveraging the unique properties of hyperbolic geometry. By better capturing hierarchical relationships between object categories and their parts, HypCD enables more effective knowledge transfer from known to unknown categories, a crucial capability for open-world learning systems.

The consistent improvements observed across various datasets and baseline methods demonstrate the effectiveness and versatility of the approach. As computer vision systems increasingly need to operate in open-world environments with evolving category sets, frameworks like HypCD that can effectively discover and classify novel categories will become increasingly important.

By bridging the gap between known and unknown categories, HypCD brings us one step closer to AI systems that can adapt to new visual concepts with limited supervision, much like humans do.
