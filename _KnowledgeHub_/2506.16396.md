# GoalLadder: Incremental Goal Discovery with Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A method called GoalLadder, developed by researchers at the University of Oxford, enables reinforcement learning agents to learn visual tasks from a single natural language instruction by incrementally discovering goals using Vision-Language Models. It mitigates noisy VLM feedback with an ELO-based rating system and improves VLM query efficiency through a learned visual embedding space, achieving approximately 95% success rates across continuous control tasks with about 4500 VLM queries for Metaworld.

## Problem

- Manually designing effective reward functions for Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents in visual environments is resource-intensive and requires significant domain expertise.
- Existing VLM-based approaches for generating reward functions from natural language instructions often suffer from noisy feedback, especially in spatial reasoning, or are computationally expensive due to frequent VLM queries.
- Prior VLM-based methods struggle with generalizability of reward signals and maintaining robustness to VLM errors without relying on environment modifications.

## Method

- **GoalLadder** incrementally discovers states closer to a ==language-specified task goal== using an iterative process involving VLM evaluations.
- It employs an ELO-based rating system to robustly rank candidate goals based on pairwise VLM comparisons, effectively absorbing noisy VLM feedback.
- A variational autoencoder (VAE) trained on unlabelled environment observations defines the reward function as the negative Euclidean distance from the current state's latent embedding to the highest-rated goal's embedding, enabling generalization and VLM query efficiency.

## Results

- GoalLadder achieved a mean final success rate of approximately **95%** across a range of continuous control tasks, nearly matching and in some cases surpassing Oracle performance with ground-truth rewards.
- The method demonstrated superior VLM query efficiency, requiring around 4500 VLM queries for Metaworld tasks, in contrast to other preference-based methods needing substantially more labels.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the ELO rating system in handling noisy VLM feedback and showed that a small, task-specific VAE for reward definition significantly outperformed large, general-purpose visual encoders.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An ELO-based rating system can effectively absorb and aggregate noisy feedback from Vision-Language Models, leading to a robust ranking of task sub-goals.
> - Defining reward functions as distances in a learned, compact visual embedding space (VAE) allows for efficient reward signal generalization to unseen states, significantly reducing the need for continuous VLM queries.
> - An incremental discovery process, guided by a VLM, can progressively identify and refine task-relevant sub-goals from a single natural language instruction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) relies on well-defined reward functions to guide agents toward desired behaviors. However, the manual crafting of these functions is resource-intensive, requiring considerable human labor and domain expertise. Natural language offers a concise and interpretable method for specifying RL tasks, but extracting effective reward functions from language instructions, particularly in visual environments, remains a challenge.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) and, more recently, vision-language models (VLMs) has presented opportunities to automate reward function generation. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into:
- **LLM-based reward generation:** These methods typically involve LLMs writing code for reward functions, often requiring access to environment code or interpretable state representations, which limits their general applicability.
- **VLM-based reward definition or learning:**
 - **Embedding-based methods** (e.g., using CLIP): These approaches align visual observations with language instructions within a VLM's embedding space, defining rewards based on similarity. A common issue is the generation of noisy reward functions due to mismatches between VLM training data and specific environment observations. Additionally, these methods can be computationally expensive as they often require embedding every observation.
 - **Preference-based methods** (e.g., RL-VLM-F): These involve prompting a VLM to rank segments of an agent's behavior based on adherence to a textual specification, then training a reward function from this preference data. While generally less noisy than direct embedding-based methods, they are still susceptible to erroneous VLM feedback, leading to mislabeled data and challenges in robust reward function training. They also necessitate a substantial number of VLM queries to learn a generalized reward function.

GoalLadder positions itself within this landscape by addressing two principal limitations of current VLM-based approaches: the robustness to noisy VLM feedback and the query efficiency of VLMs. It seeks to provide a method that can leverage VLMs for reward signal generation in visual environments from a single language instruction, mitigating the noise inherent in VLM outputs and reducing the overall number of VLM interactions. Unlike some prior work, GoalLadder does not rely on environment modifications (e.g., removing the agent from images) to assist the VLM.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel method, GoalLadder, that enables reinforcement learning agents to learn tasks in visual environments from a single natural language instruction. This overarching goal is driven by the motivation to overcome the significant challenges associated with manual reward function design, which is both labor-intensive and requires specialized domain knowledge.

To achieve this, the authors identify and aim to address several specific issues:

- **Automating Reward Function Generation:** The core motivation stems from the desire to automate the process of creating effective reward functions. Natural language offers an intuitive way for humans to describe tasks, and the capabilities of modern LLMs and VLMs in understanding semantics and common-sense knowledge suggest their potential for translating these descriptions into actionable learning signals for RL agents.
- **Robustness to Noisy VLM Feedback:** VLMs, despite their capabilities, are not infallible. They can produce noisy or incorrect feedback, particularly in spatial reasoning tasks or when encountering visual observations outside their training distribution. Existing VLM-based methods often struggle with this, leading to unstable or suboptimal reward functions. A key objective of GoalLadder is to develop a mechanism that can robustly process and utilize VLM feedback, even when it is imperfect, to avoid destabilizing the RL training process.
- **Query Efficiency for VLM Usage:** Interacting with large, pre-trained VLMs can be computationally expensive. Methods that require a large number of VLM queries to generate sufficient preference data or to embed every single state are not scalable for practical applications. GoalLadder aims to be query-efficient, minimizing the number of VLM interactions required to train an effective RL agent.
- **Generalization of Reward Signals:** Related to query efficiency, the method intends to enable reward signals that can generalize to unseen states without requiring explicit VLM feedback for every possible observation. This would allow the agent to receive informative rewards even for states not directly evaluated by the VLM.
- **Applicability in Diverse Visual Environments:** The method seeks to perform effectively across a range of continuous control tasks, including classic control and complex robotic manipulation, without requiring specific environment modifications to simplify VLM processing.

In summary, the motivation is to leverage the semantic understanding of VLMs to simplify RL task specification, while simultaneously engineering a learning framework that is resilient to the practical limitations of VLMs, namely their noise and computational cost, thereby enabling more accessible and efficient RL system development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GoalLadder is designed to incrementally discover states that lead an RL agent closer to completing a task specified by a single natural language instruction. It integrates a Vision-Language Model (VLM) for state evaluation and ranking, an ELO-based rating system for robustness, and a learned visual embedding space for efficient reward function definition.

The methodology can be broken down into four main, iterative stages:

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

- **Reinforcement Learning Formulation:** The problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the agent receives image observations (o_t) representing the environment state (s_t). The goal is to maximize discounted cumulative rewards.
- **RL Backbone:** Soft-Actor Critic (SAC) is utilized. To address the non-stationary nature of the reward function (which changes as new target goals are discovered), the agent's replay buffer is periodically relabeled with rewards derived from the latest reward function.
- **Assumptions:** Access to a language instruction (l) describing the task, and that the task's goal can be represented by one or more visual observations.

#### 3.2. Core GoalLadder Mechanism

GoalLadder uses a VLM in two primary capacities:
1. **Candidate Goal Discovery:** Identifying potential states that represent an improvement towards the task goal.
2. **Candidate Goal Ranking:** Rating these candidate states based on their proximity to the language-specified goal.

The overall process involves the following stages, repeated throughout training (Figure 1 in paper):

##### 3.2.1. Discovery of Candidate Goals

- **Purpose:** To efficiently filter observations from the agent's trajectories and select only the most promising ones for deeper evaluation, conserving VLM query capacity.
- **Process:**
 - GoalLadder maintains a buffer, B_g, of candidate goals, each represented by an image (o_i) and an associated rating (e_i).
 - The current top-rated goal, g\* = (o\*, e\*), is identified from B_g.
 - During training, a randomly sampled state image (o_j) from the agent’s latest collected trajectory is compared against o\* using the VLM, conditioned on the language instruction (l).
 - The VLM output, y ∈ {−1, 0, 1}, indicates if o_j is better (y=1), g\* is better (y=0), or no decision/equivalent (y=-1).
 - If y=1, o_j is added to B_g with a standard initial rating. Otherwise, o_j is discarded.

##### 3.2.2. Rating of Candidate Goals

- **Purpose:** To robustly estimate the utility of candidate goals in the buffer, mitigating the impact of noisy VLM comparisons and adapting to new discoveries.
- **Process:**
 - Inspired by the ELO chess rating system, GoalLadder updates candidate goal ratings based on pairwise VLM comparisons.
 - A pair of candidate goals (g_i, g_j) is sampled from B_g.
 - The VLM is queried to compare o_i and o_j with respect to the language instruction l, yielding an observed outcome S_i ∈ {−1, 1, 0}.
 - ELO ratings (e_i, e_j) are updated using the formula: `e_i ← e_i + T * (S_i − E_i)`, where E_i is the expected score for g_i against g_j (calculated using a logistic function based on current ratings and a sensitivity constant C), and T is a constant controlling adjustment speed.
 - This incremental update mechanism allows the system to converge on a stable hierarchy of goals despite individual VLM errors.

##### 3.2.3. Defining the Reward Function

- **Purpose:** To provide a continuous and generalizable reward signal to the RL agent, guiding it towards the currently best-known task objective.
- **Process:**
 - The image (o\*) of the highest-rated goal (g\*) in B_g serves as the current target state.
 - A visual feature extractor, ψ(·), implemented as a variational autoencoder (VAE), is trained on unlabelled visual observations from the environment. This VAE maps images (o_t) to a compact latent vector (z_t). The VAE training objective combines reconstruction loss and a KL divergence term (Equation 1).
 - The agent's reward at time t-1 is defined as the negative Euclidean distance between its current state's latent embedding (z_t = ψ(o_t)) and the target goal's latent embedding (z\* = ψ(o\*)): `R(s_t-1, a_t-1) = - d(z_t, z*)`.
 - The reward function is periodically updated, and all stored transitions in the replay buffer are relabeled with respect to the current top-rated candidate goal and the updated feature extractor.

##### 3.2.4. Implementation Details

- **VLM:** Gemini 2.0 Flash is used for all VLM queries.
- **Frequencies:** Reward functions are updated (and the VAE weights for feature extraction are fixed) every L = 5000 environment steps. VLM queries for discovery and ranking occur every K environment steps (2000 for OpenAI Gym, 500 for Metaworld), with M = 5 queries per session.
- **Goal Buffer:** Capped at 10 candidate goals, with lowest-rated goals removed periodically to maintain focus on promising states.
- **VAE:** A convolutional neural network architecture with a latent dimension of 16, trained with an Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.0001), batch size 128, and β=0.1. VAE weights for reward calculation are updated at the same frequency as the top-rated goal (L).
- **ELO Parameters:** Standard values C=400 and T=32 are used for the ELO rating system.
- **Reward Normalization:** Rewards are normalized to and then a non-linear transformation (r^γ with γ=20) is applied to emphasize closer distances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

GoalLadder was evaluated across a set of continuous control tasks, including two classic OpenAI Gym environments (CartPole, MountainCar) and five robotic manipulation tasks from the Metaworld suite (Drawer Close, Drawer Open, Sweep Into, Window Open, Button Press). Critically, no environment modifications were made to aid VLM performance, unlike some prior works. The method was compared against an Oracle (ground-truth reward), VLM-RM, RoboCLIP, and RL-VLM-F, using Gemini 2.0 Flash as the VLM backbone for GoalLadder and RL-VLM-F.

#### 4.1. Goal Discovery Effectiveness

- GoalLadder demonstrated the ability to effectively discover the target goal specified by the language instruction across all tested environments. Visualizations (Figure 2, 5) illustrate a clear progression of top-rated candidate goals, with states incrementally moving closer to the final task objective.
- The ELO rating system effectively discriminates between candidate goals. For instance, in the "Window Open" task (Figure 3), while initial ratings are similar, GoalLadder swiftly identifies and elevates the best goal's rating once it is discovered (around 50k steps). The goal buffer consistently contains states ordered by their proximity to the task goal, and progressively populates with higher-quality states as training advances.

#### 4.2. Task Solving Performance

- GoalLadder achieved a mean final success rate of approximately 95% averaged across all tested tasks.
- The method's performance nearly matched that of the Oracle agent, which had access to ground-truth rewards. Notably, GoalLadder even surpassed the Oracle's performance in the "Drawer Open" task. The authors suggest this may be due to the difficulty of manually designing reward functions that capture all aspects of the task (e.g., pulling the drawer handle in addition to reaching it), a challenge that GoalLadder's automatic discovery process appears to address effectively.
- In contrast, the preference-based baseline RL-VLM-F performed well on simpler tasks (CartPole, MountainCar, Drawer Close) but struggled with more complex Metaworld manipulation tasks, indicating limitations in its ability to learn effective reward functions under the given VLM feedback rate and noise conditions.
- Embedding-based baselines, VLM-RM and RoboCLIP, generally exhibited poor performance across most tasks, aligning with previously reported limitations regarding noisy reward functions and data mismatch.

#### 4.3. Sample Efficiency (VLM Queries)

- GoalLadder demonstrated superior sample efficiency in terms of VLM queries. For the Metaworld tasks, it achieved solutions after approximately 4500 VLM queries.
- This figure contrasts with other preference-based methods like PEBBLE, which, even with ground-truth reward preferences, required around 15000 preference labels to achieve similar performance levels on the same tasks.
- The core mechanism contributing to GoalLadder's efficiency is its reward definition: using distances in a learned embedding space (via VAE) on unlabelled data allows the reward signal to generalize to unseen states without necessitating explicit, frequent VLM feedback for every observation. This is a significant advantage over methods that either query a VLM for every observation (like VLM-RM) or train a reward model from a large dataset of VLM-generated preferences (like RL-VLM-F).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Appendix C)

- **ELO Rating:** An ablation study confirmed the critical role of the ELO rating system. Without ELO (i.e., a "greedy" approach trusting every VLM decision), GoalLadder's success rate on the "Drawer Open" task dropped from 0.97 ± 0.11 to 0.20 ± 0.35. This illustrates that ELO effectively absorbs noisy VLM feedback and prevents erroneous goal replacements from destabilizing training.
- **Buffer Size:** Experiments on buffer size demonstrated that GoalLadder is robust within a reasonable range (e.g., 5-50 candidate goals). Both excessively small buffers (limiting diversity and ELO's effectiveness) and excessively large buffers (requiring prohibitively many comparisons for rating convergence) degraded performance.
- **Visual Encoders:** Testing with off-the-shelf visual encoders (DINOv2, CLIP) showed that the small VAE trained on environment observations (success rate 1.00 ± 0.00) significantly outperformed these large pre-trained models (DINOv2: 0.25 ± 0.21, CLIP: 0.38 ± 0.17). This indicates that the VAE is better at capturing the fine-grained visual differences relevant for distinguishing task progress in the specific environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GoalLadder presents a significant advancement in the field of reinforcement learning from natural language instructions, particularly in visual environments. Its contributions and potential impacts are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Accessibility for RL:** By enabling RL agents to learn from a single, human-interpretable language instruction, GoalLadder substantially lowers the barrier to entry for defining and solving complex tasks. This reduces the need for extensive human labor and specialized domain expertise typically required for manual reward function engineering.
- **Robustness to Imperfect VLMs:** The integration of an ELO-based rating system addresses a fundamental challenge in leveraging VLMs for reward generation: their inherent noise and occasional errors, particularly in spatial reasoning. By incrementally refining state utilities through pairwise comparisons, GoalLadder demonstrates a method for making VLM feedback more robust, leading to stable and effective reward signals even with noisy inputs. This is crucial for practical VLM deployment in RL.
- **Improved Query Efficiency:** GoalLadder's approach of learning an embedding space for reward definition, trained on unlabelled visual data, significantly reduces the number of expensive VLM queries required. This makes the method more computationally efficient and scalable, overcoming a major practical limitation of prior VLM-based RL systems that either query VLMs for every observation or require vast amounts of preference data.
- **High Performance and Potential for "Super-Human" Rewards:** The observation that GoalLadder can match and, in some cases, exceed the performance of agents trained with ground-truth reward functions suggests that automated reward engineering can sometimes yield more effective or holistic reward signals than those designed manually. This highlights the potential for VLMs to discover task-relevant nuances that human experts might overlook or find difficult to formalize.
- **Foundational for Future Research:** GoalLadder opens several avenues for future work, including extending the methodology to video-based settings for dynamic goals and integrating more sophisticated self-supervised learning techniques for visual feature extraction to create richer, more meaningful latent spaces.
- **Ethical Considerations:** As noted by the authors, the integration of large pretrained language models in robotic systems introduces the potential for inheriting existing biases from these models. While the current work focuses on object-oriented environments, the authors appropriately caution against direct application in human-interactive settings without thorough investigation of these potential biases, underscoring a broader impact of this technology.

In conclusion, GoalLadder represents a step toward more autonomous and accessible reinforcement learning, providing a robust and efficient framework for leveraging the growing capabilities of vision-language models to bridge the gap between human language and machine learning objectives.
