# Dream-RSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Worlds

> [!summary] Summary
> Dream-RSI is a recursive self-improvement framework optimizing the exploration policy of a fixed coding agent during long-horizon discovery. Completed discovery trees become replay simulators: an LLM developer agent revises policy code, scores each version offline by replaying recorded outcomes, and redeploys the best online to grow the simulator pool. Across algorithm, math and GPU-kernel tasks it matches or beats baselines with up to 162x fewer agent calls than SimpleTES.

## Problem

- Existing LLM discovery systems such as AlphaEvolve and SimpleTES rely on hand-designed exploration strategies that stay fixed, cannot learn from accumulated experience, and keep wasting compute on ineffective directions.
- Online exploration-policy optimization, as in EvoX, faces delayed, expensive feedback: each candidate policy needs a long rollout to judge, and the vast meta-policy space demands many candidates.
- Prior work reuses discovery history only as static prompt context, memory, or fine-tuning data, ignoring that its recorded tree of decisions and outcomes can itself evaluate alternative policies.

## Method

- ==Programmable orchestration layer==: exploration policy is executable code choosing batches of root or leaf nodes of a discovery tree for parallel workers, controlling branching, parallelism and stopping without changing the coding agent.
- ==History-as-replay-simulator==: each completed online discovery tree becomes a frozen world where alternative policies reveal recorded children deterministically without re-execution, scored by best quality minus probe cost plus a parallelism bonus.
- ==Dreaming-based policy improvement==: a fixed LLM policy-development agent iteratively rewrites policy code from replay trajectories over all stored worlds; the best version, current policy included, redeploys online, so replay score never regresses.

## Results

- Lasso path discovery: with Gemini-3.1-Pro, average held-out runtime drops from **3587.1 ms** (fixed exploration) to **2931.0 ms** using **317** versus **550** agent calls, beating SimpleTES's **3804.8 ms** from **51,200** generations.
- KernelBench GPU kernels: Dream-RSI reaches comparable speed with **2.43x** and **1.79x** fewer generations on VGG16 and LayerNorm, and **2.09x** and **1.44x** higher performance on ConvDiv and ConvMax than fixed exploration.
- Math within 1k generations: Sum-Difference **1.145427** beats SimpleTES's **1.143975** and Circle Packing ties the top reported **2.635983**, but Autocorrelation **1.456375** trails SimpleTES's **1.453675** from **51,200** generations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Logged search trajectories form a free, grounded simulator: replaying recorded outcomes turns delayed meta-level feedback into cheap off-policy evaluation, though it cannot score branches never explored.
> - Injecting summarized history as directional prompt guidance underperformed unguided search on ConvDiv; strong semantic priors over-constrain parallel exploration, so history works better as an evaluator than instructions.
> - Improving the meta-level controller while freezing agent and evaluator pays off: the learned policy saves compute while progressing and re-expands exploration when progress plateaus.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a developing area of research concerned with autonomous agents that can improve their own problem-solving processes. In AI-driven scientific and algorithmic discovery, language-model-based agents generate code or mathematical constructions, execute them, evaluate their performance, and use the resulting feedback to produce subsequent candidates. Systems such as AlphaEvolve, SimpleTES, OpenEvolve, and related evolutionary coding agents primarily focus on improving the candidate solutions generated by the agent.

*Dream-RSI* shifts attention from the object level—the algorithm, mathematical construction, or GPU kernel being optimized—to the meta level: the strategy used to allocate exploration. Such a strategy determines which search branches to expand, how many branches to pursue in parallel, whether to refine an existing candidate or open a new direction, and when to stop. This is important because long-horizon discovery may require hundreds or thousands of proposal-and-evaluation cycles, making inefficient search orchestration a significant source of computational cost.

The work is related to model-based reinforcement learning and world-model methods, in which an agent learns or constructs an internal representation of an environment and uses simulated experience to improve its policy. It also relates to research on self-evolving agents, adaptive search strategies, test-time learning, memory, and agent-harness optimization. Its specific contribution is to treat prior discovery histories not merely as textual context, memory, or training data, but as replayable environments for evaluating alternative exploration policies.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. The exploration bottleneck

The paper identifies a dilemma in existing discovery systems. Manually designed exploration policies are inexpensive to implement but remain fixed, even when accumulated experience shows that some branches are unproductive or that different search patterns would be more appropriate. Conversely, directly optimizing an exploration policy online is expensive. The quality of such a policy cannot usually be assessed after a single action; it must be observed over a long rollout involving many candidate generations and evaluations.

This creates two problems. First, feedback about a meta-policy is delayed and costly. Second, the space of possible policies is very large. A policy may vary in branching, depth, parallelism, branch ordering, recovery from failures, and stopping criteria. Testing many candidate policies directly against the real discovery environment would require repeatedly invoking the coding agent and evaluator.

#### 2.2. Central research objective

The objective of *Dream-RSI* is to make exploration itself recursively self-improving while keeping the underlying discovery agent and evaluator unchanged. The authors seek to determine whether completed discovery histories can provide sufficient information to evaluate many alternative exploration strategies without rerunning the expensive generation-and-evaluation process.

The intended outcome is a closed loop in which an initial exploration policy generates real discovery experience, that experience is converted into a replay simulator, candidate exploration policies are improved offline through replay, and the revised policy is deployed in a subsequent online round. Each online round adds new experience, expanding the pool of replay environments available for later policy improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall architecture

Dream-RSI contains three stages:

1. **Online exploration.** A programmable orchestration layer directs a fixed coding agent through a discovery task. It controls which branches are opened, which existing branches are refined, how attempts are grouped into parallel batches, and when the rollout terminates. Each generation-and-evaluation result is stored.

2. **Replay-simulator construction.** The resulting discovery history is represented as one or more discovery trees. Each tree records parent-child relationships, workspace states, generated artifacts, evaluator diagnostics, scores, and the computational attempt associated with each node.

3. **Dreaming-based policy improvement.** An LLM-based policy-development agent proposes revisions to the executable exploration-policy code. Candidate policies are evaluated against the historical trees by replaying recorded branches. The highest-scoring policy is then deployed online.

The underlying coding agent, evaluator, and execution interfaces remain fixed. Only the exploration-policy code is changed.

#### 3.2. Discovery-tree representation

The root of a discovery tree represents the initial workspace. Each non-root node corresponds to a generation-and-evaluation attempt initiated from a parent workspace. A node therefore contains the inherited context and the newly produced candidate, together with its score and diagnostic information.

At every decision round, the exploration policy observes the currently revealed tree. The legal choices are the root and the leaves of the revealed tree. Selecting a leaf means refining that branch; selecting the root opens another independent branch. A batch may contain up to \(W\) selected nodes, corresponding to the number of parallel workers. An empty batch terminates the rollout.

During online exploration, selected nodes produce new candidates stochastically through fresh calls to the discovery agent. During replay, the selected nodes reveal previously recorded children deterministically. Replay therefore evaluates alternative ways of navigating the observed tree, but it does not generate new candidates or expose outcomes that were not present in the historical record.

#### 3.3. Replay objective

The replay score combines three considerations:

- the best solution quality reached during replay;
- a penalty for the number of represented generation-and-evaluation attempts; and
- a bonus for parallel execution, based on the number of attempts completed per decision round.

Formally, the score is the best node score minus a cost coefficient multiplied by the number of revealed non-root nodes, plus a parallelism coefficient multiplied by the average number of represented attempts per decision round. The average replay score of a policy is computed across all historical trees currently available.

The current policy is included among the candidates considered for selection. After a specified number of policy revisions, the policy with the highest average replay score is selected for the next online round. This guarantees that the selected policy is no worse than the current policy on the fixed replay history, although this guarantee applies only to replay performance, not necessarily to future online performance.

#### 3.4. Policy-development constraints

The replay-based policy-development prompt requires the revised policy to make decisions using only prefix-observable information: previously revealed observations, legal actions, structural metadata, and diagnostic signals. It cannot inspect unrevealed scores, use hardcoded winning branches, or rely on the true optimum.

The policy must reconstruct the trajectory of each opened branch, including successful results, regressions, failure types, repair attempts, and remaining depth. It is instructed to distinguish hard failures from potentially repairable implementation failures and from branches that are merely underexplored. It also constructs a dynamic portfolio of exploitation, exploration, and limited recovery actions, with the aim of avoiding both excessive pruning and serial execution.

The policy includes a scalar parameter, beta, governing the balance between exploratory width and selective pruning. The paper also requires a planning procedure that selects future grid width and refinement depth using historical live-cycle information rather than fixed presets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation covers eight tasks in three domains: one algorithm-engineering task, three mathematical-optimization tasks, and four GPU-kernel-engineering tasks. The main controlled baseline, Recursive Fixed Exploration, uses the same discovery agent, evaluator, initialization, and per-round budgets but keeps the exploration policy unchanged.

#### 4.1. Algorithm engineering

The Lasso regularization-path task requires an implementation that computes a complete sequence of Lasso solutions while preserving numerical correctness. Discovery uses 17 synthetic instances, with performance additionally tested on six held-out datasets from biological and non-biological domains.

With Gemini-3.1 Pro, Dream-RSI achieved an average held-out runtime of 2,931.0 milliseconds using 317 discovery-agent calls, compared with 3,587.1 milliseconds and 550 calls for fixed exploration. With Gemini-3.7 Flash, it achieved 2,350.6 milliseconds using 1,879 calls, compared with 2,516.7 milliseconds and 3,200 calls for the fixed baseline. The discovered implementations also outperformed the listed sklearn and glmnet implementations on the six held-out datasets and improved on the reported SimpleTES result while using approximately two orders of magnitude fewer generation calls.

The discovered Lasso solver combines strong-rule screening, adaptive Cauchy–Schwarz-based KKT pruning, active-set bookkeeping, lazy Gram-matrix construction, memory alignment, SIMD vectorization, and OpenMP parallelism. The reported results suggest that the exploration controller can help the agent identify implementation directions that improve both runtime and search efficiency.

#### 4.2. Mathematical optimization

The three mathematical tasks are the Sum–Difference Problem, Circle Packing, and Autocorrelation Inequalities. Dream-RSI obtained a Sum–Difference score of 1.145427, exceeding Recursive Fixed Exploration at 1.144047 and SimpleTES at 1.143975. For Circle Packing, it reached 2.635983, matching the strongest reported results in the comparison table.

For Autocorrelation, Dream-RSI obtained 1.456375, where lower is better. This was competitive with several systems but did not match SimpleTES’s reported 1.453675. The relevant comparison is also affected by budget: SimpleTES used 51,200 generations, whereas Dream-RSI used fewer than 1,000 generations in the described setup. Thus, the results indicate improved cost efficiency, while not establishing uniform superiority in final mathematical objective values.

#### 4.3. GPU kernel engineering

The KernelBench experiments cover VGG16, LayerNorm, ConvDiv, and ConvMax. Performance is measured as inverse runtime subject to correctness checks.

For VGG16 and LayerNorm, Dream-RSI reached comparable performance using 2.43 and 1.79 times fewer generations, respectively. For ConvDiv and ConvMax, it achieved 2.09 and 1.44 times higher performance under comparable discovery budgets. These results provide evidence that adaptive exploration can be useful in a domain where progress depends on algorithmic structure, memory access, parallelization, and hardware-specific implementation.

#### 4.4. Additional analyses

An ablation on ConvDiv compared replay-based use of history with explicit semantic guidance derived from previous trajectories. Adding high-level directional guidance to prompts reduced performance for both fixed exploration and Dream-RSI. The authors interpret this as evidence that strong semantic guidance can over-constrain parallel search, whereas replay allows the policy to use historical information selectively.

The policy’s behavior also changed across rounds. On ConvDiv, it initially reduced the number of evaluated attempts as performance improved, falling from 110 to 50 attempts in one sequence. When progress plateaued, it increased exploration effort again and achieved additional performance gains. This illustrates adaptation in resource allocation rather than simply monotonically increasing search breadth.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The principal significance of Dream-RSI is methodological. It provides a way to reuse expensive discovery experience for improving the controller that governs future discovery. This could reduce the cost of meta-level experimentation in autonomous coding, scientific search, algorithm design, and systems optimization.

The framework may also support more modular agent development. Because it leaves the base coding agent and evaluator unchanged, improvements can be made at the orchestration layer without retraining the underlying model. The replay representation could make exploration policies easier to inspect, compare, and debug.

However, the approach has important limitations. A replay simulator contains only the portion of the search space actually traversed online. It cannot faithfully evaluate policies that would choose branches or candidate-generation contexts absent from the recorded history. Replay scores may therefore favor policies that exploit known trajectories and may not predict performance in new online rollouts. The paper’s theoretical non-degradation guarantee is limited to the fixed replay pool, and the experiments use a small number of tasks and controlled baselines. Broader validation, statistical variation across runs, and analysis of replay bias would be needed to determine how reliably the method scales to substantially different environments.

Overall, the paper frames discovery history as an empirical environment for off-policy improvement of exploration. Its results suggest that this representation can improve the cost-quality trade-off of long-horizon agentic discovery, while also highlighting the need to manage the gap between replay performance and genuinely novel online exploration.
