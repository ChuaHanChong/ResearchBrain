# Decision Transformer: Reinforcement Learning via Sequence Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the Decision Transformer, an approach that casts Reinforcement Learning as a conditional sequence modeling task, leveraging the Transformer architecture to learn policies from offline datasets. The framework achieves strong performance across various offline RL benchmarks, demonstrating robustness to sparse and delayed rewards and providing control over the desired policy expertise.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms often suffer from instabilities like the "deadly triad" (function approximation, bootstrapping, off-policy data) and challenges with long-term credit assignment and arbitrary discount factors.
- Offline RL, learning from fixed datasets without further environment interaction, presents difficulties due to issues like distribution shift and the need for complex regularization or value pessimism.
- Existing RL methods struggle with sparse and delayed reward environments, as propagating credit over long horizons with Bellman backups is inefficient.

## Method

- The **Decision Transformer** reframes Reinforcement Learning as a ==conditional sequence modeling== problem, training a ==GPT-like Transformer== to predict future actions given a sequence of past states, actions, and desired future returns (==returns-to-go==).
- Trajectories are represented as sequences of interleaved (return-to-go, state, action) triplets, with the model trained on offline datasets using a standard supervised learning objective (==cross-entropy== for discrete actions, ==MSE== for continuous).
- During inference, the model ==autoregressively== generates actions by conditioning on a user-specified target return, current state, and previously generated actions, with the target return decremented by observed rewards.

## Results

- Decision Transformer achieves competitive or superior performance compared to state-of-the-art offline RL algorithms (e.g., CQL) across various Atari games (using **1% DQN-replay data**) and OpenAI Gym (D4RL) continuous control tasks.
- The model consistently demonstrates a strong correlation between the desired target return and the actual accumulated return, showing fine-grained control over generated policy expertise and sometimes extrapolating to returns higher than observed in the training data.
- Decision Transformer remains robust and performs well in delayed reward settings and sparse reward environments (like Key-to-Door), where traditional TD-learning methods often collapse.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Longer context lengths (beyond just immediate past states) are crucial for the Transformer's performance, suggesting the utility of extended temporal information in decision-making.
> - Reframing RL as sequence modeling on return-to-go values inherently bypasses common instabilities of traditional TD-learning, such as issues with bootstrapping, discounting, and the "deadly triad."
> - The model's ability to extrapolate to higher returns than seen in training and its robustness to sparse/delayed rewards indicate a capacity for learning and improving policies beyond simple imitation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has traditionally been framed as a problem of learning value functions (e.g., Q-learning, TD learning) or directly optimizing policies through gradient-based methods. While these approaches have achieved remarkable successes, they often come with challenges such as the "deadly triad" (the instability arising from combining function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy learning), issues with long-term credit assignment, and the need for careful hyperparameter tuning.

Concurrently, the field of natural language processing (NLP) has been revolutionized by the advent of the Transformer architecture and large-scale sequence modeling (e.g., GPT-x, BERT). These models excel at learning high-dimensional distributions over sequential data, demonstrating impressive capabilities in language generation, understanding, and even zero-shot generalization. Increasingly, these architectures are extending their influence into computer vision and other domains dealing with sequential or structured data.

This paper proposes a radical **paradigm shift** at the intersection of these two fields: **abstracting Reinforcement Learning as a sequence modeling problem.** Instead of designing algorithms around value functions or policy gradients, the authors propose training a Transformer to model the joint distribution of trajectories (sequences of states, actions, and rewards). This fundamentally reframes policy sampling as an autoregressive generative modeling task.

This work specifically contrasts with prior efforts that merely *incorporate* Transformers as architectural components within traditional RL algorithms (e.g., for relational reasoning or state representation). Decision Transformer, instead, suggests that the entire RL problem can be *replaced* by sequence modeling. It leverages the inherent capabilities of Transformers to:
- **Model long sequences:** Addressing long-term credit assignment without explicit bootstrapping or discounting.
- **Perform credit assignment directly:** Via self-attention, potentially being more robust to sparse or distracting rewards.
- **Model wide distributions of behaviors:** Facilitating generalization and transfer.

The paper focuses on **offline reinforcement learning**, a particularly challenging setting where an agent must learn from a fixed dataset of previously collected experience without further interaction with the environment. Traditional offline RL methods often employ techniques like behavior regularization or value pessimism to mitigate issues like distribution shift and value overestimation. Decision Transformer's approach inherently avoids these issues by casting the problem as supervised learning on observed trajectories, conditioning on desired future outcomes (returns-to-go).

This work builds upon and extends concepts from "upside-down RL" (UDRL) or "reward-conditioned policies," which train policies to output actions conditioned on desired future returns. However, Decision Transformer differentiates itself by emphasizing the power of the **Transformer architecture for long-context sequence modeling**, demonstrating that a larger context length (beyond just the immediate past state) and the Transformer's self-attention mechanism are crucial for performance. The paper also acknowledges concurrent work like "Trajectory Transformer" by Janner et al., further highlighting a growing trend towards unifying sequence modeling with RL.

In essence, this research attempts to bridge the gap between the successes of large-scale generative models in domains like NLP and the intricate challenges of sequential decision-making in RL, offering a simpler, more scalable, and potentially more stable algorithmic paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **Decision Transformer**, a novel framework that fundamentally redefines Reinforcement Learning as a conditional sequence modeling problem. The authors aim to demonstrate that this approach, leveraging the power and scalability of the Transformer architecture, can match or even exceed the performance of state-of-the-art model-free offline RL algorithms, despite its comparative simplicity and departure from conventional RL paradigms.

The key motivations driving this work are multifaceted:

- **Leverage Transformer Scalability and Simplicity:** The Transformer architecture, having proven incredibly effective and scalable in domains like language modeling (e.g., GPT-x, BERT), offers a "simplicity" in its training objective (autoregressive prediction) that RL typically lacks. The authors aim to bring this simplicity and scalability to RL.
- **Bypass Traditional RL Instabilities:** Conventional RL algorithms, particularly those based on Temporal Difference (TD) learning, can suffer from issues like the "deadly triad" (instability from combining function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy data). Decision Transformer aims to "bypass the need for bootstrapping for long term credit assignment," thereby avoiding these inherent instabilities.
- **Address Discounting Limitations:** TD learning typically relies on discounting future rewards, which can lead to "undesirable short-sighted behaviors." By directly conditioning on "return-to-go" (the sum of future rewards), Decision Transformer naturally handles long-term objectives without an arbitrary discount factor.
- **Improve Long-Term Credit Assignment:** Traditional Bellman backups propagate rewards slowly, making long-term credit assignment difficult, especially with sparse rewards. Transformers, through self-attention, can potentially "perform credit assignment directly," forming implicit state-reward associations over long horizons.
- **Effective Offline RL:** The challenge of offline RL (learning from fixed, potentially suboptimal data without exploration) is particularly suited for a sequence modeling objective. By training an autoregressive model on diverse trajectories, Decision Transformer can learn to generate actions corresponding to desired future returns, effectively performing policy improvement without requiring explicit dynamic programming or complex regularization schemes.
- **Generalization and Transfer:** Empirical evidence from language and vision suggests that Transformer-based generative modeling can represent wide distributions of behaviors, potentially enabling better generalization and transfer in RL settings.
- **Prompt-based Policy Generation:** The framework allows for intuitive "skill querying" by simply specifying a desired return (e.g., maximum possible return) as a conditioning input, enabling the model to generate a corresponding optimal trajectory.

In essence, the authors are motivated to explore whether the success of generative sequence modeling can be directly translated to decision-making problems, offering a more unified, stable, and powerful approach to reinforcement learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Decision Transformer reframes reinforcement learning as a conditional sequence modeling problem, leveraging the GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) architecture.

**Core Idea:** The central tenet is to model entire trajectories as sequences and train a Transformer to predict future actions based on past states, actions, and, crucially, desired future returns.

**Trajectory Representation:**
Unlike traditional RL which focuses on immediate rewards, Decision Transformer uses **returns-to-go ($\hat{R}_t = \sum_{t'=t}^T r_{t'}$) - the sum of future rewards from the current timestep.** This allows the model to be conditioned on a desired future outcome. A trajectory is represented as a sequence of (return-to-go, state, action) triplets:
$\tau = (\hat{R}_1, s_1, a_1, \hat{R}_2, s_2, a_2, \ldots, \hat{R}_T, s_T, a_T)$.

**Architecture:**
- The model uses a **GPT-like Transformer** with a causal self-attention mask, enabling autoregressive generation where predictions for a given timestep only depend on past information.
- **Input:** The last $K$ timesteps of the trajectory are fed into the Transformer, resulting in $3K$ tokens (one for each modality: return-to-go, state, action).
- **Embeddings:**
 - Each raw input (return-to-go, state, action) is projected into a shared embedding dimension using modality-specific linear layers. For visual inputs (e.g., Atari), a convolutional encoder is used for states.
 - A learned **episodic positional embedding** is added to each token. This is distinct from standard Transformer positional embeddings, as one timestep corresponds to three tokens (return, state, action), and this embedding captures the relative position within an episode.
- **Output:** The Transformer processes these interleaved tokens to predict future action tokens.

**Training:**
- The model is trained on **offline datasets** of pre-recorded trajectories, meaning there is no interaction with the environment during training.
- Mini-batches of sequences of length $K$ are sampled from the dataset.
- The prediction head corresponding to the state token $s_t$ is trained to predict the action $a_t$.
- The loss function is a standard supervised learning objective:
 - **Cross-entropy loss** for discrete action spaces (e.g., Atari).
 - **Mean Squared Error (MSE)** for continuous action spaces (e.g., OpenAI Gym).
- Losses for each timestep within the sequence are averaged.
- Crucially, the paper states that they "did not find predicting the states or returns-to-go to improve performance" for the main policy learning task, simplifying the training objective.

**Inference/Test Time (Autoregressive Generation):**
1. **Specify Target Return:** At the beginning of an episode, the user or system provides a desired "target return" (e.g., a high value for expert-level performance).
2. **Initial Context:** The model is initialized with the target return and the environment's starting state.
3. **Action Prediction:** The Decision Transformer predicts the next action based on the current context (target return, observed states, and previously generated actions).
4. **Environment Interaction (Simulated/Real):** The predicted action is executed in the environment.
5. **Context Update:** The observed reward is used to **decrement the target return** (i.e., $\hat{R}_{t+1} = \hat{R}_t - r_t$). The new state, the generated action, and the updated return-to-go are appended to the sequence.
6. **Context Windowing:** Only the latest $K$ timesteps are kept in the context window.
7. **Loop:** Steps 3-6 are repeated until the episode terminates.

**Key Deviations from Traditional RL:**
- **No Value Functions or Q-values:** The model does not explicitly learn a value function or Q-function.
- **No Policy Gradients:** There is no explicit policy gradient optimization or actor-critic framework.
- **No Bootstrapping:** The model learns directly from full trajectory returns, avoiding recursive Bellman updates.
- **No Discounting:** Rewards are summed directly as returns-to-go, eliminating the need for a discount factor.
- **No Explicit Regularization for Offline RL:** Unlike many offline RL algorithms (e.g., CQL, BEAR), Decision Transformer does not incorporate explicit behavior regularization or value pessimism.

The methodology essentially turns the RL problem into a large-scale imitation learning task conditioned on performance metrics, allowing it to leverage the robust and scalable training procedures developed for Transformer models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Decision Transformer (DT) demonstrates compelling performance across a diverse set of offline RL benchmarks, often matching or exceeding the performance of state-of-the-art methods that are explicitly designed for offline settings.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Across Benchmarks

- **Atari (1% DQN-replay dataset):** DT is competitive with Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) in 3 out of 4 games (Breakout, Qbert, Pong, Seaquest) and consistently outperforms or matches other baselines like REM, QR-DQN, and standard Behavior Cloning (BC). This is significant given Atari's high-dimensional visual inputs and the challenge of long-term credit assignment.
- **OpenAI Gym (D4RL continuous control tasks):** DT achieves the highest scores in a majority of the tasks across different dataset settings (Medium-Expert, Medium, Medium-Replay for HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker, and Reacher). It consistently outperforms BEAR, BRAC, AWR, and often CQL, showcasing its robustness and effectiveness in continuous control domains.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Percentile Behavior Cloning (%BC)

- To understand if DT simply acts as an intelligent behavior cloner on a high-performing subset of data, the authors introduced %BC, which trains a BC agent only on the top X% of trajectories.
- In data-rich environments (D4RL), DT's performance is competitive with the best %BC (which requires an oracle selection of the best data subset). This suggests DT implicitly identifies and focuses on desirable behaviors.
- Crucially, in low-data regimes (Atari 1% DQN-replay), DT significantly outperforms all versions of %BC. This indicates that DT leverages the entire dataset distribution to improve generalization, even on trajectories dissimilar from the target return, rather than just imitating the best observed.

#### 4.3. Modeling the Distribution of Returns

- Experiments show a strong positive correlation between the desired target return supplied to DT during inference and the actual return accumulated by the agent. This demonstrates DT's ability to "understand" and effectively model the return-to-go tokens, allowing for fine-grained control over the desired policy expertise.
- In some Atari tasks (e.g., Seaquest), DT can even extrapolate and generate trajectories with higher returns than the maximum observed in the training dataset, suggesting a capacity for policy improvement beyond simple imitation.

#### 4.4. Impact of Context Length

- Ablation studies on the context length ($K$) reveal that using a longer context (e.g., $K=30$ or $50$) significantly improves DT's performance compared to using only the immediate past state ($K=1$). This is particularly evident in Atari games and contradicts the typical assumption in RL that the immediate past state (possibly frame-stacked) is sufficient. This finding suggests that a longer temporal context helps the Transformer identify the underlying policy or behavior, leading to better learning and training dynamics.

#### 4.5. Long-Term Credit Assignment in Sparse Reward Settings

- On the challenging Key-to-Door environment (requiring credit propagation over long horizons with sparse rewards), DT (trained on random walks) achieves near-optimal success rates (71.8% for 1K trajectories, 94.6% for 10K), comparable to oracle %BC.
- In stark contrast, TD-learning (CQL) struggles dramatically (around 13% success rate), failing to propagate Q-values effectively. This highlights DT's superior ability to handle long-term credit assignment and sparse rewards without explicit mechanisms like Bellman backups.
- Further analysis on Key-to-Door shows that a modified DT (predicting returns) acts as an "effective critic," continuously updating reward probabilities and attending to "pivotal events" (e.g., picking up the key, reaching the door). This suggests that Transformers can implicitly form state-reward associations crucial for value prediction.

#### 4.6. Robustness to Delayed Returns

- In delayed reward versions of D4RL Hopper tasks (where the agent only receives cumulative reward at the final timestep), DT's performance is minimally affected, and it continues to perform well.
- Conversely, CQL (TD-learning) collapses completely in this sparse reward setting. Behavior Cloning (BC) and %BC are also relatively robust as they are reward-agnostic. This demonstrates DT's inherent robustness to reward sparsity, a significant advantage over traditional TD-based methods.

#### 4.7. Absence of Value Pessimism or Behavior Regularization

- A notable finding is that DT achieves strong performance without requiring the policy regularization or conservatism (e.g., value pessimism) that are standard in many offline RL algorithms to prevent exploitation of inaccurate value functions. The authors conjecture that DT's supervised learning objective, which doesn't explicitly optimize a learned value function, avoids this common failure mode.

Overall, the results strongly support the hypothesis that reframing RL as a sequence modeling problem with Transformers is a viable and highly effective approach, offering a simpler, more robust, and scalable alternative to conventional RL algorithms, particularly in challenging offline and sparse reward settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Decision Transformer represents a significant step towards unifying the fields of large-scale generative modeling and reinforcement learning, with several profound implications and potential impacts:

- **Paradigm Shift in RL:** By casting RL as a conditional sequence modeling problem, Decision Transformer challenges the traditional algorithmic foundations of RL centered around value functions, Q-learning, and policy gradients. It offers a fresh perspective that could lead to fundamentally new ways of designing and understanding RL agents. This abstraction leverages the simplicity and robustness of supervised learning, a well-understood and scalable training paradigm.
- **Unification of AI Domains:** This work brings RL closer to the successes seen in NLP and computer vision with large-scale Transformer models. The ability to apply "GPT-like" architectures and training methodologies directly to sequential decision-making problems opens avenues for cross-domain knowledge transfer, potentially leading to a more unified approach to general AI.
- **Simplicity and Scalability:** Decision Transformer achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance using a relatively simple supervised learning objective, avoiding the complex optimization and stability issues inherent in many traditional RL algorithms (e.g., the "deadly triad" of bootstrapping, off-policy learning, and function approximation). This simplicity, combined with the proven scalability of Transformers, suggests that Decision Transformer could be scaled to much larger datasets and more complex environments.
- **Robustness to RL Challenges:** The findings demonstrate remarkable robustness to long-standing RL challenges:
 - **Long-term Credit Assignment:** DT's ability to learn from full trajectories and use attention to identify critical events effectively addresses delayed rewards without explicit propagation mechanisms.
 - **Sparse Rewards:** Its performance in delayed reward settings, where TD-learning collapses, highlights its natural suitability for sparse reward environments.
 - **Offline RL Stability:** By avoiding explicit value function optimization, DT bypasses the need for complex regularization or pessimism often required in offline RL to prevent exploitation of out-of-distribution actions.
- **Enhanced Behavior Control:** The conditioning on "return-to-go" allows for intuitive and precise control over the generated behavior. Users can "prompt" the agent with a desired level of performance, making the system highly interpretable and controllable. The ability to extrapolate to higher returns than seen in training data suggests a capacity for genuine policy improvement.
- **Future of Online RL:** While focused on offline RL, the paper speculates on the implications for online RL. The robust behavior generation and ability to learn from diverse data could position Decision Transformer as a powerful "memorization engine" or a strong initialization for online fine-tuning, potentially leading to more sample-efficient online learning.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work is likely to inspire extensive follow-up research in several directions:
 - **Self-supervised pretraining:** Leveraging large unlabeled datasets of trajectories.
 - **Sophisticated embeddings:** Exploring richer representations for returns (e.g., return distributions for stochasticity).
 - **Model-based RL alternatives:** Using Transformers to model state evolution for planning.
 - **Understanding limitations:** Thoroughly analyzing the types of errors Transformers make in MDP settings and the potential for destructive biases from training data, especially as models are trained on ever-larger and more diverse datasets.

In conclusion, Decision Transformer offers a compelling vision for a future where reinforcement learning is seamlessly integrated with the powerful and scalable frameworks of modern sequence modeling, potentially unlocking new capabilities and simplifying the development of intelligent agents.
