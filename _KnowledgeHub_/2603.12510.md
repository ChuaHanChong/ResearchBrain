# Red-Teaming Vision-Language-Action Models via Quality Diversity Prompt Generation for Robust Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Southern California and Sony AI developed Q-DIG, a framework that uses Quality Diversity optimization with vision-language grounding to generate diverse and human-like adversarial instructions for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This method systematically identifies VLA vulnerabilities, leading to a 5-25% improvement in task success rates on unseen instructions after fine-tuning, with these benefits validated in real-world robot deployments.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models exhibit high sensitivity to subtle variations in language instructions, causing unexpected failures even for semantically equivalent commands.
- Existing red-teaming methods for VLAs, such as Embodied Red Teaming (ERT), lack fine-grained control over targeted failure modes and often generate adversarial instructions that are unrealistic or out-of-distribution.
- Prior Quality Diversity (QD) approaches for text generation are primarily language-focused and do not integrate visual context, which is critical for identifying multimodal vulnerabilities in embodied VLA systems.

## Method

- **Q-DIG** (Quality Diversity for Diverse Instruction Generation) frames VLA red-teaming as a ==Quality Diversity optimization== problem to automatically generate diverse, human-like adversarial prompts.
- The framework optimizes for both the quality of instructions (quantified by the ==variance of the VLA's failure rate==) and their diversity (measured by collective coverage of predefined semantic ==attack styles== using an ==LLM judge==).
- Discovered adversarial instructions are used to augment the VLA's training dataset, allowing for ==adversarial fine-tuning== that enhances the base policy's robustness and generalization to varied linguistic inputs.

## Results

- Q-DIG generated instructions exhibited superior diversity (e.g., **0.972** archive coverage for LIBERO-Goal) and received higher human-likeness rankings (average **1.67**) compared to baseline methods like ERT and Rephrase.
- Fine-tuning VLAs (OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5, GR00T N1.6) with Q-DIG-generated instructions led to a **5-10%** average increase in success rates on unseen adversarial instructions, with up to a **25%** improvement on prompts generated by the same algorithm.
- Real-world experiments demonstrated that adversarial prompts identified in simulation consistently induced failures on physical robots, and fine-tuning with Q-DIG-augmented data improved real-world task success rates for previously failing adversarial instructions (e.g., from **0%** to **70%** success for one task).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining Quality Diversity optimization with vision-language grounding effectively identifies diverse, task-relevant, and human-like adversarial prompts that specifically target VLA failure modes.
> - Optimizing for the variance of a VLA's failure rate encourages the generation of instructions at the boundary of the model's capabilities, revealing nuanced vulnerabilities while avoiding unrealistic adversarial inputs.
> - Fine-tuning VLAs with a collection of these diverse, generated adversarial instructions measurably improves their robustness and generalization to unseen linguistic variations in both simulated and real-world robotic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant advancement in robotics, enabling general-purpose robotic systems through the fine-tuning of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to produce robot actions. These models have demonstrated impressive generalization capabilities, including out-of-the-box deployment in novel environments, cross-embodiment transfer, and efficient learning of new tasks with limited data. Examples such as RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, and π0.5 highlight the potential of VLAs to integrate rich perceptual and linguistic priors for complex robotic tasks.

Despite these advancements, VLAs exhibit sensitivity to the precise wording of language instructions. Minor changes in task phrasing can lead to unexpected failures, even when instructions are semantically equivalent. This brittleness poses a challenge for reliable deployment, particularly in safety-critical applications. The field of "red-teaming" (also known as jailbreaking when the intent is malicious) has emerged to systematically discover inputs that expose such vulnerabilities in foundation models.

Prior efforts in red-teaming for VLAs include Embodied Red Teaming (ERT), which leverages in-context learning to generate adversarial instructions in simulation. While ERT can uncover failure cases, it has limitations: it does not offer fine-grained control over the types of failure modes targeted, can generate instructions that deviate from realistic user inputs, and does not inherently integrate the discovered adversarial instructions into a feedback loop for policy improvement.

Concurrent research has explored Quality Diversity (QD) optimization, an algorithmic framework designed to search for a diverse collection of high-performing solutions. QD has been applied in various robotics contexts, such as generating diverse scenarios for autonomous agents and training diverse reinforcement learning agents. More recently, QD has been adapted for text generation with Large Language Models (LLMs), as seen in methods like Rainbow Teaming and PLAN-QD. Rainbow Teaming, for instance, generates diverse adversarial prompts for LLMs by targeting different "attack styles." However, these LLM-focused QD approaches operate solely within the language domain and do not account for visual context, which is a crucial component for embodied VLA systems.

This work addresses the existing gap by proposing Q-DIG (Quality Diversity for Diverse Instruction Generation), a novel framework that integrates Quality Diversity optimization with vision-language grounding. By combining these elements, Q-DIG aims to systematically discover diverse, realistic, and task-relevant failure modes in embodied systems, and subsequently leverage these discoveries to enhance the robustness of VLA policies. This approach positions Q-DIG as a method for improving the reliability of VLA-powered robots against linguistic adversarial inputs, thereby facilitating their safer and more general deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observed fragility of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models when confronted with variations in natural language instructions. While VLAs hold significant promise for enabling general-purpose robotic systems across diverse vision-language tasks, their performance is highly sensitive to the exact phrasing of commands. This sensitivity makes it challenging to predict when a VLA-based robot might fail, thereby limiting their practical deployment, particularly in contexts demanding high reliability and safety.

The key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Systematic Identification of Vulnerabilities:** To develop a scalable method for red-teaming embodied models by identifying diverse, natural language task descriptions that consistently induce failures in VLAs. The generated instructions must remain task-relevant and avoid being maximally adversarial but unrealistic.
2. **Diverse Failure Mode Exposure:** To utilize Quality Diversity (QD) optimization in conjunction with Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to generate a broad spectrum of adversarial instructions. These instructions are intended to expose a comprehensive and meaningful range of vulnerabilities within VLA behavior, categorizing them across semantic "attack styles."
3. **Enhancement of VLA Robustness:** To demonstrate that fine-tuning VLA models using an augmented dataset comprising these diverse, generated adversarial instructions can lead to improved task success rates and better generalization to previously unseen, human-generated instructions.
4. **Generation of Human-like Prompts:** To ensure that the adversarial instructions generated are perceived as natural and human-like by human evaluators, rather than purely synthetic or out-of-distribution prompts. This objective supports the practical applicability of the generated instructions for improving real-world VLA interactions.
5. **Validation in Real-World Settings:** To evaluate the effectiveness of the Q-DIG framework, including its instruction generation and subsequent fine-tuning benefits, in real-world robotic deployments, confirming that findings from simulation transfer to physical systems.

The overarching motivation is to contribute to the development of more robust and reliable VLA-based robots by systematically addressing their linguistic vulnerabilities, thereby paving the way for their broader and safer integration into real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Q-DIG (Quality Diversity for Diverse Instruction Generation), frames the red-teaming of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models as a Quality Diversity (QD) optimization problem. The central tenet of Q-DIG is to automatically generate a diverse collection of semantically varied instructions that act as adversarial prompts, designed to expose and characterize failure modes in base VLA policies. These discovered instructions are then utilized to fine-tune the VLA, thereby enhancing its robustness.

**Quality Diversity Formulation:**
Q-DIG optimizes two primary aspects: the quality of individual solutions and the diversity across the entire solution set.
- **Solution Space:** The set of all possible natural language instructions, denoted as *C*.
- **Quality Metric:** The quality of an instruction *c* is quantified by the variance of the base VLA’s failure rate on a given task *T*. This metric, *J(c) = E[g_T(ζ)](1 - E[g_T(ζ)])*, where *g_T(ζ)* indicates task completion for trajectory *ζ*, is employed. The use of variance encourages the generation of instructions that lie at the boundary of the VLA’s linguistic capabilities, thereby revealing nuanced vulnerabilities while discouraging unrealistic, maximally adversarial prompts.
- **Diversity Metric:** Diversity among instructions is measured by their collective coverage of a predefined set of *M+1* "attack styles" *Z = {z_i}*. An LLM acts as a measure function, *m: C → Z*, mapping each generated instruction to one of these semantic categories. The objective is to discover a highest-variance instruction for each attack style, effectively filling an archive of "cells," each representing a distinct attack style. The eight attack styles used include step-by-step instructions, uncommon vocabulary, human-centric tone, use of adverbs, unnecessary action specification, overly verbose reformulation, colloquial/casual style, and mixed modality references.

**Q-DIG Pipeline for Adversarial Instruction Generation:**
Q-DIG operates through an iterative four-step process to populate its archive with diverse, failure-inducing instructions:

1. **Instruction Selection:** The process commences with an initial, standard task instruction *c_0,T*. As the archive is populated, Q-DIG samples a previously stored instruction from a filled archive cell. This instruction serves as a "stepping stone" for generating new adversarial variants.
2. **Candidate Instruction Mutation:** A Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically `gpt-4o-2024-08-06`, functions as the mutator. Given an existing adversarial instruction *c*, its classified attack style *m(c)*, the initial visual observation *o_0* of the task, and a designated *target* attack style *z*, the VLM generates new candidate instructions. This generation leverages in-context learning. To ensure diversity among candidates, *k* sets, each containing *b* instructions, are generated. The set with the lowest average pairwise cosine similarity of Sentence-BERT embeddings is selected.
3. **Instruction Evaluation:** Each candidate instruction *c* from the selected set undergoes evaluation. The base VLA policy *π(o, c)* is rolled out multiple times in the environment for task *T* to compute its failure variance *J(c)*. Simultaneously, an external LLM (also `gpt-4o-2024-08-06`, acting as an "LLM judge") categorizes the instruction into one of the predefined semantic attack styles *z_i*.
4. **Archive Update:** The Q-DIG archive maintains an elitist cell for each attack style. A newly evaluated instruction is added to the archive if:
 - Its assigned attack style cell is currently empty, thus improving the archive's coverage across *Z* (diversity).
 - It yields a higher failure variance *J(c)* than the instruction already present in its assigned cell, thereby improving the quality of the archive for that specific attack style.

**Fine-Tuning the VLA:**
Upon the discovery of a set of diverse adversarial instructions, they are utilized to augment the base VLA’s training dataset. The framework assumes access to a dataset of expert demonstrations *D = {τ_1..., τ_K}*, each associated with an original language instruction. Without collecting additional expert data, this dataset is segmented, and subsets of demonstrations are paired with the newly generated adversarial instructions. This creates an augmented dataset, *D_aug*, that incorporates both original and adversarial instructions. The base VLA *π(o, c)* is then fine-tuned using supervised learning on *D_aug*, aiming to improve its robustness against varied and adversarial linguistic inputs.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted in SimplerEnv and LIBERO-Goal simulation domains, using OpenVLA, π0.5, and GR00T N1.6 as base VLAs. Baselines included Embodied Red Teaming (ERT) and Rephrase. ERT selects in-context examples based purely on failure induction, without explicit attack style targeting. Rephrase generates variants by prompting an LLM to rephrase original instructions. Q-DIG and baselines were run for comparable iterations (3-12 iterations, batch size *b*=10, *k*=5 candidate sets), and `gpt-4o` was used consistently across all LLM/VLM roles. Real-world experiments involved a Kinova JACO arm and RealSense Depth Cameras.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across prompt generation, user studies, VLA fine-tuning, and real-world experiments.

**A. Prompt Generation Results**

- **Diversity of Generated Instructions:**
 - Q-DIG demonstrated superior performance in generating diverse instructions compared to baseline methods (Rephrase and ERT). It achieved higher pairwise BERT diversity (Figure 3a, p < 0.01 for LIBERO-Goal) and maintained a high or competitive dissimilarity to the original instruction (Figure 3c).
 - Crucially, Q-DIG-generated instructions consistently induced high failure variance (Figure 3b, p < 0.01 for both benchmarks), indicating they were challenging for the VLA but remained within its operational capabilities, rather than being completely out-of-distribution.
 - While BLEU diversity (average pairwise BLEU score) was comparable across all three methods, Q-DIG significantly outperformed baselines in "archive coverage" (Table III). Q-DIG achieved 0.972 coverage for LIBERO-Goal and 0.913 for SimplerEnv, meaning it successfully generated instructions mapping to nearly all predefined attack styles. In contrast, Rephrase and ERT had much lower coverage (e.g., 0.363 and 0.325 for LIBERO-Goal, respectively), indicating a limited ability to target diverse failure modes. A heatmap of a sample Q-DIG archive further visually confirmed the discovery of instructions with high failure variance across diverse attack styles (Figure 5).

- **Human-likeness of Instructions:**
 - A user study (n=40 participants) was conducted to assess the human-likeness of generated instructions. Q-DIG-generated instructions received the highest average ranking for human-likeness (1.67) compared to ERT (2.24) and Rephrase (2.10). Statistical analysis (Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction) indicated that Q-DIG’s ranking was significantly better than both baselines (p < 0.001).
 - Likert scale ratings further supported this, with Q-DIG instructions rated significantly more human-like than ERT-generated instructions (mean difference of 1.07; p < 0.001), and comparable to Rephrase. These findings suggest that Q-DIG's explicit search for diverse attack styles and optimization for failure variance contribute to the generation of more natural and human-like adversarial prompts.

**B. VLA Fine-Tuning Results**

- **Improved Robustness on Unseen Instructions:** Fine-tuning VLAs with augmented datasets containing adversarial instructions consistently improved performance on *unseen* adversarial instructions across various VLA architectures (OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5, GR00T N1.6) in the LIBERO-Goal task suite (Table II).
 - OpenVLA-OFT fine-tuned with Q-DIG and ERT instructions showed an average success rate increase of 5–10% on unseen adversarial instructions, with up to a 25% improvement on instructions generated by the same algorithm.
 - Similarly, π0.5 fine-tuned with Q-DIG instructions achieved a 5% average performance improvement on unseen adversarial prompts, and GR00T N1.6 saw a 15% improvement. These results validate the hypothesis that augmenting training data with diverse adversarial instructions enhances VLA robustness.
- **Q-DIG's Effectiveness in Fine-Tuning:**
 - For OpenVLA-OFT and GR00T N1.6, fine-tuning with Q-DIG-generated instructions led to the best performance on unseen Q-DIG-generated instructions, which were also identified as the most human-like.
 - In the SimplerEnv domain, all three augmentation strategies improved robustness compared to the base VLA (37.0% success rate). Q-DIG achieved a 52.2% success rate, ERT 54.3%, and Rephrase 60.5%. When excluding a specific challenging task ("open top drawer"), Q-DIG achieved the highest success rate (63.6%), surpassing Rephrase (61.5%) and ERT (57.2%). This suggests Q-DIG is particularly effective when the base VLA possesses a baseline level of task competence.
 - The observed performance degradation when fine-tuning OpenVLA-OFT with Rephrase instructions was hypothesized to be due to Rephrase's lack of visually-grounded failure mode coverage. Without visual grounding, Rephrase may introduce linguistic variations that, while semantically diverse, do not target multimodal vulnerabilities, potentially leading to overfitting on irrelevant textual nuances. In contrast, Q-DIG’s failure variance objective ensures task-relevance, human-likeness, and adherence to the model’s physical execution boundaries.

**C. Real-World Experiments**

- **Sim-to-Real Consistency:** Real-world evaluations confirmed that adversarial prompts generated by Q-DIG in simulation exhibited consistent failure trends on physical robots (Kinova JACO arm). For example, prompts (P1 and P3) that yielded lower success rates in simulation for the "push the coke can" task also resulted in lower success rates in the real world. Conversely, a prompt (P4) with a 100% success rate in simulation also achieved 100% in the real world (Table IV).
- **Real-World Robustness Improvement:** Fine-tuning a VLA with an augmented dataset (original + Q-DIG-generated instructions) improved the robot's performance on unseen adversarial prompts in the real world. For the "push the coke can" task, an unseen adversarial prompt (P1) that originally failed 10 out of 10 times with the base model, succeeded 7 out of 10 times after fine-tuning with the Q-DIG-augmented dataset (Table V). This validated the transferability of robustness improvements from simulation-based augmentation to real-world robot deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Q-DIG represents a notable step in enhancing the robustness and reliability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, addressing a critical limitation that currently impedes their widespread deployment in real-world robotic applications. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Systematic Vulnerability Discovery:** Q-DIG provides a systematic and scalable methodology for red-teaming VLA models. By leveraging Quality Diversity optimization and integrating visual grounding, it moves beyond ad-hoc or purely language-based adversarial prompt generation. This allows for the comprehensive identification of diverse, meaningful, and task-relevant failure modes, categorized by specific "attack styles," which was a limitation of prior methods like Embodied Red Teaming. This targeted approach ensures that the identified vulnerabilities are relevant to how humans might actually instruct a robot, rather than merely uncovering unrealistic edge cases.

2. **Enhanced VLA Robustness and Generalization:** A key contribution is the demonstration that fine-tuning VLAs with the adversarial instructions generated by Q-DIG substantially improves their robustness to unseen, varied language instructions. This is crucial for creating general-purpose robots that can reliably understand and execute tasks even when instructions deviate from their initial training data. The ability to generalize to diverse linguistic inputs directly translates to more adaptable and dependable robotic systems.

3. **Bridging Simulation and Reality for Safety:** The validation of Q-DIG's approach in real-world robotic deployments is a significant finding. The consistency between simulation-identified adversarial trends and real-world robot failures, coupled with the observed improvements in real-world task success after fine-tuning with Q-DIG-generated prompts, underscores the practical utility of the framework. This sim-to-real transferability is essential for developing safe and reliable robots, as it allows for extensive testing and improvement in controlled simulated environments before physical deployment.

4. **Advancement in Quality Diversity and AI Red-Teaming:** This work extends the applicability of Quality Diversity algorithms to the complex domain of embodied AI, integrating multimodal inputs (vision and language) and physical action feedback. It also contributes to the broader field of AI red-teaming by offering a structured method for uncovering vulnerabilities in foundation models that control physical systems, a domain with direct safety implications.

5. **Future Directions for Scalable Robustness:** The identified limitations, particularly the computational cost associated with VLA rollouts during instruction evaluation, highlight avenues for future research. Exploring techniques such as surrogate modeling could drastically reduce computation, enabling even more scalable and efficient red-teaming of VLAs. This foresight suggests a clear path for continued development towards truly robust and generalist robots.

In conclusion, Q-DIG contributes to making VLA-powered robots more resilient to the inherent variability of human language. By systematically identifying and mitigating linguistic vulnerabilities, this research paves the way for the safer, more effective, and widespread adoption of intelligent robotic systems in diverse real-world scenarios.
