# AdaJEPA: An Adaptive Latent World Model

> [!summary] Summary
> AdaJEPA introduces an adaptive latent world model that continuously recalibrates its predictions during deployment, improving planning success for goal-conditioned control under visual and dynamics distribution shifts. This approach significantly boosts performance, such as doubling success rates on unseen object shapes, while incurring minimal computational overhead.

## Problem

- Existing latent world models, when "frozen" after training, suffer from prediction inaccuracies during deployment due to test-time distribution shifts.
- These inaccuracies compound over planning horizons, leading to significant performance degradation in goal-conditioned control tasks.
- Prior test-time adaptation techniques have not been fully integrated into the closed-loop planning process of Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs).

## Method

- **AdaJEPA** integrates a ==test-time adaptation== step directly into the ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== loop, creating a "plan-execute-adapt-replan" cycle.
- Recent transitions collected during execution are stored in an ==online buffer== and used as ==self-supervised== signals for model recalibration.
- A small number of gradient updates are applied to a subset of the ==world model=='s parameters (e.g., final encoder/predictor layers) at each MPC step.

## Results

- AdaJEPA consistently improved planning success rates, even within the training data distribution, showing gains exceeding **20%** in some scenarios.
- The model achieved improvements against various distribution shifts, **nearly doubling** planning success on unseen object shapes in PushObj and providing consistent gains against visual, dynamics, and layout changes.
- The test-time adaptation step introduced negligible latency, typically **0.01-0.03 seconds** per MPC replanning step, due to efficient parameter updates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuously recalibrating latent world models during deployment, using self-supervised signals from executed actions, drastically improves their robustness to environmental shifts.
> - Substantial performance gains can be achieved with minimal computational overhead, often through a single gradient step on a small subset of model parameters per planning cycle.
> - Test-time adaptation complements offline training data by effectively boosting generalization, especially in low-data regimes, even outperforming models trained with significantly more static data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly reinforcement learning and robotics, has a long-standing objective of developing intelligent agents capable of efficient planning and generalization from high-dimensional observations. Latent world models represent a significant paradigm in this pursuit, aiming to capture environment dynamics within a compact latent space. This compact representation facilitates efficient prediction of future states and subsequent planning, often through techniques like Model Predictive Control (MPC).

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) have emerged as a prominent class of world models within this landscape. JEPAs typically learn an encoder to map high-dimensional sensory inputs to a latent space and a predictor to forecast future latent states, usually trained on reward-free offline trajectories. When combined with MPC, these world models form a standard approach for goal-conditioned control and planning.

However, a prevalent limitation in existing world model implementations is that they are typically "frozen" after their initial training phase. This rigidity renders them susceptible to prediction inaccuracies during deployment, especially when faced with test-time distribution shifts. Such shifts can manifest as visual alterations (e.g., noise, lighting, background changes) or dynamics alterations (e.g., changes in friction, mass, contact properties). Even minor single-step prediction errors can compound over a planning horizon, causing actions that appear effective in latent imagination to fail in the real environment. This phenomenon often leads to substantial performance degradation, hindering the practical application of world models in dynamic real-world settings.

Prior work has explored various avenues to mitigate this train-test gap. Some research has focused on data synthesis during training to reduce this disparity, while others in the broader machine learning community have developed "test-time training/adaptation" (TTT/TTA) methods. TTT/TTA typically updates a pre-trained model using self-supervised or unsupervised objectives computed directly from test-time inputs. While these methods have been applied across diverse domains, including image classification and embodied decision-making, their direct integration into the closed-loop planning process of JEPA world models has been less explored. Similarly, adaptive control literature and online model-based reinforcement learning have a history of updating predictive models during decision-making, but these often involve coupled policy or value learning, target-domain fine-tuning data, or additional data collection phases.

AdaJEPA distinguishes itself by proposing a test-time adaptation framework specifically for JEPA world models, operating *within* the closed loop of MPC. This approach aims to continuously recalibrate the world model using self-supervised signals derived from its own executed actions, drawing inspiration from biological systems where sensorimotor adaptation continuously updates internal models with new experience. The work presents an advancement in making world models more robust and adaptable to unforeseen environmental changes encountered during active deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate an adaptive latent world model, named AdaJEPA, that can mitigate the performance degradation of traditional, frozen world models when confronted with test-time distribution shifts. The core motivation stems from the observed fragility of static world models in dynamic environments.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:
- **Address Prediction Inaccuracies:** Latent world models, despite their capacity, frequently generate inaccurate predictions due to the inherent limitations of finite offline training data and discrepancies between training and testing data distributions. This leads to model-predicted outcomes diverging from true environmental dynamics, rendering planning ineffective. AdaJEPA aims to continuously correct these prediction errors.
- **Enhance Robustness to Distribution Shifts:** Test-time distribution shifts, encompassing visual changes (e.g., blur, noise, lighting, color alterations) and dynamics changes (e.g., altered physics properties, unseen layouts), significantly impair the performance of frozen world models. The research seeks to enable the world model to adapt to these shifts online, thereby improving its robustness and generalization capabilities in novel or changing environments.
- **Improve Planning Success:** Inaccurate world model predictions directly lead to suboptimal or failed actions during Model Predictive Control (MPC), as the controller optimizes against an incorrect internal representation of the world. By improving prediction accuracy through adaptation, the objective is to enhance the overall success rate of goal-conditioned planning tasks.
- **Integrate Adaptation within Closed-Loop Control:** The existing paradigm often separates model training from deployment, leaving the world model static during planning. This work aims to tightly couple learning and planning by integrating test-time adaptation directly into the MPC loop. This "plan-execute-adapt-replan" cycle is motivated by the idea that the consequences of an agent's actions provide a valuable, self-supervised signal for model refinement.
- **Ensure Efficiency and Practicability:** The proposed adaptation mechanism must be both sample- and compute-efficient to be viable for real-world deployment. This implies that adaptation should occur with minimal additional data (ideally from the agent's immediate experience) and low computational overhead, such as a small number of gradient steps and restricted parameter updates, without requiring additional expert demonstrations or separate data-collection phases.

By achieving these objectives, AdaJEPA seeks to advance the utility of latent world models for autonomous agents, enabling them to operate more reliably and effectively in complex, non-stationary environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AdaJEPA proposes an adaptive latent world model that operates within the closed loop of Model Predictive Control (MPC) to continuously recalibrate itself during deployment. The methodology is built upon existing Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) and extends them with a test-time adaptation mechanism.

#### 3.1. Background: JEPA World Models

A JEPA latent world model typically consists of three main components:
- **Sensory Encoder ($\mathcal{E}_s$):** Maps high-dimensional observations $o_t$ (e.g., images) to a compact latent state $z_t$.
- **Action Encoder ($\mathcal{E}_a$):** Maps actions $a_t$ to an action embedding $u_t$.
- **Predictor ($f_\theta$):** Takes the current latent state $z_t$ and action embedding $u_t$ to predict the next latent state $\hat{z}_{t+1}$. The predictor may also incorporate a short history of latent states and action embeddings.

These components are jointly trained on reward-free offline trajectories $\{(o_t, a_t, o_{t+1})\}$. The training objective, $\mathcal{L}_{pred}$, is a latent prediction loss (e.g., Mean Squared Error) that minimizes the discrepancy between predicted future latents ($\hat{z}_{t+k}$) and target latents ($z_{t+k}$). To prevent representational collapse, anti-collapse stabilizers such as stop-gradient operators on the target representation or regularization terms are typically employed.

Once trained, the world model is used for goal-conditioned latent planning. Given a goal observation $o_g$ with its latent representation $z_g = \mathcal{E}_s(o_g)$, MPC optimizes an action sequence $a_{t:t+H-1}^*$ by performing rollouts with the predictor $f_\theta$. The objective is to minimize a latent goal-reaching cost, typically the squared Euclidean distance $d(\hat{z}, z_g) = \|\hat{z} - z_g\|_2^2$ over a planning horizon $H$. Gradient-based or sampling-based planners (e.g., CEM) can solve this optimization. Standard MPC executes the first action $a_t$, observes the next state $o_{t+1}$, and then replans from this new observation.

#### 3.2. Closed-Loop Plan-and-Adapt (AdaJEPA)

AdaJEPA modifies the standard MPC loop by inserting an adaptation step after action execution and before replanning. This creates a "plan-execute-adapt-replan" loop:
1. **Plan:** The agent plans an action sequence using the current (potentially adapted) world model to minimize the goal-reaching cost.
2. **Execute:** The first action $a_t$ from the planned sequence is executed in the environment.
3. **Observe and Adapt:** The environment provides the next observation $o_{t+1}$. This new transition $(o_t, a_t, o_{t+1})$ is used as a self-supervised signal to update the world model.
4. **Replan:** The updated world model is then used for the next planning iteration.

The key components of this test-time adaptation are:
- **Online Buffer ($\mathcal{B}$):** A buffer stores recent transitions collected during MPC. After executing $a_t$ and observing $o_{t+1}$, the transition $(o_t, a_t, o_{t+1})$ is added to $\mathcal{B}$. The buffer is capped at a fixed size $N$. Two strategies are considered: (i) `recent-N`, which keeps only the $N$ most recent transitions, and (ii) `hard-N`, which keeps $N$ transitions resulting in the largest prediction errors. The `recent-N` strategy is typically used as a default.
- **Adaptation Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{ada}$):** AdaJEPA utilizes the same self-supervised prediction loss as during pretraining. For each transition $(o_i, a_i, o_{i+1})$ in the buffer $\mathcal{B}$, the adaptation loss minimizes the error between the model's prediction $f_\theta(z_i, \mathcal{E}_a(a_i))$ and the target latent $sg(z_{i+1}) = sg(\mathcal{E}_s(o_{i+1}))$. The stop-gradient operator (`sg`) is applied to the target representation to prevent collapse, maintaining consistency with pretraining practices.
- **Adapted Parameters ($\Omega$):** At each MPC step, a small number ($U$, typically one) of gradient updates are performed on a selected subset of the world model's parameters $\Omega \subseteq \{\phi, \psi, \theta\}$, where $\phi$ are encoder parameters, $\psi$ are action encoder parameters, and $\theta$ are predictor parameters. This targeted update strategy is designed to be lightweight and computationally efficient. The update rule is $\Omega \leftarrow \Omega - \eta\nabla_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{ada}(\mathcal{B})$, using a learning rate $\eta$. The adapted model is immediately utilized for the subsequent planning step.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on several environments and distribution shift types:
- **PushT / PushObj:** A contact-rich manipulation task. PushObj introduces shape shifts (unseen block geometries) and visual shifts (Gaussian blur, salt-and-pepper noise, dark lighting, color changes).
- **PointMaze:** A 2D navigation task. Variations include dynamics shifts (low mass, high damping) and layout shifts (unseen maze configurations).

A pretrained JEPA world model (ResNet encoder, transformer predictor) is used as the baseline. AdaJEPA's default configuration involves adapting only the final layers of the visual encoder and predictor, performing a single gradient step per MPC replanning step, using the same learning rates as pretraining, and maintaining a replay buffer of the 5 most recent samples. Both gradient-based (GD) and sampling-based (CEM) planners are evaluated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AdaJEPA demonstrated consistent improvements in planning success across various environments and distribution shift scenarios, with minimal computational overhead.

#### 4.1. In-distribution Performance

Even in test environments that match the training distribution, AdaJEPA demonstrated beneficial effects. On PushObj training shapes and default PushT rendering, test-time adaptation significantly improved planning success rates for both GD and CEM planners, with gains exceeding 20% in some cases (e.g., PushObj). On PointMaze with default dynamics, adaptation preserved the strong performance of the frozen model. This suggests that AdaJEPA can specialize the model to the current environment, even when it is in-distribution, and is robust enough not to degrade performance when the baseline is already high.

#### 4.2. Distribution Shifts

AdaJEPA consistently yielded significant gains across all out-of-distribution test environments:
- **Shape Shifts (PushObj):** On unseen shapes, where the frozen model's performance substantially decreased, AdaJEPA nearly doubled the planning success rate. The success rate for AdaJEPA continuously increased over replanning steps, indicating its ability to recover from initial prediction inaccuracies, whereas the frozen model often saturated early.
- **Visual Shifts (PushT):** AdaJEPA improved robustness to common visual corruptions like blur, salt-and-pepper noise, and dark lighting. Gains were modest under certain color shifts (red-anchor, red-block), potentially because the model relies on color for object distinction and this specific mismatch was more challenging.
- **Dynamics Shifts (PointMaze):** Even against a strong frozen model baseline that already performed well under new dynamics (possibly due to in-context learning with a history window), AdaJEPA showed consistent additional gains in both low mass and high damping scenarios.
- **Layout Shifts (PointMaze):** For unseen maze layouts, AdaJEPA (specifically adapting earlier predictor layers) improved performance over the frozen model. Furthermore, the resulting planning trajectories were observed to be closer to the shortest path after adaptation.

#### 4.3. Latency

The test-time adaptation step introduced a negligible increase in latency, typically 0.01-0.03 seconds per MPC replanning step. This efficiency is attributed to updating only a small subset of parameters with a single gradient step. The authors also noted that adaptation often enabled the agent to reach goals in fewer replans, potentially decreasing overall task completion time despite the per-step overhead.

#### 4.4. Visualization

Qualitative analysis of imagined rollouts after adaptation, using a decoder trained on the pretrained latent space, indicated that AdaJEPA primarily recalibrates predictions while maintaining the learned latent manifold. For instance, visually corrupted or unseen PushObj shapes were often decoded retaining the training-domain structure (e.g., an unseen red block might be decoded as a gray block, its training color).

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Which Parameters to Adapt:** AdaJEPA's performance was robust to the choice of adapted parameters. All tested variants (e.g., adapting last predictor + last encoder stages, first predictor + last encoder, or using LoRA) improved over the frozen model. `predlast+enclast` was consistently competitive, while adapting earlier predictor layers (e.g., `predfirst+enclast`) proved particularly effective for layout shifts. LoRA also showed improvements but did not consistently outperform direct updates to specific layers.
- **Adaptation Hyperparameters:** A single gradient step with the training learning rate served as a robust default. While larger learning rates could be effective for a single step, they became less stable with more steps. Replay buffer design had a smaller impact on performance, but a `recent-N` sliding-window buffer generally provided the most stable gains. AdaJEPA's effectiveness was observed across all buffer choices, including no buffer, all outperforming the frozen model.
- **Generality Across JEPA Implementations:** AdaJEPA demonstrated consistent planning success improvements (ranging from 0.7% to 7.3%) across various JEPA world model implementations, including those with global vs. spatial representations, different architectures, and training objectives. This indicates broad applicability and efficiency.

#### 4.6. Training Data Scale

Experiments on PushObj varying shape diversity ($K$) and trajectories per shape ($N$) revealed:
- Larger and more diverse training data generally improved both frozen and AdaJEPA models.
- Shape diversity ($K$) was particularly important for generalization, outperforming models with more trajectories from fewer shapes, even for the same total data budget.
- AdaJEPA consistently improved success rates across all data scales, with its most pronounced benefits observed in low-data regimes. For example, on seen shapes with limited data (𝐾=1, 𝑁=1k), AdaJEPA improved success from 28.1% to 60.8%, surpassing a frozen model trained with 16 times more data per shape (43.5%). This suggests that test-time adaptation can compensate for limited training data coverage.

#### 4.7. Limitations

The study acknowledged that while AdaJEPA significantly improves generalization, its effectiveness is bounded by the coverage of the pretrained representation. If a test environment requires novel features entirely absent from training data, adaptation might improve planning but may not fully close the performance gap.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of AdaJEPA represents a notable contribution to the field of latent world models and their application in sequential decision-making. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** AdaJEPA directly addresses a critical weakness of existing world models: their brittleness to test-time distribution shifts. By enabling continuous, closed-loop adaptation, the method makes world models more resilient to unforeseen visual, dynamics, and layout changes, moving towards more robust and generalizable autonomous agents.
- **Paradigm Shift for World Models:** The work advocates for a shift from "frozen" world models to "adaptive" ones during deployment. This concept of continuously calibrating predictions and updating representations while acting, rather than relying solely on pre-trained static models, aligns with biological learning principles and suggests a promising direction for future research in embodied AI.
- **Practical Applicability in Real-World Scenarios:** The ability to adapt online with minimal computational overhead and without additional expert data makes AdaJEPA particularly relevant for real-world robotics and control applications. Environments outside of controlled labs are inherently dynamic, and an adaptive world model can significantly improve the reliability and safety of autonomous systems operating in such conditions.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The demonstration that substantial performance gains can be achieved with as little as a single gradient step per MPC replanning cycle, on a small subset of parameters, highlights the method's computational efficiency. This aspect is crucial for its adoption in latency-sensitive applications.
- **Complementary to Data Scaling:** AdaJEPA offers a strategy that is complementary to increasing training data size and diversity. It provides a sample-efficient route to improved generalization, particularly valuable in data-scarce domains or when deploying models where collecting exhaustive training data for all possible scenarios is impractical. It effectively leverages limited interaction data for adaptation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for further research, specifically in combining lightweight test-time adaptation with more comprehensive continual and active learning strategies. This could potentially lead to world models that not only adapt to immediate shifts but also progressively expand their representational coverage over longer deployment periods.

In essence, AdaJEPA provides a practical and effective mechanism for enhancing the operational robustness of latent world models, paving the way for more dependable and intelligent autonomous systems capable of thriving in complex and changing environments.
