# SLAP: Shortcut Learning for Abstract Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed SLAP, a method integrating reinforcement learning with abstract planning to autonomously discover efficient low-level "shortcuts" for robotic tasks. The approach reduced plan lengths by 32% to 73% across various complex environments and achieved a 100% success rate, significantly outperforming pure reinforcement learning methods.

## Problem

- Addressing long-horizon decision-making in robotics where tasks have sparse rewards and continuous state/action spaces.
- Existing Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) methods use predefined skills, limiting agents to programmed behaviors and often leading to inefficient execution plans.
- Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) approaches have shown limited success in complex robotic manipulation, struggling with sparse rewards and generalization.

## Method

- **SLAP** builds a two-level ==planning graph==, combining abstract planning for high-level structure with low-level execution details.
- It autonomously learns efficient "shortcut" policies using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for connections between abstract states.
- Learned shortcuts generalize over objects by identifying relevant objects for a policy's input and applying object substitution at evaluation time.

## Results

- Achieved plan length reductions ranging from **32%** to **73%** in four complex robotic manipulation environments compared to pure planning.
- Maintained a **100%** success rate across all challenging, long-horizon tasks, significantly outperforming pure and hierarchical reinforcement learning baselines (**0%** success in most cases).
- Showcased generalization capabilities to novel object configurations, varying object counts, unseen goals, and robustness in stochastic or partially observable environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating classical abstract planning with model-free reinforcement learning allows for the discovery of dynamic, efficient low-level behaviors (shortcuts) beyond human-defined skills.
> - Learned shortcuts, often involving multi-object interactions, enable substantial reductions in task execution time by replacing sequences of sub-optimal predefined actions.
> - Leveraging object-oriented representations from abstract planning allows learned policies to generalize to novel object configurations and counts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The problem of long-horizon decision-making in environments characterized by sparse rewards and continuous state-action spaces remains a significant challenge in artificial intelligence and robotics. Traditional approaches to this problem typically fall into two main categories: Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) and Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL).

Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) is a model-based framework that addresses complexity by hierarchically organizing actions using abstract options. These options, such as "pick," "place," or "move," are typically predefined by human engineers. While TAMP excels at long-horizon reasoning and ensuring task completion by breaking down problems into symbolic subproblems and performing continuous geometric motion planning, its reliance on manually specified skills limits an agent's behaviors to those explicitly programmed. This often results in solutions that are logically sound but operationally inefficient or unable to exploit dynamic, improvised physical interactions. For instance, a TAMP system might sequentially unstack obstacles before placing a target block, rather than dynamically clearing the obstacles with a single action.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) aims to learn hierarchical policies, often by discovering options from low-level environmental cues using methods like entropy-based objectives. These "tabula rasa" approaches attempt to learn skills from scratch. However, HRL has demonstrated limited success in the context of the long-horizon, complex robotic manipulation tasks that are the focus of this work, often struggling with sparse rewards and the need for robust generalization in continuous spaces.

SLAP positions itself by identifying a gap between these two paradigms. It acknowledges the structural advantages of TAMP's abstract planning graph and the improvisational flexibility of model-free reinforcement learning. Instead of either strictly adhering to predefined skills or learning entirely new skills from raw observations, SLAP proposes to leverage the existing high-level structure provided by TAMP's options to guide the learning of *new*, more efficient low-level skills (shortcuts). This approach aims to bridge the gap, enabling the discovery of dynamic, often multi-object interaction behaviors that are beyond the scope of traditional TAMP, while providing better generalization and higher success rates than pure or hierarchical RL in challenging robotic domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that enhances the efficiency and effectiveness of abstract planners in long-horizon, continuous-state-and-action robotic tasks. This is driven by several key motivations:

Firstly, current Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) systems, while effective for decomposing complex problems, suffer from a fundamental limitation: their reliance on a finite set of manually defined skills or "options." These options embody strong assumptions about physical interactions, such as contact occurring only through fingertips or skills influencing only pre-specified objects. This constraint limits robotic agents to behaviors that human engineers can explicitly program, often leading to "satisficing but long and inefficient" solutions. The motivation is to overcome this limitation by enabling agents to autonomously improvise and discover more dynamic and efficient low-level behaviors.

Secondly, the paper aims to reduce the execution time of planned solutions. While TAMP guarantees task completion under its defined abstractions, the resulting plans can be lengthy and suboptimal in terms of real-world execution steps. The goal is to find "shorter and dynamic solutions" that are currently beyond the capabilities of classical planners. This implicitly means discovering actions that might involve complex, multi-object interactions or unconventional physical manipulations (e.g., "slapping" objects aside).

Thirdly, the work is motivated by the practical challenge that existing Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) methods, which attempt to discover options from low-level cues, have had limited success in the domain of long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. These "tabula rasa" approaches often struggle with sparse rewards and the complexity of continuous action spaces, making them less practical for many real-world robotic applications. SLAP seeks a more practical approach by building upon the structure provided by existing skills, rather than learning from scratch.

Finally, a key objective is to provide a "plug-and-play" solution from a user's perspective. The motivation is to create a module that can autonomously learn improved execution efficiency for an abstract planner in a given domain, without requiring additional user input beyond the initial set of TAMP options. This aims to simplify the process of developing efficient robotic solutions and foster progress toward a unified system that combines the long-horizon reasoning and generalization of TAMP with the improvisational flexibility of reinforcement learning. The system is designed to gracefully degrade to pure planning if shortcuts are too difficult to learn, or simplify to pure RL if tasks are sufficiently easy for a single shortcut to solve.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Shortcut Learning for Abstract Planning (SLAP), integrates model-free reinforcement learning with abstract planning to autonomously discover and leverage efficient low-level skills (shortcuts).

The methodology begins with a **problem formulation** situated in fully-observable and deterministic environments with continuous states $x \in X$ and actions $u \in U$, governed by a known transition function $f$. Tasks are goal-based $(x_0, g)$, with the objective of minimizing trajectory length (execution time). The agent has access to an abstract state space $s \in S$ through a partitioning function $abstract(x) = s$, and an initial set of options $A$. Each option $a \in A$ is defined by an initial abstract state $s_a^{init}$, a terminal abstract state $s_a^{term}$, and a policy $\pi_a$. These options are assumed sufficient to generate solutions but may be suboptimal in terms of execution time.

#### 3.1. Planning with Abstract States

SLAP constructs and searches within a **two-level abstract planning graph** to find solutions:
- **Top Level:** Nodes represent abstract states $s$, and edges represent options $a$. This level is built using Breadth-First Search (BFS) starting from $abstract(x_0)$, applying symbolic planning operators (preconditions, add/delete effects) associated with each option to expand to new abstract states until a goal-satisfying state is reached. This process does not require simulation.
- **Bottom Level:** Nodes represent concrete environment states $x$, and edges represent environment actions $u$. This level is built by simulating option executions from the top-level graph, starting from $x_0$ and performing BFS to record actual low-level states reached. This process prunes abstract paths that are geometrically infeasible in the continuous environment.
A shortest path algorithm, specifically a path-dependent adaptation of Dijkstra’s, is then applied on the bottom level of this graph to find an execution-time-minimizing solution.

#### 3.2. Learning Shortcuts with RL

To improve the efficiency of plans, SLAP learns **shortcuts**, defined as new options $\hat{a} = \langle s_{init}, \pi_\theta, s_{term} \rangle$ where $s_{init}$ and $s_{term}$ are abstract state pairs not already covered efficiently by existing options, and $\pi_\theta$ is a learnable policy.
- **Shortcut Identification:** SLAP identifies promising shortcut connections between abstract states from the training planning graphs.
- **MDP Instantiation:** For each potential shortcut, a self-contained, indefinite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) is created. The MDP has the environment's state space $X$, action space $U$, and transition function $f$. A sparse reward function $R(x)=-1$ is used (penalizing each step), and $s_{term}$ defines the terminal states.
- **Initial State Distribution:** Initial states for shortcut learning are sampled from actual states encountered in the abstract planning graphs during training, rather than directly from the abstract state $s_{init}$.
- **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to learn the policies $\pi_\theta$ for continuous state and action spaces.
- **Pruning Mechanism:** To manage the potentially large number of $O(|S|^2)$ possible shortcuts, a simple pruning mechanism is employed. For each candidate shortcut, $N_{rollout}$ random rollouts (up to $T_{rollout}$ steps) are executed from initial states. If the terminal abstract state $s_{term}$ is not reached in at least $K_{rollout}$ of these rollouts, the shortcut is pruned and not subjected to further RL training. This is based on the heuristic that RL requires some initial success to bootstrap learning.

#### 3.3. Planning with Learned Shortcuts

During evaluation, for a new task $(x_0, g)$:
- The abstract planner constructs a planning graph, but this time it includes both the original options $A$ and the newly **learned shortcut policies $\hat{A}$**.
- Since learned policies may not be perfectly robust, SLAP checks if the abstract terminal state of a shortcut is reached within a maximum execution time $T_{eval}$ steps. If not, the shortcut edge is effectively pruned from the graph for that specific planning instance.
- The Dijkstra's algorithm then finds the shortest path, potentially incorporating these newly learned shortcuts to yield shorter execution times. This allows SLAP to automatically adapt between pure planning (if shortcuts are ineffective) and pure RL (if a single shortcut solves the task).

#### 3.4. Generalizing Over Objects

SLAP leverages the object-oriented and relational nature of TAMP to generalize learned shortcuts to tasks with varying objects:
- **Relevant Objects:** For each shortcut $\hat{a}$, the system identifies "relevant atoms" (add/delete effects) and "relevant objects" $rel(\hat{a})$ involved in these atoms.
- **State Projection:** The policy $\pi_\theta$ for a shortcut receives a projected state $proj_{\hat{a}}(x)$ as input, formed by concatenating feature vectors $\alpha(o,x)$ only for objects $o \in rel(\hat{a})$, in a fixed order. This makes policies invariant to irrelevant objects.
- **Object Substitution:** At evaluation time, when new objects are present, SLAP attempts to find a type-preserving, injective object mapping $\sigma$ between the relevant objects of a trained shortcut $\hat{a}_{train}$ and the current task objects. If such a mapping exists and preserves the add/delete effects, the learned policy $\pi_{\hat{a}_{train}}$ is applied using the substituted objects as inputs, enabling generalization to novel object configurations, counts, and even some dynamic property changes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated SLAP in four simulated robotic environments: Obstacle 2D, Obstacle Tower, Cluttered Drawer, and Cleanup Table, which feature long horizons, sparse rewards, and continuous states/actions. The environments involve diverse tasks such as clearing obstacles, retrieving objects from clutter, and organizing items.

#### 4.1. Empirical Results (Q1 & Q2: Plan Length and Sample Efficiency)

- **Reduction in Plan Length:** SLAP consistently reduced plan lengths compared to Pure Planning across all environments. Reductions ranged from 32% in Obstacle 2D to 73% in Cleanup Table, demonstrating that SLAP effectively finds shorter, more efficient execution paths by leveraging learned shortcuts.
- **Success Rate vs. RL Baselines:** SLAP achieved a 100% success rate in all environments. In contrast, pure RL methods (PPO and SAC+HER) failed to solve any of the long-horizon tasks, achieving 0% success rates, primarily due to the sparse reward problem. Hierarchical RL (PPO) and Scalable Option Learning (SOL) also struggled, achieving 0% success in the complex PyBullet environments (Obstacle Tower, Cluttered Drawer, Cleanup Table), succeeding only in Obstacle 2D. This indicates that while hierarchical structure helps, learning effective skill selection sequences for a large number of grounded skills remains challenging for these HRL methods.

#### 4.2. Training Dynamics (Q3: Improvement over Training)

- Analysis of training steps revealed a positive correlation between training time and performance. As the number of training steps increased, SLAP successfully learned and incorporated more shortcuts from the candidate set. This led to a continuous decrease in average plan lengths, as shortcut policies became more stable and effective.

#### 4.3. Generalization Capability Analysis (Q4: Generalization)

- **Generalization Over Objects:** In the Obstacle Tower environment, SLAP was trained on tasks with a stack of three obstacles and no distractors. When evaluated on tasks with varying numbers of stacked obstacles and additional scattered distractor objects, and even with doubled mass and friction, SLAP maintained short plan lengths. Pure Planning, conversely, scaled poorly with additional obstacles. The discovered multi-object RL skills (e.g., "slap") enabled this generalization by physically affecting entire towers, even when only a subset of objects was deemed "relevant" symbolically.
- **Generalization Over Goals (Appendix D.1):** SLAP demonstrated the ability to generalize to new tasks with randomly sampled goals unseen during training. It consistently found shorter plans on average compared to Pure Planning, utilizing the pre-trained shortcuts effectively within the abstract planning graph for novel goal configurations.
- **Stochastic and Partially Observable Environments (Appendix D.4):** Experiments in stochastic versions of Obstacle Tower (noisy actions, object physics, stack alignment, random dropping) showed SLAP achieving higher success rates (92% vs. 84% for Pure Planning) and shorter plans, indicating robustness to environmental noise. In a partially observable scenario where the top block was occluded, SLAP attained a 78% success rate (vs. 2% for Pure Planning), leveraging multi-object shortcuts like "slap" that do not require full knowledge of all interacting objects.
- **Out-of-Distribution Physical Configurations (Appendix D.5):** SLAP trained on deterministic physics demonstrated robustness to heavily perturbed physics (e.g., increased noise in actions, object size, mass, friction). While performance was improved further when SLAP was trained directly on perturbed physics, the initial robustness highlights the adaptability of the learned shortcuts.

#### 4.4. Shortcut Policy Learning Analysis (Q5: RL Design Decisions)

- **Independent vs. Universal Policies:** Comparing different schemes for learning shortcut policies, independent training (each shortcut has its own policy, as in default SLAP) consistently outperformed universal policy learning schemes (Abstract Subgoals, Abstract HER), which attempt to train a single shared policy across all shortcuts. This suggests that the varying difficulty of different shortcuts may make shared representation learning less effective, as universal policies might allocate resources to learning infeasible shortcuts.
- **Choice of RL Algorithm (Appendix B.2):** Ablations showed that while PPO slightly outperformed SAC and SAC+HER as the backbone for shortcut learning, all three algorithms eventually converged to similar results in terms of execution-time efficiency and overall plan lengths. This supports the claim that the SLAP framework is robust to the specific choice of continuous-state/action RL algorithm.

#### 4.5. Compute Footprint and Time Efficiency (Appendix D.2)

- The compute footprint for shortcut learning varied by environment (e.g., Obstacle Tower involved 92 shortcuts, 4.8 x 10^7 environment interactions, and ~9 hours of RL training).
- At test time, SLAP generally incurred slightly higher planning time than Pure Planning in some environments (e.g., Cluttered Drawer, Cleanup Table), as Pure Planning used heuristics for acceleration. However, the substantial reduction in execution steps (up to 73%) meant that SLAP was preferable for time efficiency under realistic per-step execution times (e.g., a lower bound of 0.5-1.2 seconds per step for the PyBullet environments).

#### 4.6. Suboptimal Abstractions (Appendix D.3)

- Experiments with noisy grounding functions for predicates (mimicking perception errors) in Cluttered Drawer showed that SLAP could improve success rates over Pure Planning when abstractions were moderately noisy. The learned RL shortcuts could connect noisy abstract states to goal-leading states, effectively compensating for misclassified predicates. However, if the abstractions became excessively noisy (e.g., thresholds sampled from [w, 2w]), the abstract planning graph lost its useful structure, leading to a significant degradation in SLAP's performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Shortcut Learning for Abstract Planning (SLAP) represents a notable contribution to the field of AI and robotics, particularly in addressing the challenges of long-horizon decision-making in complex, continuous environments.

The primary significance of SLAP lies in its ability to **autonomously discover new, more efficient low-level skills (shortcuts)** that go beyond the limitations of manually programmed options in Task and Motion Planning (TAMP). By leveraging the existing abstract planning graph induced by human-defined skills, SLAP enables the learning of dynamic, often multi-object interaction behaviors—such as "slapping" obstacle towers or "wiggling" through clutter—that are difficult or impossible to specify with traditional symbolic operators. This automatic skill discovery has the potential to significantly expand the behavioral repertoire of robotic systems.

The empirical results demonstrate that SLAP leads to **substantially shorter and more efficient execution plans** compared to pure TAMP, with plan length reductions exceeding 50% in several complex robotic manipulation tasks. Simultaneously, SLAP achieves consistently high success rates, outperforming both pure and hierarchical reinforcement learning methods that struggle with sparse rewards and the complexity of these long-horizon tasks. This dual advantage of efficiency and robustness makes SLAP a practical approach for deployment in challenging robotic applications.

Furthermore, SLAP exhibits **strong generalization capabilities**. It can adapt to tasks with varying numbers of objects, novel goal configurations, and demonstrates robustness to environmental stochasticity, partial observability, and out-of-distribution physical properties of objects. This generalization is achieved by leveraging the relational inductive bias inherent in TAMP, where learned policies operate on projected states of "relevant objects" and can be re-applied via object substitution. This suggests a pathway for robotic systems to operate effectively in diverse and changing environments without extensive retraining.

By strategically combining the strengths of classical planning (long-horizon reasoning, task decomposition, generalization over high-level structure) with modern model-free reinforcement learning (improvisational flexibility, fine-grained control, learning dynamic behaviors), SLAP makes **progress toward a unified system**. This integration offers a promising direction for creating intelligent agents that can both reason abstractly about complex tasks and execute them efficiently and robustly in the physical world.

Potential impacts include accelerating the development of autonomous robotic systems for intricate assembly, logistics, and household tasks, where both high-level planning and adaptive low-level manipulation are critical. The "plug-and-play" nature of SLAP suggests it could be easily integrated into existing TAMP frameworks to enhance their performance without requiring extensive manual re-programming of skills.

While the work acknowledges limitations such as reliance on user-provided options and a simplified planner, it outlines clear paths for future research, including exploring more advanced hierarchical RL for problem decomposition, integrating sophisticated TAMP techniques, incorporating safety constraints, and developing methods to learn from real-world data without direct simulator access.
