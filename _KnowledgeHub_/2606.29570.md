# Hierarchical Policy Learning via Spectral Decomposition

> [!summary] Summary
> The Causal Spectral Policy (CSP) framework, developed by researchers from Georgia Institute of Technology and Harvard University, decomposes robot actions into low-frequency (coarse) and high-frequency (fine) components using the Discrete Cosine Transform. This approach models action generation as a causal coarse-to-fine process, improving precision and robustness to noisy human demonstrations in robot manipulation tasks, achieving up to 91.9% success on LIBERO-90 and outperforming baselines on real-robot tasks like thin block stacking with 90% success.

## Problem

- Traditional time-domain action representations in imitation learning struggle with complex, long-horizon tasks requiring both broad planning and precise execution.
- Existing methods often treat all temporal components of robot actions uniformly, failing to explicitly capture their inherent hierarchical structure.
- Robot manipulation policies trained via imitation learning are highly susceptible to errors and performance degradation from noisy human demonstrations, particularly in precision-critical stages.

## Method

- The **Causal Spectral Policy (CSP)** framework represents action sequences in the ==temporal frequency domain== using the ==Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)==.
- It partitions spectral coefficients into ==low-frequency== (coarse, global motion) and ==high-frequency== (fine, reactive control) components.
- A ==two-stage causal coarse-to-fine prediction== process generates coarse components first from observations and language, then generates fine components conditioned on the predicted coarse trajectory and current observation (enforced with a ==stop-gradient==).

## Results

- CSP consistently matched or outperformed strong baselines on LIBERO (e.g., **91.9%** success on LIBERO-90) and MimicGen benchmarks, and showed robustness to increased action chunk sizes where other policies degraded.
- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of both spectral representation and the explicit hierarchical, causal decomposition for performance and robustness, especially under noisy supervision.
- Real-robot experiments on precision-critical tasks (e.g., keyboard pressing, thin block stacking) validated CSP's higher execution accuracy and success rates (e.g., **90%** for thin block stacking) compared to baselines, even with noisy demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot manipulation actions inherently exhibit a hierarchical temporal structure, where coarse planning and fine-grained execution play distinct causal roles.
> - In the spectral domain, low-frequency components predominantly capture global motion and task-level intent, while high-frequency components encode execution-level refinements like precise timing and contact behaviors.
> - High-frequency action components are crucial for achieving precision in manipulation, even though they contribute minimally to the overall motion energy of a trajectory.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation learning, particularly imitation learning, frequently encounters challenges related to handling complex, long-horizon tasks that require both broad movement planning and precise, fine-grained execution. Traditional approaches to action representation in imitation learning predominantly operate in the time domain. These methods often predict actions autoregressively, generating one action at a time, or predict fixed-length action chunks. Examples include works utilizing autoregressive policies (e.g., Zhao et al., 2023; Haldar et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024) or diffusion- and flow-based approaches for continuous action chunks (e.g., Chi et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2025). A common limitation of these time-domain paradigms is their uniform treatment of all temporal components, which can obscure the hierarchical nature of robot actions. This makes them susceptible to issues in tasks demanding high precision, timing, or reactive control, and can lead to sensitivity to temporal resolution.

Recently, frequency-domain representations, such as those utilizing the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), have gained traction in other machine learning domains like image generation (e.g., Yu et al., 2025; Ning et al., 2025) and audio processing (e.g., Kong et al., 2021). In robotics, several methods have begun exploring frequency-based action representations for improved efficiency and robustness (e.g., Quest (Mete et al., 2024), FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025), FreqPolicy (Yu et al., 2025)). However, these existing frequency-based methods primarily focus on optimizing representation efficiency or enabling iterative refinement without explicitly modeling the causal relationship between coarse motion planning and fine-grained execution.

The concept of coarse-to-fine decomposition has also been explored in prior work (e.g., Johns, 2021; Cui et al., 2025), often by progressively refining actions over different temporal resolutions. While this offers some benefits in terms of controllability, these approaches typically remain anchored in the time domain or do not fully leverage the distinct characteristics of different frequency components.

This work positions itself within this landscape by identifying a semantic decomposition within robot action sequences in the spectral domain. It aims to bridge the gap between efficient frequency-based representations and the explicit modeling of the causal, hierarchical structure of robot actions. Furthermore, it addresses the pervasive issue of noisy human demonstrations in imitation learning, drawing parallels with neuroscience research on human movement variability and corrective motions. By proposing a framework that specifically accounts for the distinct roles of coarse and fine action components, the paper seeks to enhance robustness and precision in robot manipulation, particularly under realistic, imperfect data conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from an observation that robot manipulation actions inherently exhibit a hierarchical temporal structure. Specifically, these actions comprise both long-horizon trajectory planning, which dictates the overall path and intention, and fine-grained reactive control, which governs precise timing, alignment, and contact interactions. Prior methods, by representing actions uniformly in the time domain, often treat all temporal components as equally important. This uniform treatment can hinder their ability to explicitly capture and leverage this inherent hierarchical structure, leading to suboptimal performance in tasks requiring high speed or precision.

To substantiate this intuition, the authors conducted an empirical analysis by converting robot action sequences into the spectral domain using the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT). Through this analysis, they observed a functional separation: low-frequency components predominantly capture global motion trajectories and coarse task-level intent, while high-frequency components encode execution-level refinements such as precise timing, alignment, and contact behaviors. Controlled interventions, where high-frequency components were selectively removed and trajectories reconstructed, further supported this. Experiments showed that removing high-frequency components largely preserved coarse motion but systematically degraded fine-grained accuracy, leading to errors that accumulated and caused failures in precision-critical stages. This effect was amplified in real-robot teleoperation demonstrations, where factors like latency and execution variability disproportionately affect short-timescale corrections.

Motivated by these observations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **To identify and characterize a fundamental asymmetry in robot action learning**: This involves demonstrating that coarse and fine action components play distinct causal roles in task execution, particularly in the spectral domain.
2. **To demonstrate the critical role of high-frequency actions**: Through controlled frequency-domain interventions, the objective is to show that high-frequency action components are essential for achieving precision in manipulation, even though they may contribute minimally to the overall motion energy.
3. **To propose and validate a novel policy learning framework**: The aim is to develop a "Causal Spectral Policy" (CSP) that explicitly decomposes robot actions into low-frequency (coarse) and high-frequency (fine) components. This framework models action generation as a causal coarse-to-fine process, where coarse motion is predicted first from observation and language, and fine corrections are subsequently generated conditionally on the realized coarse trajectory. The objective is to validate that this structured approach improves reliability and execution accuracy, especially on precision- and speed-sensitive tasks, and enhances robustness to noisy demonstrations in both simulated and real-robot settings.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to develop a more robust and effective approach to learning complex robot manipulation skills from demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper, named Causal Spectral Policy (CSP), leverages a hierarchical decomposition of robot actions in the temporal frequency domain.

**Problem Statement and Notation:**
The problem is framed within the context of imitation learning for a Markov Decision Process (MDP). A policy $\pi(a | o, l)$ is learned from a dataset of expert demonstration trajectories, where $o_t$ is the observation, $l$ is the language instruction, and $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a continuous action.

**Temporal Spectral Representation:**
The core of the approach involves representing discrete time series action sequences in the temporal frequency domain using the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT). For an action sequence $x = (x_0, \ldots, x_{K-1})$, the DCT produces spectral coefficients $\omega = Fx$, where $F$ is an orthonormal DCT basis. The inverse transform $x = F^{-1}\omega$ reconstructs the original sequence exactly. This transform is applied independently to each dimension of an action sequence, providing an invertible reparameterization that explicitly separates temporal structures at different scales.

**Frequency-Domain Intervention via Coefficient Truncation:**
To empirically investigate the functional roles of different frequency components, the authors perform controlled interventions. An action trajectory is divided into fixed-length chunks of length $K$. The DCT is applied to each action dimension to obtain spectral coefficients $c$. High-frequency components are then truncated by zeroing out coefficients beyond a specified cutoff $\lambda$. The modified coefficients $\tilde{c}$ are inversely transformed back to the time domain ($\tilde{a} = F^{-1}\tilde{c}$) and replayed in the environment. This procedure isolates the causal effect of specific frequency bands on task execution by varying the number of retained low-frequency components while keeping observations fixed. This was demonstrated effectively on a real-robot dart insertion task.

**Decomposition Across Temporal Scales:**
Motivated by the finding that low-frequency components govern coarse motion and high-frequency components handle fine refinements, the paper proposes modeling action generation as a coarse-to-fine process. This involves factorizing the action chunk distribution as $p(a_{t:t+t'} | o_t, l) = p(a_{\text{coarse}} | o_t, l) p(a_{\text{fine}} | o_t, a_{\text{coarse}})$. This causal structure assumes that coarse motion context constrains subsequent fine-grained behaviors, contrasting with standard time-domain policies that predict actions without explicitly modeling this hierarchy.

**Action Representation in the Spectral Domain:**
The DCT is chosen because it inherently aligns with this coarse-to-fine structure. Low-frequency coefficients correspond to slowly varying motions over longer horizons, while high-frequency coefficients capture rapid, short-timescale variations. This spectral ordering enables a principled separation of global motion planning from local execution refinement. Since the DCT is orthonormal, Euclidean distances are preserved, meaning the spectral representation is an exact reparameterization of the original action sequence.

**Causal Spectral Policy (CSP) Architecture:**
CSP formally implements this causal temporal decomposition in the spectral domain. Given an action chunk $a_{t:t+t'}$ and its spectral coefficients $c = F a_{t:t+t'}$, the coefficients are partitioned into $c_{\text{low}}$ (coarse) and $c_{\text{high}}$ (fine) based on a cutoff parameter $\lambda$. The policy models their generation with a causal dependency:
$p(c | o_t, l) = p_{\text{low}}(c_{\text{low}} | o_t, l) p_{\text{high}}(c_{\text{high}} | o_t, c_{\text{low}})$.
This factorization is implemented with two separate predictors:
1. A "low-frequency" predictor ($p_{\text{low}}$) generates the coarse components $c_{\text{low}}$ based on the current observation $o_t$ and language instruction $l$. This is typically a GPT-style transformer.
2. A "high-frequency" predictor ($p_{\text{high}}$) generates the fine components $c_{\text{high}}$ conditioned on the current observation $o_t$ and the predicted coarse components $c_{\text{low}}$.

**Training:**
The training objective minimizes a weighted sum of squared errors for both low and high-frequency coefficients: $L = \|\hat{c}_{\text{low}} - c_{\text{low}}\|_2^2 + \|\hat{c}_{\text{high}} - c_{\text{high}}\|_2^2$. A stop-gradient operator is applied to $\hat{c}_{\text{low}}$ when training the high-frequency predictor, enforcing the causal dependency and preventing fine-scale supervision from influencing coarse motion learning.

**Frequency Split Selection:**
The cutoff parameter $\lambda$ (or $K_{split}$) for separating low and high frequencies is determined using a data-driven heuristic. This involves computing the empirical mean power spectrum of demonstrated action chunks, averaging squared coefficients across demonstrations and action dimensions. The smallest index whose cumulative energy exceeds a threshold (e.g., 90%) is used as an energy-based cutoff ($K_{\text{energy}}$). Additionally, a "spectral elbow" $K_{\text{elbow}}$ is detected by finding the point of maximum negative curvature in the log-power spectrum. The final split index $K_{\text{split}}$ is taken as the maximum of these two values, ensuring robust selection.

**Human-Inspired Teleoperation Noise Injection:**
To evaluate robustness under realistic conditions, the paper proposes a structured noise model for data augmentation. This noise model approximates human teleoperation behavior, incorporating:
- **Signal-dependent motor noise**: Variance of motor commands increases with command magnitude.
- **Temporally correlated execution drift**: Noise is smooth and correlated over time, leading to low-frequency drift.
- **Intermittent corrective submovements**: Brief, high-frequency corrections are introduced, especially during precision-critical phases.
- **Task-phase modulation and safety**: Noise magnitude is modulated based on task progress (e.g., decaying near completion) and can be disabled at critical events.
This noise primarily perturbs high-frequency components, leaving low-frequency, task-level motion largely intact. This allows for controlled studies on how policies degrade under noisy training data and whether CSP better preserves task-critical behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings across simulation and real-world experiments that support the proposed hierarchical policy learning approach.

**Frequency-Domain Intervention:**
Through controlled frequency-domain interventions on a real-robot dart insertion task, the authors empirically demonstrated that low-frequency components of action trajectories primarily govern global reaching motions towards the target. In contrast, high-frequency components were found to be critical for precise alignment and successful contact, such as hitting the dartboard center. As high-frequency components were progressively removed from a successful demonstration, the robot's coarse approach remained intact, but its ability to achieve precise insertion degraded, leading to off-center hits or failure to make contact (Figure 2). This confirms the distinct functional roles of different frequency bands.

**Ideal Simulation Experiments:**
- **Performance on Benchmarks**: Experiments on LIBERO (long-horizon, language-conditioned) and MimicGen (precision-sensitive) benchmarks showed that CSP consistently matched or outperformed strong baselines. With an action chunk size of 16, CSP achieved 91.9% and 80.8% success rates on LIBERO-90 and LIBERO-10, respectively, and a mean success rate of 65.5% on MimicGen-style tasks (Table 1).
- **Robustness to Chunk Size**: Unlike standard chunk-based and autoregressive policies that degraded significantly with increased temporal horizon (e.g., chunk size 64), CSP maintained strong performance (87.6% / 68.8% on LIBERO-90 / LIBERO-10 and 50.0% mean on MimicGen at chunk size 64). This indicated its ability to preserve performance while increasing look-ahead during prediction.
- **Ablation Studies**: Ablations confirmed the importance of both spectral representation and hierarchical decomposition. Removing the hierarchical structure or the causal factorization resulted in performance drops, particularly under noisy supervision (Table 2). Pure temporal abstraction (Action Binning) provided only moderate gains and deteriorated sharply at longer horizons, suggesting its insufficiency without frequency-domain structure. Frequency-Autoregressive methods, while benefiting from spectral representation, performed below CSP without explicit coarse-to-fine conditioning.
- **Sensitivity to Frequency Split**: The policy's performance remained stable across a range of low-frequency cutoffs (approximately 20%–40% of coefficients), indicating low sensitivity to the exact partition between coarse and fine components (Figure 5).

**Counterfactual Interventions:**
Analysis of counterfactual interventions on CSP's conditioning structure revealed an asymmetric, causal dependency.
- Removing language conditioning from the low-frequency predictor caused a substantial performance drop, indicating that task-level intent is primarily encoded in coarse motion.
- Removing the low-frequency conditioning from the high-frequency predictor also degraded performance, demonstrating that fine corrections are dependent on the realized coarse trajectory.
- Reversing the prediction order (generating high-frequency components before low-frequency components) resulted in significantly worse performance than the proposed low-to-high ordering (Table 3). These results provide evidence that the causal ordering in CSP is an empirically beneficial structural bias.

**Real Robot Evaluation:**
Real-robot experiments on precision-critical tasks (keyboard pressing, block stacking, lid closing) further validated CSP's effectiveness. CSP consistently achieved higher execution accuracy and success rates compared to baseline methods. For instance, it attained 80% success on "Press C" and 100% on "Press Enter," outperforming all baselines. On challenging tasks like thin block stacking (where teleoperation success was low), CSP achieved a 90% success rate, whereas baselines struggled with misalignment (Table 4, Figure 6). This indicated its ability to handle noisy demonstrations and generate more reliable contact and alignment in real-world scenarios.

**Simulated Human Noise Injection:**
To assess robustness under realistic supervision, human-inspired teleoperation noise (signal-dependent, temporally correlated drift, intermittent corrective bursts) was injected into simulated demonstrations. CSP consistently achieved higher success rates and demonstrated significantly less performance degradation under both moderate and high noise conditions compared to all baselines (Figure 8, Table 2). This robust performance was attributed to CSP's ability to isolate task-level motion (low-frequency) from noisy fine-grained execution (high-frequency), supporting the hypothesis that its structured decomposition improves robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a structured perspective on robot action generation by identifying and formalizing a semantic decomposition within robot action sequences. The key contribution is the observation that task-level motion intent and execution-level refinements, which are distinct, naturally emerge as low-frequency and high-frequency components, respectively, in the spectral domain. This identification of a fundamental asymmetry in robot action learning provides a theoretical basis for more effective policy design.

The proposed Causal Spectral Policy (CSP) introduces a methodological advancement by modeling action generation as a causal coarse-to-fine process in the spectral domain. By first predicting coarse motion from observations and language, and then generating fine corrections conditionally on the realized coarse trajectory, CSP explicitly captures and leverages the inherent hierarchical structure of manipulation tasks. This approach enables policies to learn stable task-level behaviors before predicting granular adjustments, leading to several practical benefits.

The potential impacts of this work are substantial, particularly for advancing the capabilities of robotic manipulation systems:
- **Improved Precision and Reliability**: CSP consistently demonstrated superior performance on precision-sensitive manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world settings. This can lead to robots performing complex, contact-rich operations with greater accuracy and consistency, which is critical for applications in manufacturing, assembly, and delicate handling.
- **Enhanced Robustness to Noisy Demonstrations**: A significant outcome is CSP's strong robustness to human-inspired teleoperation noise. This is crucial for imitation learning, as real-world human demonstrations often contain variability, latency, and intermittent corrective motions. By effectively filtering or accounting for such noise, CSP reduces the dependence on idealized, perfectly clean datasets, thereby making robot learning more practical and scalable from imperfect human supervision.
- **Broader Applicability**: The increased reliability and robustness can extend the applicability of imitation learning to a wider range of complex tasks and environments where precision and adaptability to real-world variability are paramount. This could accelerate the deployment of autonomous systems in diverse domains, from service robotics to assistive technologies.
- **Scalability of Robot Learning Systems**: By enabling more stable learning from imperfect data, the approach contributes to the scalability of robot learning systems, potentially requiring fewer, less curated demonstrations to achieve proficient performance.

The paper acknowledges standard ethical considerations for autonomous robotic systems, emphasizing that responsible deployment requires appropriate safety validation, human oversight, and domain-specific risk assessment, and does not identify risks specific to this method beyond those common to learning-based control approaches. Overall, this work offers a valuable contribution towards building more capable and robust robot manipulation policies by providing a principled way to decompose and learn the hierarchical structure of actions.
