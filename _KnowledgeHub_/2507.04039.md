# Generalized Locomotion in Out-of-distribution Conditions with Robust Transformer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed the Robust Locomotion Transformer (ROLT), a neural network architecture designed for generalized robot locomotion in out-of-distribution dynamic and perceptual conditions. ROLT demonstrates superior performance against baselines in handling unseen limb damage and diverse sensor noise in simulation, along with successful zero-shot transfer to a physical robot across various novel failure scenarios.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with 'distribution shift' in real-world environments, encountering dynamic gaps (unforeseen physical changes like limb damage) and perceptual gaps (novel or corrupted sensor data).
- Existing generalization methods like domain randomization are limited by the inability to simulate all possible real-world scenarios and cannot guarantee robustness to truly novel out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions.
- Adaptive control mechanisms add system complexity and may still fail with completely novel scenarios if their underlying models were not exposed to sufficiently diverse data during training.

## Method

- Proposed the **ROLT** (==Robust Locomotion Transformer==), a ==multi-modal transformer== architecture focused on network design to enhance generalization and robustness to OOD conditions.
- Introduced ==Body Tokenization== with an ==action token== to provide data-dependent position information, enabling knowledge transfer across different robot limbs for fault tolerance.
- Implemented a ==Consistent Dropout== strategy, applying the same dropout mask during rollout and update phases for both features and patch position embeddings (PEs) to improve resilience to unseen perceptual noise.

## Results

- ROLT consistently outperformed baseline methods in simulated OOD dynamic conditions, demonstrating strong generalization to unseen limb damage patterns on both quadrupedal and hexapodal robots.
- The proposed ROLT architecture exhibited superior robustness to unseen low-frequency and high-frequency perceptual noise in elevation maps, maintaining stable performance as noise levels increased.
- Achieved successful **zero-shot transfer** to a physical Unitree Go1 robot, significantly outperforming a strong baseline (**RMA**) in challenging real-world OOD scenarios such as weakened limbs, dragging a box, and navigating on rolling skates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit network architecture design can inherently enhance generalization and robustness to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, moving beyond reliance on extensive domain randomization or explicit adaptation mechanisms.
> - Utilizing data-dependent position information in transformer architectures, rather than fixed position embeddings, is crucial for facilitating knowledge transfer across similar robot modules (e.g., legs) and preventing overfitting to specific spatial positions.
> - A consistent dropout strategy, particularly for patch position embeddings, encourages the policy to infer reliable spatial information from the interaction of proprioception and exteroception, leading to robustness against diverse unseen perceptual noise patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The reliable deployment of robots in real-world environments necessitates controllers that can perform effectively despite encountering situations different from those experienced during training. This disparity, termed "distribution shift," is a significant challenge in robotics, particularly for legged systems. The paper specifically addresses two main categories of such shifts: dynamic gaps, which involve unforeseen changes in the robot's physical properties or environment interactions (e.g., limb damage, varied surfaces), and perceptual gaps, referring to novel or corrupted sensor data (e.g., sensor noise, occlusions).

Previous research has largely approached this problem through two main avenues. One common strategy involves **domain randomization**, where policies are trained across a wide range of simulated dynamic parameters or observation noises to enhance their generalization capabilities. These methods often require additional adaptation modules or distilling stages to infer dynamic parameters or ground truth observations in real-time, sometimes combining reinforcement learning with techniques like VAEs or contrastive learning. Examples include methods like Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) and related work focusing on perceptive locomotion. While effective in some scenarios, this approach is limited by the practical impossibility of simulating every potential real-world situation a robot might encounter.

Another line of research, exemplified by paradigms like "single-life reinforcement learning," focuses on enabling robots to adapt to new instances within a single episode or through limited fine-tuning. Approaches such as those involving finetuning locomotion policies in out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions or utilizing prior behaviors (e.g., ROAM) aim to achieve robustness through specific training or adaptation procedures.

This paper positions itself as distinct from these prior works by primarily focusing on **network structure design** to enhance generalization and robustness, rather than relying solely on elaborate training paradigms or real-time adaptation mechanisms. It builds upon the increasing application of transformer architectures in robot locomotion, which have demonstrated strong sequence modeling capabilities. While transformers have been used for tasks such as inferring motor actions from observation sequences, planning from depth images, and controlling bipedal or quadrupedal robots, this work explicitly aims to leverage the transformer's inherent potential for generalization and robustness, drawing inspiration from its success in computer vision, natural language processing, and other reinforcement learning applications.

Furthermore, the paper engages with the concept of **dropout** as a technique to prevent overfitting and enhance model robustness. It acknowledges existing dropout methods in supervised learning and their adaptation to reinforcement learning, such as "consistent dropout" and "Roll-drop," which stabilize dropout operations in RL. This work proposes a tailored dropout strategy within the transformer architecture, aiming to inject randomness and improve resilience to unseen perceptual noise beyond the capabilities of previous dropout applications in RL. By exploring architectural modifications, the research seeks to contribute a fundamental understanding of how network design can inherently lead to more trustworthy and adaptable robot controllers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a locomotion controller for legged robots that exhibits robust and generalized performance in out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, specifically concerning unseen dynamic variations and perceptual noise. The authors aim to achieve this by focusing on the design of the neural network architecture itself, rather than solely on training paradigms or explicit adaptation mechanisms.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in deploying autonomous robots in real-world environments:

- **Distribution Shift in Real-World Deployment:** Robots inevitably encounter scenarios that differ from their training data. For legged robots, these "distribution shifts" manifest as challenging dynamic gaps (e.g., unexpected limb damage, changes in surface friction, varied payloads) and perceptual gaps (e.g., sensor failures, environmental clutter, unforeseen noise patterns in observation data). Current simulation-to-real transfer methods often struggle with these fundamental differences.
- **Limitations of Existing Generalization Methods:**
 - **Domain Randomization (DR):** While DR trains policies across a range of dynamic parameters or observation noises, it is inherently limited by the human effort and computational cost required to simulate *all* possible challenging situations a robot might encounter. It cannot guarantee robustness to truly novel and unseen OOD conditions that fall outside the randomized distribution.
 - **Adaptation Mechanisms:** Methods like RMA or ROAM, which involve inferring dynamic parameters or selecting suitable behaviors, often add complexity to the system and might still struggle with completely novel scenarios if their underlying adaptation models were not exposed to sufficiently diverse data.
- **Desire for Intrinsic Robustness:** The authors posit that a controller capable of "truly understanding the underlying nature of locomotion problem" would inherently perform well in novel scenarios. This implies a network design that fosters deeper, transferable insights into locomotion principles, rather than merely memorizing responses to trained variations or relying on external adaptation modules.
- **Leveraging Transformer Capabilities:** Transformers have demonstrated significant potential for generalization and robustness in other domains (e.g., vision, language processing). The research is motivated by the hypothesis that these capabilities can be effectively harnessed for robot locomotion through specific architectural innovations.
- **Addressing Specific OOD Scenarios:** The paper highlights practical scenarios such as a search-and-rescue robot damaging a leg or sensors producing noise. It aims to develop a controller that can continue to function and complete its task even under such severe, unforeseen impairments.
- **Efficiency in Training:** By developing a robust and generalized controller from an architectural perspective, the goal is to reduce the need for extensive, scenario-specific training and adaptation procedures, potentially enabling robust performance even when trained in a limited set of dynamic settings and with clean observations.

In essence, the key objective is to build a "trustworthy robot controller" that can robustly generalize to a wide variety of previously unseen dynamic and perceptual challenges, with the motivation that architectural innovation can provide a more fundamental and efficient path to achieving this than solely through comprehensive training data or adaptive algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named RObust Locomotion Transformer (ROLT), formulates robot locomotion as a reinforcement learning problem and addresses OOD generalization through a novel transformer architecture.

**Reinforcement Learning Framework:**
- **State Space:** The robot's state is composed of two parts:
 - **Proprioceptive state ($s_p$):** Includes the robot's body linear and angular velocities, gravity projection, velocity command, and the position, velocity, and previous action of each joint.
 - **External perception ($s_e$):** Consists of height values from scandots uniformly sampled around the robot, forming an elevation map.
- **Action Space:** The actions are target joint positions, which are then fed into a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller.
- **Reward Function:** The training utilizes a comprehensive reward function (Table I) that includes terms for:
 - XY and yaw velocity tracking.
 - Penalties for z-velocity, roll-pitch velocity, collision, joint torques, action rate, and joint error.
 - Positive rewards for foot airtime and new contacts.

**ROLT Architecture Overview (Figure 2):**
ROLT is a multi-modal transformer designed to dynamically fuse proprioceptive and exteroceptive information. It consists of three key components: body tokenization, a transformer encoder, and a consistent dropout strategy.

**A. Body Tokenization:**
This design aims to facilitate knowledge transfer across different robot limbs and provide data-dependent position information.
- **Limb-based Tokenization:** The robot is viewed as a collection of limb modules. Each leg is treated as a separate token, allowing for shared dynamic and control knowledge across similar leg structures. For example, experience gained from a damaged leg can theoretically transfer to other legs.
- **Addressing Position Embedding Limitations:** Traditional position embeddings (PEs), which are often data-independent and learned, can hinder knowledge transfer by causing leg tokens to overfit specific spatial positions. This makes different limb controllers heterogeneous.
- **Action Token for Data-Dependent Position:** To overcome this, ROLT introduces an "action token." Instead of directly adding PEs to leg tokens, an action token (a linear embedding of the previous step's actions) is concatenated with the leg tokens. Leg modules then query their position information indirectly through interaction with this action token via the attention mechanism. This provides position information in a data-dependent manner, reducing overfitting to fixed PEs and enhancing knowledge transfer.
- **Implementation:** Each leg token ($e_l_i$) is derived from its past 10 steps of observation history (joint positions, velocities, previous actions, velocity command, base IMU) projected through a two-layer MLP. The last target angles ($a_{t-1}$) are projected into the action token ($e_a$) via a linear layer. The proprioception token embedding ($e_{prop}$) is the concatenation of all leg tokens and the action token: $E_{prop} = [e_{l0}..., e_{lL-1}, e_a]$.

**B. Transformer Encoder:**
This component is responsible for processing and fusing the tokenized information.
- **Exteroception Patchification:** The elevation map ($S_h \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$) is reshaped into a sequence of flattened 2D patches ($s_h$). These patches are then fed into a linear layer to obtain latents. Learnable patch position embeddings ($p_e$) are added to these latents to form patch embeddings ($e_h$).
- **Token Concatenation:** The proprioception token embedding ($E_{prop}$) and the patch embedding ($e_h$) are concatenated to form the complete token embedding ($E_0 = [E_{prop}, e_h]$).
- **Multi-modal Fusion:** Two stacked transformer encoder layers process $E_0$. The attention mechanism allows flexible and robust fusion between tokens from different modalities (proprioception and exteroception).
- **Action Decoding:** After token embeddings are updated by the transformer encoder, a linear layer decodes the tokens corresponding to the leg modules to obtain the joint target angles ($a_t$). These are then flattened and fed to the PD controller.
- **Lightweight Design:** The transformer encoder is designed to be lightweight with 2 layers and a hidden dimension of 160 to reduce computational cost.

**C. Consistent Dropout:**
This strategy is designed to enhance robustness to various unseen noise patterns by injecting randomness consistently.
- **Consistent Application:** Unlike some previous dropout methods, ROLT applies the same dropout mask during both the rollout phase (when collecting experience) and the update phase (when training the policy). This consistency stabilizes training and allows for the use of significantly higher dropout rates without destabilizing policy updates.
- **Feature Dropout:** Neurons in the neural network are randomly suppressed by setting their activations to zero with a dropout rate $p=0.1$. This is applied after both the self-attention mechanism and the feed-forward network. This domain-agnostic randomness forces the policy to be robust enough to make decisions using different subsets of neurons, improving robustness to diverse unseen noise.
- **PE Dropout:** Patch position embedding ($p_e$) is dropped at a high rate ($0.75$). During training, a single learnable vector replaces all dropped position embeddings. During testing, all position embeddings are applied. The rationale is that exteroception can be highly noisy, and directly relying on spatial information from PEs might be misleading. By dropping PEs, the robot is encouraged to infer spatial information of patches more reliably through the interaction of its proprioception and exteroception via self-attention, reducing overfitting to potentially corrupted spatial data.

**Training Details:**
The network is trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. Experiments involve 4096 parallel agents in the IsaacGym simulator, with policies trained for 400 million simulated time steps on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ROLT's performance against several baselines (MLP, Roll-Drop, RMA, ROAM) and ablations (Module PE, No PE Drop, No Feature Drop) through extensive experiments on quadrupedal (Unitree A1) and hexapodal robots in simulation, and on a physical Unitree Go1 robot.

**A. Generalization in Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Dynamics:**

1. **Stiffness Test (Unitree A1):**
 - **Scenario:** Trained in healthy conditions and with the front-right leg limping (calf joint locked). Tested with two *different* unseen legs limping (front-left and hind-right).
 - **ROLT Results:** Demonstrated strong generalization, maintaining walking ability even when new legs were limping, performing significantly better in terms of distance walked and fall time compared to other methods. This was interpreted as the policy successfully transferring "fault-tolerant experience" from one leg to others.
 - **Baseline Performance:** RMA quickly fell in two out of three failure cases, attributed to its inability to capture the underlying problem structure for cross-limb knowledge transfer. ROAM failed to walk in all test failures, likely due to limited generalization of its independently trained behavior modules and inability to query a suitable policy.
 - **Ablation (Module PE):** Performed poorly, similar to RMA. This suggests that traditional data-independent position embeddings caused the transformer to overfit individual leg PEs, hindering knowledge sharing and confirming the importance of data-dependent position information via the action token.

2. **Damage Test (Hexapodal Robot):**
 - **Scenario:** Trained with random motor disabilities on a few specific joints (front-right thigh, mid-right thigh, mid-left calf). Tested on *all other* calf and thigh joints, which were entirely unseen during training.
 - **ROLT Results:** Showed competence in generalizing to a wide range of previously unseen leg joint failures, effectively utilizing the hexapod's redundancy for fault-tolerant operation. ROLT consistently outperformed all other methods in both distance walked and time to first fall.
 - **Baseline Performance:** All other methods exhibited limited generalization, failing to maintain stable locomotion under novel damage conditions.
 - **Gait Visualization:** ROLT learned "unstructured gaits" that adapted the phase and frequency of each leg to walk safely despite damage, suggesting a deeper understanding of leg motions. In contrast, other methods often converged to a regular tripod gait in healthy conditions but exhibited unstable, "wired" gaits when faced with new damage.

**B. Robustness to Unseen Noise:**

1. **Low-Frequency Noise (Elevation Map):**
 - **Scenario:** Trained A1 on multiple terrains with clean elevation observations. Tested on stairs by offsetting a certain number of elevation map patches as a whole in random directions.
 - **ROLT Results:** Maintained the best performance as the offset scale increased. The attention mechanism was effective in handling abnormal patches by selectively querying trustworthy proprioception, demonstrating flexibility.
 - **Baseline Performance:** Roll-Drop, despite using dropout, significantly decreased in performance with increasing offset, indicating its dropout strategy was less effective against low-frequency noise.

2. **High-Frequency Noise (Elevation Map):**
 - **Scenario:** Tested A1 on stairs by adding independent Gaussian noise vertically and laterally to every height point in the elevation map.
 - **ROLT Results:** Achieved superior performance as the noise scale increased. This indicates the effectiveness of the proposed consistent dropout strategy in injecting randomness and enhancing robustness.
 - **Baseline Performance:** MLP and RMA, even with Gaussian noise injected during their training, failed to generalize to higher levels of Gaussian noise. Roll-Drop performed poorly due to its extremely small dropout rate and limited randomness injection.
 - **Ablation (No Feature Drop):** Performed the worst, providing evidence for the critical role of feature dropout in resisting high-frequency noise.

**C. Real-World Experiments (Unitree Go1):**

- **Scenario:** Zero-shot transfer of a policy trained in simulation (front-right leg limping) to a physical Unitree Go1 robot. Three novel OOD conditions were tested: "weaken & half-lock" (weakened motor, string-locked hind leg), "pulling box" (dragging a heavy box with one leg), and "rolling skates" (two front feet tied to roller skates).
- **ROLT Results:** Consistently outperformed RMA across all three real-world tasks.
 - **Weaken & half-lock:** ROLT initially faltered but quickly adapted and resumed normal walking.
 - **Pulling box:** ROLT maintained balance and walked a considerable distance despite lateral forces.
 - **Rolling skates:** ROLT recovered from sudden sliding and moved forward steadily.
 - These results demonstrate ROLT's ability to generalize from limited simulation failure experience to complex, novel real-world situations.
- **RMA Results:** Failed in all tasks (e.g., stuck, spun around, slid and fell), indicating its inability to leverage knowledge and generalize in these OOD scenarios.

**D. Visualization Analysis (Attention Maps):**

- **Observation:** Analysis of attention maps for the elevation map under clean and noisy conditions.
- **No PE Drop:** Under clean observations, attention focused on terrain immediately surrounding the robot. With masked patches, it continued to pay attention to similar *spatial positions* regardless of the masked content, suggesting overfitting to patch position embeddings and inability to shift attention when noise was present.
- **ROLT:** Under clean conditions, ROLT paid attention to more diverse patches similar to the terrain near its body. When some patches were masked, ROLT shifted its attention to unmasked areas to estimate terrain information. This implies that PE dropout promoted a more flexible flow of attention, allowing the robot to reliably estimate terrain by leveraging uncorrupted information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces the Robust Locomotion Transformer (ROLT), which represents a network-architecture-centric approach to developing generalized and robust locomotion controllers for legged robots. The findings suggest that specific structural designs within a transformer framework can significantly enhance a robot's ability to operate in challenging, out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions without requiring extensive domain randomization or explicit real-time adaptation mechanisms typically employed in prior work.

The significance of this work lies in several key contributions:

- **Demonstrated Generalization to Unseen Dynamics:** ROLT exhibits robust performance in dynamic conditions not encountered during training, such as novel limb damage patterns (e.g., "raise one and infer three" capability and hexapod damage tests). This indicates that the proposed body tokenization scheme successfully fosters knowledge transfer across different robot limbs, leading to a more fundamental understanding of locomotion principles rather than scenario-specific learning. This could reduce the need for exhaustive training in every possible failure mode.
- **Robustness to Unseen Perceptual Noise:** The consistent dropout strategy, particularly the PE dropout, is shown to enable the policy to handle various challenging perceptual noise patterns (both low-frequency offsets and high-frequency Gaussian noise) that were not present in the training observations. This is critical for real-world deployment where sensor data is inherently imperfect and unpredictable. The findings suggest a mechanism for the robot to infer reliable environmental information even from corrupted exteroceptive data.
- **Architectural Innovation:** The introduction of body tokenization with an action token for data-dependent position embedding and the consistent dropout strategy are specific architectural innovations that contribute to the observed generalization and robustness. This shifts the focus from purely data-augmentation or adaptive control to fundamental network design.
- **Improved Real-World Performance:** The successful zero-shot transfer of ROLT to a physical Unitree Go1 robot under novel, complex OOD conditions (weaken & half-lock, pulling box, rolling skates) is a strong validation of its practical utility. This demonstrates that the robustness achieved in simulation can translate to real-world scenarios, a crucial step for robotics research.
- **Towards Trustworthy Robot Controllers:** By providing a controller that can cope with unforeseen failures and noise, this research contributes to the development of more trustworthy and autonomous robots. Such robots would be more reliable in critical applications, such as search and rescue, exploration, or industrial tasks, where unpredictable environments are common.

The potential impact of this research is substantial. It offers a new direction for designing robot controllers that are inherently more capable of handling the inherent variability of real-world environments. This could lead to:

- **Reduced Development Costs:** Less reliance on exhaustive domain randomization or the design of complex adaptation modules could simplify the development pipeline for new robotic systems.
- **Enhanced Autonomy:** Robots equipped with such controllers could operate more independently in unstructured and unpredictable environments, requiring less human intervention.
- **Increased Safety and Reliability:** The ability to recover from unexpected damage or sensor failures would make robots safer and more reliable in deployment.

Future work suggested by the authors includes exploring further generalization abilities, such as traversing new obstacles and acquiring novel motor skills, which indicates a path towards even more capable and versatile robotic systems.
