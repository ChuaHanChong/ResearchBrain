# Revisiting the "Push-T" Robot Manipulation Task with Agentic Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Prompts an LLM coding agent (Claude Code with Fable 5) to write, run, and iteratively refine procedural code-as-policy controllers for Push-T and a new Push-Alphabet benchmark, with no demonstration data or learned policy. The agent-generated state and vision MPC controllers beat a diffusion policy trained on 200 demonstrations and generalize across letters and robot embodiments.

## Problem

- Diffusion-policy imitation learning on Push-T needs hundreds of human demonstrations and only reaches partial target coverage, often missing strict success thresholds.
- Classical contact-mechanics methods like Dubins pushing and contact-implicit trajectory optimization need heavy task-specific algorithm design and do not scale to diverse concave shapes.
- Data-driven world-model approaches for pushing require substantial interaction-data collection and do not test generalization beyond the single T shape.

## Method

- ==Agentic self-learning loop== where an LLM coding agent writes, executes, diagnoses, and revises a procedural controller in simulation without operating inside the control loop.
- Geometry-based hybrid state controller (**controller_v0**) with plan-approach-push-retreat phases and a discrete contact library exploiting face-normal versus offset pushes for translation versus rotation.
- ==Closed-loop vision perception pipeline== with color-segmentation tracking, one-time geometric calibration, and per-push friction-gain adaptation feeding an ==MPC controller== generalized to 26 letter shapes.

## Results

- On gym_pusht over 200 seeds, the agent-generated state controller reaches **100%** success and **96.8%** coverage versus **62.5%** success and 87.4% coverage for LeRobot diffusion policy, using 120.1 versus 223.7 steps.
- On Push-Alphabet (650 episodes), agent MPC with state input reaches **99.4%** success and **90.9%** coverage using 5.15 pushes, beating the adapted Dubins-pushing baseline's **97.2%** success and 12.15 pushes.
- Vision-guided MPC ported to simulated Franka and UR5e arms achieves **89.8%** and **98.5%** success respectively, showing cross-embodiment transfer without changing task-level contact reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit geometric contact reasoning plus closed-loop replanning can substitute for demonstration-heavy visuomotor imitation learning on planar manipulation tasks.
> - Letting a coding agent run its own curriculum and diagnose failure logs autonomously can escalate from search heuristics to a stronger MPC formulation without external supervision.
> - Agents exploit simulator-specific regularities as if authoritative, so simulation-honed controllers need treatment as uncertain hypotheses before real-world transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The "Push-T" task serves as a canonical benchmark in robot learning, originally introduced for evaluating learning manipulation policies from human demonstrations, particularly through methods like Implicit Behavioral Cloning and Diffusion Policy. These approaches primarily focus on visuomotor policy learning, where robots learn direct mappings from visual inputs to actions.

Beyond data-driven imitation learning, classical robotics research has established geometric principles for manipulating objects through contact. This includes work on orienting polygonal objects and reducing planar pushing to a Dubins-car system for time-optimal trajectories under fixed contact. More recent procedural methods, such as CRISP, address the complexities of Push-T's concave geometry, friction, and coupled motion by formulating it as a contact-implicit trajectory-optimization problem solved via sequential convex programming. Data-driven world models, like the Interactive World Simulator, also learn interaction dynamics for policy training.

A common characteristic of these existing methods, whether imitation learning or classical procedural approaches, is their requirement for substantial task-specific effort. This effort can manifest as collecting extensive demonstration data, meticulous algorithm design, complex optimization modeling, or detailed interaction data collection.

This current work positions itself within the emerging paradigm of "agentic robotics," which leverages advancements in large language models (LLMs) to generate robot control code without the LLM directly controlling high-frequency actions. This paradigm aims to automate the development of robotic controllers by allowing an AI coding agent to iteratively construct, execute, evaluate, and refine procedural control logic. The paper explores whether such an agentic system can autonomously derive effective procedural solutions for tasks like Push-T, thereby reducing the manual engineering effort typically associated with both learning-based and classical methods. It specifically contrasts the agent-generated procedural solutions with policies learned from human demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to investigate the capabilities of an AI coding agent in solving complex robot manipulation tasks and to evaluate the performance of agent-generated procedural controllers against established learning-based policies.

The specific questions the paper aims to address are:
1. **Can an AI coding agent generate procedural methods to solve the Push-T task?** This objective probes the fundamental capability of an LLM-driven agent to autonomously develop functional robotic control code from high-level natural language prompts.
2. **How do these agent-generated solutions compare with policies learned from human demonstrations?** This objective seeks to benchmark the performance, efficiency, and robustness of procedural controllers against state-of-the-art imitation learning policies, particularly those based on diffusion models, in the context of Push-T. The comparison focuses on metrics such as success rate and interaction efficiency.
3. **Can the agent systematically adapt its geometric reasoning, contact strategies, and control logic to solve a diverse set of concave and asymmetric character shapes at scale (Push-Alphabet)?** This extends the inquiry beyond a single, canonical shape (T) to evaluate the agent's generalization capabilities. It assesses whether the agent can autonomously generate a curriculum and refine its strategies for a broader range of object geometries without requiring new demonstrations or explicit human redesign for each new shape.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several factors:
- **Reducing Task-Specific Effort:** Traditional robot learning and control methods often demand significant human effort in data collection, model design, or algorithm engineering. Agentic robotics offers a potential pathway to automate these steps.
- **Leveraging LLM Capabilities:** The increasing sophistication of LLMs in code generation, reasoning, and iterative refinement presents an opportunity to develop robotic systems that can self-program and self-improve.
- **Exploring Alternatives to Visuomotor Learning:** While visuomotor imitation learning has made strides, its reliance on extensive demonstration data and opaque policy structures can be a limitation. Procedural controllers, if autonomously generated, could offer interpretability and potentially higher efficiency.
- **Generalization Across Tasks and Embodiments:** Demonstrating that an agent can generalize its generated code to new object shapes and different robot embodiments would highlight the robustness and scalability of the agentic approach.

In essence, the research investigates the efficacy of an "agentic self-learning" paradigm where an LLM coding agent iteratively designs, executes, evaluates, and refines robotic control code, aiming to achieve high performance with minimal human intervention or pre-existing demonstration data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an agentic self-learning framework where a large language model (LLM) coding agent, specifically Claude Code with Fable 5, is tasked with generating procedural controllers for robot manipulation. The approach is structured around iterative code generation, execution, evaluation, and refinement within a simulated environment.

**Problem Setup:**
The study focuses on planar pushing with a single point-contact pusher.
- **Objects:** The canonical T-shaped block for Push-T, and 26 letter-shaped blocks (A-Z) for Push-Alphabet. These letters feature diverse geometries, including concavities, symmetries, and holes.
- **Observation Modalities:** Controllers receive either privileged state information (object pose, pusher configuration, target pose) or visual observations (RGB images) from which these quantities must be estimated.
- **Metrics:** Success rate (final object pose within task-specific tolerances) and push efficiency (number of discrete pushes in successful episodes). An episode succeeds if target coverage (intersection area / target area) reaches 95% for Push-T and 90% for Push-Alphabet.

**Agentic Self-Learning Process:**
The core methodology involves an "agentic coding loop." The LLM agent receives a natural language task description and then:
1. **Generates Initial Code:** Synthesizes a procedural controller based on the prompt. For Push-T, it searches for existing gym simulations to understand the environment.
2. **Executes in Simulation:** Runs the generated code as a policy within a simulated environment.
3. **Observes and Evaluates:** Records execution outcomes, successes, and failures, and assesses performance based on defined metrics.
4. **Diagnoses and Revises:** Identifies model or control errors and iteratively refines the code (`ϕ`) for perception, planning, dynamics, and control. This includes fitting friction behavior from simulation experiments to construct an internal quasi-statics model. The LLM does not operate within the high-frequency control loop; rather, it generates the higher-level control logic.

**Solving Push-T:**
- **Initial Solution (Claude_state_controller):** Prompted with a basic task description, the agent generated `controller_v0`. This is a geometry-based hybrid feedback controller implemented as a state machine with four phases: plan, approach, push, and retreat. It uses a 5-dimensional state vector (pusher position, T-block pose). The controller leverages the structural decomposition of planar pushing: face-normal pushes for translation and offset pushes for rotation. It utilizes a discrete contact library, selecting actions greedily based on pose error reduction under a quasi-static contact model, with short-horizon execution and state-feedback replanning. Recovery pushes are included to handle boundary constraints.
- **Vision Integration (Claude_vision_controller):** The agent was further prompted to replace privileged state input with vision feedback. It rewrote the controller to integrate a closed-loop perception pipeline:
 - **Per-Frame Workspace Perception:** Color segmentation and physical sensor model to isolate block, goal, and agent masks from RGB frames. Closed-loop tracking updates object poses via Intersection-over-Union (IoU) fitting.
 - **One-Time Calibration:** Before manipulation, the agent fits a scale parameter to letter geometry, resolves multi-basin goal poses, and initializes object parameters (polygon bounds, center of mass, inertia, contact points).
 - **Per-Push Parameter Adaptation:** After each push, the controller buffers state setpoints, replays predicted orientation changes in a digital twin, and refines friction gain parameters (`f ← f · r^-η`).

**Scaling to Push-Alphabet:**
- The agent was prompted to generalize from Push-T to Push-A-Z, generating both the environment suite (2D gym simulation with 26 distinct letter shapes, detailed in Appendix) and controller iterations.
- **Self-Improvement Iterations:** The agent improved its solution over three rounds:
 - **Round v1:** Initial controller generation achieved 47.1% success rate.
 - **Round v2:** Autonomous overnight self-refinement raised the success rate to 73.1%.
 - **Round v3:** The agent autonomously inspected failure logs, read simulation source code to diagnose kinematic bugs, and transitioned from search-based heuristics to a Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulation, ultimately reaching 99.4% overall success. This included a self-generated curriculum focusing on struggling letters (Figure 2) and refining strategies for specific geometric challenges (e.g., curved letters, thin strokes, difficult geometries like 'Q').
- **Classical Baseline Comparison:** The agent's performance was compared against a classical model-based method, Dubins pushing. The coding agent was tasked with adapting this fixed-contact method by constructing an adapter for contact selection, collision-free approach, command conversion, and re-engagement logic to operate on the Push-Alphabet benchmark.

**Cross-Embodiment Transfer:**
The vision-guided MPC controller generated by the agent was ported to simulate two different physical robotic arms: the Franka Panda and the Universal Robots UR5e, to evaluate its portability across distinct embodiments without modifying the task-level contact reasoning.

**Model Cost:**
The agentic development process consumed approximately 9.5 million output tokens from Claude Code with Fable 5, incurring an API cost of $1,500–$2,000 and requiring about 221 hours in "auto mode" under human interactions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the capabilities of agentic robotics in robot manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Performance on Push-T

Table 1 summarizes the performance on the `gym_pusht` environment over 200 evaluation seeds:

| Policy | Success Rate | Mean steps | Mean Pushes | Coverage |
| :---------------------- | :----------- | :--------- | :---------- | :------- |
| Diffusion_Policy_LeRobot | 62.5% | 223.7 | 6.38 | 87.4% |
| Claude_state_controller | 100.0% | 120.1 | 3.72 | 96.8% |
| Claude_vision_controller | 97.2% | 170.4 | 4.76 | 94.2% |

- **Agent Outperforms Learned Policy:** The `Claude_state_controller` achieved a 100.0% success rate, significantly outperforming the Diffusion Policy baseline (62.5% success rate), which was trained with 200 human demonstrations.
- **Efficiency:** The agent-generated state-based controller was also more efficient, requiring 50% fewer control steps (120.1 vs. 223.7) and 41% fewer pushes (3.72 vs. 6.38) on average compared to the Diffusion Policy. It achieved a higher mean final target coverage (96.8% vs. 87.4%).
- **Vision Integration:** When vision feedback was introduced (`Claude_vision_controller`), the success rate remained high at 97.2%, though with a slight increase in mean steps (170.4) and pushes (4.76) compared to the state-based controller. The mean coverage was 94.2%. These results indicate that the explicit geometric contact reasoning and closed-loop replanning capabilities generated by the agent can provide an effective alternative to demonstration-based visuomotor policy learning.

#### 4.2. Scaling to Push-Alphabet (Push-A through Push-Z)

The agent demonstrated the ability to generalize and self-improve for the more complex Push-Alphabet benchmark, featuring 26 diverse letter shapes.

- **Self-Learning Progress:** Starting with a 47.1% success rate in round v1, the agent autonomously refined its controller to 73.1% in round v2 and ultimately to 99.4% in round v3. This improvement involved autonomous inspection of failure logs, diagnosis of kinematic bugs, and a shift from search-based heuristics to an MPC formulation. The agent also generated a self-paced curriculum, focusing on letters it was struggling with (Figure 2).
- **Complex Strategy Development:** The agent's self-generated optimization process included distinct phases: establishing baseline planner mechanics, formulating rotation-versus-translation trade-offs for curved letters, fine-tuning corner coverage for thin-stroked characters, optimizing leverage mechanics for challenging geometries like 'Q', and implementing recovery rules for physical livelocks and wall-trapped states.

Table 2 compares different controllers on the Push-Alphabet benchmark over 650 episodes (25 per letter):

| Environment | Method / Input | Mean Steps | Mean Pushes | Coverage | Success Rate |
| :---------- | :------------------------------------- | :--------- | :---------- | :------- | :----------- |
| gym | Dubins pushing + state | 291.9 | 11.00 | 83.8% | 90.2% |
| gym | Dubins pushing + state (agent adapter) | 293.8 | 12.15 | 90.3% | 97.2% |
| gym | MPC + state | 248.7 | 5.15 | 90.9% | 99.4% |
| gym | MPC + vision | 269.7 | 5.36 | 90.9% | 99.2% |
| Franka | MPC + state | 477.0 | 4.10 | 90.3% | 99.2% |
| Franka | MPC + vision | 668.1 | 5.78 | 83.4% | 89.8% |
| UR5e | MPC + vision | 545.8 | 5.06 | 90.0% | 98.5% |

- **Superiority of Agent-Generated MPC:** The agent-generated MPC controller (state-based) achieved the highest success rate of 99.4% and 90.9% mean coverage in the `gym` environment, while requiring 248.7 control steps and 5.15 pushes.
- **Comparison with Classical Baseline:** An agent-authored adapter improved the classical Dubins pushing method from 90.2% to 97.2% success, demonstrating the agent's ability to enhance existing theoretical controllers. However, the agent's self-generated MPC still surpassed this, achieving higher success with less than half the number of pushes.
- **Vision-Guided MPC:** The MPC controller with vision input maintained a high success rate (99.2%) and coverage (90.9%) in the `gym` environment, with slightly more steps (269.7) and pushes (5.36) than its state-based counterpart.

#### 4.3. Cross-Embodiment Transfer

The agent-generated vision-guided MPC controller demonstrated portability across distinct simulated robot embodiments:
- **UR5e:** Achieved a 98.5% success rate with 5.1 mean pushes.
- **Franka Panda:** Achieved an 89.8% success rate with 5.8 mean pushes.

These results suggest that the task-level contact reasoning developed by the agent can transfer across different robot platforms in simulation without requiring fundamental modifications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research suggest several significant implications and potential impacts for robotics and AI.

**Advancements in Agentic Robotics:** The study provides empirical evidence for the capability of LLM coding agents, specifically Claude Code with Fable 5, to autonomously generate complex procedural controllers for robot manipulation tasks. This demonstrates a pathway for AI to design and optimize robot control algorithms, moving beyond simply learning policies from demonstrations or requiring extensive manual engineering.

**Reduced Dependency on Human Demonstrations and Manual Engineering:** The agent-generated solutions not only matched but, in several key metrics (success rate, push efficiency), surpassed policies learned from large sets of human demonstrations (e.g., Diffusion Policy on Push-T). Furthermore, by iteratively refining its code, the agent significantly reduced the need for human-designed algorithms or task-specific models, exemplified by its progression from initial heuristics to an MPC formulation for Push-Alphabet. This could potentially accelerate the development cycle for new robotic tasks.

**Generalization Capabilities:** The agent successfully generalized its approach from a single T-shape to the full alphabet (Push-A to Push-Z), which includes objects with diverse and challenging geometries. Its ability to autonomously develop a curriculum and refine strategies for these varied shapes indicates a degree of adaptability in its geometric reasoning and contact strategy generation. The demonstrated cross-embodiment transfer to simulated Franka and UR5e arms further highlights the potential for these generated controllers to be portable across different robotic platforms.

**Implications for Autonomous System Development:** The agentic self-learning loop, which involves code generation, simulation-based execution, evaluation, diagnosis, and revision, represents a step towards more autonomous system development in robotics. This paradigm could enable robots to adapt to new environments or tasks by autonomously modifying their control software, rather than requiring retraining of a black-box policy or extensive human reprogramming.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
- **Simulator-Reality Gap:** A primary limitation is that the agent currently treats the simulation as a complete and authoritative specification. In reality, simulators are approximations. The generated controller's robustness to real-world complexities like perception errors, calibration drift, latency, compliance, and unmodeled contacts is not guaranteed. Future work should focus on methods for agentic systems to treat simulation as an uncertain hypothesis and develop strategies to bridge the sim-to-real gap, possibly through direct real-world interaction or more sophisticated error modeling.
- **Computational Cost and LLM Sensitivity:** The current process is computationally expensive, incurring significant inference costs (e.g., $1,500–$2,000 and 221 hours for this study) and sensitivity to prompt wording. An important research direction is to optimize the LLM usage, potentially by employing a hierarchical approach where a powerful frontier model handles high-level diagnosis and architectural changes, while smaller, more efficient models manage localized code edits, log analysis, and parameter tuning. This could make agentic development more economically viable and faster.
- **Interpretability and Safety:** While procedural controllers can offer more interpretability than learned policies, the sheer volume of agent-generated code and the autonomous refinement process raise questions about verification, validation, and ensuring safety in critical applications.

In conclusion, this research presents a compelling demonstration of agentic robotics for complex manipulation tasks, suggesting a future where AI systems play a more active role in designing and refining their own robotic control software, potentially leading to more adaptable and efficient robotic solutions.
