# The Wanderings of Odysseus in 3D Scenes

> [!summary] Summary
> GAMMA populates 3D scenes with perpetually moving digital humans. It decomposes long motion into 0.25-second motion primitives, models each with a marker-based conditional VAE plus a recursive body regressor that recovers SMPL-X meshes, and drives motion toward goals with a PPO-trained latent-space policy and tree-based search. It beats HuMoR on body-ground contact and perceptual realism, and runs 256 bodies in real time.

## Problem

- Motion-graph and motion-matching animation pipelines scale poorly to many characters, need retargeting across body shapes, and yield deterministic motions close to recorded clips.
- Generative mesh-motion models produce only a few seconds of motion with jitter and foot-skating; a single network likely cannot represent perpetual, ever-more-uncertain motion.
- Existing controllable synthesis either conditions on goals and breaks under train-test domain gaps, or uses costly optimization or physics simulation with hand-built humanoids.

## Method

- Motion primitives of 0.25 seconds, matched to human visual reaction time, are modeled by a ==marker-predictor CVAE== with robust-KL regularization and rollout fine-tuning over eight primitives.
- A recursive ==HMR==-style body regressor maps predicted surface markers plus a shape parameter to ==SMPL-X== pose using 6D rotations, replacing MOJO's slow optimization-based reprojection, with marker blending for stable recursion.
- Goal reaching is a ==Markov decision process==: an actor-critic policy samples CVAE latents as actions under path, orientation, contact, VPoser and goal rewards, trained by ==PPO== plus a KL term, with test-time ==tree search== keeping the best K primitives.

## Results

- Policy-driven motion attains a **0.94** contact score and the top perceptual score of **4.79** on a six-point scale, versus HuMoR's lower contact medians and weaker perceived realism.
- Against a goal-conditioned CVAE baseline (**0.91** success, **0.80** contact), the policy reaches **1.0** success rate; adding top-1 tree search gives **0.97** contact and **0.04** average distance.
- Foot-skating over 900 ACCAD bodies and 20 primitives is **0.064**, far below MOJO's **0.341** and **0.278** and near ground truth **0.067**; **256** bodies run in real time on one GPU.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Chunking a long-horizon generative task into short, physiologically motivated primitives makes each unit near-deterministic and lets recursion extend the horizon indefinitely.
> - Learning a policy over a frozen generative model's latent space controls behavior more robustly than conditioning the generator on goals, which fails on out-of-distribution targets.
> - Fast regression plus blending can replace expensive per-step optimization, but purely data-driven motion still violates physics; end-to-end training gave no measurable benefit here.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The work addresses the challenge of automatically populating digital environments with realistic, diverse, and perpetually moving virtual humans that maintain plausible body-scene contact. This area is critical for advancing applications in augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), architectural visualization, and the gaming industry.

Traditionally, character animation in graphics, particularly in gaming, relies on pre-designed 3D characters and pre-recorded motion datasets. Techniques such as motion graphs or motion matching are employed to blend existing clips to respond to user inputs or environmental events. While these methods can achieve high motion realism, they face limitations in scalability for animating a massive number of characters with diverse behaviors, often requiring additional motion re-targeting procedures for different character shapes. Furthermore, the generated motions tend to be deterministic, limiting diversity.

The emergence of large-scale motion capture (mocap) datasets, such as AMASS, has facilitated the development of learned generative motion models. These models can produce motions conditioned on factors like past motion, action labels, scene context, or music, and can generalize across various body shapes without being tied to specific characters. However, existing generative models often produce motions of limited duration (e.g., a few seconds) and may suffer from artifacts such as jittering or foot-skating. While some recent works incorporate expressive parametric body models like SMPL-X to enhance realism, the challenge of generating infinitely long, realistic, and controllable motions for a wide variety of human shapes, especially within complex 3D scenes with geometric constraints, remains largely unaddressed by existing single deep neural network solutions due to the escalating uncertainty of human motion over time.

Motion control techniques also vary within the landscape. Some approaches extend generative motion models by introducing control signals as additional conditions, but these can be sensitive to domain gaps between training and test data. Optimization-based methods, which treat control signals as data terms and use generative models for regularization, produce high-quality results but incur high computational costs. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been widely used to control infinitely long motions for specific tasks, often relying on physical simulations to model body dynamics. However, these methods typically require complex humanoid rigging and physics-aware motion estimation. Ling et al. (2020) proposed a notable approach combining a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) for generative motion modeling with reinforcement learning for task-driven synthesis.

This paper positions its proposed method, GAMMA, as a data-driven solution that aims to overcome the identified limitations. It builds upon ideas from prior work, such as the marker-based representation in MOJO (Zhang et al., 2021) and the CVAE-RL combination in MotionVAE (Ling et al., 2020), but extends them with novel motion models and control methods. Specifically, GAMMA focuses on generating long-term, diverse, stochastic, and controllable motions for diverse 3D bodies in a computationally efficient manner, without requiring physical simulation.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automated and scalable solution for populating digital environments with realistic virtual humans. This overarching goal encompasses several specific aims:

1. **Diverse Body Shapes:** To enable virtual humans to exhibit a variety of body shapes, moving beyond generic character models. This is motivated by the need for more varied and relatable digital populations in applications like AR/VR.
2. **Perpetual and Realistic Motion:** To generate motions that are indefinitely long, realistic in appearance, and consistent over extended periods. Current data-driven methods often produce short motion segments, and the cumulative uncertainty in long-term prediction presents a significant challenge. The motivation is to create dynamic digital environments where virtual inhabitants can perform continuous activities.
3. **Plausible Body-Scene Contact:** To ensure that virtual humans interact naturally and plausibly with the ground and other geometric elements within a 3D scene. This is a critical aspect for visual realism, as artifacts like foot-skating or floating bodies detract from immersion.
4. **Controllable Motion towards Goals:** To allow virtual humans to navigate and reach designated destinations within a 3D scene while maintaining realistic and natural movement. This capability is essential for interactive applications and for simulating purposeful behavior.

The motivations for achieving these objectives are rooted in various application domains:
- **AR/VR Experiences:** Populating digital models of cities or environments with realistic virtual humans can significantly enhance user immersion, for example, by providing guided tours or creating a more vibrant scene.
- **Architectural Design and Urban Planning:** Simulating pedestrian flow and interaction with built environments can provide architects and planners with insights into design functionalities, potential defects, and user experience before physical construction.
- **Gaming and Animation:** The ability to automatically generate diverse, long-term, and controllable motions for a large number of characters can reduce manual animation efforts and allow for more dynamic and engaging virtual worlds.
- **Addressing Limitations of Current Methods:** The work is motivated by the identified shortcomings of existing character animation and generative motion models, including issues with scalability, diversity, realism of long-term motion, and effective control within complex environments. The core challenge lies in modeling the inherent uncertainty of human motion over prolonged durations and integrating seamless body-scene interactions.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The proposed method, named GAMMA (GenerAtive Motion primitive via body surface MArkers), addresses the synthesis of long-term, controllable, and realistic human motion through a multi-component, data-driven pipeline.

#### 3.1. Motion Decomposition and Representation

GAMMA decomposes long-term motion into a sequence of "motion primitives," each spanning 0.25 seconds (10 frames at 40fps) with overlaps. This decomposition is based on the premise that shorter motion segments primarily contain unconscious body dynamics, which are more deterministic and thus easier to model than infinitely long sequences.
The body in motion is represented using body surface markers, a strategy inspired by MOJO (Zhang et al., 2021). These markers, selected from the SMPL-X mesh template, convert body motion into a time sequence of point clouds. This marker-based representation is intended to provide richer body shape information and more constraints on the body's degrees-of-freedom compared to joint-based representations, making it generalizable to various body shapes.

#### 3.2. Generative Motion Primitives Model

The core of GAMMA comprises two main neural network components:

- **Marker Predictor:** This component is implemented as a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE). It takes a "motion seed" (X), consisting of 1 or 2 frames of body surface markers, and predicts the subsequent 10 frames of body surface markers (Y). During inference, the latent variable `z` is sampled from a standard normal distribution (N(0, I)) to introduce diversity into the generated motions. The model is trained in two phases:
 1. **Primitive-level training:** An initial training phase optimizes the predictor to learn individual motion primitives using a loss function that combines a reconstruction term for marker positions and velocities, along with a robust KL-divergence term to regularize the latent space (Eq. 1).
 2. **Rollout fine-tuning:** The predictor is subsequently fine-tuned by recursively generating longer sequences. In this phase, the last 1 or 2 frames of a generated primitive serve as the motion seed for the next primitive. This process helps the model account for prediction errors and produce stable long-term motions, even when the motion seed is not perfectly aligned with ground truth. Two versions, 1-frame and 2-frame predictors, are trained separately.

- **Body Regressor:** This network learns to recover the full SMPL-X body parameters (global translation, orientation, joint rotations, and hand poses) from the predicted body surface markers. It takes the predicted markers, an initial body parameter set (Θ₀, set to zero), and a given body shape (β) as input. The network employs residual blocks in a recursive manner, similar to approaches like HMR (Kanazawa et al., 2018). Body orientation and joint rotations are internally represented using a 6D continuous representation for improved backpropagation. To account for gender-specific morphology, separate body regressors are trained for male and female bodies. The training loss (Eq. 2) is based on minimizing the distance between markers extracted from the regressed mesh and the ground truth body surface markers, with an additional regularization term for hand poses.

#### 3.3. Motion Synthesis and Control Pipeline

GAMMA integrates these components into a recursive pipeline for motion synthesis:

- **Recursion Mechanism:** Generated motion primitives are continuously fed back into the system. The pipeline includes:
 - **SMPL-X Module:** Used to obtain markers from the regressed body mesh.
 - **Motion Blending Module:** This module is critical for maintaining stability. While the paper mentions "blending" predicted and reprojected markers, the primary text focuses on the regressor recovering the SMPL-X body. The appendix clarifies that in `ours-ro`, predicted markers and reprojected markers (markers derived from a re-fitted SMPL-X body) are averaged. This suggests a mechanism to stabilize the motion seed against accumulated prediction errors, ensuring the generated body remains valid.
 - **Canonicalization Module:** Transforms the current body configuration into a canonical coordinate system (pelvis at origin, specific axis alignments) before generating the next primitive.

- **Goal-driven Motion Policy:** To control motion towards a designated goal, GAMMA formulates motion synthesis as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and employs reinforcement learning (RL).
 - **State (s_t):** Defined as the concatenation of the motion seed (X) and normalized vectors from individual markers to the goal (g) in the canonical space (Eq. 3).
 - **Action (a_t):** Corresponds to the latent variable `z` sampled from the CVAE's latent space.
 - **Reward (r):** A composite reward function (Eq. 4) guides the policy. It includes terms for:
 - Path following (r_path, Eq. 5): Encourages movement towards the goal along a straight path.
 - Body orientation (r_ori, Eq. 6): Encourages the body to face the goal.
 - Body-ground contact (r_contact, Eq. 7): Penalizes pelvis vertical shift and high marker speeds (to prevent skating).
 - Valid body pose (r_vposer, Eq. 8): Promotes poses consistent with the VPoser model.
 - Goal-reaching (r_goal, Eq. 9): A bonus for reaching proximity to the target.
 - **Policy Network Training:** An actor-critic mechanism (Sutton & Barto, 1998) is used, where the actor outputs a diagonal Gaussian distribution π(z_t|s_t) for actions. The critic estimates the value function. Training occurs in a simulated environment (20x20m ground plane) with random characters and goals. The policy network is updated by minimizing a loss function (Eq. 10) that combines the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) loss (Schulman et al., 2017), a critic value loss, and a regularizing KL-divergence term between the policy output and a standard normal distribution, which encourages naturalness of motion in the latent space.

- **Tree-based Search:** During testing, a tree-based search mechanism is implemented to filter out motion primitives that exhibit inferior body-ground contact. This process organizes motion generation into a tree where each node represents a motion primitive. At each generation step, multiple candidate primitives are generated, evaluated based on a defined "node quality/cost" (e.g., foot-ground contact and distance to goal), and only the best K are retained for subsequent generation. This technique helps to maintain motion quality and plausibility.

#### 3.4. Dataset and Implementation Details

The system is trained using the large-scale AMASS motion capture dataset, which integrates various mocap datasets (e.g., CMU, MPI HDM05, ACCAD, KIT, Eyes Japan, BMLrub, Transitions, TotalCapture). Mocap sequences are downsampled to 40fps, and motion primitives are canonicalized before training.

### 4. Main findings and results

The evaluation of GAMMA focused on two primary aspects: motion realism and motion controllability.

#### 4.1. Motion Realism

- **Comparison with HuMoR:** GAMMA methods consistently demonstrated superior performance over HuMoR (Rempe et al., 2021), a state-of-the-art generative model for long-term 3D body motions, regarding body-ground contact. The contact score (Eq. 11), which measures minimal height from ground and minimal marker speed, showed higher values for GAMMA.
- **Perceptual Study:** A user study using Amazon Mechanical Turk (Likert scale evaluation) indicated that GAMMA produced perceptually more realistic motions than HuMoR. Specifically, the 'ours-ro-policy' version, which incorporates goal-driven behavior, achieved a mean perceptual score of 4.79, notably higher than HuMoR's 4.33, suggesting that goal-oriented motions are perceived as more realistic by users.
- **Influence of Policy Network:** The policy network ('ours-ro-policy') contributed to a substantial increase in both body-ground contact scores and perceptual realism compared to random motion generation ('ours-ro-2f'), highlighting its effectiveness in balancing realism with goal-driven behavior.
- **Model Architecture:** The study observed that end-to-end training of the predictor and regressor ('ours-e2e') did not yield better results compared to training them separately. Furthermore, 2-frame-based models ('-2f') produced smoother motions and mitigated stiffness issues observed in 1-frame-based models, especially for slow and low-frequency movements, by better preserving higher-order dynamics.
- **Visualizations:** Visual inspections confirmed that GAMMA generated smoother motions with more plausible body-ground contact, in contrast to the observable body jitters and high-frequency non-plausible movements produced by HuMoR.

#### 4.2. Motion Control

- **Comparison with g-CVAE:** Policy-based GAMMA methods significantly outperformed 'g-CVAE' (a baseline where goal-based features are directly conditioned as input to the generative model) across all evaluated metrics. Policy-based methods achieved a 100% success rate in reaching goals within 60 primitives (for policy(1)), compared to 91% for g-CVAE, with fewer average steps and a higher contact score. 'g-CVAE' frequently struggled to maintain valid body states.
- **KLD Term Weight:** The regularization term's weight in the policy training (KLD term in Eq. 10) influenced the trade-off between speed and realism. A lower KLD weight (e.g., 'policy(1)') led to faster goal-reaching but slightly less plausible motions (contact score 0.93 vs. 0.94 for 'policy(10)').
- **Tree-based Search:** The application of the tree-based search mechanism ('top k') consistently improved control performance, particularly in reducing the average distance to the goal (e.g., 'policy(10) & top1' achieved 0.04m average distance to goal, compared to 3.32m for 'policy(10)' without search) and enhancing contact scores. Its effectiveness was not highly sensitive to the value of `k`.
- **Policy Training Dynamics:** Visualization of the policy training process showed an evolution from initial goal-agnostic random motions to an early stage focused on rapid goal-reaching (sometimes at the expense of realism), and finally to a balanced state where both goal-reaching and motion naturalness were well-maintained through continued regularization by the KLD term.

#### 4.3. Additional Findings (from Appendix)

- **Marker Predictor Analysis:** 1-frame predictors exhibited higher diversity but lower accuracy compared to 2-frame predictors, reflecting greater uncertainty from a single static pose. Rollout training generally improved diversity.
- **Body Regressor Accuracy:** The proposed body regressors demonstrated high accuracy, with averaged marker distance (AMD) and averaged mesh vertices distance (AVD) comparable to established methods like Mosh and Mosh++.
- **Influence of Body Parameters:** Body shape showed limited influence on the general action to reach a goal, with diverse shapes achieving similar results. However, the initial body pose significantly impacted motion generation, with non-walking poses sometimes requiring preparatory adjustments.
- **Comparison with Other Representations:** The marker-based representation employed by GAMMA showed advantages in maintaining rigid body deformation and preventing invalid results (e.g., twisted bodies) compared to direct generation of SMPL parameters or HuMoR kinematic features. The motion primitive setting itself improved contact scores compared to the full HuMoR model.
- **Runtime Performance:** GAMMA demonstrated efficient runtime, capable of driving 256 SMPL-X bodies in real-time on a single GPU (0.25 seconds per primitive for a batch of 256 bodies).

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The research introduces GAMMA as an automated and scalable framework for generating diverse, perpetual, and goal-controllable motions for 3D human models within digital environments. This work offers several significant contributions to the field of computer graphics and human animation.

**Technical Advancements:**
- **Novel Motion Primitive Decomposition:** By breaking down long-term motion into short, marker-based motion primitives modeled by a CVAE, the method effectively addresses the challenge of generating infinitely long and diverse motions, which is often difficult for monolithic deep learning models. This decomposition is grounded in psychological studies of human reaction time, linking it to unconscious body dynamics.
- **Efficient Body Recovery:** The development of a recursive body regressor for recovering SMPL-X parameters from predicted markers represents a speed improvement over optimization-based reprojection schemes, making real-time applications more feasible without significantly degrading accuracy.
- **Robust Goal-driven Control:** The integration of a policy network trained with reinforcement learning and a tree-based search mechanism for test-time refinement provides a robust solution for controlling human motion towards designated goals. This approach effectively balances goal-reaching efficiency with the preservation of motion realism and plausible body-scene contact, outperforming baselines that rely solely on conditioning generative models.
- **Data-driven Approach:** The entirely data-driven nature of GAMMA avoids the complexities and computational costs associated with physical simulations often used in character control, making it a potentially more accessible and scalable solution.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced AR/VR Experiences:** The capability to populate virtual scenes with large numbers of diverse, autonomously moving, and realistic digital humans can significantly enhance the immersion and interactivity of AR/VR applications, such as virtual tourism, social VR platforms, or training simulations.
- **Improved Architectural and Urban Planning:** Architects and urban planners can utilize this technology to simulate pedestrian traffic and human interactions within proposed designs, enabling better foresight into design functionalities, accessibility, and potential bottlenecks before construction.
- **Next-Generation Gaming and Animation:** GAMMA offers a pathway towards more scalable and diverse character animation in video games and film. It could facilitate the automatic generation of large crowds with individual behaviors, reducing manual animation efforts and increasing the dynamism of virtual worlds. The ability to generate non-deterministic, long-term motions can also lead to more varied and less repetitive character movements.
- **Foundation for Complex Interactions:** While the current work focuses on wandering and goal-reaching, the framework for generating and controlling realistic human motion could serve as a foundation for synthesizing more complex body-scene and human-object interactions in the future.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations. The generated motions, being purely data-driven, may not be fully physically plausible (e.g., occasional tilting that ignores gravity). The realism of motion, while improved, still has room for refinement when compared directly to ground-truth motion capture sequences. Additionally, the policy might not always generate the most human-like efficient paths for very short distances to a goal. Future work aims to address these by extending the framework to synthesize more complex body-scene interactions, potentially by incorporating physics-aware modeling or more robust body optimization techniques. The potential for bias due to imbalanced training data for different demographics (e.g., male and female models) is also noted as a social impact consideration.
