# ContactRL: Safe Reinforcement Learning based Motion Planning for Contact based Human Robot Collaboration

> [!summary] Summary
> ContactRL enables robots to perform tasks requiring safe, intentional physical contact with humans by integrating a carefully designed reinforcement learning policy for force modulation with a kinetic energy-based Control Barrier Function shield for runtime safety guarantees. Real-world experiments demonstrated consistent contact forces below 10 N during object handovers, well within human comfort limits.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic safety frameworks focus on collision avoidance, which is insufficient for collaborative tasks that inherently require intentional physical contact with humans.
- Existing human-robot interaction methods struggle to simultaneously guarantee human safety (minimizing discomfort from contact forces) and task efficiency in dynamic, contact-rich scenarios.
- Pure reinforcement learning lacks formal safety guarantees essential for human-robot collaboration, while conventional compliance control methods are difficult to tune and adapt to dynamic human environments.

## Method

- Developed ContactRL, a Reinforcement Learning framework with a multi-component reward function to enable the robot to learn safe, low-force contact behaviors (e.g., reach, safety, jerk, proximity rewards).
- Augmented the learned RL policy with a kinetic energy-based Control Barrier Function (eCBF) shield during deployment, which implicitly regulates contact forces by bounding the end-effector's kinetic energy.
- The eCBF shield acts as a real-time safety filter, utilizing a Quadratic Program (QP) to project commanded velocities, thereby ensuring kinetic energy and implicitly maximum contact force remain within predefined safe limits without requiring policy retraining.

## Results

- The optimized reward function in simulation achieved an 87.17% task success rate and a safety violation rate of 0.20%, outperforming state-of-the-art constrained RL baselines.
- Real-world experiments with a UR3e robot demonstrated that the shielded ContactRL policy maintained measured normal contact forces consistently below 10 N (with a mode near 1 N), well within human comfort limits across diverse conditions and participants.
- The eCBF shield effectively eliminated jitter-induced motion and prevented unsafe kinetic energy excursions in simulated stochastic environments, proving essential for reliable and safe real-world deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A comprehensive multi-component reward function, incorporating explicit safety and motion smoothness terms, is critical for guiding RL agents to learn both high performance and safe, comfortable physical contact behaviors.
> - Combining the adaptive learning capabilities of deep reinforcement learning with the provable, hard safety guarantees of a Control Barrier Function shield offers a robust and effective solution for safe human-robot physical interaction.
> - Kinetic energy-based CBFs can implicitly and efficiently regulate contact forces by bounding robot kinetic energy, providing a lightweight runtime mechanism for safety without relying on explicit contact dynamics models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of collaborative robots (cobots) is a cornerstone of Industry 5.0, where human and robot agents are expected to work closely, often in shared workspaces and engaging in physical contact. Traditionally, robotic safety has focused primarily on collision avoidance, treating any contact as a failure event. This paradigm, however, is insufficient for a growing number of collaborative tasks that inherently require intentional physical contact, such as handovers, assistive dressing, or co-manipulation of objects.

This paper addresses a significant gap in the safe reinforcement learning (Safe RL) and human-robot interaction (HRI) landscape: enabling robots to engage in *safe, intentional physical contact* while maintaining task efficiency. Existing approaches in HRI and motion planning often fall into several categories:
- **Compliance Control (e.g., Impedance/Admittance Control):** While effective for regulating contact forces, these methods rely heavily on precise environmental models and require laborious gain-tuning (e.g., stiffness/damping), making them less adaptive to dynamic human environments and difficult to guarantee safety-efficiency trade-offs across varied tasks.
- **Safe RL based on Collision Avoidance:** Many Safe RL techniques aim to penalize unsafe behaviors or enforce constraints to prevent collisions in static or dynamic environments. However, these methods typically equate safety with *non-contact*, failing to provide solutions for scenarios where contact is necessary.
- **Constrained RL:** Approaches like Model Predictive Control (MPC) or Lagrangian-based optimization can incorporate safety constraints directly into the cost functions. While some (e.g.,) predict forces for assistive tasks, they may lack the generalizability for precise, direct contact tasks.
- **Shielding/Barrier Methods:** Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) are increasingly used to provide formal safety guarantees by acting as a runtime safety filter. While powerful, some prior CBF methods can lead to abrupt actions, affecting interaction fluidity, or rely on learned models/dynamics estimations that are often inaccurate in RL-generated trajectories.

ContactRL differentiates itself by explicitly integrating contact safety, quantified by contact force, into the RL reward function, allowing the robot to *learn* to modulate forces during intentional contact. Furthermore, it addresses the inherent lack of formal safety guarantees in RL by augmenting the learned policy with a kinetic energy-based Control Barrier Function (eCBF) shield during deployment. This hybrid approach – learning safe contact behavior through reward shaping and ensuring hard safety limits with a provable shield – represents a significant advancement over methods that either treat all contact as unsafe or struggle with the adaptability and robustness required for dynamic, contact-rich HRI. The paper directly tackles the critical challenge of moving beyond collision avoidance to safe, intentional physical interaction, paving the way for more natural and capable human-robot collaboration in diverse applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robots to perform collaborative tasks that require **safe, intentional physical contact** with humans, specifically focusing on minimizing discomfort or injury. This objective is motivated by the increasing prevalence of human-robot collaboration in contexts like Industry 5.0, where robots need to interact physically with humans in close proximity.

The key motivations driving this work can be summarized as follows:
- **Addressing the Gap in HRC Safety:** Traditional robotic safety frameworks primarily focus on collision *avoidance*. However, many collaborative tasks (e.g., object handovers, assistive tasks like dressing) inherently necessitate physical contact. The current lack of robust methods to ensure safety during these necessary contact events presents a significant barrier to wider HRC deployment.
- **Ensuring Human Comfort and Safety during Contact:** The paper highlights the need for robots to pick up small objects directly from a human hand, a task where unintended contact is highly probable. The goal is to perform such tasks without causing discomfort or injury, defining safety specifically in terms of limiting the normal contact force (FN) exerted on the human. The paper aims to keep these forces below established human comfort limits.
- **Balancing Safety and Task Efficiency:** Learning to maintain low contact forces while simultaneously achieving task goals (e.g., rapid and precise object retrieval) introduces a complex trade-off. Existing methods often sacrifice one for the other. This research seeks to develop a framework that effectively balances these two critical aspects, allowing for both safe and efficient interaction.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Conventional Methods:** Compliance control techniques, while useful, struggle with adaptability to dynamic human environments and require extensive manual tuning. Pure RL methods often lack formal safety guarantees, which are non-negotiable for human-robot collaboration. The motivation is to combine the strengths of adaptive learning with provable safety mechanisms.

In essence, the authors aim to develop a robust and adaptive motion planning framework that explicitly learns and enforces safe contact behavior for robots, thereby facilitating the deployment of collaborative robots in real-world scenarios that demand close and direct physical interaction with humans.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **ContactRL**, a novel Reinforcement Learning (RL) based framework, to achieve safe motion planning for human-robot close-contact interaction. Their approach combines a carefully designed reward function for RL training with a kinetic energy-based Control Barrier Function (eCBF) shield for runtime safety guarantees.

**A. Reinforcement Learning Framework (ContactRL Core)**
1. **Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation:** The "pick-from-hand-safely" task is modeled as an MDP.
 - **Observation Space (S):** A 9-dimensional vector consisting of the robot end-effector's 3D position (pr), the human hand's 3D position (ph), and the end-effector's 3D velocity (vr). Hand position is randomized at each episode to improve generalizability.
 - **Action Space (A):** A 3D continuous space representing Cartesian displacements of the robot's end-effector ([δx, δy, δz]). The magnitude of these actions is bounded to ensure smooth execution.
 - **Reward Design (R):** A dense, multi-component reward function is crucial for balancing performance and safety:
 - **Reach Reward (rr):** Penalizes distance between the robot end-effector and the hand, incentivizing movement towards the target. A significant bonus (500) is awarded upon successful contact with the object.
 - **Safety Reward (rs):** This is the core safety component. It gives a positive reward for maintaining contact forces (FN) below a predefined threshold (Fτ = 50 N) and a penalty if FN exceeds Fτ. This directly encourages gentle contact.
 - **Jerk Reward (rj):** Penalizes large differences between consecutive actions to promote smooth, non-jerky motions, which are vital for human comfort.
 - **Proximity Reward (rp):** Encourages larger steps initially and smaller, more precise steps as the robot approaches the human hand, optimizing task completion time while ensuring a careful approach.
 - An ablation study is performed to empirically determine optimal weights for these reward components.
 - **Transition Dynamics (P):** Utilizes deterministic rigid-body dynamics in PyBullet, with episodes terminating upon object grasp or after 150 timesteps.
2. **Training Setup:**
 - **Algorithm:** Soft Actor–Critic (SAC), an off-policy algorithm suitable for continuous action spaces, chosen for its ability to maximize both expected return and entropy, promoting robust exploration.
 - **Environment:** PyBullet simulation environment, featuring a UR3e robotic manipulator with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper. A simplified flat-plane model is used for the human hand to reduce computational complexity while mimicking real-world hand physics with defined mass and friction coefficients.
 - **Robustness:** Policies are trained with five independent seeds to account for stochastic elements and report mean and standard deviation.

**B. Energy Barrier Safety Shield (eCBF)** To address the lack of formal safety guarantees in RL, ContactRL augments the learned policy with a kinetic energy-based Control Barrier Function (eCBF) shield at deployment.
1. **Implicit Force Regulation:** Unlike force model-based CBFs, which require explicit contact dynamics, the eCBF implicitly regulates contact force by bounding the end-effector's kinetic energy (KE).
2. **QP Formulation:** The shield works by solving a Quadratic Program (QP) that unifies an energy-based zeroing CBF with a goal-tracking Control Lyapunov Function (CLF). This allows for real-time force safety to be embedded into policy execution.
3. **Shield Operation (Algorithm 1):**
 - **Low-Pass Filter (LPF):** The raw Cartesian velocity command from the SAC policy is first smoothed using a first-order LPF to suppress high-frequency jitter, enhancing fluidity.
 - **Kinetic Energy Constraint:** The eCBF enforces the safety invariant: ½ * m * ||v||² ≤ Emax, where m is the end-effector mass and Emax is an empirically chosen kinetic energy budget (0.30 J), derived from safe force limits (Fτ = 50 N).
 - **Action Projection:** If the commanded velocity from the LPF exceeds the speed limit implied by Emax, the eCBF shield radially scales the velocity vector down to ensure the kinetic energy constraint is met. Otherwise, the command passes unchanged. This projection is direction-preserving and computationally lightweight.
4. **Guaranteed Safety:** The eCBF shield acts as a runtime safety filter, provably preventing the robot's kinetic energy (and thus implicitly, maximum contact force) from exceeding safe limits without retraining the RL policy. It ensures forward-invariance of a bounded energy set, guaranteeing safe and dissipative contact behavior.

**C. Real-World Validation:**
The shielded ContactRL policy is deployed on a physical UR3e robotic platform. Experiments involve object handovers from human participants, with contact forces measured by the robot's integrated force/torque sensor. Multiple objects, poses, and participants are used to validate real-world performance and safety.

In summary, the methodology combines the adaptability and learning capabilities of deep reinforcement learning (SAC with a tailored reward function) for acquiring complex contact behaviors, with the robust, provable safety guarantees provided by an energy-based Control Barrier Function shield, ensuring safe deployment in human-robot collaboration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness and safety of the ContactRL framework.

**A. Ablation Study of Reward Function:**
The ablation study, conducted over 1000 simulated episodes, highlighted the critical role of each reward component:
- **RF1 (Reach-only):** Achieved the highest cumulative reward but resulted in a high safety violation rate of 52%, with frequent impulsive contacts exceeding 50 N.
- **RF2 (Reach + Safety):** Significantly reduced the safety violation rate to 13% by introducing the force-safety reward, without compromising task completion time.
- **RF4 (Reach + Safety + Jerk):** Further improved motion smoothness by tightening speed distribution.
- **RF5 (Reach + Safety + Jerk + Proximity):** Emerged as the optimal configuration, achieving the fastest safe completion time, a median peak force below 10 N, and a minimal safety violation rate of **0.2%**. This demonstrated that a balanced reward function is key to achieving both high performance and safety.

**B. SOTA Comparison in Simulated Environment:**
ContactRL was compared against state-of-the-art constrained RL baselines, Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) and SAC-Lagrangian (SACLag), over 1000 simulated episodes.
- **Task Success Rate:** ContactRL significantly outperformed baselines, achieving an 87.17% success rate, compared to CPO's 33.33% and SACLag's 55.60%.
- **Completion Time:** ContactRL achieved competitive average completion times (0.50 ± 0.12 s).
- **Safety Violation Rate:** ContactRL maintained a very low safety violation rate of **0.20%**, matching SACLag and significantly outperforming CPO (2.41%).
- **Motion Smoothness (RMS Jerk):** ContactRL exhibited substantially lower RMS jerk (931.32 m/s³) compared to CPO (1355.47 m/s³) and SACLag (3924.11 m/s³), indicating smoother, more comfortable motion profiles.
These results confirm ContactRL's superior performance in terms of task success, safety, and motion smoothness.

**C. Shielded vs. Unshielded Policy in Simulation:**
Evaluation with stochastic actions (representing real-world dynamics) revealed the necessity of the eCBF shield:
- **Unshielded Policy:** Showed persistent low-amplitude oscillations and sporadic late-stage impulses in kinetic energy and velocity profiles, indicating potential for unsafe interactions despite LPF smoothing.
- **Shielded Policy (LPF + eCBF):** Eliminated jitter-induced motion, yielding monotonically converging trajectories without secondary transients. The eCBF shield dynamically projects commanded velocities to stay within safe kinetic energy limits, proving essential for dependable real-world deployment. While occasionally lengthening approach time slightly, the safety benefits were paramount.

**D. Contact Force Analysis with Physical Robotic Platform:** Real-world experiments involved 12 participants, 5 different objects, and 2 hand poses, totaling 360 object handover trials using the UR3e robot with the eCBF-shielded ContactRL policy.
- **Consistent Safe Contact:** The most critical finding was that measured normal forces (FN) remained consistently below **10 N** across all participants and conditions, well within the safe human contact limit of 50 N. The majority of trials recorded forces below 3 N, with a mode near 1 N, confirming comfortable physical interaction.
- **Effect of Object Geometry:** Object geometry significantly influenced maximum forces (p < 0.001), with the cylinder and screw generally leading to higher forces, and the cube to the lowest.
- **Effect of Pose:** Pose 2 (closer approach) resulted in higher contact forces than Pose 1 (far-side approach) (p = 0.009).
- **Inter-participant Variability:** Notable differences in contact force regulation were observed between participants, reflecting individual hand positioning and comfort thresholds. These results unequivocally validate that ContactRL, augmented with the eCBF shield, enables safe object handovers within human comfort limits in real-world scenarios.

**E. Limitations of Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
While safe, minor discrepancies in positional accuracy were observed in real-world reaching trials:
- **Positional Error:** Mean position errors ranged from 0.0168 m (x-axis) to 0.0420 m (z-axis). Approximately 50% of reaches had a radial position error below 1 cm, but not all.
- **Causes:** Attributed to sim-to-real gap (modeling discrepancies) and the inherent safety-accuracy trade-off where eCBF-constrained control suppresses aggressive corrective motions near the target.

**F. Discussion on Advantages:**
The paper highlights that ContactRL with eCBF shield offers explicit safety guarantees independent of human hand impedance or object geometry, unlike variable impedance control (VIC) which requires sensitive gain tuning. This decoupling of performance optimization and safety enforcement allows for fluent motions while ensuring interventions only when necessary, avoiding the trade-offs inherent in conservative VIC tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ContactRL framework represents a significant step forward in the field of human-robot collaboration, particularly for tasks requiring physical contact. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Enabling Safe and Intentional Physical Contact:** Unlike prior Safe RL frameworks that equate safety with non-contact, ContactRL explicitly allows and enforces safe, intentional contact. This is crucial for expanding the range of tasks robots can perform in close collaboration with humans, moving beyond mere collision avoidance to genuine physical interaction. The demonstrated sub-10 N contact forces in real-world trials validate that this can be achieved within human comfort limits.

2. **Addressing a Critical HRC Barrier:** The ability to retrieve objects directly and safely from a human hand, especially small or delicate items, opens up new possibilities in precision manufacturing, healthcare, and assistive robotics. This ensures secure handling of objects that might be difficult to transfer in alternative, non-contact poses, enhancing efficiency and reliability in collaborative workflows.

3. **Robustness through Hybrid Approach:** The combination of an RL-based motion planning policy (for adaptability and learning complex contact behaviors) and a kinetic energy-based Control Barrier Function (eCBF) shield (for provable, hard safety guarantees at runtime) offers a powerful and robust solution. This hybrid methodology addresses the inherent limitations of pure RL (lack of formal safety guarantees) and traditional control methods (reliance on precise models, manual tuning, and limited adaptability).

4. **Advancing Safe Reinforcement Learning:** By demonstrating that a carefully designed reward function can effectively guide an RL agent to learn low-force contact behaviors, and that an external shield can robustly guarantee safety, ContactRL contributes to the broader development of truly safe learning-based control systems for physical interaction.

5. **Generalizability to Diverse Applications:** While demonstrated for object handovers, the principles of ContactRL—learning to modulate contact forces and ensuring safety through energy-based barriers—are highly generalizable. The authors explicitly mention potential applications in assistive scenarios such as dressing and bathing, where intentional and gentle physical contact is paramount. This could lead to more effective and widely accepted assistive robots.

6. **Paving the Way for More Intuitive HRI:** The framework's ability to achieve smooth, non-jerky motions, as evidenced by low RMS jerk, contributes to a more natural and intuitive interaction experience for humans. Reduced abruptness and guaranteed safety increase user trust and acceptance of collaborative robots.

In conclusion, ContactRL is a landmark contribution that tackles the challenging problem of safe human-robot physical interaction head-on. By providing a practical and provably safe framework for intentional contact, it significantly advances the state-of-art in collaborative robotics and paves the way for the broader deployment of robots in a myriad of contact-rich tasks, enhancing both productivity and safety in future human-robot work environments. Future work will continue to refine these capabilities, including incorporating more subjective measures of human comfort and narrowing the sim-to-real gap.
