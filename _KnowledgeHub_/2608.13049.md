# H2R-Bench: Benchmarking Human-to-Robot Manipulation Video Generation in World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> H2R-Bench is a benchmark evaluating whether video world models can turn egocentric human manipulation demonstrations into robot videos of a specified embodiment. It pairs 120 EgoDex clips with parallel-jaw-gripper and dexterous-hand targets, scoring goal completion, action events, functional contact, embodiment correctness, and video quality via MLLM judges. Across eleven generators, H2RCore spans 30.0-84.6 while video quality barely varies.

## Problem

- General video-generation benchmarks measure only visual fidelity, temporal coherence, and text alignment, so they cannot tell whether a generated robot execution is a valid transfer.
- Robotics world-model benchmarks condition generation on text, images, or robot-centric initial states, never on a human demonstration, leaving cross-embodiment human-to-robot transfer unevaluated.
- Robot demonstrations are expensive while egocentric human video is abundant, yet no protocol diagnoses failures like human actor leakage, unsupported contact, or wrong end-effector morphology.

## Method

- ==Source-conditioned transfer task==: each instance pairs an egocentric clip, a target embodiment, and a prompt; 120 EgoDex clips times two embodiments give 240 cases over six manipulation families.
- ==Human-verified structured annotations==, initialized by a multimodal language model, record initial and final states, required action events, manipulated entities, and source-side functional-contact evidence without prescribing any robot trajectory.
- ==Transfer-aware protocol== scores goal, action-event, functional-contact, and embodiment metrics with three ==MLLM judges== on a shared 0-4 rubric, plus task-agnostic quality; **H2RCore** weights them 0.15/0.15/0.30/0.30/0.10.

## Results

- Video-conditioned models lead: Seedance 2.0 reaches H2RCore **77.3** gripper and **84.6** hand, ahead of Wan2.7 (**76.5**/**83.1**) and Kling-V3 (**74.5**/**81.7**), separated mainly by contact and embodiment.
- Quality decouples from transfer: video quality spans only **0.73-0.81** while H2RCore spans **30.0-84.6** with rank correlation **0.14**; HunyuanVideo tops quality at **0.806** but scores **30.0** core.
- Dexterous-hand targets gain **3.3** H2RCore on average, with contact transfer rising for all **11** models; three human raters agree with the automated judges at Spearman **0.883**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual plausibility is not evidence of embodied transfer; evaluation must score functional contact and morphology separately, or polished failures rank first.
> - A target morphology closer to the human demonstrator preserves contact more easily, making gripper-shaped end-effectors the harder cross-embodiment generalization case.
> - Target-robot reference images help only some models, adding 6.6 points for one generator while costing another 13.5, so conditioning aids stay model-dependent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a challenge in robot learning related to data acquisition and transfer. Recent advancements in robot learning underscore the necessity of extensive manipulation data encompassing diverse tasks, objects, and environments. However, obtaining robot demonstrations is resource-intensive, requiring specialized hardware, teleoperation systems, calibrated cameras, and adherence to safety protocols. This process is difficult to scale effectively.

Conversely, egocentric human manipulation videos are widely available and naturally capture rich behavioral experiences, including object affordances, hand-object contacts, manipulation intent, and physical state changes. A key challenge lies in effectively transferring these human-centric observations across different embodiments to robots, given the morphological and kinematic disparities between human hands and robotic end-effectors.

Video world models have emerged as a method for synthesizing robot-centric manipulation videos from human observations. However, the capability of these models to perform cross-embodiment transfer, specifically from human to robot actions, remains largely unexamined. Existing benchmarks for video generation primarily focus on visual fidelity, temporal consistency, text-video alignment, and low-level motion quality. More recent robotics-oriented benchmarks evaluate aspects such as physical plausibility, action completeness, and robot structure, but they typically condition generation on text, images, or initial robot states, rather than explicitly evaluating cross-embodiment transfer from human demonstrations. This limitation means that current evaluation protocols often cannot diagnose failures specific to human-to-robot (H2R) transfer, such as incorrect embodiment replacement or functional interactions inconsistent with the source human demonstration. H2R-Bench positions itself to fill this gap by providing a specific framework for evaluating this cross-embodiment transfer capability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce H2R-Bench, a benchmark designed to systematically evaluate the cross-embodiment human-to-robot manipulation video generation capabilities of world models.

The motivation for developing H2R-Bench stems from several factors:
- **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robot Learning:** Collecting large-scale, diverse robot manipulation data is a significant bottleneck for advancing robot learning. Human manipulation videos offer an abundant, scalable alternative, but their utility for robot training is contingent on effective transfer mechanisms.
- **Evaluating Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The potential of video world models to bridge the gap between human and robot embodiments by synthesizing robot-centric videos from human observations is a promising avenue. However, a dedicated framework for assessing the effectiveness and fidelity of this transfer was lacking. The authors aim to explore the extent to which these models can convert human observations into a format usable by robots.
- **Diagnosing Transfer-Specific Failures:** Existing video generation benchmarks and robotics-specific world model evaluations are not equipped to diagnose the nuances of H2R transfer. They may rate a generated video highly based on visual plausibility or general action completion, even if it fails to accurately transfer the human's functional interaction or adhere to the specified robot embodiment. The authors are motivated to create a benchmark that can identify these specific failure modes.
- **Establishing a Systematic Evaluation Protocol:** The paper seeks to provide a comprehensive and structured evaluation protocol that goes beyond surface-level visual quality. This protocol is intended to scrutinize whether generated robot videos preserve crucial elements from the source human demonstration, including task goals, required action events, functional object interactions, and object-state changes, while accurately rendering the specified robot morphology and end-effector.
- **Guiding Future Research:** By identifying the limitations of current state-of-the-art models in human-to-robot manipulation transfer, H2R-Bench aims to provide insights that can guide the development of more capable video world models, ultimately facilitating the transformation of human manipulation observations into valuable robot-centric training resources.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of H2R-Bench involves a multi-faceted approach encompassing task formulation, benchmark construction, a standardized generation protocol, and a detailed, transfer-aware evaluation framework.

**Task Formulation:**
H2R-Bench defines a transfer task where a model receives an egocentric human manipulation video ($V^h_i$), a target robot embodiment ($e_i$), and a text prompt ($p_i$). The model's objective is to generate a robot manipulation video ($V^r_{i,m}$) of the same task. A key aspect is that the source human video is treated as functional evidence, not merely a visual style reference. Successful generation requires preserving the task goal, necessary actions, and visible human-object interactions, while adapting the execution to the specified robot morphology and end-effector. The benchmark does not require exact pose or trajectory imitation.

**Benchmark Construction:**
- **Source Videos:** 120 egocentric clips from the EgoDex test split (Hoque et al. 2025) are utilized as source demonstrations. These clips were selected for visible task-relevant entities, interactions, and state changes.
- **Transfer Cases:** 240 transfer cases are created by pairing each of the 120 source videos with two target robot embodiments: a parallel-jaw gripper and a dexterous hand.
- **Task Families:** The source videos are evenly distributed across six manipulation families (F1-F6), categorized by the physical state change defining task success: rigid-object rearrangement, mechanism actuation, insertion and assembly, deformable-object configuration, bulk-material transfer, and surface or material transformation.
- **Structured Annotations:** A multimodal language model (Qwen3.7-Plus) generates an initial structured annotation for each 5-second source clip. This annotation details initial and final states, relevant objects, required action events, and source-side contact evidence. For each target embodiment, it further specifies functional contact regions, manipulation modes, expected object responses, and bimanual roles. All annotations undergo manual verification against their respective source videos.

**Native-Interface Generation Protocol:**
All evaluated models receive the same source cases, target embodiments, and task specifications. Models utilize their strongest publicly documented source-conditioning interface. Video-conditioned models receive the full source clip, while image/frame-conditioned models receive an ordered subset of source frames (e.g., first and last, or up to seven uniformly spaced frames). The text prompt consistently specifies the task goal and target embodiment, without including evaluation weights or evidence-frame indices. The main evaluation setting does not provide a target-robot reference image; the target is specified textually. Generated videos are kept at their native resolution, duration, and frame rate, but evaluation employs a fixed number of uniformly sampled frames.

**Transfer-Aware Evaluation:**
H2R-Bench employs five complementary metrics (M1-M5) to score generated videos:
- **M1: Goal-State Completion (S_goal):** Assesses whether the task's final state is achieved based on weighted predicates (e.g., spatial relation, mechanism state, attachment). Judges receive 25 uniformly sampled frames to determine predicate satisfaction.
- **M2: Action-Event Completion (S_action):** Evaluates if the required action events (e.g., grasping, inserting, pouring) are clearly performed, without requiring exact human pose or trajectory imitation. Judges use 25 sampled frames.
- **M3: Functional Contact Transfer (S_contact):** Determines if the robot establishes an interaction functionally equivalent to the human's. Judges compare 25 source frames and 25 generated frames against a contact specification. This includes evaluating contact region, establishment, manipulation mode, object response, and embodiment compatibility. A prerequisite is source grounding; substantial scene or task-entity substitution results in a zero score for this metric.
- **M4: Embodiment Correctness (S_emb):** Measures consistency with the requested robot embodiment. Judges assess robot presence, human hand absence, broad embodiment category, end-effector type, and structural consistency over time. A zero score is assigned if no robot is visible or if a human performs the main manipulation.
- **M5: Video Quality (S_video):** A task-agnostic metric computed automatically. It averages four normalized components: imaging quality (MUSIQ), aesthetic quality (LAION linear predictor on CLIP features), temporal stability (adjacent-frame differences), and motion smoothness (AMT-S midpoint interpolation error).

For M1-M4, three independent Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) judges (Gemini 3.5 Flash, Qwen3.7-Plus, and GPT-5.4) score the visual evidence using a shared 0-4 rubric, normalized to, and averaged. M5 is computed automatically.

**H2RCore (Aggregate Score):**
The primary benchmark score, H2RCore, combines the five normalized components with specific weights:
`H2RCore = 100 * (0.15 * S_goal + 0.15 * S_action + 0.30 * S_contact + 0.30 * S_emb + 0.10 * S_video)`
This weighting scheme emphasizes functional contact transfer and embodiment correctness, allocating 60% of the total score, while giving a smaller contribution to general video quality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved 11 representative video generation models (five proprietary, six open-source) across 240 transfer cases.

- **Overall Model Performance:**
 - The three video-conditioned models (Seedance 2.0, Wan2.7, and Kling-V3) generally occupied the top positions in H2RCore scores.
 - Seedance 2.0 achieved the highest H2RCore scores (77.3 for the Parallel-Jaw Gripper and 84.6 for the Dexterous Hand). Wan2.7 and Kling-V3 followed with slightly lower scores.
 - The performance difference among these leading models was primarily attributed to variations in contact transfer (M3) and embodiment correctness (M4), rather than goal completion (M1) or action completion (M2).

- **Discrepancy Between Visual Quality and Transfer Capability:**
 - A significant finding was the observed disparity between models' general video quality (M5) and their human-to-robot transfer capability (H2RCore).
 - Models achieving high M5 scores (e.g., HunyuanVideo 1.5-I2V, highest M5 for both targets at ~0.80) often performed poorly on H2RCore (e.g., ~30.0) due to low functional contact transfer and embodiment correctness scores.
 - The Spearman rank correlation (ρ) between M5-based Video Quality and H2RCore was 0.14, indicating a weak association between visual plausibility and effective embodied transfer. This suggests that a visually polished video does not guarantee successful robot manipulation transfer.

- **Embodiment Effects:**
 - **Parallel-Jaw Gripper vs. Dexterous Hand:** Across the 11 models, 9 showed higher H2RCore scores when generating for the Dexterous Hand, with an average H2RCore gain of 3.3 points. This improvement was primarily driven by higher scores in functional contact transfer (+0.055) and embodiment correctness (+0.047). The authors suggest this may be due to the Dexterous Hand's morphology being closer to the human hand, which could facilitate better preservation of contact during actor replacement.
 - **Impact of Target-Robot Reference Images:** Providing a target-robot reference image did not offer a uniform benefit. While Wan2.7 showed an increase in H2RCore (from 76.5 to 83.1 for gripper, 83.1 to 84.2 for hand), Kling-V3 and Seedance 2.0 experienced decreases in their H2RCore scores with reference images. Video quality remained largely unaffected. This indicates that the utility of reference images is model-dependent and can influence adherence to both robot appearance and source interaction.

- **Diagnostic Failures:** Analysis of structured M2-M4 diagnostics revealed common failure types. Image/frame-conditioned models frequently exhibited human-led manipulation and failures in visible contact support or contact region/mode. Transfer to the Parallel-Jaw Gripper often suffered from end-effector mismatch, while missing required action events was observed across both embodiments.

- **Human Evaluation Agreement:** Human raters independently scoring M1-M4 on a subset of generated videos showed strong agreement with the MLLM judges. The macro-average Human–MLLM Spearman correlation for within-scene rankings was ρ = 0.883, and the aggregate Pearson correlation for transfer subscores (M1-M4) was r = 0.930. This suggests that the automated MLLM-based evaluation effectively captures human judgments regarding transfer quality.

- **Performance Across Task Families and Attributes:** H2RCore scores varied by manipulation family. Deformable-object configuration (F4) generally showed the strongest performance for both target embodiments. Performance also differed between tasks involving direct manipulation versus tool use, and tasks with fewer versus more required action events, indicating task-specific challenges in transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

H2R-Bench makes a contribution to the field of robot learning and video generation by providing a dedicated framework for evaluating human-to-robot manipulation video transfer. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Systematic Diagnostic Framework:** The benchmark introduces a granular evaluation protocol with five complementary dimensions (goal completion, action-event completion, functional contact transfer, embodiment correctness, and video quality). This allows for systematic diagnosis of specific failure modes in generated robot videos, moving beyond generalized visual quality assessments. Researchers can pinpoint whether failures stem from task execution, interaction fidelity, or adherence to the target embodiment.
- **Revealing a Critical Gap:** The study identifies a substantial discrepancy between the visual generation quality of current video world models and their actual capability for embodied human-to-robot transfer. Models that produce visually plausible videos may still fail significantly in accurately translating functional interactions or adhering to specified robot morphologies. This finding highlights a key challenge that needs to be addressed for video world models to be truly useful in robotics.
- **Guiding Future Model Development:** By exposing the limitations of current state-of-the-art models, H2R-Bench provides clear targets for future research and development. It indicates that advancements are needed not just in generating realistic video frames, but in deeply understanding and transferring the underlying physical interactions, task semantics, and embodiment constraints from human demonstrations to robotic systems.
- **Enabling Scalable Robot Learning:** The long-term impact of H2R-Bench lies in its potential to facilitate the transformation of abundant human manipulation observations into reliable, robot-centric training resources. By providing a robust means to evaluate the fidelity of such synthetic data, the benchmark can accelerate the development of world models capable of bridging the human-to-robot embodiment gap, thereby addressing the significant data scarcity problem in robot learning.
- **Standardization for Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** H2R-Bench establishes a standardized benchmark for an important, yet underexplored, area. This standardization can foster direct comparisons between different video generation models and encourage research into novel architectures and methodologies specifically designed for cross-embodiment task transfer.
