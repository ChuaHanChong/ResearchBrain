# Optimizing Bipedal Locomotion for The 100m Dash With Comparison to Human Running

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Oregon State University optimized the bipedal robot Cassie for high-speed locomotion, achieving a Guinness World Record for the 100m dash in 24.37 seconds. The study systematically identified efficient gait parameters and compared Cassie's running mechanics to human locomotion, finding notable similarities and specific differences.

## Problem

- High-speed running for general-purpose bipedal robots like Cassie lacked systematic optimization of gait parameters (e.g., stride frequency, swing ratio), relying on fixed or hand-tuned values.
- Existing high-speed bipedal locomotion research was often limited to simplified 2D models or robots specifically designed for running, not general-purpose platforms like Cassie.
- A robust controller capable of stably executing a full 100m dash, including starting, accelerating, maintaining speed, and stopping, on a bipedal robot was not available.

## Method

- A hierarchical controller, based on a recurrent LSTM neural network, was trained using sim-to-real reinforcement learning (PPO with dynamics randomization) on the Cassie robot platform.
- A systematic search in simulation identified efficient swing ratio and stride frequency values across a spectrum of speeds, generating an optimized speed-to-parameter curve.
- A specialized two-policy control system with robust transition management was developed to execute the full 100m dash, integrating the optimized running policy with a static standing policy.

## Results

- Cassie achieved a Guinness World Record for the Fastest 100m Dash by a Bipedal Robot with a time of **24.37** seconds on hardware, corresponding to an average speed of **4.10** m/s.
- Simulation indicated potential for even faster 100m dash times (approximately 22 seconds at 5 m/s average speed), highlighting a remaining sim-to-real gap.
- The robust policy transition strategy, particularly from stepping to standing, improved reliability from under **10%** to **100%** across multiple trials.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimized gait parameters for higher speeds favored 'long, infrequent steps' with a significant aerial phase, a strategy that reduced the need for excessively high ground reaction forces.
> - Cassie's optimized running mechanics showed qualitative similarities to human running in terms of stride length, frequency, and swing/aerial times, suggesting shared principles of efficient bipedal locomotion.
> - A key difference from human running was Cassie's effective ground reaction force (GRF_eff) tapering at approximately 3 m/s, attributed to hip-roll motor torque limitations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of bipedal robot locomotion has seen significant advancements in recent years, largely driven by reinforcement learning (RL) techniques and sim-to-real transfer capabilities. Prior research has successfully enabled bipedal robots to perform a variety of gaits such as walking, running, and skipping, navigate complex terrains like stairs, and handle dynamic loads. Notably, robots have demonstrated endurance in tasks like completing a 5k run. These achievements typically involve moderate speeds, often up to approximately 2 meters per second (m/s) on physical hardware.

However, existing work on high-speed running for bipedal robots has faced several limitations. Some approaches have relied on simplified 2D approximations of robot dynamics, which may not translate effectively to complex 3D environments. Other research has involved robots specifically designed for running, limiting the generalizability of their findings to multi-purpose bipedal platforms like Cassie, which are capable of a wider range of gaits. A significant challenge in this domain has been the selection and modulation of gait parameters (such as stride frequency and swing ratio) with respect to varying speeds. Previous studies either fixed these parameters or employed hand-tuned mappings, lacking a systematic approach to optimize them across a continuous spectrum of velocities.

This paper addresses these limitations by focusing on enabling high-speed bipedal running for a general-purpose robot (Cassie) through a principled optimization of gait parameters. It extends the capabilities of bipedal locomotion beyond moderate speeds and investigates the underlying mechanics in comparison to human running. By exploring and optimizing gait parameters systematically, the research aims to achieve unprecedented running speeds on hardware and establish a new benchmark, specifically the 100m dash world record for a bipedal robot. This work contributes to the broader understanding of efficient bipedal locomotion for both artificial and biological systems, bridging the gap between theoretical models and real-world high-performance robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to investigate the upper limits of a bipedal robot's running speed and to develop a robust, efficient controller capable of maintaining stability across a wide range of velocities. This overarching goal is articulated through three specific objectives:

1. **Optimize Gait Efficiency Across a Spectrum of Speeds for the Cassie Robot:**
 The existing methods for selecting gait parameters in bipedal robots, such as stride frequency and swing ratio, have largely relied on fixed values or hand-tuned mappings from speed to parameters. There has been a lack of a systematic approach to explore how these parameters should be modulated to achieve optimal efficiency and performance across different speeds on a real-world bipedal robot. This objective aims to fill this gap by developing a principled method to explore the gait parameter space. The motivation is to generate an optimized speed-to-parameter curve that enables extremely high-speed running on hardware, surpassing the limitations of prior heuristic approaches. This also seeks to understand how gait parameters should intrinsically change with commanded speed for maximum efficiency and stability.

2. **Conduct a Comparative Analysis of Optimized Robot Gaits with Human Running Mechanics:**
 Human running is recognized as a highly efficient form of bipedal locomotion, often cited as a benchmark for robotic systems. Despite significant morphological differences between robots like Cassie and humans, understanding the kinematic and dynamic similarities or differences that emerge from optimized robot gaits can provide valuable insights. The motivation behind this objective is to empirically compare the optimized running mechanics of Cassie to established human biomechanical studies. This comparison aims to reveal whether fundamental principles of efficient bipedal locomotion observed in humans spontaneously manifest in an optimized robotic system, thus offering potential bio-inspiration for future robot design and control. It also serves to validate the "naturalness" and efficiency of the optimized robotic gaits.

3. **Integrate Optimized Running Gaits into a Full Controller for the 100m Dash and Demonstrate on Hardware:**
 The final objective is to integrate the optimized running gaits into a comprehensive control system that can execute a real-world, high-stakes task: the 100m dash. This task presents additional challenges beyond steady-state running, including reliably starting from a static standing position, accelerating to top speed, maintaining that speed, decelerating after crossing the finish line, and returning to a static standing pose, all without falling. The motivation for this objective is to demonstrate the practical applicability and robustness of the developed optimization and control strategies in a complete, challenging scenario. Successfully executing the 100m dash and establishing a Guinness World Record serves as a tangible benchmark, showcasing the state-of-the-art capabilities in bipedal robot locomotion and providing a public milestone for future research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach involving reinforcement learning, gait parameter optimization, biomechanical comparison, and robust controller integration for a real-world challenge.

**Robot Platform and Controller Architecture:**
The bipedal robot Cassie served as the hardware platform for this research. The control system utilized a hierarchical architecture: a slower, high-level controller (policy) generated PD (Proportional-Derivative) targets at 40 Hz, which were then executed by low-level PD controllers operating at 2 kHz with fixed gains. The running controller's input consisted of a 35-dimensional state vector (joint positions/velocities, pelvis orientation/rotational velocity), a clock signal (phase) encoding the gait cycle, gait parameters (swing ratio and stride frequency), and a target forward velocity command. The controller itself was implemented as a recurrent LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) neural network, chosen for its ability to incorporate memory into the state, which has been shown to improve sim-to-real transfer.

**Sim-to-Real Training Paradigm:**
Controllers were trained using sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) via the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with a clipped objective. Training was conducted within the MuJoCo physics engine, incorporating dynamics randomization (e.g., varying friction and center of mass) to enhance the transferability of learned policies from simulation to the real robot. A dense reward function, adapted from prior work, was designed to encourage controllers to match specified gait parameters (swing ratio and stride frequency) by rewarding behaviors aligned with the clock signal, such as a foot in stance having zero velocity.

**Gait Parameter Optimization:**
To systematically optimize gait parameters, the researchers first trained a general policy across a broad spectrum of swing ratio (`ratio`) and stride frequency (`freq`) values. This involved varying these parameters within offsets (0.2 for `ratio`, 0.625 for `freq`) from a previously hand-tuned mapping, across commanded speeds ranging from 0 to 5 m/s.

Following this broad training, a search was conducted in simulation to identify the most efficient `ratio` and `freq` values for discrete speed commands. For each speed and parameter combination, Cassie was allowed 50-250 policy steps to accelerate and settle into a stable gait, followed by 100 policy steps for data collection. The performance of each gait was assessed using an overall score derived from four cost terms, each weighted to contribute similarly:
- **Speed error:** Deviation from the commanded speed.
- **Cost of transport:** Energy consumed per unit distance traveled.
- **Torque cost:** Average torque applied by the motors.
- **Motor velocity:** Average speed of the motors.
Simulations where Cassie fell were assigned a score of zero. This scoring metric primarily prioritized efficiency over speed fidelity, although top-scoring combinations exhibited low speed error. The top five scoring gait parameter combinations at each speed were then used to derive an optimized speed-to-parameter curve, fit via a 3rd-degree polynomial regression.

**Comparison to Human Running Mechanics:**
To evaluate the "naturalness" and efficiency of the optimized gaits, various running mechanics of Cassie were measured and compared to published human biomechanical data. The human data, particularly for effective ground reaction force (GRF_eff), was sourced from (Weyand et al., 2000). Key metrics analyzed included stride length, stride frequency, swing-time, aerial time, effective ground reaction force, and effective impulse. These measurements were presented in body-agnostic units to facilitate direct comparison between the robot and humans. The comparison specifically focused on speeds where Cassie developed an aerial phase (running, starting from 2 m/s).

**100m Dash Controller Integration:**
For the 100m dash task, a specialized controller was developed to meet the Guinness World Record rules, which involve five stages: static standing start, acceleration, steady-state running, deceleration after crossing the finish line, and static standing stop.
- **Controller Specialization:** The running policy was trained only for non-negative forward speeds up to 5 m/s, with strafing and turning commands removed from its command set to specialize its representational power.
- **Two-Policy System:** Due to the specialized running policy's inability to maintain a static standing pose, a two-policy system was implemented: the optimized running policy for stages 2-4 and a pre-trained standing policy for stages 1 and 5.
- **Policy Transition Management:** Critical attention was given to robustly transitioning between these policies:
 - **Standing to Stepping:** The transition from the standing policy to the running policy was synchronized with the running policy's internal gait `phase`. The controller would wait for a specific `phase` where both feet were at rest, pressing down (identified by crossing ground reaction forces, Figure 5), before swapping policies and initializing the running policy's `phase`.
 - **Stepping to Standing:** This transition was more challenging. To enable the less stable standing policy to effectively plant a foot, the controller waited for a `phase` where either foot was at its apex (highest point in its swing phase, Figure 6). This allowed the standing policy to position the foot for a wide, stable base. Additionally, the speed command was set to a small negative value (e.g., -0.1 m/s) just before the transition, ensuring the running policy stepped in place rather than creeping forward, which significantly improved the transition's reliability from below 10% to 100% in trials.

**Hardware Experiments:**
Official 100m dash attempts were conducted on a track, with independent witnesses and timekeepers, to establish the Guinness World Record for the Fastest 100m Dash by a Bipedal Robot. Complementary trials were also performed in simulation to compare performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings related to gait optimization, comparison with human running, and performance in the 100m dash.

**Gait Parameter Optimization:**
The systematic exploration and optimization of gait parameters (swing ratio and stride frequency) revealed a new, more efficient mapping from speed to gait parameters, which qualitatively differed from the previously hand-tuned mapping. The optimized results indicated that at moderate speeds (2 to 4 m/s), the preferred stride frequency (`f_req`) decreased and then held steady, before increasing again at higher speeds. Concurrently, the swing ratio (`ratio`) was found to increase to near its maximum and then plateau. This combination of a lower stride frequency and a higher swing ratio suggests a preference for "long, infrequent steps" characterized by a significant aerial phase where both feet are off the ground. This strategy allows for longer stride lengths and reduces the necessity for excessively high ground reaction forces during the stance phase, making the locomotion more efficient. In contrast, the hand-tuned mapping's higher stride frequencies at these speeds would have necessitated greater ground forces for equivalent speeds, leading to reduced efficiency.

**Comparison to Human Running Mechanics:**
A detailed comparison of Cassie's optimized running mechanics with human biomechanical data demonstrated notable similarities.
- **Stride Length and Frequency:** The optimized Cassie displayed similar behavior to humans, with stride length increasing with speed and then tapering at higher velocities, while stride frequency remained relatively flat before increasing at top speeds.
- **Swing and Aerial Times:** Both Cassie's optimized gaits and human running showed a decrease in swing-time at moderate to high speeds. The aerial time constituted approximately two-thirds of the total swing-time, aligning with human observations.
- **Effective Impulse:** For both Cassie and humans, the effective impulse (product of effective ground reaction force and stance time) was found to be maximized at moderate speeds.
- **Effective Ground Reaction Force (GRF_eff):** A key difference was observed in the effective GRF. In humans, GRF_eff generally increases linearly with speed across the full range. However, for Cassie, the GRF_eff reached a limit and began to taper after approximately 3 m/s. The researchers hypothesized that this limitation was due to the hip-roll motors reaching their maximum torque, which would otherwise cause instability in the pelvis if higher forces were attempted.
Despite this quantitative difference in GRF_eff at higher speeds, the overall qualitative similarities between Cassie's optimized running mechanics and human running were compelling, particularly when compared to Cassie's performance with the hand-tuned gait parameters. This suggests that the optimized gaits exhibit more natural and efficient locomotive behaviors.

**100m Dash Performance and World Record:**
The integrated 100m dash controller successfully completed the task on hardware, establishing the Guinness World Record for the Fastest 100m Dash by a Bipedal Robot.
- **Hardware Performance:** Cassie achieved a best hardware time of 24.37 seconds, corresponding to an average speed of 4.10 m/s. Other successful trials recorded times of 26.11 seconds (3.83 m/s) and 27.38 seconds (3.65 m/s). The variation in hardware times was primarily attributed to user error in adjusting robot orientation.
- **Simulation Performance:** In simulation, the controller demonstrated consistent performance, with times tightly clustered around the best hardware time (e.g., 24.53s, 24.38s, 24.43s). Furthermore, in simulation, Cassie could achieve higher commanded speeds (up to 5 m/s), resulting in 100m dash times of approximately 22 seconds, with an average speed exceeding 4.5 m/s. This difference between hardware and simulation performance highlights a remaining "sim-to-real gap," attributed to inaccuracies in the simulated model that limit the maximum stable speed achievable on the physical robot.
- **Policy Transition Robustness:** The meticulously designed policy transition strategy, particularly the method for transitioning from stepping to standing at a foot's apex phase combined with a slightly negative speed command, proved highly effective. This approach improved the success rate of this critical transition from less than 10% to 100% across over 20 trials, ensuring the robot could reliably start and stop without falling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a significant advancement in the field of bipedal robot locomotion, particularly in the domain of high-speed running.

Firstly, the work provides a **principled and systematic approach to optimizing gait parameters** across a spectrum of speeds. By moving beyond hand-tuned heuristics, the developed method offers a data-driven strategy for discovering efficient locomotion strategies. This methodology can be generalized and applied to other bipedal platforms and different gaits, paving the way for more autonomous and adaptable robot control design. The discovery of optimal gait parameter curves that prioritize long, infrequent steps with a significant aerial phase provides a fundamental insight into high-speed bipedal mechanics.

Secondly, the **compelling similarities between the optimized robot gaits and human running mechanics** are highly significant. Despite inherent morphological differences, the emergence of analogous kinematic and dynamic behaviors suggests that there may be universal principles of efficient bipedal locomotion. This bio-inspired validation not only enhances the credibility of the robotic control strategies but also offers valuable insights for biomechanists and robot designers. Understanding which aspects of human running are naturally reproduced in an optimized robot can inform the development of more bio-mimetic and efficient bipedal robots in the future.

Thirdly, the **establishment of a Guinness World Record for the Fastest 100m Dash by a Bipedal Robot** serves as a concrete benchmark and a public demonstration of the current capabilities in bipedal robotics. This achievement pushes the boundaries of performance for general-purpose bipedal robots beyond previously observed moderate speeds. Such milestones often stimulate further research and competition within the robotics community, driving innovation in both hardware design and control algorithms. The ability to complete a complex task like the 100m dash, including reliable starting and stopping, underscores the robustness and practicality of the sim-to-real reinforcement learning paradigm employed.

Finally, the research also highlights **critical areas for future investigation**. The existence of a "sim-to-real gap" that limits the maximum speed on hardware, despite higher speeds being achievable in simulation, points to ongoing challenges in robot modeling and dynamics randomization. Closing this gap through a combination of hardware refinements and advanced software techniques remains a compelling problem for the bipedal robotics community. Further improvements could also arise from exclusively training controllers on the newly optimized gait parameters, rather than broadly across a spectrum, potentially leading to even greater efficiency and speed. The achieved speeds, currently around one-third of top human runners, indicate substantial room for continued progress towards more agile and human-like robotic locomotion.
