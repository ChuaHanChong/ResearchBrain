# Why Larger Models Learn More: Effects of Capacity, Interference, and Rare-Task Retention

> [!summary] Summary
> Larger machine learning models learn rare and complex tasks more effectively than smaller models primarily due to reduced interference, enabling them to retain information from infrequent observations and gradually accumulate knowledge. This research demonstrates that increased capacity allows larger models to allocate sufficient resources to common tasks, thereby weakening their gradients and freeing up capacity for stable learning of low-utility features.

## Problem

- Understanding the underlying mechanisms for why larger models learn specific tasks that smaller models fail to learn, even with sufficient data.
- Addressing the limitations of smaller models in multi-task settings, where performance degrades on rare or complex tasks due to resource competition.
- Providing a mechanistic, data-centric explanation for the phenomenon of "learnable via model scaling," where model size, not just data, is critical for acquiring certain capabilities.

## Method

- Developed a synthetic multi-task linear regression setup with controllable task frequency and complexity to rigorously test hypotheses about feature learning and interference.
- Conducted theoretical analysis to derive bounds on feature learning based on 'utility' (product of task frequency and feature eigenvalue) and characterize gradient dynamics and interference reduction with increased model width.
- Validated findings on the OLMo large language model pre-training pipeline by injecting controlled rare tasks and analyzing behavioral patterns, internal representations, and gradient dynamics across different model scales.

## Results

- Synthetic experiments showed that larger models successfully learned tasks with lower 'utility,' retaining their features over time, while smaller models consistently failed due to interference.
- In OLMo, larger models (up to 4B parameters) demonstrated superior learning and retention of injected rare tasks at low frequencies, with task-relevant features appearing more strongly and earlier in training.
- Gradient analysis in OLMo revealed that larger models effectively minimize interference from general language modeling updates on rare-task gradients, supporting the reduced competition mechanism.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Features are learned in order of their 'utility,' which combines task frequency and feature complexity; larger models are necessary to acquire low-utility features.
> - Increased model capacity reduces interference between tasks, particularly for common tasks whose decreasing residual signal weakens their gradients, freeing up resources for rare tasks.
> - Larger models exhibit improved retention of rare-task observations, accumulating knowledge incrementally, whereas smaller models suffer from an 'update-and-forget' dynamic due to continuous parameter competition.
