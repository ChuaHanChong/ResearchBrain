# Why Larger Models Learn More: Effects of Capacity, Interference, and Rare-Task Retention

> [!summary] Summary
> Larger machine learning models learn rare and complex tasks more effectively than smaller models primarily due to reduced interference, enabling them to retain information from infrequent observations and gradually accumulate knowledge. This research demonstrates that increased capacity allows larger models to allocate sufficient resources to common tasks, thereby weakening their gradients and freeing up capacity for stable learning of low-utility features.

## Problem

- Understanding the underlying mechanisms for why larger models learn specific tasks that smaller models fail to learn, even with sufficient data.
- Addressing the limitations of smaller models in multi-task settings, where performance degrades on rare or complex tasks due to resource competition.
- Providing a mechanistic, data-centric explanation for the phenomenon of "learnable via model scaling," where model size, not just data, is critical for acquiring certain capabilities.

## Method

- Developed a synthetic multi-task linear regression setup with controllable task frequency and complexity to rigorously test hypotheses about feature learning and interference.
- Conducted theoretical analysis to derive bounds on feature learning based on 'utility' (product of task frequency and feature eigenvalue) and characterize gradient dynamics and interference reduction with increased model width.
- Validated findings on the OLMo large language model pre-training pipeline by injecting controlled rare tasks and analyzing behavioral patterns, internal representations, and gradient dynamics across different model scales.

## Results

- Synthetic experiments showed that larger models successfully learned tasks with lower 'utility,' retaining their features over time, while smaller models consistently failed due to interference.
- In OLMo, larger models (up to 4B parameters) demonstrated superior learning and retention of injected rare tasks at low frequencies, with task-relevant features appearing more strongly and earlier in training.
- Gradient analysis in OLMo revealed that larger models effectively minimize interference from general language modeling updates on rare-task gradients, supporting the reduced competition mechanism.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Features are learned in order of their 'utility,' which combines task frequency and feature complexity; larger models are necessary to acquire low-utility features.
> - Increased model capacity reduces interference between tasks, particularly for common tasks whose decreasing residual signal weakens their gradients, freeing up resources for rare tasks.
> - Larger models exhibit improved retention of rare-task observations, accumulating knowledge incrementally, whereas smaller models suffer from an 'update-and-forget' dynamic due to continuous parameter competition.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of increasingly larger machine learning models, particularly large language models (LLMs), has been accompanied by empirical observations that these models often achieve capabilities and performance levels unattainable by smaller models. This phenomenon, sometimes referred to as "emergent abilities," has driven the trend towards aggressive scaling. Prior work has largely focused on establishing scaling laws, which predict performance improvement as a function of model size, dataset size, and computational budget. These laws, like those observed by Kaplan and Chinchilla, characterize *that* models scale predictably but do not fully elucidate *why* larger models acquire specific tasks that smaller ones fail to learn.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by seeking to move beyond mere descriptive scaling laws and provide a mechanistic, data-centric account for the observed benefits of larger models. It addresses a gap in understanding by investigating the underlying learning dynamics and resource allocation within models, particularly in multi-task learning settings where tasks vary in frequency and complexity. The work contrasts with explanations centered solely on model expressivity (what a model *can* represent) or sample efficiency (how effectively a model learns from data), arguing that these do not fully capture situations where smaller models fail even with asymptotic data.

The paper draws connections to several related research areas:
- **Multi-task Learning and Continual Learning:** It acknowledges the existing literature on "catastrophic interference," where learning new tasks can degrade performance on previously learned ones, and "gradient starvation," where infrequent tasks' gradients are overwhelmed by frequent ones. This work extends these concepts by integrating model scale as a critical factor in mitigating such interference and improving retention.
- **Memorization and Grokking:** The study explores how a model's ability to retain "memories" of infrequent observations might be a prerequisite for eventually generalizing to abstract task representations. This connects to the "grokking" phenomenon, where models transition from memorizing training data to achieving strong generalization after prolonged training, often observed in algorithmic tasks. The paper posits that scaling enables this memorization and subsequent consolidation of signal.
- **Generalization and Scaling Laws:** While leveraging established scaling law concepts, the paper delves into the micro-mechanisms of how scaling interacts with data characteristics (frequency, complexity) to enable the learning of specific features, thus complementing work on how natural data statistics influence scaling exponents.
- **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis:** The work implicitly touches upon the idea of larger models possessing subnetworks capable of learning specific tasks, suggesting that increased capacity might make it more probable for such "lottery tickets" to emerge or be preserved during training for low-utility features.

Overall, this research aims to provide a mechanistic explanation, grounded in learning dynamics and data properties, for the qualitative difference in capabilities between large and small models, rather than just quantitative performance improvements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to understand *why* larger models learn tasks that smaller models do not. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific questions and motivations:

1. **Challenging the "Undertraining" Hypothesis:** The paper begins by observing that power-law scaling laws, which describe predictable performance improvements with increased model size and data, suggest a regime where a smaller model might fail to learn parts of a data distribution even with infinite training data, distinguishing this from situations where a smaller model is merely "undertrained" and could catch up with more data (learnable via data scaling). The primary motivation is to investigate this more fundamental limitation, termed "learnable via model scaling," where the larger model enjoys a genuine advantage due to its size, irrespective of data quantity.

2. **Identifying What is Learned:** A key motivation is to precisely identify *what* specific types of tasks or features are exclusively learned by larger models. The authors hypothesize that larger models are better equipped to learn tasks that are either rare (infrequently observed) or complex (requiring many features or directions to represent), or both. This involves defining "utility" of features based on task frequency and spectral properties.

3. **Uncovering the Mechanisms:** The core objective is to uncover the underlying mechanisms by which larger models overcome the challenges posed by rare and complex tasks. The authors propose a "data-centric bottleneck" explanation, positing that smaller models struggle due to resource competition, where neurons are preferentially allocated to high-frequency or low-complexity tasks. This leads to poor performance on rare/complex tasks, even if the model's architecture could theoretically express the solution.

4. **Investigating Interference and Retention:** Specifically, the research aims to assess how increased model capacity (width) alleviates this competition. The proposed mechanism involves a "reduced interference mechanism": larger models can allocate sufficient resources to common tasks such that their gradient updates become weak, preventing them from overwriting features relevant to rare tasks. This, in turn, allows for the gradual accumulation and "retention" of rare-task features across infrequent observations, building knowledge incrementally rather than repeatedly "updating-and-forgetting."

5. **Validating across Scales:** The motivation extends to validating these theoretical and synthetic findings in realistic large language models (LLMs). This ensures that the proposed mechanisms are not merely artifacts of simplified synthetic environments but are relevant to the behavior of state-of-the-art models in practical pre-training scenarios. This involves analyzing behavioral patterns, internal representations, and gradient dynamics within LLMs.

In essence, the paper is motivated by a desire to move beyond statistical observations of scaling to a mechanistic understanding of *how* and *why* model size unlocks certain capabilities, with a particular focus on the interplay between model capacity, data distribution characteristics, and learning dynamics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-stage approach, combining theoretical analysis, experiments on a synthetic multi-task regression setup, and validation on a realistic Large Language Model (LLM) pre-training pipeline.

#### 3.1. Phenomenological Model and Definitions

The work begins by leveraging existing power-law scaling relations ($L(N, D) = L_0 + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta$) to theoretically differentiate two scenarios:
- **Learnable via data scaling:** A smaller model can eventually match a larger model's performance by observing more data (e.g., larger model is simply more sample efficient).
- **Learnable via model scaling:** Even with infinite data, a smaller model fundamentally cannot achieve the same loss as a larger model, implying a qualitative advantage for larger models. This latter scenario is the focus of the investigation.

#### 3.2. Synthetic Multi-Task Regression Setup (Section 3)

To rigorously test hypotheses and isolate variables, a controlled synthetic environment is developed:
- **Data Generation:** A mixture of `K` linear regression tasks is used. Samples for task `k` appear with frequency `π_k` (power-law distribution `k^-β`). Each task `k` has a covariance `C_k` defined by eigenvalues `λ_k,j` (power-law spectrum `j^-α_k`) and orthonormal eigenvectors `b_k,j`, with different tasks occupying orthogonal blocks in the input space. This allows for controlled manipulation of both task frequency and task complexity (number of features required).
- **Model Architecture:** A "student" model consists of a shared width-`N` encoder `U` (mapping input `x` to an `N`-dimensional hidden representation) and task-specific linear decoders `D_k`. The objective is to minimize a weighted sum of per-task MSE losses.
- **Theoretical Analysis (Theorems & Propositions):**
 - **Theorem 3 (Features are Learned in Order of Utility):** Analytically demonstrates that an optimal width-`N` encoder will capture the `N` features (`b_k,j`) with the largest "utilities" (`u_k,j = π_k λ_k,j`). This provides a concrete ranking rule for which features are learned, linking frequency and complexity directly to learning priority.
 - **Theorem 4 (Residual Controls Learning) & Corollary 5 (Width-Scaling Reduces Competition):** Analyzes the gradient dynamics. It shows that as a model learns common tasks, their residual signal (unexplained variance) decreases, weakening their gradients and freeing up capacity for rare tasks. This quantifies how width reduces competition.
 - **Proposition 6 (Interference Reduces via Scaling):** Characterizes the stability condition under which a rare feature can be learned without displacing a well-learned common feature, demonstrating how larger models (lower `μ_F,N`) can stably integrate rare features.
- **Experimental Verification:**
 - Models of varying width (`N`) are trained on the synthetic task mixture using Adam optimizer.
 - Metrics tracked include per-task loss, normalized per-task signal (subspace alignment `s_k(U)`), and the residual common-task signal (`δ_F(U)`).
 - A **matched-frequency injection experiment** is conducted: a rare task is withheld for `G` steps, then injected in a larger batch to maintain its long-run frequency. This isolates the effect of retention dynamics.

#### 3.3. Validation on OLMo Pretraining Pipeline (Section 4)

To corroborate findings in a realistic setting, experiments are conducted with the OLMo LLM.
- **Models:** OLMo models ranging from 4M to 4B parameters are utilized.
- **Controlled Task Injection:** A "data injection framework" is used. Instead of relying on natural task frequencies, novel "special" tasks are manually injected into the Dolma v1.7 pre-training corpus at controlled frequencies (7.8x10^-3 to 2.4x10^-8 per token).
 - **Comparison Task (T_CMP):** Requires learning global token order for numerical comparison.
 - **Modular Addition Task (T_ADD):** Requires learning arithmetic over 100-modulo, known to elicit grokking phenomena (generalization beyond memorization).
- **Multi-Level Analysis:**
 - **Behavioral Evidence:** Measures training loss and test accuracy for injected tasks across different model sizes and frequencies. Replicates the matched-frequency injection experiment from the synthetic setup to study retention.
 - **Representational Evidence:**
 - **Feature Localization:** Uses Distributed Alignment Search (DAS) for T_CMP to find 1-D subspaces encoding global token order. For T_ADD, Fourier analysis is applied to residual streams to detect Fourier modes, which are known to be relevant for modular arithmetic.
 - **Feature Quantification:** Measures the extent to which these identified task-relevant features are present in the model's representations across layers and training checkpoints.
 - **Gradient Evidence:**
 - **Task Neuron Identification:** Identifies top MLP neurons causally related to task predictions.
 - **Gradient Alignment:** Computes cosine similarity between batch gradients (full, task-token, non-task-token) and a task-specific reference gradient (`g_r`) to quantify interference between general language modeling updates and injected task updates.

This comprehensive methodology, spanning theoretical derivation, synthetic experiments, and large-scale LLM validation, aims to build a robust, multi-faceted argument for the proposed data-centric explanation of scaling benefits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents a series of findings from both synthetic experiments and real LLM pre-training, converging on a data-centric account for why larger models learn tasks that smaller models fail to.

#### 4.1. Findings from Synthetic Multi-Task Regression Setup

- **Larger Models Learn Rarer, More Complex Tasks (Section 3.1):**
 - **Theoretical Prediction (Theorem 3):** The optimal width-N model learns features in order of their "utility," defined as the product of task frequency and feature eigenvalue (complexity contribution). Features with lower utility (e.g., from infrequent tasks or tasks requiring many low-eigenvalue features) are learned only by larger models that can afford to capture them.
 - **Empirical Verification (Fig. 2, App. D):** Experiments with a mixture of regression tasks varying in frequency (power-law `k^-β`) and complexity (power-law `j^-α`) confirmed this. Larger models consistently achieved lower loss on infrequent tasks compared to smaller models. The empirical "phase diagram" of which features are retained aligned with the theoretical predictions based on feature utility.

- **Scaling Reduces Interference and Allows for Retention of Rare Task Observations (Section 3.2):**
 - **Theoretical Explanation (Theorem 4, Corollary 5, Proposition 6):** As models increase in width, they can allocate enough resources to common tasks. This reduces the "residual signal" remaining for common tasks, leading to weaker gradient updates for them. Consequently, the competition for model parameters decreases, allowing rare tasks to be learned more stably. Proposition 6 specifically shows that a rare feature can only become locally stable if its utility is higher than the weakest occupied common-task utility, and increasing width lowers this threshold.
 - **Empirical Verification (Fig. 3):** When model width was small, frequent tasks still had a high residual signal to explain, and rare tasks were not learned better than random. As width increased beyond a predicted threshold (where frequent tasks' signal was largely explained), rare tasks began to be learned effectively.
 - **Retention Dynamics (Fig. 4, App. E.3):** Using a matched-frequency injection protocol, the study demonstrated that larger models accumulate and retain rare-task signal across infrequent observations. Smaller models, in contrast, briefly encode the rare task upon injection but rapidly lose this signal due to interference from frequent-task updates, resulting in an "update-and-forget" dynamic.

#### 4.2. Findings from OLMo Pretraining Pipeline

- **Behavioral Evidence (Section 4.2):**
 - **Larger Models Learn Rarer Tasks (Fig. 5):** Consistent with synthetic findings, larger OLMo models (from 4M to 4B parameters) learned injected tasks (Comparison T_CMP and Modular Addition T_ADD) much better at lower frequencies than smaller models.
 - **Learning Order (Fig. 6a):** Tasks were learned in an order correlated with their frequency.
 - **Retention Effects (Fig. 6b):** The matched-frequency injection experiment showed that larger gaps between rare task instances led to higher task loss, even with equivalent global task frequency. This indicated that models struggle to retain information over longer periods without frequent reinforcement, and larger models are better at this retention.

- **Representational Evidence (Section 4.3):**
 - **More Task Features in Larger Models (Fig. 7):** Analysis of OLMo model representations revealed that task-relevant features (e.g., global token order for T_CMP via Distributed Alignment Search, Fourier modes for T_ADD via Fourier analysis) were more strongly present and learned faster in larger models and in models trained with higher task frequencies. The presence of these features correlated with high test accuracy, indicating generalized learning rather than mere memorization of training instances.

- **Gradient Evidence (Section 4.4):**
 - **Reduced Gradient Interference (Fig. 9):** By analyzing gradients in task-specific neurons, larger OLMo models showed higher cosine similarity between batch gradients and the task reference direction at injection steps. Crucially, in larger models, gradients from non-task tokens were almost orthogonal to the task reference direction, indicating minimal interference from general language modeling updates. In smaller models (e.g., 20M), non-task gradients showed significant, fluctuating similarity with the task direction, suggesting "random collisions" and active interference. This supports the hypothesis that larger models have enough capacity to minimize interference, allowing rare-task features to accumulate.

**Overall Hypothesis:** The findings support the central hypothesis that scaling enables learning of rare and complex tasks primarily through a reduced interference mechanism. Larger models can retain parts of updates from infrequent observations and build upon them, whereas smaller models face continuous competition for parameters, leading to forgetting of rare-task features between observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a data-centric account for why larger models exhibit superior learning capabilities, moving beyond general notions of expressivity or sample efficiency to detailed mechanisms related to learning dynamics. Its significance and potential impact span both theoretical understanding and practical applications:

#### 5.1. Theoretical Contributions

- **Mechanistic Explanation of Scaling:** Provides a concrete, mechanistic explanation for the observation that larger models learn certain tasks smaller models do not. It attributes this to reduced competition for model parameters and improved retention of infrequent signals in larger models, offering an alternative or complementary perspective to expressivity or sample efficiency alone.
- **Reframing "Emergence":** By showing how larger models can steadily accumulate and consolidate signals from rare tasks through reduced interference and better retention, the work offers a granular view of how "emergent abilities" might manifest from underlying learning dynamics rather than abrupt phase transitions.
- **Integrating Diverse Fields:** Unifies insights from scaling laws, multi-task learning, continual learning, and grokking phenomena under a coherent framework centered on utility, interference, and retention.
- **Distinction between Data and Model Scaling:** Clearly defines and empirically validates the difference between tasks learnable simply by providing more data to a smaller model versus those requiring genuinely larger model capacities.

#### 5.2. Practical Implications

- **Informing Model Sizing and Architecture:** The findings suggest that when designing models for multi-task environments or data distributions with long tails of rare tasks, model capacity should be considered not just for overall performance, but specifically to enable the stable learning and retention of these low-frequency or complex capabilities.
- **Optimizing Training Data Mixtures:** The data-centric perspective highlights that strategically adjusting task frequencies in training data mixtures could be a more efficient way to elicit target capabilities than indiscriminately scaling up model size. For instance, increasing the frequency of a rare but critical task might allow smaller models to learn it more effectively. This resonates with ongoing work on data curation and curriculum learning.
- **Reassessing Memorization:** The paper suggests that what might be perceived as "memorization" in models, especially for rare tasks, could be a necessary step towards eventual generalization. Retaining specific task instances allows models to accumulate sufficient signal over time to abstract generalized structures. This could lead to a more nuanced view of memorization as a supportive mechanism for learning, rather than an undesirable property.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future research to explore these dynamics in more varied architectures, across broader ranges of task frequencies and complexities, and in different training regimes (e.g., over-trained models). It also encourages investigation into the "counter" dynamics for frequent tasks, such as how scaling might prevent overfitting in those scenarios.

In summary, this research contributes to a deeper understanding of the fundamental advantages of model scaling, providing both theoretical grounding and empirical evidence for the role of interference reduction and memory retention in enabling larger models to learn otherwise inaccessible tasks.
