# FADA: Few-Shot Domain Adaptation via Dynamics Alignment for Humanoid Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed FADA, a few-shot domain adaptation method that utilizes a factored policy and dynamics alignment to achieve precise whole-body control for humanoids, enhancing hardware task success rates to 90% using approximately two minutes of target-domain data.

## Problem

- High-precision humanoid control faces significant performance degradation when policies trained in simulation are transferred to real-world environments due to dynamics mismatch.
- Existing zero-shot transfer methods yield conservative policies lacking specialization, while other target-domain adaptation techniques (e.g., system identification, policy finetuning) demand extensive data, expert demonstrations, or computable reward signals.
- Small errors in tightly coupled whole-body dynamics of humanoids can lead to instability, posture drift, and tracking failures, which are difficult to address with current methods.

## Method

- **FADA** employs a ==Planner–Inverse Dynamics Model (Planner–IDM)== framework, factoring the control policy into a ==planner== for intent prediction and an ==inverse dynamics model (IDM)== for action generation.
- The policy is pretrained in a source simulator by ==distilling== an oracle policy's behavior via a ==DAgger==-style approach, where the IDM learns to map realized futures to executed actions, and the planner learns to produce ==action-grounded futures== through the frozen IDM.
- For few-shot target adaptation, only ==Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)== adapters within the IDM are finetuned using a small amount (approximately 2 minutes) of self-generated target-domain observation-action data, without rewards or privileged information.

## Results

- FADA increased average success rates on hardware tasks (Unitree G1 and Booster T1) from **20%** (zero-shot) to **90%** and reduced normalized error by **27.4%** compared to zero-shot policies.
- In sim-to-sim transfers (IsaacSim to MuJoCo), FADA reduced normalized error by an average of **24.7%** relative to its zero-shot variant, with largest gains in force-adaptive tasks.
- LoRA adaptation consistently outperformed full IDM finetuning, and performance saturated rapidly after approximately **two minutes** of target-domain data, demonstrating the method's few-shot efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Factoring the control policy into a dynamics-invariant Planner (for intent) and a dynamics-dependent IDM (for execution) allows for targeted adaptation of the execution component.
> - Few-shot adaptation of the IDM, guided by self-generated observation-action trajectories and without explicit rewards, is sufficient to align action generation with target-domain dynamics.
> - Training the planner through a stop-gradient IDM with an action-prediction loss and supervising the IDM only on the first executed action are crucial for a sufficiently deployable zero-shot policy to collect target-domain data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

High-precision humanoid control often encounters significant challenges due to dynamics mismatch between simulation environments, where policies are typically trained, and real-world deployment conditions. This mismatch can arise from variations in terrain, payload, or actuator responses, leading to discrepancies between intended control objectives and realized motions. Existing research approaches to address this "sim-to-real gap" for robot control generally fall into two categories.

The first category focuses on achieving zero-shot robustness or in-context adaptation. Methods in this area, such as domain randomization, aim to train policies that are inherently robust to a range of potential dynamics variations by exposing them to diverse simulated conditions during training. Other approaches, like history-conditioned adaptation, modify the policy input at inference time based on observed history to adapt to unknown dynamics without updating policy weights. While these methods can improve robustness, they generally result in conservative policies that do not specialize to specific target-domain conditions.

The second category involves using target-domain data for adaptation. This includes techniques like system identification, where target-domain data is used to calibrate or refine a dynamics model; residual dynamics learning, which learns corrections to a nominal dynamics model or policy; and policy finetuning, which directly updates policy parameters using real-world experience. These methods offer specialization to the target domain but typically impose substantial requirements. They may necessitate predefined models for fitting, new expert demonstrations for imitation learning, or a computable target-domain reward signal for reinforcement learning, which can be challenging and data-intensive to obtain in real-world scenarios.

FADA (Few-Shot Domain Adaptation via Dynamics Alignment) positions itself in the second category but seeks to overcome the high data and supervision requirements. It proposes a few-shot adaptation method that utilizes a small amount of target-domain interaction data, specifically paired actions and observations, without requiring rewards, privileged information, or accurate prior models. The work suggests that for humanoid control, the high-level task intention often remains valid even under dynamics shifts, but the precise actions required to achieve that intention change. This perspective motivates a factored policy architecture and a targeted adaptation strategy that aims to recalibrate the execution mechanism rather than re-learn the entire control policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable precise whole-body control for humanoid robots in real-world environments, specifically addressing performance degradation caused by target-domain dynamics mismatch. This mismatch, encompassing differences in terrain, payload, and actuator response, causes the same control objective to produce varied and often suboptimal robot motions when transferring policies from simulation to reality.

The core motivation stems from the observation that despite significant advancements in reinforcement learning for humanoid locomotion and manipulation in simulation, precise control on real hardware remains a challenge. Small errors in foot placement, contact timing, or force delivery, amplified by the tightly coupled whole-body dynamics of humanoids, can lead to instability, posture drift, and degraded tracking performance. These failures are often attributed not to a flawed high-level command but to a disconnect between the commanded motion or control output and the actual physical response in the target domain.

Existing solutions for sim-to-real transfer were deemed insufficient in addressing this specific problem comprehensively. Zero-shot transfer methods, relying on domain randomization or in-context adaptation, often yield policies that are overly conservative and lack the specialization needed for high-precision tasks. Conversely, target-domain data-driven adaptation methods, such as model calibration, residual learning, or full policy retraining, typically demand extensive data, expert demonstrations, or a well-defined reward function in the real world, which are costly and difficult to acquire.

This led the authors to pose a specific research question: "Is it possible to achieve few-shot target-domain adaptation for precise humanoid control without rewards or accurate prior models?" The motivation behind FADA's design is based on the hypothesis that under dynamics shifts, the robot's *intent* (what it should do) often remains appropriate for the task, but the *execution* (how it should act to realize that intent) needs adjustment. Therefore, rather than retraining a monolithic policy or an entire dynamics model, the goal is to develop a framework that can selectively adapt the execution component of a control policy using only a limited amount of target-domain interaction data, specifically observation-action trajectories generated by the robot itself. This selective adaptation aims to align the robot's action generation with the specific dynamics of the target environment while preserving the overall task planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FADA employs a three-stage Planner–Inverse Dynamics Model (Planner–IDM) framework for few-shot domain adaptation. The core principle is to factorize the control policy into distinct modules for planning and execution, and then selectively adapt the execution module using minimal target-domain data.

#### 3.1. Policy Factorization

The deployable student policy π_s is factorized into a planner P_φ and an inverse dynamics model I_ψ.
- **Planner (P_φ):** This module takes the robot's recent proprioceptive observation history (O_H_t) and the current task command (c_t) as input. It predicts a short-horizon (K-step) future proprioceptive sequence (ˆY_K_t), representing the robot's intended motion or "proprioceptive intent." The planner is implemented as a transformer encoder that predicts a residual future chunk relative to the latest observation.
- **Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM, I_ψ):** This module receives the recent observation-action history (O_H_t, A_H_t) and the planner-predicted future proprioceptive sequence (ˆY_K_t). Its function is to convert this intent into an K-step action chunk (ˆU_K_t). The IDM is an encoder–decoder transformer, where future-state tokens apply self-attention over the predicted chunk and cross-attend to encoded history tokens. At deployment, only the first action (Π_1(ˆU_K_t)) from the predicted chunk is executed, following a receding-horizon control scheme.

#### 3.2. Source-Domain Training (Pretraining)

FADA's source training involves two sub-stages:
1. **Oracle Policy Training:** A privileged oracle policy (π_o) is initially trained in a source simulator (IsaacSim) using task rewards and access to privileged state information (e.g., base linear velocity, contact forces, terrain geometry, actuator parameters). This oracle represents an expert-level controller.
2. **Planner–IDM Distillation:** The behavior of the privileged oracle is then distilled into the deployable Planner–IDM student using a DAgger-style approach.
 - **IDM Learning (Eq. 4.2):** The IDM is trained to map *realized* proprioceptive futures to *executed* actions from DAgger-style source rollouts. Specifically, for a given history (O_H_t, A_H_t) and a realized future proprioceptive sequence (Y_K_t,exec), the IDM is trained to predict the first action (Π_1(U_K_t,exec)) that was actually executed in that rollout segment. This objective uses the robot's own execution as supervision, making it robust to suboptimal trajectories and applicable to target-domain data where optimal demonstrations are unavailable.
 - **Planner Learning (Eq. 4.3):** The planner is trained to produce "action-grounded" futures. This means the planner (P_φ) is optimized such that when its predicted future (P_φ(O_H_t, c_t)) is fed into the *current, frozen* IDM (I_ψ with gradients stopped), the resulting first action (Π_1(I_ψ(O_H_t, A_H_t, P_φ(O_H_t, c_t)))) closely matches the privileged oracle action (a*_t) for that state. Oracle relabeling is employed: for states visited during student rollouts, simulator snapshots are restored, and the privileged oracle is rolled forward to obtain the true oracle action (a*_t) and corresponding future observations (Y_K_orac).
 - **Data Usage:** Source training utilizes both realized trajectory data (for IDM supervision) and oracle-shadow data (for planner supervision). Suboptimal rollouts from intermediate oracle checkpoints are also included to diversify the IDM's training coverage.

#### 3.3. Few-Shot Target Adaptation

At deployment in the target domain (e.g., real hardware or a different simulator like MuJoCo), FADA performs the following steps:
1. **Data Collection:** A small amount (approximately 2 minutes) of target-domain rollouts (D_tgt) is collected by executing the source-trained Planner–IDM policy. These rollouts consist of proprioceptive observations, commands, and executed actions.
2. **IDM Finetuning:** The planner parameters (P_φ) and the base IDM weights (ψ) are frozen. Only a small set of introduced parameters, specifically Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters (∆ψ) within the IDM, are optimized. This finetuning uses supervised learning with the same inverse-dynamics objective as in source training (Eq. 4.5), where the IDM (I_ψ+∆ψ) is trained to predict the first executed action from the observed history and realized future proprioceptive sequence within the target rollouts. This aligns the IDM's action generation with the specific target-domain dynamics without requiring target-domain rewards or privileged information.
3. **Deployment:** The adapted policy (P_φ, I_ψ+∆ψ) is then deployed. The planner continues to predict futures based on task intent, and the finetuned IDM translates these intents into actions, now calibrated to the target-domain dynamics.

#### 3.4. Architecture and Parameters

Both the planner and IDM modules are implemented using transformer-based architectures. In the main experiments, a history length (H) of 30 frames and a prediction horizon (K) of 6 steps are used. The planner and IDM encoder employ 3-layer, 4-head transformers with a hidden size of 128. The IDM decoder uses 2 transformer decoder layers with similar specifications. LoRA adapters with rank 8, scaling 16, and dropout 0.05 are used for target adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated FADA across various tasks, robots, and domain shifts, comparing it against zero-shot and end-to-end adaptation baselines.

#### 4.1. Sim-to-Real Performance after Few-Shot Adaptation

FADA was evaluated on five hardware tasks using Unitree G1 (29 DoF) and Booster T1 (23 DoF) robots, encompassing locomotion, whole-body tracking, and loco-manipulation.
- **Success Rate Tasks:** FADA increased the average success rate from 20% (FADA-zs, zero-shot) to 90% on tasks like G1 Slope Traversal and T1 Basket Pulling. The TF–DAgger baseline failed both tasks.
- **Normalized Error Tasks:** For tasks such as G1 Loco. + Payload, G1 Kungfu + Soft Terrain, and T1 Loco. + Payload, FADA reduced task error by an average of 27.4% relative to FADA-zs and 15.9% relative to TF–DAgger.
- **Qualitative Observations:** The improvements corresponded to better execution during slope contacts, better posture recovery under payload shifts, and more stable tracking on soft terrains.
- **IDM Loss Correlation:** A consistent decrease in IDM loss was observed across all tasks after adaptation, indicating improved alignment of the IDM's action prediction with target-domain dynamics.
These results suggest that approximately two minutes of target-domain observation-action rollouts are sufficient to significantly enhance hardware performance under dynamics shifts, without requiring rewards or privileged labels.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-Sim Transfer across Embodiments and Tasks

Evaluation of IsaacSim-to-MuJoCo transfer across five tasks on G1 and T1 demonstrated the following:
- **Performance Gains:** FADA reduced normalized error by an average of 24.7% compared to FADA-zs (zero-shot) and 26.8% compared to TF–DAgger. The largest gains were observed in force-adaptive tasks like T1 Falcon, which are sensitive to dynamics mismatch.
- **Comparison to TF–CoPred-ft:** The TF–CoPred-ft baseline, which finetunes a shared co-prediction backbone with an observation-prediction loss, showed degraded performance relative to its zero-shot variant. This indicates that improving future-observation prediction alone is insufficient for better deployment if the action-generation mapping remains mismatched.
- **Consistency:** Similar improvements were observed in IsaacSim-to-IsaacSim transfers under held-out payload and terrain shifts, confirming that the gains are not merely due to cross-simulator implementation differences.

#### 4.3. Influence of Prediction Horizon on Performance

A study on the prediction horizon (K) showed:
- **Optimality at K=6:** Moving from K=1 to K=6 reduced normalized error by approximately 18% on representative tasks, but longer horizons (K=10, K=15) provided no further substantial gains. This suggests that useful information for action generation is concentrated within a short future window.
- **Critical Future Steps:** Analysis of visible-prefix and leave-one-out studies indicated that the first few future tokens are most important, with the first and third predicted steps being particularly influential. The IDM's use of full attention over future tokens, despite supervising only the first executed action, suggests it learns a higher-order plan-to-action map.

#### 4.4. Effect of Planner and IDM Training Objectives on Zero-Shot Transfer

Ablations on source-training objectives impacting zero-shot deployability:
- **Planner Training through IDM (Objective A, Eq. 4.3):** Training the planner through the stop-gradient IDM using an action-prediction loss (rather than direct oracle future observation regression) improved zero-shot success rates from 4/10 to 7/10. This ensures planner predictions are compatible and actionable by the IDM.
- **IDM Supervision on First Action (Objective B, Eq. 4.2):** Supervising the IDM only on the first executed action (rather than the full predicted action window) was crucial, increasing zero-shot success from 4/10 to 10/10. This aligns IDM training with the receding-horizon execution used at deployment.
Both objectives were found to be important for achieving a sufficiently deployable zero-shot policy to collect target rollouts.

#### 4.5. Adaptation Design Ablations

- **LoRA vs. Full IDM Finetuning:** LoRA adaptation consistently outperformed full IDM finetuning, which often degraded performance due to overfitting to the limited target rollout budget.
- **Target Rollout Budget:** Performance improved rapidly with increasing data up to approximately 6000 control steps (about 2 minutes), after which gains saturated. This supports the "few-shot" nature of FADA.

#### 4.6. Controlled Analysis of Planner–IDM Role Separation

A fixed-base arm tracking task with varying wrist payloads was used to validate the separation of intent and execution:
- **Planner Invariance:** The planner predicted nearly identical joint configurations for the same end-effector targets across different payloads. Mean per-joint planner RMSE changed minimally (7%) from 0 to 5 kg payload, supporting its role as a payload-invariant kinematic/intent map.
- **IDM Adaptation:** Finetuning only the IDM improved end-effector tracking error by approximately 24% and reduced the IDM consistency gap (between actions from planner-predicted vs. actual next observations) by about 54% on average. This indicates that IDM finetuning learns a structured, plan-conditioned inverse-dynamics correction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FADA presents a practical and effective solution to a critical problem in humanoid robotics: achieving high-precision control in real-world environments despite significant dynamics mismatches between training simulations and deployment conditions. The work's significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap with Efficiency:** FADA offers a method for domain adaptation that requires only a few minutes of target-domain interaction data, specifically paired observations and actions, rather than extensive real-world reinforcement learning, human demonstrations, or complex system identification. This significantly reduces the cost and logistical challenges associated with deploying and maintaining complex humanoid control policies.

2. **Robustness and Precision in Diverse Real-World Tasks:** The empirical results demonstrate FADA's ability to enable real humanoid robots (Unitree G1 and Booster T1) to stably execute a variety of high-precision whole-body tasks, including locomotion on slopes, carrying asymmetric payloads, and performing complex motions on deformable terrains. These are tasks where zero-shot policies typically exhibit significant failures.

3. **Validation of Factored Policy Design:** The framework validates the hypothesis that separating task intent generation (via a Planner) from dynamics-dependent action realization (via an Inverse Dynamics Model) is beneficial for adaptation. The controlled analysis on the fixed-base arm task clearly demonstrated the planner's invariance to dynamics shifts and the IDM's ability to adapt to them. This factored approach allows for targeted updates, avoiding the re-learning of higher-level task logic when only low-level dynamics change.

4. **Implications for Adaptive Control Paradigms:** FADA's success with supervised learning on self-generated rollout data, without explicit rewards or privileged information in the target domain, suggests a new direction for adaptable control. It implies that for many transfer failures, the issue lies not with the high-level plan but with the execution module's understanding of the current physical reality. This insight could inform future designs of generalizable and adaptable robot control architectures.

5. **Potential for Broader Application:** While demonstrated on humanoids, the core principle of adapting an execution-focused inverse dynamics module could potentially be applied to other complex robotic systems facing similar sim-to-real challenges, particularly those with tightly coupled dynamics sensitive to environmental variations.

Despite its current limitations, such as the dependence on a non-catastrophically failing zero-shot policy and the task-specific nature of the current IDM, FADA offers a promising avenue for scalable post-deployment adaptation. It paves the way for more practical and robust deployment of advanced robotic skills in unpredictable real-world environments, thereby expanding the capabilities and applicability of humanoid robots.
