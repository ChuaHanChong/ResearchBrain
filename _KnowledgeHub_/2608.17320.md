# Robust Brachiation on a Life-Sized Dual-Arm Robot Using Waypoint-Guided Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Waypoint-Guided Reinforcement Learning (WGRL) trains brachiation on a life-sized dual-arm robot by sparsely specifying end-effector waypoints for early exploration, then relaxing guidance via curriculum, combined with success and mechanical-energy rewards. Achieves zero-shot sim-to-real brachiation across 4 bars with emergent failure-recovery behavior.

## Problem

- Reward design for brachiation lacks established guidelines unlike walking, and simple distance/velocity rewards fail to induce the non-linear whole-body motion required.
- High-quality full-body reference motions for brachiation compatible with robotic end-effectors are scarce, making imitation-learning methods like DeepMimic hard to apply.
- Brachiation lacks leg support so any failure directly causes falling and episode termination, making hardware experiments high-risk and prior RL work confined to simulation or small-scale systems.

## Method

- **WGRL** guides early exploration with 5 sparse hand-position waypoints per reach (detach, pass under bar, wrap-and-hook from above) using a ==distance-direction coupled reward==, then relaxes guidance via a curriculum that widens the reaching threshold.
- Reward combines ==waypoint-following terms== (position, progress, success) with a bar-reaching success reward and a ==mechanical-energy tracking reward== targeting stable dual-hand suspension energy to balance progression and stability.
- ==Asymmetric actor-critic PPO== trains in Legged Gym with 4096 parallel environments, domain randomization, and geometric bar randomization, with a two-phase weight schedule shifting emphasis from waypoint guidance to success/energy terms.

## Results

- Sim-to-Sim in MuJoCo achieved continuous traversal of over **30 bars** across 10 trials, and **93.3% (28/30)** success on an unseen inclined course and **91% (91/100)** on a circular course.
- Ablation of the position-reward formulation showed distance-direction coupling reached **2.6 bars** at 8000 iterations versus **0.257 bars** for direction-only, with faster convergence (**954** vs **1368** iterations to exceed 0.5 bars).
- Reward-component ablation showed brachiation emerged only with all three WGRL terms combined (or success paired with one other), reaching **0.9-1.3 bars** for partial combinations versus full success with all three; zero-shot hardware test completed all **3** reaching phases across **4 bars** without falling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sparse waypoints used only to shape early exploration, then curriculum-relaxed, can substitute for missing imitation data to induce complex non-linear motion without constraining final behavior.
> - Pairing a locomotion-driving success reward with an energy-stabilization reward lets the policy spontaneously switch between progression and stabilization, producing emergent recovery behavior after failures.
> - Mechanical design (wide hook clearance, 180-degree wrap angle) and reward structure jointly determine sim-to-real robustness; passive hardware tolerance can compensate for unmodeled contact-friction gaps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic locomotion has seen significant advancements, particularly with the application of reinforcement learning (RL), leading to robust control for legged robots in tasks such as walking, running, ladder climbing, and parkour. However, these successes are primarily confined to environments that provide stable footholds. A distinct category of locomotion observed in nature, exemplified by primates, involves movement using primarily the upper limbs, allowing traversal in environments lacking ground support. This form of movement, known as brachiation, presents a substantial challenge for robotic implementation due to its requirements for highly coordinated whole-body movement, precise timing for contact events (grasping and releasing), and management of discontinuous contact dynamics.

Historically, research into robotic brachiation has largely employed theory- or model-based approaches, including energy-based control and virtual holonomic constraints. While these methods have contributed to understanding the underlying principles of brachiation, their performance can be limited by the accuracy of the underlying models, particularly when confronted with uncertain contact conditions or environmental disturbances.

More recently, RL techniques have been explored for brachiation, showing promise in terms of robustness compared to optimal control on minimalistic robotic platforms. However, most RL applications in brachiation have been restricted to simulation environments or small-scale robotic systems, with deployment on life-sized, complex hardware remaining a significant challenge.

The primary obstacles to applying RL for brachiation on life-sized robots, as identified by the authors, include:
1. **Reward Design Complexity:** Unlike legged locomotion where reward design guidelines are relatively established, systematic knowledge for brachiation reward functions is less developed. The intricate nature of brachiation makes it difficult to acquire the behavior using only simple distance- or velocity-based rewards. Furthermore, high-quality reference motion data, suitable for robotic end-effectors and necessary for imitation learning approaches, are scarce.
2. **High-Risk Nature of the Task:** Brachiation, by its nature, lacks leg support, meaning that any failure can directly lead to a fall and episode termination. This makes training, especially in hardware experiments, a high-risk endeavor that can impede learning progress.

This study positions itself within this landscape by addressing these identified challenges. It seeks to develop an RL-based method that can achieve robust brachiation on a life-sized dual-arm robot, moving beyond simulation or small-scale systems and providing a framework for robust arm-based locomotion in complex, real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a learning method capable of realizing robust brachiation on a life-sized dual-arm robot operating in a monkey-bar environment. This objective is motivated by the desire to expand the mobility capabilities of robots into environments where traditional legged locomotion is not feasible, such as overhead structures or terrains without stable footholds.

The specific objectives of the study are detailed as follows:

- **Development of Waypoint-Guided Reinforcement Learning (WGRL):** A primary objective is to propose WGRL as a novel learning framework. This framework aims to induce complex, non-linear motions without relying on extensive imitation learning data, which is often unavailable for challenging tasks like brachiation. Instead, WGRL guides behavior acquisition by specifying sparse waypoints for the end-effector trajectory, allowing reinforcement learning to generate the full-body motion.
- **Analysis of Waypoint-Following Reward Formulations:** The research aims to systematically examine and analyze various formulations of the waypoint-following reward within WGRL. This objective seeks to understand how different definitions of the reward function, particularly those combining distance and directional alignment, influence the efficiency of motion acquisition and the stability of learning convergence.
- **Integration of Success and Energy Rewards for Stability and Progression:** Another key objective is to demonstrate that combining a task success reward (for forward locomotion) with a mechanical energy regulation reward can achieve both consistent forward progression and overall motion stability. The motivation here is to develop a reward structure that prevents excessive energy buildup during dynamic phases while encouraging stability upon grasping.
- **Validation of Robustness and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The study aims to validate the robustness of the acquired brachiation behavior through extensive Sim-to-Sim experiments, including environments with geometric variations and previously unseen courses. Crucially, it also seeks to demonstrate zero-shot Sim-to-Real transfer of the learned policy onto a physical life-sized robot, including the emergence of failure recovery behaviors, thereby confirming the practical applicability of the method.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to overcome the limitations of prior work and current RL techniques in robotic brachiation, paving the way for more versatile and capable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a reinforcement learning-based approach, specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to enable a life-sized dual-arm robot to perform brachiation. The methodology involves a carefully designed learning framework, environment setup, and reward structure tailored for this complex task.

**Robot Platform and Environment:**
The robot utilized is a life-sized dual-arm system, mirroring the upper body of an adult human. It stands 1093 mm tall and weighs 19.6 kg. Each arm possesses five degrees of freedom. The end-effectors are specifically designed as hook-shaped hands to facilitate secure suspension from bars. This robot is based on the open-source hardware MEVITA.
The environment consists of monkey bars, which are horizontal cylindrical bars (30 mm diameter, 1200 mm length) arranged at a constant height with 400 mm intervals. To enhance robustness and bridge the Sim-to-Real gap, the simulation environment incorporates random variations in bar geometry, including up to ±20 mm in intervals, ±40 mm vertically, and ±0.2 rad in yaw orientation.

**Policy Architecture and Training Setup:**
An Actor-Critic architecture is adopted, operating under an asymmetric observation setting.
- **Actor:** Receives observations available on hardware, including base angular velocity, gravity direction, all joint positions and velocities, and a motion phase indicator (left-hand or right-hand motion). The actor's output is target joint angles, fed into a PD controller. The policy operates at 50 Hz, while the PD controller runs at 200 Hz.
- **Critic:** In addition to the actor's observations, the critic receives privileged information available only in simulation, such as historical joint data, IMU data, end-effector contact states and forces, friction coefficients, end-effector positions, and geometric details of nearby bars. These privileged observations are intended to stabilize and improve value estimation during learning.

The RL environment is built using Legged Gym, with training conducted across 4096 parallel environments. Domain randomization is applied to parameters like friction coefficients, link masses, and centers of mass. Random noise is added to the actor's observations to further enhance robustness and transferability.

**Waypoint-Guided Reinforcement Learning (WGRL):**
WGRL is the core learning framework. It induces non-linear and complex motions by guiding the end-effector trajectory through sparsely defined waypoints. The whole-body motion generation is left to the RL algorithm. Key aspects of WGRL include:
- **Waypoint Design:** Five specific waypoints are defined in the sagittal (XZ) plane for the reaching hand. These waypoints guide the hand through a sequence: detaching by rotating backward, moving forward beneath the current bar to avoid collision, and then hooking onto the target bar from above. The "hooking from above" trajectory includes lifting the end-effector sufficiently and wrapping it around the bar, intended to improve grasping success.
- **Curriculum Strategy:** Waypoint guidance is gradually relaxed through a curriculum. Initially, a small distance threshold (~ℓ_th = 0.03) is used for reaching waypoints. As learning progresses and a sufficient number of agents pass a waypoint, its distance threshold is increased, allowing for greater exploration and diversity in the acquired motions.
- **Applicability:** A key feature of WGRL is its ability to acquire complex motions even when full-body reference motion data for imitation learning is unavailable, which is a common challenge for novel robotic tasks.

**Reward Function Definitions:**
The reward function is a critical component, structured to encourage specific behaviors and properties (Table III).
- **WGRL Reward (r_WGRL):** Comprises three terms:
 - `r_pos`: A position-based reward that encourages the end-effector to approach the current target waypoint from the direction of the previous waypoint. It uses a function of cosine similarity (`cos θ`) between the approach vector and the target direction, and a normalized distance (`~ℓ`) term. The formulation `cos θ - 0.25 ~ℓ^2` was selected for stable approach guidance from a wide range of positions.
 - `r_prog`: A progress reward, given for the decrease in normalized distance to the target waypoint over time, incentivizing continuous movement towards the target.
 - `r_succ`: A sparse, binary reward given when the end-effector successfully reaches a waypoint, triggering an update to the next waypoint. This serves as a clear incentive for waypoint traversal.
- **Bars Reached Reward:** A large, positive reward proportional to the number of bars successfully reached. This term is weighted to dominate the total reward, prioritizing forward locomotion.
- **Mechanical Energy Reward:** Penalizes deviations from a target mechanical energy value (`e_ref = -72 J`), corresponding to stable dual-hand suspension. This encourages stabilization after grasping and suppresses excessive, destabilizing swings.
- **Penalty Terms:** Various penalties are included for undesirable states or actions, such as hook orientation, high linear/angular velocities, joint torque/acceleration limits, action rate, and collisions. A significant negative reward is imposed for episode termination due to failure (e.g., falling).

**Training Procedure:**
The PPO algorithm is used in a two-stage training process:
1. **Initial Motion Acquisition (4000 iterations):** The WGRL reward terms are given high weights to guide the policy in acquiring a basic brachiation motion pattern.
2. **Motion Refinement and Robustness (9000 additional iterations):** Reward weights are adjusted, with the "Bars Reached" success reward and "Mechanical Energy" reward terms dominating. This stage refines the acquired motion, emphasizing robust forward progression and stability, in preparation for Sim-to-Real transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents findings from ablation studies on WGRL reward definitions, followed by comprehensive Sim-to-Sim and hardware experiments.

**WGRL Training Experiment (Ablation Studies):**
Ablation studies were conducted to analyze the effectiveness of different components within the WGRL framework.

- **r_pos Definition Ablation:**
 - Comparing four formulations for `r_pos` (Fig. 5), the study found that definitions incorporating both distance and directional alignment yielded higher performance and more consistent learning convergence.
 - A `distance-only` reward initially showed fast progress but often led to policies that approached the bar from directions incompatible with hooking, resulting in stagnation and high performance variance (standard deviation of 1.016 bars reached at 8000 iterations).
 - A `direction-only` reward resulted in the lowest performance (0.257 bars reached), lacking sufficient incentive to approach the target waypoint.
 - Formulations combining `distance and direction` (multiplicative and additive) both achieved stable convergence and high performance. The multiplicative form `cos θ * exp(-~ℓ^2)` showed the highest performance (average 2.6 bars reached, exceeding 0.5 bars after 954 iterations), attributed to its narrow positive reward region that restricts exploration. The additive form `cos θ - 0.25~ℓ^2` was selected for subsequent experiments due to its ability to provide stable guidance from a broader range of initial positions, despite slightly lower peak performance.
- **Reward Component Ablation for WGRL:**
 - An ablation study on the three WGRL reward terms (`r_pos`, `r_prog`, `r_succ`) showed that brachiation behavior emerged only when specific combinations were used (Fig. 6).
 - `r_succ` (sparse waypoint-reaching success reward) was deemed indispensable; learning did not progress without it. However, `r_succ` alone also failed to progress, indicating the necessity of continuous feedback terms.
 - Combinations like `{r_pos, r_succ}` and `{r_prog, r_succ}` led to some progress but converged at lower performance levels (0.9 and 1.3 bars respectively). `r_pos` alone could cause motion stagnation, while `r_prog` did not always guarantee sufficient approach to the waypoint.
 - Only when all three WGRL terms (`r_pos`, `r_prog`, `r_succ`) were included did stable learning and successful brachiation acquisition occur, demonstrating their complementary roles in motivation, potential guidance, and temporal progress.

**Brachiation Result and Discussion:**

- **Sim-to-Sim Evaluation:**
 - The trained policy demonstrated robust brachiation in a MuJoCo simulation environment. In standard monkey-bar configurations, the robot continuously traversed over 30 bars in all ten trials.
 - Under geometrically varied bar configurations (±40mm vertical, ±0.2 rad yaw), the robot maintained continuous forward progression (Fig. 7, α).
 - Crucially, the robot exhibited **failure recovery behavior** when failing to grasp a bar. This involved the support-side arm flexing and extending to absorb disturbances and generate a re-swing, allowing repeated attempts at grasping.
 - The policy also adapted to entirely new environments, including a 5-degree inclined up-and-down course (93.3% success) and a circular course (91% success) (Fig. 7, β and γ), demonstrating generalization beyond training conditions.
 - The robustness was attributed to two factors: the hook mechanism's design (60mm arc diameter provides passive tolerance for a 30mm bar) and the acquired "wrap-around" hooking trajectory, which allows the hook to engage the bar even if displaced. The emergence of recovery behavior was linked to the reward design, particularly the large penalty for falling and the substantial reward for successful bar grasping, which incentivized avoiding termination and maximizing future rewards. The 180-degree central angle of the hook also contributed to resisting detachment during disturbances.

- **Hardware Experiment (Zero-shot Sim-to-Real):**
 - The policy, trained solely in simulation, was successfully deployed on the physical life-sized dual-arm robot in a zero-shot manner (Fig. 8).
 - The robot traversed four cylindrical bars arranged at 400 mm intervals without falling.
 - During each of the three reaching phases (right, left, right), the initial hooking attempt failed. However, the **failure recovery behavior** observed in simulation consistently emerged on the hardware, enabling successful reattempts and ultimate completion of the course.
 - Analysis of hardware logs (Fig. 9) showed that forward progression was achieved through repeated swings during single-hand support. During phases of repeated grasping failures, the kinetic energy peaks increased stepwise, interpreted as the robot amplifying its swing amplitude to extend reach (Fig. 10). Upon successful grasping and stable dual-hand support, the mechanical energy decayed and converged towards the target value.
 - Asymmetry was observed between left and right-hand motions; the right-hand motion converged earlier in training and appeared more refined on hardware. This was attributed to the lack of explicit symmetry constraints during learning.
 - The consistent emergence of failure recovery on hardware was highlighted as a significant finding, indicating the acquired motion strategy's effectiveness even under real-world complexities and Sim-to-Real gaps (e.g., contact modeling errors).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study makes several significant contributions to the field of robotics and reinforcement learning, with potential impacts on robot mobility and control.

Firstly, it successfully demonstrates **robust brachiation on a life-sized dual-arm robot hardware platform** using reinforcement learning. This moves beyond previous work that was often limited to simulations or small-scale systems, establishing a benchmark for complex, dynamic, arm-based locomotion in physically large and heavy robots.

A core contribution is the introduction of **Waypoint-Guided Reinforcement Learning (WGRL)**. This framework provides an effective methodology for inducing non-linear and complex motion trajectories, such as the specific hook detachment and wrap-around hooking movements, even when comprehensive full-body reference motion data for imitation learning is unavailable. This addresses a significant practical limitation in applying RL to novel and challenging robot behaviors. The detailed ablation studies on the WGRL reward function offer valuable guidelines for designing effective waypoint-following rewards, highlighting the importance of combining distance, directional, and sparse success components for stable learning.

The **reward design strategy**, which pairs a strong bar-reaching success reward for locomotion with a mechanical energy tracking reward for stability, is another notable impact. This combination was shown to foster a sophisticated control strategy: during grasping failures, the robot actively increases swing amplitude to build momentum for reattempts, prioritizing forward progression. Once a successful grasp is achieved, the policy shifts to stabilizing the motion by reducing mechanical energy. This adaptive control objective demonstrates a level of intelligence in the learned behavior that is crucial for robust real-world deployment.

The successful **zero-shot Sim-to-Real transfer** and the consistent emergence of **failure recovery behavior** on hardware are critical validations of the methodology. The ability of the robot to recover from missed grasps and reattempt without falling demonstrates the robustness of the learned policy to the inevitable discrepancies between simulation and reality, including contact model approximations and frictional variations. This indicates that the learned control policies are not brittle but generalize well to the physical world.

The potential impact of this work extends to **expanding the traversable workspace of robots**. By enabling robust arm-based locomotion, robots can operate in environments without stable footholds, such as overhead pipe networks, scaffolding, or arboreal settings, which are currently inaccessible to many ground-based or legged systems. This could have implications for infrastructure inspection, search and rescue in disaster zones, or maintenance in complex industrial facilities.

Future research directions suggested by the authors, such as incorporating symmetry constraints to improve motion consistency and integrating environment perception (e.g., visual information) for more generalized arm-based locomotion, indicate avenues for further enhancing the capabilities and versatility of such robotic systems. This study, therefore, provides both a concrete demonstration of complex robotic skill acquisition and a foundational framework for future developments in advanced robotic mobility.
