# Learning Humanoid Locomotion with World Model Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Harbin Institute of Technology introduced the World Model Reconstruction (WMR) framework, an end-to-end reinforcement learning system that enables a humanoid robot to perform robust, blind locomotion for 3.2 km across diverse, challenging real-world terrains like ice and snow. The framework achieves this by explicitly reconstructing a clean world state from noisy sensor histories and leveraging a novel gradient cutoff mechanism to maintain estimator fidelity.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with robust, blind locomotion in complex, unstructured real-world environments due to inherent instability and high degrees of freedom.
- Real-world sensor data is often noisy and incomplete, failing to capture crucial 'privileged information' (e.g., ground friction, payload) needed for effective control.
- Existing learning-based methods often suffer from the sim-to-real gap and policy gradients interfering with state estimation accuracy during concurrent training.

## Method

- ==The World Model Reconstruction (WMR)== framework integrates a World Model Estimator, a Locomotion Policy, and a Value Network in an end-to-end asymmetric actor-critic architecture.
- A novel gradient cutoff mechanism prevents policy gradients from propagating to the estimator, ensuring its objective of accurate world state reconstruction remains uncompromised.
- The framework uses motion capture data for command space generation, promoting more natural and human-like locomotion patterns, and a sparse reward function for adaptive gait learning.

## Results

- The WMR-controlled humanoid robot demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, completing a **3.2** km hike autonomously over 20 hours in challenging outdoor ice and snow environments.
- WMR achieved superior performance in simulation, exhibiting the lowest velocity and angular tracking errors and the highest terrain mastery and reward compared to all baselines.
- In real-world deployment, the framework accurately estimated payload and friction trends, effectively suppressed IMU data drift, and demonstrated dynamic gait adaptation to varying speeds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The gradient cutoff mechanism significantly improves world state reconstruction accuracy by approximately 40%, preventing 'goal dispersion' caused by competing gradients.
> - Explicitly reconstructing unobservable privileged information like friction, base velocity, and payload from noisy sensor histories is critical for robust blind locomotion.
> - Training with motion capture-derived commands, rather than random sampling, helps robots learn more natural, dynamically adaptive, and human-aligned gait patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid locomotion is a grand challenge in robotics, aiming to replicate human-like movement capabilities for robots to navigate environments designed for humans. The broader research landscape is characterized by two main approaches: traditional control methods and modern learning-based techniques, particularly reinforcement learning (RL).

**Traditional Control Methods**, such as Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) and Model Predictive Control (MPC), have enabled reliable locomotion in structured environments. However, extending these methods to complex, unstructured real-world terrains often requires intricate step planning and sophisticated sensory perception, making them brittle and computationally intensive in dynamic, noisy conditions.

**Learning-Based Methods**, especially Reinforcement Learning, have emerged as powerful alternatives, allowing robots to learn adaptive and robust locomotion policies through trial-and-error. Significant progress has been made, particularly with quadrupedal robots demonstrating agility on diverse terrains. Bipedal and humanoid robots, however, present greater challenges due to their inherent instability, high degrees of freedom, and complex dynamic balance requirements. Recent RL work has shown promise for humanoids in structured settings and even some challenging terrains, but a major hurdle remains: the **sim-to-real gap**. Policies trained in simulation often struggle with the unpredictable dynamics, sensor noise, and unmodeled complexities of the real world.

A key area of active research within learning-based locomotion is **state estimation and adaptation**. Robots need to infer crucial "privileged information" (e.g., ground friction, payload, base velocity) that is not directly observable by onboard sensors, or to denoise noisy sensor readings. Previous works have explored encoding states as latent variables, training adaptation modules, or explicitly estimating privileged information. However, many existing approaches either involve multi-stage training processes, do not guarantee full preservation of information, or suffer from policy gradients interfering with the estimation network's objective, leading to suboptimal reconstruction.

This work, "World Model Reconstruction (WMR)," addresses a critical gap by providing an end-to-end RL framework for **blind humanoid locomotion across truly challenging real-world terrains** while explicitly tackling the issues of **sensor noise and limited information**. It stands out by proposing a novel **gradient cutoff mechanism** that enhances the accuracy and interpretability of the world state estimator, a problem largely unaddressed in previous concurrent training methods. Furthermore, the utilization of a **motion capture dataset for command space** is a significant step towards generating more natural and human-aligned locomotion patterns, differentiating it from approaches relying solely on random command sampling. By demonstrating robust performance in extreme outdoor conditions, this paper pushes the boundaries of practical humanoid deployment beyond controlled lab settings, contributing a robust and scalable solution to the ongoing quest for versatile humanoid locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:

1. **Develop a robust, end-to-end learning-based controller for blind humanoid locomotion:** The goal is to enable humanoid robots to navigate complex, challenging real-world terrains without relying on explicit terrain perception, using only proprioceptive and reconstructed sensor data.
2. **Overcome limitations and noise in sensor data:** The authors aim to address the critical challenge that real-world environments present richer information than sensors can capture, and the captured data is often noisy, hindering the robot's self-understanding and environmental awareness.
3. **Explicitly reconstruct the world state:** A core objective is to design an estimator that can accurately recover denoised sensor data and reconstruct crucial "privileged information" (e.g., contact mask, base velocity, foot friction, payload) from noisy sensor histories.
4. **Enhance locomotion policy performance through reconstructed information:** The policy should leverage this explicitly reconstructed and clean world state as its sole input to achieve superior adaptability and robustness.
5. **Ensure estimator accuracy independent of policy updates:** A specific objective is to prevent the policy's learning gradients from distorting the estimator's goal of accurate world reconstruction, thereby maintaining the interpretability and fidelity of the estimated state.
6. **Achieve seamless zero-shot sim-to-real transfer:** The developed framework should effectively generalize from simulation training to real-world deployment on a variety of terrains, including ice and snow, without requiring additional real-world fine-tuning.
7. **Generate precise and human-like command tracking:** By utilizing motion capture data for command generation, the aim is to produce more natural speed transitions and locomotion patterns aligned with human movement distributions.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the current gap in humanoid robotics: while robots can perform well in controlled lab settings, navigating complex, dynamic, and unpredictable real-world environments remains a significant challenge. The authors highlight that the inherent instability of humanoids, coupled with sensor limitations and noise, necessitates a novel approach to state estimation and policy learning to unlock their full potential for human-accessible tasks. The desire to make humanoids truly autonomous and capable of long-duration missions in demanding outdoor conditions is a strong driving force.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **World Model Reconstruction (WMR)**, an end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework for blind humanoid locomotion. The methodology integrates a novel state estimation component with an asymmetric actor-critic policy learning architecture.

**A. World Model Reconstruction (WMR) Framework Overview:**
WMR consists of three main components:
1. **World Model Estimator:** Explicitly reconstructs the world state from noisy sensor histories.
2. **Locomotion Policy:** Maps the *reconstructed* world state (not raw sensor data) to motor actions.
3. **Value Network:** Evaluates the policy by receiving noise-free world information.

**B. Context-Aided Estimator:**
- **Purpose:** To accurately recover sensor data (denoising) and reconstruct privileged information (e.g., contact mask, base velocity, foot friction, payload) from noisy observations.
- **Input:** Historical observations (O_m_t).
- **Architecture:** An encoder-decoder framework. A single-head LSTM encoder processes the perceptual history to extract features, while a multi-head decoder reconstructs the comprehensive world model.
- **Decoder Branches:**
 - **Continuous Decoder:** Uses Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for continuous states (e.g., angular velocity, joint positions/velocities, base velocity, friction).
 - **Discrete Decoder:** Uses Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss for discrete states (e.g., contact mask), incorporating a sigmoid activation.
- **Regularization:** An L1 regularization term is applied to the latent representation to promote sparsity and improve efficiency.
- **Overall Reconstruction Loss (L_recon):** A weighted sum of L_MSE, L_BCE, and L_L1.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Employed during training to simulate realistic sensor noise and environmental variations (e.g., varying friction, payload, gravity, joint properties) in simulation, facilitating robust sim-to-real transfer.

**C. Policy Learning:**
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** The value network receives noise-free, privileged world information for efficient learning, while the locomotion policy receives inputs solely from the reconstructed world state provided by the estimator.
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to update the locomotion policy (π) by maximizing expected return, utilizing an advantage function.
- **Loss Functions:** L_π for the policy and L_v for the value network (minimizing error between predicted and actual returns).

**D. Gradient Cutoff Mechanism (Critical Innovation):**
- **Mechanism:** During the joint training procedure, gradients from the policy loss (L_π) are *intentionally cut off* before back-propagating to the estimator.
- **Purpose:** This ensures that the estimator's optimization remains focused exclusively on accurate world reconstruction, preventing it from being influenced or "distracted" by the policy's objective of maximizing reward. This maintains the interpretability and fidelity of the estimated state.

**E. Unified Training Procedure:**
- The estimator, value network, and locomotion policy are trained simultaneously using a single optimizer.
- The overall loss function (L) is a weighted sum of L_recon, L_v, and L_π. This creates a positive feedback loop where an improving policy provides better trajectories for the estimator, and a more accurate estimator provides better inputs for the policy.

**F. Reward Formulation:**
- The reward function is **deliberately designed without predefined motion references** (e.g., periodic ground contact, foot lift height, reference trajectory tracking, stylized imitation).
- It comprises a **task reward** (encouraging accurate tracking of commanded linear and angular velocities) and **regularization rewards** (promoting stable, human-like standing/walking, penalizing energy consumption, excessive angular velocity, joint acceleration, action rate, base orientation deviation, joint limit/deviation, and undesired contacts, while rewarding appropriate foot air time and force, and penalizing feet sliding and flying). This sparse reward design allows the robot to learn adaptive gaits.

**G. Terrain Curriculum:**
- Training begins on flat ground, progressively increasing difficulty based on walk length.
- Terrains include random rough surfaces, box obstacles, pyramid slopes, threshold barriers, and stairs, with randomized friction and restitution.

**H. Command Configuration:**
- The goal space (commanded linear and angular velocities) is derived from the **AMASS motion capture dataset**.
- This provides a more natural and human-aligned distribution of command velocities, with smoother transitions, compared to fixed or randomly sampled commands, enhancing the robot's ability to generate realistic and versatile movements.

**I. Architecture Details:**
- LSTM (256 hidden dimension) is used for the history encoder, policy network, and value network to process sequential data and maintain memory.
- Multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with ELU activations form the decoder heads, policy output layers, and value network output.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The WMR framework demonstrated superior performance through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world scenarios.

**A. Comparisons with Baselines (Simulation):**
- **Reconstruction Accuracy (Q1 & Q3):** WMR consistently outperformed all baselines in world state reconstruction accuracy (E_recon). The introduction of the **gradient cutoff mechanism was crucial, improving reconstruction accuracy by approximately 40%** compared to WMR without gradient cutoff. This validated that the cutoff prevents "goal dispersion" from competing gradients, enabling the estimator to truly focus on reconstruction. Notably, privileged information (like friction, base velocity) was reconstructed accurately without direct supervision.
- **Episode Metrics (Q2 & Q3):** WMR achieved the lowest mean episode linear velocity tracking error (E_vel), mean episode angular velocity tracking error (E_ang), and highest mean terrain levels mastered (M_terrain) and mean episode reward (M_reward) compared to all baselines (WMR without Gradient Cutoff, WMR with Random Command Sample, PPO, Denoising World Model Learning).
 - WMR significantly improved performance over PPO (without reconstruction) and other reconstruction methods lacking effective gradient isolation.
 - WMR with motion capture (MoCap) commands showed slight improvements in velocity and angular tracking compared to WMR with random command sampling, highlighting the benefit of human-like command distribution.

**B. Ablation Study on Architecture (Simulation) (Q4):**
- Comparing LSTM, GRU, MLP, and Transformer architectures for the history encoder, policy, and value network:
 - LSTM-based WMR showed superior overall performance across most metrics (E_ang, E_recon, M_terrain, M_reward), demonstrating its effectiveness in handling time series data and maintaining memory.
 - The Transformer achieved the lowest E_vel but was slightly less robust in other aspects, possibly due to limited training episodes or context window length.
 - GRU performed slightly worse than LSTM, and MLP was the least satisfactory due to its limited temporal processing ability.

**C. Sim-to-Real Evaluation (Q1 & Q5):**
- **Real-World Deployment:** The humanoid robot, controlled by WMR, successfully performed **zero-shot sim-to-real transfer**. It was deployed for approximately **20 hours over one week** in an outdoor environment covered in **ice and snow**, navigating rough, gravel, sloping, and deep snow terrains.
 - The robot completed a **3.2 km hike without any human assistance or gantry support**, demonstrating robust adaptability and resistance to interference.
 - It maintained stability on low-friction surfaces and resisted impact from humans.
- **Reconstruction Evaluation (Real-World):**
 - During a stair descent scenario, the WMR framework demonstrated precise reconstruction of the world state, including contact mask and linear velocity, even in an environment not directly encountered during training.
 - **Payload Estimation:** WMR accurately estimated added mass in real-world scenarios, with an average reconstruction accuracy of about 0.86 for loads ranging from -10 kg to 10 kg, demonstrating the ability to infer unobservable states.
 - **Gait Patterns Study:** Due to the reward function deliberately omitting motion references, the robot learned to adapt its step frequency dynamically to commanded speeds, mimicking natural human walking patterns.
 - **Real-World Reconstruction Accuracy:** Analysis of a 10-second segment during deployment showed WMR accurately estimating friction trends on brick, marble (low friction), and asphalt (high friction) surfaces. It responded quickly to changes in ground friction (e.g., right foot contacting marble). The framework also **effectively suppressed accumulated data drift from IMU noise**, providing more accurate yaw angle estimates than raw IMU integration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The World Model Reconstruction (WMR) framework represents a significant advancement in humanoid locomotion, offering several profound implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Truly Robust Real-World Humanoid Locomotion:** This work addresses a critical bottleneck in humanoid robotics by delivering a controller capable of reliable, blind locomotion across highly challenging and unstructured outdoor terrains (ice, snow, rough ground, slopes). The successful 3.2 km autonomous traversal in such conditions is a testament to its robustness and sets a new benchmark for practical humanoid deployment. This paves the way for humanoids to operate in environments traditionally requiring human intervention, expanding their utility in logistics, disaster response, and exploration.

2. **Addressing the Sensor Limitation and Noise Challenge:** The WMR framework provides an elegant and effective solution to the pervasive problem of noisy and incomplete sensor data in real-world robotics. By explicitly reconstructing a clean and comprehensive world state, including unobservable privileged information, the robot can make more informed decisions, leading to significantly enhanced control and adaptability. This approach is highly relevant for any complex robotic system operating in dynamic, uncertain environments.

3. **Novelty of Gradient Cutoff Mechanism:** The proposed gradient cutoff is a key methodological contribution. By ensuring the estimator's accuracy is uncompromised by the policy's learning objectives, it maintains the fidelity and interpretability of the world model. This design principle could be widely applicable in other multi-component learning systems where distinct objectives need to be preserved.

4. **Advancing Sim-to-Real Transfer for Complex Platforms:** The demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a high-degree-of-freedom humanoid robot, operating flawlessly for extended periods in diverse real-world conditions, is a major achievement. The combination of domain randomization, a robust state estimator, and a carefully designed reward function offers a powerful paradigm for bridging the simulation-reality gap, which has historically plagued robotics research.

5. **Promoting Natural and Adaptive Gaits:** The use of motion capture data for command generation, coupled with a reward function that avoids predefined motion references, allows the robot to learn natural, human-like gait patterns that adapt dynamically to commanded speeds. This enhances both the robot's efficiency and its acceptance in human environments.

6. **Foundation for Future Advanced Robotics:** This work lays a strong foundation for integrating more advanced capabilities. As the authors suggest, incorporating environmental terrain perception (e.g., height maps) into the WMR framework could further unlock navigation in even more complex scenarios (e.g., discrete jumping obstacles, high platforms). The robust state estimation provided by WMR would be an ideal input for such perceptual modules.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge two main limitations:
1. **Lack of Terrain Shape Sensing:** The robot cannot sense the specific shape of the terrain (e.g., height information), which limits its mobility on obstacles requiring precise height negotiation. The proposed solution is to integrate height map input and denoise it using WMR.
2. **Reliance on Reconstructed Information:** The locomotion policy solely relies on reconstructed information, which, despite its demonstrated accuracy, carries an inherent risk of estimation errors leading to system crashes. While not observed during 20 hours of deployment, this remains a theoretical vulnerability, especially in truly novel situations.

Overall, WMR represents a significant leap forward in enabling humanoids to move beyond controlled settings and operate autonomously in the unpredictable complexity of the real world, thereby accelerating the practical adoption of humanoid robotics.
