# LocoTouch: Learning Dynamic Quadrupedal Transport with Tactile Sensing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and Google DeepMind developed LocoTouch, a system enabling quadrupedal robots to dynamically transport diverse unsecured objects using a custom high-density tactile sensor and a reinforcement learning pipeline. It achieved robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for varied objects across uneven terrains and sustained locomotion, with the tactile student policy reaching up to 96.7% success in challenging transport tasks.

## Problem

- Quadrupedal robots struggle with dynamic object interactions requiring sustained and precise contact control, especially for long-distance transport of unsecured objects.
- Existing quadrupedal object transport methods are restrictive, relying on containers or custom mounts that limit object variety and adaptive robot actions.
- Accurately simulating distributed tactile sensors, particularly "contact spread," is computationally expensive for reinforcement learning, and enforcing gait symmetry often depends on robot-specific kinematic assumptions.

## Method

- Designed a high-density, large-area ==piezoresistive tactile sensor== covering the robot's back (17x13 array, 221 taxels) for precise contact sensing.
- Implemented an "==expanded collision model==" in simulation, assigning enlarged collision areas to taxels to efficiently reproduce realistic contact spread and enable high-fidelity tactile feedback.
- Developed a two-stage ==reinforcement learning== pipeline (==teacher-student== approach) with an ==adaptive gait reward== to learn robust, symmetric, and frequency-adaptive locomotion policies from binary tactile inputs, enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- LocoTouch successfully transported a diverse range of unsecured cylindrical and non-cylindrical objects (**0.03-1.45kg** mass) over long distances (up to **60m**) and varied terrains in real-world, zero-shot deployment.
- The tactile student policy achieved high success rates in simulation (e.g., **96.7%** for 5s transport, **96.4%** for 10s transport), while policies lacking object interaction feedback or tactile inputs failed.
- The expanded collision model accurately reproduced real tactile contact patterns, and the adaptive gait reward significantly improved real-world gait symmetry and velocity tracking compared to baselines, preventing asymmetric gaits and lateral drifting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-density, distributed tactile sensing on the robot's back is crucial for perceiving and adapting to the dynamic state of unsecured objects during transport.
> - Efficiently simulating contact spread using an "expanded collision model" is vital for effective tactile-aware policy learning in reinforcement learning, bridging the sim-to-real gap.
> - An adaptive gait reward function that promotes synchronized diagonal and alternating lateral leg contacts, informed by an object-task-aware symmetricity score, improves gait symmetry and task performance for dynamic object transport.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Recent advancements have significantly enhanced the agility and robustness of quadrupedal robots in traversing complex and challenging terrains. Building upon these locomotion capabilities, research has begun to explore loco-manipulation tasks, such as object pushing, dribbling, and pulling. However, many of these efforts have focused on relatively simple or short-duration interactions. A notable gap exists in enabling quadrupedal robots to perform dynamic object interactions that require sustained and precise contact control, particularly tasks like long-distance transport of unsecured objects.

Previous approaches for quadrupedal object transport frequently involve containing objects within boxes or relying on custom, object-specific mounting and fastening mechanisms. These methods impose limitations on the variety of object shapes and sizes that can be handled, prevent adaptive robot actions to object movement within containers, and increase the robot's physical footprint when not transporting objects. Transporting unsecured objects, which move dynamically on the robot's back, presents challenges related to object state perception, successful sim-to-real transfer, and ensuring policy robustness and precision in highly dynamic systems.

The integration of tactile sensing in quadrupedal robots has gained interest, with applications such as foot-mounted sensors for locomotion stability and terrain perception, and torso-mounted sensors for passive data collection to classify reactive behaviors. This work extends the application of tactile sensing by actively leveraging high-density, wide-coverage tactile feedback from the entire robot back for autonomous, dexterous object transport, moving beyond passive data collection or purely locomotion-focused sensing.

In the domain of tactile simulation and policy learning, various methods exist. Point-based tactile sensors are often simulated as individual rigid bodies, with policies learning from binary contact signals. For distributed tactile sensors, advanced simulation techniques can model material softness and force spread, but these are often computationally expensive for reinforcement learning (RL) which requires parallel simulation. Simpler compliant contact models may only capture coarse binary signals, failing to represent realistic contact spread. This research aims to develop an efficient simulation method that accurately captures the coupled contact spread of distributed sensors without incurring high computational costs, specifically for a highly dynamic quadrupedal system with dense tactile input (221 taxels). Furthermore, while existing works have explored enforcing gait symmetry in RL-based locomotion, they often rely on robot-specific kinematic assumptions, may mismatch kinematic and physical symmetries, struggle with high-dimensional states, and sometimes overlook temporal symmetry. This work introduces an adaptive gait reward that addresses these limitations by promoting gait-level symmetry based on swing-stance phases, independent of robot morphology.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable quadrupedal robots to robustly transport a diverse range of unsecured cylindrical objects over long distances in real-world environments. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and addresses identified challenges:

- **Addressing Dynamic Object Interactions:** Quadrupedal robots have traditionally struggled with dynamic interactions where contact must be precisely sensed and controlled. Transporting unsecured objects presents a challenging instance of this problem, as the object's position and orientation on the robot's back are constantly changing and require continuous adaptation. The motivation is to bridge this gap by enabling robots to handle these complex, sustained contact scenarios.

- **Overcoming Limitations of Conventional Transport Methods:** Current quadrupedal transport methods, such as using containers or custom mounting mechanisms, are restrictive. They limit the types of objects that can be carried, prevent the robot from adapting to object movements, and add bulk to the robot. The objective is to develop a method that allows for flexible, unconstrained transport, removing the need for object-specific fastening or containment.

- **Enabling Object State Perception through Tactile Sensing:** Directly perceiving the state of an unsecured, dynamically moving object on a robot's back is challenging. Traditional visual sensing might be occluded or struggle with close-range contact dynamics. The research is motivated by the hypothesis that high-density, distributed tactile sensing can provide the necessary feedback for precise contact sensing and control, allowing the robot to infer and react to the object's dynamic state effectively.

- **Achieving Efficient and High-Fidelity Tactile Simulation:** Learning complex tactile-aware policies through reinforcement learning requires extensive training in simulation. A critical challenge is accurately simulating the physical behavior of distributed tactile sensors, particularly the phenomenon of "contact spread" where a localized force activates adjacent sensing units due to material deformation. The objective is to develop an efficient and scalable simulation method that can reproduce high-fidelity tactile signals, capturing these coupled contact characteristics, without being computationally prohibitive for large-scale RL training.

- **Developing Robust and Adaptive Locomotion Gaits:** Transporting an unsecured object necessitates a locomotion policy that is not only stable but also adaptable to the object's dynamics. Traditional approaches often rely on predefined gait patterns with fixed timing, which can hinder adaptability. The motivation is to design a reward function that encourages stable, symmetric, and frequency-adaptive locomotion gaits, allowing the robot to dynamically adjust its stepping patterns in response to both command changes and object movements, without external timing references.

- **Ensuring Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** For practical deployment, policies trained in simulation must transfer effectively to the real world without extensive real-world fine-tuning. This is particularly challenging for tactile-aware policies due to potential discrepancies between simulated and real tactile signals. The objective is to design the sensor, simulation, and learning pipeline to minimize the sim-to-real gap, thereby enabling robust zero-shot transfer.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The LocoTouch system integrates custom hardware design with a two-stage reinforcement learning pipeline to enable quadrupedal robots to transport unsecured objects using tactile feedback.

#### 3.1. Distributed Tactile Sensing and Simulation

- **Sensor Design:** A high-density, large-area tactile sensor is designed to cover the entire back of a quadrupedal robot. It measures 250 × 180 mm and consists of a piezoresistive film sandwiched between orthogonally aligned conductive fabric electrodes. Each intersection forms a taxel, resulting in a 17x13 array of 221 taxels, with an effective sensing area of 14.3 × 12.8 mm per unit. Signal acquisition is achieved by sequentially processing rows, applying a constant current, and measuring voltage drop. The design emphasizes minimizing gaps between taxels and using conductive fabric for higher sensitivity and scalable fabrication compared to conductive threads.

- **Simulation of Coupled Contacts:** A key challenge is simulating the signal coupling (contact spread) that occurs in real distributed tactile sensors due to mechanical deformation propagating through soft materials. Standard taxel-based approaches fail to capture this. To address this efficiently, the researchers implement an "expanded collision model" in simulation. Each simulated taxel is given a calibrated enlarged collision area of 18.3 × 17.5 mm. This allows contact forces applied near the edge of a taxel to trigger readings in multiple nearby taxels, effectively reproducing the spatial spread of real contact signals without explicitly simulating detailed material deformation.

- **Binary Contact Signals:** To mitigate the sim-to-real gap caused by noisy and non-linear voltage-force relationships in real sensors, contact signals are converted to binary 0-1 readings. In simulation, a fixed force threshold (0.05N) is used, while in the real sensor, a calibrated voltage change value defines the threshold. Further reduction of the sim-to-real gap is achieved by randomly flipping 0.5% of contact and non-contact signals in simulation, and by simulating the real-world tactile signal latency (0.02 – 0.04s).

#### 3.2. Tactile-Aware Quadrupedal Policy Learning

A teacher-student approach is adopted for policy training, similar to other visual locomotion policy learning methods.

- **Teacher Policy Training:**
 - **Environment:** The teacher policy is trained using reinforcement learning (PPO algorithm) in IsaacLab, utilizing 4096 parallel simulation environments. The environment includes a diverse set of cylindrical objects with randomized physical properties (radius, length, mass, friction) and initial poses.
 - **Observations:** The teacher policy receives privileged information: a 6-step history of object pose and velocity, robot proprioception (gravity vector, base angular velocity, joint positions and velocities), velocity commands, and the last action. Noise is added to observations during training to enhance robustness and narrow the domain gap for the student policy.
 - **Actions:** The policy outputs target joint positions, which are then tracked by a PD controller.
 - **Domain Randomization:** To improve robustness, the training environment incorporates randomization of robot trunk mass/friction, foot friction, initial joint states, and applies random external pushes to both the object and the robot. A velocity command curriculum is also used.
 - **Reward Design:** The reward function consists of five components:
 - `r_alive`: Constant positive reward for episode survival.
 - `r_task`: Encourages velocity command tracking and object balancing.
 - `r_reg`: Standard regulatory terms for stable and smooth locomotion (e.g., base height, roll-pitch, joint limits, velocities, accelerations, torques).
 - `r_drag`: A novel binary penalty for each leg, activated when a foot is near the ground with high lateral velocity. This mitigates the sim-to-real gap caused by real foot deformation causing earlier ground contact than simulated rigid feet.
 - `r_gait`: An adaptive gait reward designed to promote symmetric and stable locomotion without predefined gait patterns or timing references. It consists of two terms: one encouraging synchronized contact for diagonal leg pairs, and another promoting alternating contact for lateral leg pairs. A key feature is the adaptive coefficient `γ_sym` for the diagonal term, which combines a normalized task performance score (`α_task`) with a symmetricity score (`f_sym`). The `f_sym` score is computed based on the current and previous air times of diagonal pairs, encouraging longer air times when previous ones were short and penalizing excessively long air times, thereby promoting balanced swing durations.

- **Student Policy Distillation:**
 - After the teacher policy is trained with object state information, a student policy is trained to replicate the teacher's behavior using tactile inputs.
 - **Architecture:** The student policy replaces the object state input with a binary tactile map. A ConvNet-GRU encoder processes the tactile map, and the resulting embedding is fed, along with proprioception and command inputs, into an MLP-based policy backbone.
 - **Distillation Process:** A two-stage distillation is employed:
 1. **Behavior Cloning (BC):** The student policy is pre-trained on a dataset of 400,000 state-action pairs collected from the teacher.
 2. **DAgger Refinement:** The student policy is further refined over 7 iterations, with each iteration collecting 200,000 additional state-action pairs, allowing the student to learn from its own data collection and teacher corrections.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several findings regarding the LocoTouch system's capabilities in quadrupedal transport, tactile simulation fidelity, and the impact of the adaptive gait reward.

#### 4.1. Dexterous Quadrupedal Transport with Tactile Sensing

LocoTouch, deployed on a Unitree Go1 robot equipped with the custom distributed tactile sensor, demonstrated the ability to dynamically balance and transport a 1.45kg cylinder through dense obstacles and sharp turns in real-world environments. This indicated proficiency in velocity command tracking and object stability maintenance.

The system exhibited generalization to a diverse range of unsecured cylindrical everyday objects, including those with varying dimensions (0.03 to 0.18m diameter, 0.10 to 1.26m length) and masses (0.03 to 1.45kg). This included objects with slippery surfaces, such as a drink can. LocoTouch also demonstrated robustness to external perturbations and disturbances during transport.

Observations of the robot's behavior under time-varying forward velocity commands revealed agile and adaptive responses. The robot rapidly tracked command changes, maintaining object balance. An inverse coupling between the robot's pitch angle and the object's forward displacement in the robot frame was noted, indicating active compensation for object motion. The foot-contact pattern showed adaptive stepping, with the robot adjusting its frequency in response to velocity command changes (e.g., slowing when command dropped, speeding up when the object slid backward despite constant command). These behaviors suggest the policy's capacity to adapt gait frequency based on both commanded velocity and object state. LocoTouch also demonstrated stable transport of non-cylindrical objects (e.g., cups, wrenches) and exhibited self-correction for off-center objects. It successfully generalized to diverse uneven terrains (slopes, gravel, rough surfaces) and maintained stability during long-distance transport (up to 60m with a slippery object).

#### 4.2. Quantitative Evaluation of LocoTouch (Simulation)

Quantitative evaluations were performed in simulation across two task categories: pure balancing (zero-velocity commands) and object transport (non-zero velocity commands), for durations of 5 and 10 seconds, over 8,000 trajectories.
- The **teacher policy** achieved high success rates (99.4% for B-5s, 98.8% for B-10s, 99.8% for T-5s, 99.8% for T-10s).
- The **tactile student policy** also maintained high performance (96.2% for B-5s, 95.8% for B-10s, 96.7% for T-5s, 96.4% for T-10s).
- In contrast, a **teacher variant without object state inputs**, a **student variant without tactile inputs**, and a **locomotion-only policy** (trained without object interaction) all exhibited near-complete failure (0.0% to 0.77% success rates across tasks). This indicated that both rich object interaction feedback and tactile sensing are critical for these tasks.

#### 4.3. High-Fidelity Tactile Simulation with Expanded Collision Model

The fidelity of the proposed tactile simulation method was evaluated by comparing simulated tactile maps with real sensor signals for specific object poses.
- The **expanded collision model** (Ours) reproduced real sensor contact patterns with high faithfulness in both shape and spatial distribution.
- An **intersection-only model** (InterSect) underestimated the contact spread.
- A **Gaussian-smoothed model** (Filtered) excessively blurred the contact patterns.
This confirmed that the expanded collision model is effective in efficiently simulating realistic contact spread.

#### 4.4. Symmetricity Function Improves Gait Symmetry and Task Performance

The impact of the adaptive gait reward's symmetricity function on gait behavior was assessed.
- In simulation, comparing policies trained with the **symmetricity function** versus a **constant `γ_sym`** value, the symmetricity function led to well-aligned air times for the two diagonal foot pairs and encouraged a lower overall stepping frequency, indicating improved gait symmetry. The baseline policy with a constant `γ_sym` showed significant air time discrepancies between diagonal pairs.
- In real-world deployment (0.3m/s forward velocity), the policy trained with the symmetricity function maintained symmetric gaits and accurately tracked commanded velocity. The baseline policy, however, exhibited asymmetric gaits and lateral drifting, which also degraded tracking performance due to uneven foot deformation. This demonstrated that the symmetricity function contributes to more natural, stable, and accurate real-world locomotion.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The LocoTouch system represents a notable advancement in the capabilities of quadrupedal robots, particularly in dynamic object interaction and dexterous manipulation. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

- **Bridging the Gap in Quadrupedal Manipulation:** LocoTouch addresses a significant challenge in robotics by enabling quadrupedal robots to perform dynamic, sustained contact tasks involving unsecured objects. This capability moves beyond simple loco-manipulation or static object handling, expanding the potential applications of legged robots in complex, unstructured environments.

- **Enhancing Robot Autonomy and Versatility:** By eliminating the need for custom mounting mechanisms, containers, or manual fastening, LocoTouch allows quadrupedal robots to transport a wider variety of everyday objects with diverse physical properties. This increases the autonomy and versatility of these robots, making them more adaptable to unforeseen tasks and objects in real-world scenarios.

- **Advancement in Tactile Sensing Integration:** The work highlights the critical role of high-density, wide-coverage tactile sensing for complex dynamic tasks in legged robots. The custom sensor design and its effective integration into a learning-based control system demonstrate a viable pathway for incorporating sophisticated tactile feedback for active interaction, moving beyond passive data collection.

- **Innovation in Simulation and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed "expanded collision model" for tactile simulation is a significant contribution. It provides an efficient method for generating high-fidelity tactile signals that accurately capture coupled contact phenomena, which is crucial for successful policy learning via reinforcement learning. The achievement of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for such a complex, tactile-aware policy on a dynamic quadrupedal system is a key enabler for rapid deployment and scalability.

- **Development of Robust and Adaptive Control Policies:** The adaptive gait reward function, which promotes symmetric and frequency-adaptive locomotion without reliance on predefined patterns or timing, contributes to more natural, stable, and robust robot behaviors. This method allows the robot to dynamically adjust its gait based on task commands and real-time object dynamics, enhancing its adaptability to unforeseen disturbances and environmental changes.

- **Potential Real-World Applications:** The demonstrated ability to transport objects reliably over long distances, on uneven terrains, and under perturbations opens up various practical applications. These could include logistics in warehouses, material handling in construction sites, assistance in domestic environments, or carrying equipment in search and rescue operations, where traditional wheeled or fixed-base manipulators might struggle.

- **Future Research Directions:** The authors identify future work including the integration of multi-modal sensing (e.g., visual-tactile sensing) to further enhance versatility, and expanding tactile coverage to the full robot body for omnidirectional interaction and more comprehensive whole-body manipulation. This suggests a roadmap for continued research in perception-driven dexterous locomotion.

**Limitations** noted by the authors include difficulties balancing cylindrical objects whose axes align with the robot’s forward torso direction, attributed to the training setup's yaw angle randomization range and the robot's morphology. Additionally, while robust on uneven terrain through domain randomization, performance degrades on challenging terrains like stairs due to training primarily on flat ground. These acknowledged limitations provide specific avenues for future research and development.
