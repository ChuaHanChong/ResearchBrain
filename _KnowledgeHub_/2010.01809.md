# Long-tailed Recognition by Routing Diverse Distribution-Aware Experts

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed RoutIng Diverse Experts (RIDE), a multi-expert system for long-tailed recognition, demonstrating a principled method to simultaneously reduce model bias and variance by training diverse, distribution-aware experts. RIDE consistently achieved state-of-the-art accuracy, outperforming prior methods by 5-7% on benchmarks like ImageNet-LT and iNaturalist 2018, while maintaining balanced performance across all class frequency splits and improving computational efficiency through dynamic expert routing.

## Problem

- Real-world datasets often exhibit a long-tailed distribution, where most classes have very few examples, leading to poor model performance on these "tail" classes.
- Existing long-tailed recognition methods frequently suffer from a "head-tail performance trade-off," sacrificing accuracy on common (head) classes to improve rare (tail) ones.
- Many current approaches increase model variance and leave a substantial bias gap between head and tail classes, indicating unstable predictions and persistent systematic errors for rare categories.

## Method

- A multi-expert system, **RoutIng Diverse Experts (RIDE)**, featuring a ==shared backbone== and independent later layers for 'n' experts to promote efficiency and specialization.
- A two-stage training process: Stage 1 involves joint optimization of experts using individual classification losses and a ==distribution-aware diversity loss== with ==class-wise temperature scaling==.
- Stage 2 trains a ==dynamic router== to selectively activate experts during inference, significantly reducing computational cost while maintaining performance.

## Results

- RIDE achieved SOTA on CIFAR100-LT, ImageNet-LT, and iNaturalist 2018 (**5-7%** over prior SOTA, e.g. **55.4%** on ImageNet-LT/ResNet-50, +**7.7%**), improving all class splits and overcoming the head-tail trade-off.
- The dynamic expert router reduced computational costs to be comparable or lower than single-expert baselines (e.g., **0.9x GFlops** for 2 experts on ImageNet-LT), making the multi-expert system efficient for deployment.
- RIDE demonstrated universality, enhancing performance when integrated with various backbone networks (including Swin-Transformers) and existing long-tail algorithms.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing long-tailed recognition methods often increase model variance across class splits and maintain a significant bias gap between head and tail classes despite improving overall tail accuracy.
> - Training individual experts with separate classification losses and explicitly encouraging expert diversity through a distribution-aware loss with class-wise temperature scaling is crucial for reducing both bias and variance.
> - Dynamic routing of experts based on instance characteristics allows for efficient inference, deploying more experts for challenging tail instances and fewer for easily recognizable head instances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the problem of long-tailed recognition in machine learning, a prevalent challenge where real-world datasets exhibit an imbalanced distribution of classes. In such datasets, a few "head" classes contain many instances, while most "tail" classes have very few. Standard classification models often perform poorly on these tail classes, as they are overwhelmed by the more frequent head classes during training.

Existing approaches to long-tailed recognition primarily fall into two categories:
1. **Class re-balancing/re-weighting strategies:** These methods assign greater importance to tail instances, either by re-sampling data (under-sampling head classes or over-sampling tail classes) or by modifying the loss function to give higher weights to tail classes. Examples include LDAM (Cao et al., 2019), cRT, and $\tau$-norm (Kang et al., 2020), and Focal Loss (Lin et al., 2017).
2. **Ensembling or multi-expert frameworks:** These approaches divide the long-tailed data into groups (e.g., head, medium, tail) and train separate models (experts) on each group, subsequently combining their predictions. Examples include BBN (Zhou et al., 2020) and LFME (Xiang et al., 2020).

A common limitation identified in the broader landscape is that while these methods generally improve performance on tail classes, they frequently incur a performance reduction on head classes, creating a "head-tail performance trade-off." Furthermore, the authors' initial analysis suggests that many existing long-tail methods, despite improving tail accuracy, tend to increase the model variance across all classes and maintain a substantial bias gap between head and tail classes.

This paper positions itself by offering a principled analysis of these limitations through the lens of model bias and variance. It then proposes a multi-expert framework designed to simultaneously mitigate these issues, aiming to reduce both bias and variance while improving performance across all class splits without sacrificing head accuracy, and doing so with computational efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are rooted in a detailed analysis of the limitations of existing long-tailed recognition methods.

**Motivation from Limitations of Existing Methods:**
The authors observe that natural data often follows a long-tail distribution, which poses two main challenges:
1. **Small-data learning for tail classes:** Models struggle to generalize from the limited examples available for rare categories.
2. **Extreme imbalanced classification:** The disparity in instance counts between head and tail classes can cause models to prioritize head classes, leading to poor discrimination for tail classes.

Current methods, such as re-balancing/re-weighting or multi-expert ensembles, attempt to mitigate these issues. However, the authors' empirical analysis, using a dynamic view of the training data (evaluating model performance as the training set fluctuates), reveals systematic drawbacks:
- **Head-Tail Performance Trade-off:** While existing methods may increase overall, medium-, and few-shot accuracies, they often decrease many-shot (head) accuracy.
- **Increased Model Variance:** A key finding from their bias-variance analysis (Table 1a) is that state-of-the-art long-tail methods generally *increase* the model variance across all class splits compared to a standard cross-entropy classifier. This indicates instability in predictions when the training data varies.
- **Persistent Head-Tail Bias Gap:** Although existing methods reduce the overall model bias by lowering the tail bias, the gap between the bias for head classes (e.g., 0.3) and tail classes (e.g., 0.8) remains large. This suggests that tail classes are still significantly more prone to systematic errors.
- **Increased Confusion for Tail Classes:** Further statistical analysis (Table 1b) indicates that tail classes experience more frequent and larger confusion with hard negative classes (classes with the largest softmax score among incorrect predictions), contributing to their higher bias.

**Key Objectives of the Proposed Work:**
Based on these identified limitations, the authors aim to develop a new long-tailed classifier with the following objectives:
1. **Reduce Model Variance:** Introduce mechanisms to enhance the stability of predictions across all classes, directly addressing the observed increase in variance from existing methods.
2. **Reduce Model Bias for Tail Classes:** Specifically target the high bias observed in tail classes, while maintaining or improving performance on head classes, thereby closing the head-tail bias gap.
3. **Overcome the Head-Tail Performance Trade-off:** Develop a method that improves accuracy across all class splits (many-shot, medium-shot, and few-shot) simultaneously, rather than sacrificing one for another.
4. **Maintain Computational Efficiency:** Ensure that the proposed multi-expert framework does not incur a disproportionate increase in computational cost during inference, potentially even achieving lower costs than common baselines.
5. **Develop a Universal Framework:** Design a method that is broadly applicable to various backbone network architectures (e.g., ResNet, ResNeXt, Swin Transformer) and can integrate with existing long-tail algorithms and training mechanisms to offer consistent performance improvements.

The motivation is to move beyond intuitive solutions that address only parts of the problem and instead provide a principled, data-distribution-aware approach that simultaneously tackles the issues of bias, variance, and class imbalance without performance compromises.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel long-tailed classifier called **RoutIng Diverse Experts (RIDE)**. RIDE is structured as a multi-expert system with dynamic routing, designed to address the identified issues of model bias and variance. The methodology involves a two-stage training process and specific architectural and loss components.

**A. Multi-Expert Architecture with Shared Backbone:**
- **Shared Backbone:** To promote efficiency and generalization, RIDE uses a shared early backbone (denoted as $f_\theta$) across all experts. This is based on the premise that early layers of CNNs encode generic, low-level features applicable across different classes.
- **Independent Later Layers:** Following the shared backbone, there are $n$ independent later layers ($\Psi = \{\psi_{\theta_1}, \dots, \psi_{\theta_n}\}$), each forming part of an individual expert.
- **Channel Reduction:** To mitigate overfitting, particularly on the small training data of tail classes, the number of filter channels in these independent later layers is reduced (e.g., by 1/4).

**B. Stage 1: Joint Optimization of Diverse Distribution-Aware Experts:**
In this stage, all $n$ experts are trained together using a combination of individual classification losses and a distribution-aware diversity loss.
- **Individual Expert Classification Loss ($L_{individual}$):**
 - Unlike some multi-expert methods that apply a single classification loss to aggregated logits (which the authors call "collaborative loss" and observe leads to correlated experts), RIDE applies a separate classification loss to the output of *each* individual expert.
 - The loss is defined as $L_{individual}(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} L(\psi_{\theta_i}(f_\theta(x)), y)$, where $L$ can be any classification loss (e.g., Cross-Entropy, LDAM, Focal Loss). This design encourages each expert to perform well independently, promoting diversification.
- **Distribution-Aware Diversity Loss ($L_{D-Diversify}$):**
 - A regularization term is introduced to explicitly encourage complementary decisions among experts. This is achieved by maximizing the KL-divergence between the classification probabilities of different experts for a given instance $x$.
 - $L_{D-Diversify}(x, y; \theta_i) = \frac{-1}{n-1} \sum_{j=1, j \neq i}^{n} D_{KL}(p^{(i)}(x, y) \parallel p^{(j)}(x, y))$.
 - **Class-wise Temperature Scaling:** To make the diversity loss more effective for long-tailed data, the softmax temperature ($T_k$) for each class $k$ is scaled linearly with its normalized class size ($\beta_k$). Specifically, tail classes (smaller $n_k$) receive a lower temperature, making their classification probabilities more sensitive to feature changes. This helps prevent overfitting on tail classes while allowing complexity for head classes. The temperature adaptation is applied after several epochs when features are stabilized.
- **Total Loss:** The overall loss for Stage 1 is a weighted sum of the individual classification losses and the distribution-aware diversity losses: $L_{Total}(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (L_{Classify}(x, y; \theta_i) + \lambda \cdot L_{D-Diversify}(x, y; \theta_i))$. Due to the symmetrical nature of the loss terms, the learned experts are both effective and distinctive.

**C. Stage 2: Routing Diversified Experts:**
This stage aims to reduce the inference-time computational cost by dynamically activating experts only when necessary.
- **Router Training:** A separate router module is trained. Given an instance $x$ and the mean logits from the first $k$ deployed experts, the router makes a binary decision ($y_{on}$) whether to deploy the $(k+1)$-th expert.
- **Router Input:** The router takes the normalized image feature $f_\theta(x)$ and the top-$s$ ranked components of the mean logits from the already active experts.
- **Router Architecture:** It consists of a shared fully connected layer to reduce feature dimensions, followed by ReLU and flattening, concatenated with the mean logits, and then an individual fully connected layer ($W_2^{(k)}$) for each router (for $k=1 \dots n-1$). A Sigmoid function converts the output to an activation value in $$.
- **Routing Loss ($L_{Routing}$):** The router is optimized using a weighted binary cross-entropy loss: $L_{Routing}(r(x), y_{on}) = -\omega_{on} y_{on} \log(r(x)) - (1 - y_{on}) \log(1 - r(x))$, where $\omega_{on}$ (set to 100) controls the preference for switching on an expert.
- **Inference:** During inference, experts are deployed sequentially. If the router's activation for the $k$-th expert is below a threshold (0.5), the decision is finalized using the logits of the $k$ active experts. Otherwise, the $(k+1)$-th expert is engaged. The final prediction combines the logits of all active experts via averaging before applying softmax.

**D. Optional Self-Distillation:**
For further performance enhancement, knowledge distillation (KD) can be applied. A more powerful model (e.g., RIDE with 6 experts) can serve as a teacher to transfer knowledge to a student model with fewer experts, slightly boosting its accuracy.

**E. Experimental Setup:**
- **Datasets:** Experiments are conducted on CIFAR100-LT (imbalance factor 100), ImageNet-LT (naturally imbalanced subset of ImageNet-2012), and iNaturalist 2018 (naturally imbalanced with 8,142 categories).
- **Backbones:** ResNet-32 (CIFAR100-LT), ResNet-10, ResNet-50, ResNeXt-50 (ImageNet-LT, iNaturalist), and Swin-Transformer Tiny/Small (ImageNet-LT).
- **Evaluation:** Performance is measured by top-1 accuracy (overall, many-shot, medium-shot, few-shot) and computational cost (MFlops/GFlops).
- **Baselines:** A range of state-of-the-art long-tailed recognition methods are used for comparison, including CE, Focal Loss, OLTR, LDAM+DRW, BBN, $\tau$-norm, cRT, LWS, M2m, and LFME.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several findings, which include empirical analyses of existing methods, performance of the proposed RIDE model, and ablation studies.

**A. Bias and Variance Analysis of Existing Methods:**
- The authors' dynamic bias-variance analysis on CIFAR100-LT indicates that a standard cross-entropy (CE) classifier exhibits a significant head-tail bias gap (0.3 for head vs. 0.9 for tail).
- Existing state-of-the-art long-tail methods (e.g., $\tau$-norm, cRT, LDAM) reduce the overall bias primarily by lowering tail bias, but the head-tail bias gap remains substantial (e.g., 0.3 vs. 0.8).
- Crucially, these existing long-tail methods are observed to *increase* the model variance across all class splits compared to the CE classifier. This suggests a trade-off where bias reduction in the tail comes at the cost of reduced stability across the model.
- Further analysis shows that tail classes are more prone to confusion with hard negative classes (highest softmax score among incorrect classes), contributing to their higher bias.

**B. Performance of RIDE on Benchmarks:**
RIDE consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods across multiple benchmarks and backbone networks.
- **CIFAR100-LT (Table 1):**
 - RIDE with 4 experts achieves an overall accuracy of 49.1%, surpassing the SOTA LFME by 5.3%.
 - It also demonstrates improved accuracy across many-shot (69.3%), medium-shot (49.3%), and few-shot (26.0%) categories.
 - RIDE with 2 experts even achieves a slightly lower computational cost (0.9x MFlops) than the baseline while still providing performance gains.
- **ImageNet-LT (Table 2):**
 - With ResNet-50, RIDE (4 experts) achieves 55.4% overall accuracy, outperforming SOTA LWS by 7.7%.
 - Similar gains are observed with ResNeXt-50, where RIDE (4 experts) reaches 56.8% overall accuracy, a 6.9% improvement over SOTA LWS.
 - RIDE (2 experts) also shows a reduced computational cost (0.9x GFlops) while offering substantial accuracy improvements (6.7% for ResNet-50).
- **iNaturalist 2018 (Table 3):**
 - On this highly challenging dataset with 8,142 classes, RIDE (4 experts) achieves 72.6% overall accuracy, surpassing SOTA BBN by 6.3%.
 - RIDE exhibits a more balanced performance across class splits: 70.9% for many-shot, 72.4% for medium-shot, and 73.1% for few-shot. This contrasts with BBN, which significantly decreases many-shot performance (49.4%).
- **Swin-Transformer on ImageNet-LT (Table 5):** RIDE extends its effectiveness to Vision Transformers. RIDE (2 experts) with Swin-S achieves 56.3% overall accuracy, an improvement of 6.8% over Swin-S + LDAM-DRW. This demonstrates its universality beyond CNN architectures.

**C. RIDE as a Universal Framework (Figure 3):**
RIDE consistently enhances performance when integrated with various long-tail recognition algorithms and training processes. For example, RIDE applied to a simple cross-entropy loss without any re-balancing strategies can outperform previous SOTA methods on CIFAR100-LT. It delivers accuracy gains when combined with end-to-end methods (Focal Loss, CE, LDAM) and two-stage methods (cRT, $\tau$-norm, Cosine classifier).

**D. Class-wise Performance (Figure 4):**
RIDE provides improvements across all categories—many-shot, medium-shot, and few-shot classes—without sacrificing performance on head classes. This is a distinction from SOTA methods like cRT and BBN, which often reduce many-shot accuracy to gain in tail classes.

**E. Ablation Studies (Table 4):**
- **Architectural Change:** The multi-expert architecture with shared backbone and channel-reduced later layers contributes 2.7% to 4.3% of the overall accuracy gain.
- **Individual Classification Loss:** Applying classification loss to each expert individually (L_Individual) instead of a collaborative loss on aggregated logits yields a 1.5% accuracy increase.
- **Distribution-Aware Diversity Loss:** The $L_{D-Diversify}$ contributes an additional 0.9% to accuracy.
- **Dynamic Expert Router:** The router module significantly reduces computational cost (e.g., from 1.8x to 1.3x GFlops for 4 experts) with negligible accuracy degradation, by deploying experts on an as-needed basis.
- **Knowledge Distillation:** An optional self-distillation step from a larger RIDE model provides a further 0.6% gain.

**F. Impact of Number of Experts (Figure 5 & 6):**
- Increasing the number of experts benefits few-shot classes more significantly than many-shot classes. For example, few-shot accuracy shows a 16.1% relative improvement, compared to 3.8% for many-shot classes.
- The dynamic routing mechanism confirms that instances from few-shot classes typically require more experts, while a majority of many-shot instances are resolved by the first expert.

**G. Embedding Space Visualization (Figure 8):**
t-SNE visualization shows that RIDE produces more compact and better-separated feature embeddings for both head and tail classes compared to baseline LDAM, which simplifies classification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for long-tailed recognition, RIDE, that addresses several identified limitations of existing methods, leading to notable improvements.

**A. Principled Foundation:** The work is significant for its approach of analyzing long-tailed recognition through a model bias and variance decomposition. This analysis identified that existing methods often increase model variance and leave a substantial head-tail bias gap. By directly addressing these findings, RIDE offers a more principled approach to the problem, moving beyond heuristic solutions.

**B. State-of-the-Art Performance:** RIDE consistently surpasses prior state-of-the-art methods by margins of 5% to 7% on challenging benchmarks such as CIFAR100-LT, ImageNet-LT, and iNaturalist 2018. This level of performance improvement indicates a substantial advancement in the field of long-tailed recognition.

**C. Balanced Performance across Class Splits:** A key impact of RIDE is its ability to improve accuracy across all class splits—many-shot, medium-shot, and few-shot—simultaneously. This overcomes the common head-tail performance trade-off observed in previous methods, which often boosted tail accuracy at the expense of head accuracy. This balanced improvement is crucial for real-world applications where consistent performance across all categories is desired.

**D. Computational Efficiency:** The dynamic expert routing module allows RIDE to achieve its performance gains with computational costs that are comparable to, and in some configurations even lower than, single-expert baseline models. This efficiency makes the multi-expert approach more practical for deployment in scenarios with computational constraints.

**E. Universality and Adaptability:** RIDE is demonstrated as a universal framework, showing consistent performance gains when applied with various backbone networks (including ResNet, ResNeXt, and Swin Transformer) and integrated with different long-tail algorithms and training mechanisms (e.g., Focal Loss, LDAM, cRT). This versatility broadens its applicability and suggests it can serve as a foundational component for future long-tailed recognition systems.

**F. Potential for Real-World Applications:** The ability of RIDE to handle class imbalance effectively, provide stable predictions, and perform well across rare and common classes makes it highly relevant for diverse real-world applications. These include, but are not limited to, fine-grained species classification in biology (e.g., iNaturalist), medical image analysis (where rare disease instances are critical), e-commerce product categorization, and autonomous driving object detection, where robust recognition of infrequent objects is essential for safety and functionality.
