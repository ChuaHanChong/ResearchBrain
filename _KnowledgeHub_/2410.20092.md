# OGBench: Benchmarking Offline Goal-Conditioned RL

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive benchmark called OGBench, developed by researchers at UC Berkeley and Princeton, is presented for offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, encompassing 8 environments and 85 diverse datasets. The platform facilitates systematic comparison of GCRL algorithms, finding that no single method is universally superior and highlighting the strong performance of hierarchical strategies like HIQL.

## Problem

- The absence of a standardized and comprehensive benchmark for Offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) hindered systematic algorithm comparison.
- Prior evaluation relied on D4RL (not designed for multi-goal), simple online GCRL tasks, or custom, non-comparable tasks.
- A lack of systematic assessment for critical GCRL capabilities such as goal stitching, long-horizon reasoning, and learning from suboptimal, unstructured data.

## Method

- Introduction of **OGBench**, a novel benchmark comprising 8 distinct environments (locomotion, manipulation, drawing) and 85 diverse datasets for ==offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning== research.
- Environments are designed to challenge algorithms with ==goal stitching==, ==long-horizon reasoning==, stochasticity, and high-dimensional (pixel-based) inputs.
- Provides high-quality, ==JAX-based reference implementations== for 6 representative offline GCRL algorithms (e.g., ==HIQL==, ==CRL==, ==GCIQL==) for standardized comparison.

## Results

- OGBench empirically validates that multi-goal evaluation offers clearer, more pronounced signals for algorithmic strengths and weaknesses compared to single-goal evaluation.
- Algorithms not relying on separate Q-functions can be optimistically biased in stochastic environments, while Monte Carlo value methods (like CRL) exhibit greater robustness to stochasticity.
- Ablation studies show that appropriate data coverage and diversity, even with some noise, are more critical for good performance in manipulation tasks than strict data optimality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - No single algorithm consistently outperforms all others across OGBench's diverse tasks, indicating the varied challenges and the need for further algorithmic development.
> - Hierarchical methods, specifically HIQL, demonstrate strong performance, particularly in complex locomotion and visual manipulation, suggesting the importance of hierarchical control for long-horizon problems.
> - The ability to generalize to novel goals through 'goal stitching' is enhanced by full RL-based methods that learn comprehensive value functions, rather than just one-step behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical need within the rapidly expanding field of **Offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL)**. Offline GCRL is a paradigm that aims to learn a general-purpose policy capable of reaching any desired state from any other state, solely from a pre-recorded, static dataset of interactions, and without requiring explicit reward functions or online environmental interaction. The authors highlight this setting as a crucial analogy to data-driven unsupervised learning successes observed in computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP), which leverage vast amounts of unlabeled data to learn powerful, general-purpose representations and models.

The broader reinforcement learning landscape is currently grappling with challenges related to data efficiency, scalability, and the cumbersome nature of reward engineering. Offline RL, by decoupling data collection from policy learning, offers a pathway to address these by enabling the use of large, diverse, pre-collected datasets. GCRL further extends this by aiming for *general-purpose* behavior acquisition, where a single policy can tackle a multitude of tasks specified by different goals, rather than just one fixed task. This positions offline GCRL as a promising avenue for developing "foundation models" for generalist agents.

However, the paper argues that despite the increasing interest and importance of offline GCRL, the research community has **lacked a standardized, comprehensive benchmark** to systematically evaluate and compare algorithms. Prior works typically relied on:
- **Existing offline RL benchmarks (e.g., D4RL):** These datasets were not specifically designed for multi-goal evaluation, leading to limited assessment of GCRL policies often on only a single, fixed goal.
- **Simple online GCRL tasks (e.g., Fetch):** These tasks often involve atomic behaviors and do not adequately capture the complexities of long-horizon reasoning or goal stitching required in more challenging scenarios.
- **Bespoke tasks:** Researchers often created custom tasks tailored to highlight specific features of their proposed methods, making fair cross-algorithm comparison difficult and potentially obscuring other weaknesses.

OGBench directly fills this void by introducing a unified, diverse, and challenging benchmark that systematically probes various capabilities of offline GCRL algorithms. By providing a common ground for evaluation, OGBench aims to accelerate progress in identifying robust algorithms, understanding their strengths and weaknesses, and ultimately advancing the development of truly general-purpose agents from data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce **OGBench**, a novel and high-quality benchmark specifically designed for algorithms research in offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning. This central goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing the Benchmarking Gap:** The most prominent motivation is the current absence of a standardized and comprehensive benchmark for offline GCRL. The existing fragmented evaluation practices hinder systematic comparison and progress in the field. OGBench aims to rectify this by offering a unified platform.
2. **Systematic Evaluation:** The authors want to move beyond superficial evaluations to systematically assess diverse capabilities crucial for robust GCRL, such as:
 - **Goal Stitching:** The ability to combine segments of different trajectories from the dataset to achieve novel, longer-horizon goals.
 - **Long-Horizon Reasoning:** Navigating complex environments or performing multi-step tasks that require many sequential actions.
 - **Handling Stochasticity:** Robustly operating in environments with unpredictable dynamics, a common characteristic of real-world scenarios.
 - **Learning from Suboptimal, Unstructured Data:** Developing policies from diverse datasets that may not consist of expert trajectories, mimicking real-world data collection challenges.
 - **High-Dimensional Inputs:** Effectively learning from pixel-based observations rather than just low-dimensional state information.
3. **Facilitating Algorithmic Research:** By providing a clear, reproducible, and challenging set of tasks, OGBench intends to enable researchers to easily test, iterate on, and compare new algorithmic ideas. The benchmark is designed to yield strong signals for algorithmic strengths and weaknesses.
4. **Unlocking GCRL's Potential:** The authors believe that offline GCRL holds significant promise as a paradigm for unsupervised, general-purpose RL pre-training. By providing challenging tasks that current methods struggle with, OGBench aims to push the boundaries of what GCRL algorithms can achieve, ultimately realizing this potential.
5. **Promoting Scientific Understanding:** Through controllable dataset generation and rigorous evaluation, OGBench aims to foster a deeper scientific understanding of the challenges inherent in offline GCRL and the design principles for effective algorithms.

The overarching motivation is to establish a robust foundation for the development of scalable and performant offline GCRL algorithms, which can lead to the creation of generalist agents capable of learning complex behaviors from raw, unlabeled data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OGBench is meticulously designed with several core principles and components:

#### 3.1. Benchmark Components

- **Environments:** 8 distinct types, spanning locomotion, manipulation, and drawing tasks.
- **Datasets:** 85 diverse datasets, offering various levels of difficulty, types of data collection (e.g., `navigate`, `stitch`, `explore`, `play`, `noisy`), and observation modalities.
- **Algorithms:** Reference implementations of 6 representative offline GCRL algorithms (GCBC, GCIVL, GCIQL, QRL, CRL, HIQL) for standardized comparison.

#### 3.2. Task Design Principles

The tasks are crafted to embody the major challenges in offline GCRL:
- **Realistic and Exciting Tasks:** Environments like robot arm solving puzzles or humanoid robots navigating labyrinths are designed to be engaging and demonstrate complex emergent behaviors.
- **Appropriate Difficulty:** Tasks are calibrated to be challenging but solvable by current methods (aiming for 20-30% success for state-of-the-art), ensuring meaningful signals for research. Multiple difficulty variants are provided.
- **Controllable Datasets:** Datasets are synthetically generated with full control, allowing for ablation studies and scientific investigation into data properties (e.g., effects of noise, coverage).
- **Minimal Compute Requirements:** Prioritizes algorithmic challenges over computational overhead. State-based observations are default, and pixel-based observations (64x64x3 RGB) are optimized for low resolution without sacrificing essential information (e.g., transparent robot arm, colored floors).
- **High Code Quality:** JAX-based, minimal dependencies (MuJoCo), clean, well-tuned reference implementations to facilitate ease of use and extension by researchers.

#### 3.3. Environment Categories

- **Locomotion Tasks:**
 - **PointMaze, AntMaze, HumanoidMaze:** Maze navigation tasks with varying degrees of freedom (2D point mass, 8-DoF Ant, 21-DoF Humanoid). Variants include `medium`, `large`, `giant` (increasing path length) and `teleport` (stochastic teleportation).
 - **AntSoccer:** Control an Ant agent to dribble a soccer ball, introducing object manipulation challenges.
 - **Observation:** Both state-based and pixel-based (visual) observations are supported, with visual tasks using third-person camera views.
- **Manipulation Tasks:**
 - **Cube:** Pick-and-place and stacking tasks with 1-4 cubes, challenging sequential and logical reasoning.
 - **Scene:** Multi-object manipulation (drawer, window, buttons), designed for long-horizon sequential reasoning.
 - **Puzzle:** "Lights Out" puzzle with a robot arm, testing combinatorial generalization over enormous state spaces.
 - **Observation:** Both state-based and pixel-based (visual) observations are supported, with careful rendering for full observability (e.g., transparent arm).
- **Drawing Tasks:**
 - **Powderworld:** Drawing on a grid using different "powder" brushes with unique physical properties. Challenges high intrinsic dimensionality, stochastic dynamics, and generalization of physics. Discrete action space.

#### 3.4. Dataset Types

- `navigate`: Standard goal-reaching.
- `stitch`: Short trajectories, explicitly requiring "stitching" of behaviors.
- `explore`: Random, high-coverage, low-quality exploratory data.
- `play`: Non-Markovian expert policies with temporally correlated noise (for manipulation tasks).
- `noisy`: Markovian expert policies with uncorrelated Gaussian noise (for manipulation tasks).

#### 3.5. Evaluation Protocol

- Each task includes five pre-defined state-goal pairs for evaluation.
- Performance metric: Average binary success rate across these five goals.
- Multiple rollouts (50 per goal pair) are performed with slight randomization to ensure robustness.
- Task-specific success criteria (e.g., distance threshold for locomotion, object configuration for manipulation).
- Training: 1M gradient steps (500K for pixel-based tasks). Evaluation metric is averaged over the last three evaluation epochs.

#### 3.6. Benchmarked Algorithms

- **GCBC (Goal-Conditioned Behavioral Cloning):** Simplest baseline, directly cloning actions to reach future states.
- **GCIVL (Goal-Conditioned Implicit Value Learning):** A value-only variant of IQL using expectile regression.
- **GCIQL (Goal-Conditioned Implicit Q-Learning):** Direct goal-conditioned extension of IQL, learning Q and V functions.
- **QRL (Quasimetric RL):** Learns an asymmetric distance function (quasimetric) between states.
- **CRL (Contrastive RL):** Learns a one-step Monte Carlo value function using contrastive learning.
- **HIQL (Hierarchical Implicit Q-Learning):** Extracts a two-level hierarchical policy from a single GCIVL value function.

Hyperparameters are extensively tuned for fair comparison, with specific tuning for policy extraction methods (AWR, DDPG+BC) per dataset category.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The benchmarking results across OGBench's diverse tasks yield several crucial insights into the capabilities and limitations of current offline GCRL algorithms:

- **No Universal Dominance:** A key finding is that no single algorithm consistently outperforms all others across all categories of tasks. This starkly highlights the diverse challenges presented by OGBench and indicates that the "ideal" offline GCRL algorithm is yet to be discovered.
- **Strength of Hierarchical Methods (HIQL):** HIQL, which leverages hierarchical policy extraction, generally exhibits strong performance, particularly in complex locomotion and visual manipulation tasks. This suggests that hierarchical control might be a crucial component for tackling long-horizon and multi-stage problems effectively.
- **Goal Stitching Capabilities:** The comparison between `navigate` and `stitch` datasets reveals that full RL-based methods like HIQL, which aim to learn an optimal value function, are generally better at goal stitching than one-step RL methods like CRL (which learns a behavioral value function). This indicates that the ability to synthesize novel behaviors from fragmented data is enhanced by more comprehensive value learning.
- **Robustness to Stochasticity:** Algorithms that do not rely on a separate Q-function, such as value-only HIQL and QRL, can be optimistically biased in stochastic environments (e.g., `teleport` mazes), leading to poorer performance. Conversely, CRL, which employs a Monte Carlo value function, demonstrates greater robustness to environmental stochasticity.
- **Handling Pixel-based Observations:** While state and pixel observations convey similar information, some methods struggle more with the added representational learning burden of images. CRL shows notable robustness across input modalities, likely due to its core representation learning objective. HIQL also performs well in visual manipulation, but struggles in visual drawing tasks, suggesting potential difficulties in learning low-dimensional subgoal representations from states with extremely high intrinsic dimensionality.
- **High-Quality Reference Implementations:** The authors rigorously tuned their reference implementations, demonstrating that they often achieve *better* performance on established D4RL tasks than figures previously reported in original papers. This ensures a strong, reliable baseline for future research.
- **Critique of Single-Goal Evaluation:** OGBench empirically validates the inadequacy of single-goal evaluation (a common practice with D4RL tasks). It shows that multi-goal evaluation can significantly alter algorithm rankings and provide clearer, more pronounced signals for research, reinforcing OGBench's design choice.
- **Data Collection Insights:** An ablation study on action noise in manipulation datasets (e.g., `cube-single-noisy`) reveals a critical insight: an appropriate amount of data noise (i.e., state coverage) is paramount for good performance, even more so than strict data optimality. This suggests that prioritizing diverse coverage over pristine expert trajectories might be beneficial when collecting real-world data for offline GCRL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

OGBench represents a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning, poised to have a substantial impact in several ways:

1. **Standardizing Offline GCRL Evaluation:** By providing a comprehensive, high-quality, and standardized benchmark, OGBench fills a critical void. It enables researchers to perform fair, systematic comparisons of algorithms, fostering more rigorous scientific progress and clearer communication of results within the community.
2. **Accelerating Algorithmic Innovation:** The diverse and challenging tasks expose current limitations and highlight specific areas where existing algorithms struggle (e.g., very long horizons, complex combinatorial generalization, handling extreme stochasticity). This provides clear targets for future algorithmic development, driving the creation of more robust, scalable, and generalizable offline GCRL methods.
3. **Guiding Algorithmic Design:** The detailed performance analysis across various task categories offers valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different GCRL paradigms. Researchers can use this information to inform the design of new algorithms, potentially combining the best aspects of different approaches or developing novel techniques for specific challenges. For instance, the strong performance of HIQL suggests the importance of hierarchical approaches for complex tasks, while CRL's robustness to stochasticity points to effective value estimation strategies.
4. **Promoting Reproducibility and Accessibility:** The commitment to open-sourcing high-quality, well-tuned JAX-based code with minimal dependencies significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers. This not only promotes reproducibility of results but also encourages wider participation and contributions to the field.
5. **Advancing General-Purpose RL:** The core philosophy of offline GCRL aligns with the vision of developing generalist "foundation models" for AI agents. OGBench provides a concrete platform to test this hypothesis, demonstrating that GCRL can indeed learn rich, diverse behaviors from unlabeled data, and pushing towards policies that can truly achieve complex outcomes that go beyond simply imitating observed data. It sets the stage for exploring how GCRL can serve as a powerful pre-training objective for fine-tuning agents to solve a wide array of downstream tasks.
6. **Fostering Deeper Scientific Understanding:** The inclusion of controllable datasets and opportunities for ablation studies encourages a more scientific approach to understanding the underlying mechanisms of offline GCRL. The initial findings on the importance of data coverage, for example, offer practical guidance for real-world data collection and spark new research questions about dataset properties.
7. **Defining Future Research Directions:** The paper concludes with a clear articulation of open research questions and "unsolved tasks." This explicit roadmap for future work directly invites the research community to engage with OGBench, contributing to its longevity and impact as a central tool for advancing offline GCRL.

In summary, OGBench is not merely a collection of tasks; it is a thoughtfully constructed scientific instrument designed to catalyze progress in offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, pushing the field closer to developing truly general and intelligent autonomous agents.
