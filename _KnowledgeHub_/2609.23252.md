# Robot World Models Are Not Invariant to How the Actions Are Written

> [!summary] Summary
> Action-conditioned latent world models are shown to be non-invariant to action parameterization: a JEPA-latent predictor trained on absolute joint targets collapses when handed the same trajectory written as state-relative deltas, retrieval degrading 2.6-13.4x and goal-conditioned action selection falling from 53% to 15%. An admissibility test certifies the re-parameterization as information-preserving, and orbit-averaged training with a disagreement penalty restores accuracy and worst-case agreement.

## Problem

- Invariance work on visual models audits only pixel-side nuisances such as crops, jitter and camera pose, leaving the action channel the robot is commanded through unaudited.
- Action-conditioned dynamics are validated only by action-shuffle ablations, which destroy information, generate no orbit, and cannot test stability under a command-preserving re-presentation.
- Covariate-shift results confound a different input distribution with a less informative one, so degradation cannot be judged a defect to repair rather than correct behaviour.

## Method

- An ==admissibility test== gates on ==predictive parity==, with a theorem pricing its failure as the parity gap, and reports ==mutual reconstructibility== as a certificate, both evaluated on the joint state-action input.
- ==Orbit averaging== placed on the objective rather than the outputs: one predictor fits the target under both encodings, plus a weighted pairwise ==disagreement penalty== across presentations.
- A proposition rules out output averaging for direction-valued prediction by construction, the normalized orbit mean scoring below every member; a two-part ==MDL threshold== prices when invariance pays.

## Results

- Cross-encoding retrieval collapse is **9.9x** on ALOHA cabinet, **13.4x** on ALOHA coffee and **2.6x** on PushT, whose cross-encoding cosine is **0.067** with worst case **-0.377**.
- Pooled two-encoding training takes held-out rank **61.5** to **5.98** and action selection **15.4%** to **40.1%**; the penalty adds no average gain but lifts worst-case agreement **0.779** to **0.995**.
- Orbit-mean distillation reaches rank **11.55** against **5.25** for objective averaging; collapse grows **2.5x** to **9.9x** from n=150 to 1200 while cross-encoding cosine stays flat near **0.37**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A representation that never encodes the distinction is invariant for free; gaps exist only where both conventions are natural and neither is canonical.
> - Where the average goes depends on the output space: the Jensen safety of averaging probabilities does not transfer to unit-vector predictions scored by cosine.
> - Scale does not heal this defect: more data sharpens only the trained encoding, making a constant disagreement progressively more costly instead of shrinking it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot controller can express the same commanded movement in two ways: as an absolute joint target, or as an offset from the current joint state. That choice is often made for practical interface reasons. A learned world model nevertheless sees the numbers, and may mistake the notation for part of the command. The same action, written two ways, can make the model retrieve two different future scenes. In the authors’ frame examples, the absolute-encoding prediction retrieves the shown future while the relative-encoding prediction does not. This is a model’s predicted future, not evidence that a robot physically moved differently.

Each row is one test instance. The columns show the observation at t, the true future at t + Δ, and the futures retrieved under absolute and relative encoding. Green outlines mark correct retrievals and orange outlines mark incorrect ones. The examples come from absolute-correct/relative-wrong cases whose wrong retrieval is from a different episode. Of 384 test cases, 125 are absolute-correct and relative-wrong, and 95 of those are from a different episode; two are shown.

### 2. Same command, different forecast

Here, a world model predicts a future visual representation from the current representation and an action window. The experiments use a frozen I-JEPA encoder and a learned latent predictor. The actions are absolute joint targets or deltas relative to current state. The predictor is trained on one notation and evaluated on the other, and held-out episodes are separate from training.

Retrieval rank is the position of the true future latent among 384 held-out latents under cosine similarity; lower is better. On ALOHA mobile cabinet, a predictor trained on absolute actions has mean retrieval rank 6.19 on that encoding. When given the equivalent relative encoding, its rank is 61.5, a 9.9× collapse. Across the three datasets (two 14-dimensional ALOHA tasks and 2-dimensional PushT), the reported collapse ranges from 2.6× to 13.4×. On PushT, the mean cosine agreement between predictions under the two notations is 0.067 and the worst-case cosine is −0.377. A negative cosine means the two predicted directions oppose one another.

Action selection serves as an offline proxy for control. It chooses the best-scoring of 32 candidate action windows. On cabinet, top-1 selection is correct 53.1% of the time on the training encoding and 15.4% on the equivalent encoding; chance is 0.031.

These failures appear even though the encodings are mutually reconstructible with the joint state and action input at R² = 0.996, with a task-R² gap of 0.003. Action-only reconstruction gives R² = 0.757. Testing only the action channel would therefore wrongly reject the valid re-parameterization.

### 3. Certify the symmetry, then train on both

The paper does not propose making models invariant to every transformation. Instead, it says to establish that a proposed transformation preserves predictive information before enforcing invariance. Its test found three of its four candidate axes inadmissible: photometric jitter, camera choice, and lossy action-window summaries.

The repair is to train on both certified action encodings, averaging the task loss across them. An optional penalty can be added when their predictions disagree. For cabinet, two-encoding training without the penalty improves held-out retrieval rank from 61.5 to 5.98. It also improves held-out action selection from 15.4% to 40.1%.

The penalty does not establish a further average-accuracy gain. At λ = 16, held-out rank is 5.19 and action selection is 41.1%, and the canonical action-selection score is lower than pooled training’s 44.3%. What improves decisively is the tail: worst-case cross-encoding cosine rises from 0.779 with pooled training to 0.995 with the penalty.

The paper also tested an alternative that fits an orbit-mean teacher prediction. This student reaches held-out rank 15.1 and worst-case cosine 0.804. Output averaging has a non-worse-average-log-loss guarantee for probability predictions under log loss. That guarantee does not extend to direction-valued predictions scored by cosine.

Averaging alone does not fix every case. On PushT, pooled training alone leaves worst-case rollout agreement negative. The rollout feeds the model’s predicted latent back in while supplying the true action window and state at each hop, so it is not closed-loop robot control. At the final PushT hop, pooled training has mean/worst agreement 0.677/−0.348, while λ = 16 has 0.872/0.139.

### 4. What the result establishes

In these tests, a change of notation that preserves the command, given the joint state, can collapse a predictor’s retrieval and action selection. Training on both certified encodings restores average performance on cabinet, and the disagreement penalty mainly improves worst-case agreement. The recommended sequence is to establish that the alternate notation preserves the command, train the predictor to handle both, and test whether worst-case agreement matters for the intended use.

These are three-seed, episode-disjoint, offline experiments, not real-robot control trials. The predictor is a small MLP on frozen latents, and the authors note that three seeds are a thin basis for estimating effect sizes.
