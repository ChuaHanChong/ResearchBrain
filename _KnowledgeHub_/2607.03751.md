# Look Before You Leap: Distilling Tree Search into Action Evaluation for Frozen VLA Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The SVA (Search, Value, and Act) framework improves the performance of frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by distilling Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) into a lightweight Q-value model for action evaluation. This approach achieved significant success rate gains across embodied reasoning and robot manipulation benchmarks, outperforming baselines while preserving VLA generalist capabilities and demonstrating more cost-effective test-time scaling than increasing model size.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, despite large-scale pretraining, exhibit fragility and limited generalization to tasks slightly outside their training distribution.
- Existing post-training methods (supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning) for VLAs involve updating multi-billion-parameter backbones, leading to high computational costs and often sacrificing generalist capabilities for specialist performance.
- VLAs typically produce locally plausible actions without explicit long-term consequence awareness, lacking a mechanism to reliably distinguish successful actions from suboptimal ones.

## Method

- The **SVA** framework enhances frozen VLA policies by equipping them with ==consequence-aware action evaluation==, decoupling action proposal from outcome assessment.
- ==Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)== is employed in a simulated environment to explore the VLA's policy distribution, generate diverse trajectories, and assign empirical returns for action quality over long horizons.
- Knowledge from MCTS is distilled into a lightweight ==Q-value model== (ˆQ_ϕ), trained with ==LoRA adapters== and MLP value heads, to predict the long-term consequences of candidate actions without requiring simulator access at deployment.
- At test time, the trained ˆQ_ϕ model guides action selection by evaluating N VLA-generated candidates and choosing the one that maximizes an ==uncertainty-regularized Q-value==, thereby implementing an efficient '==best-of-N==' strategy.

## Results

- A diagnostic study showed VLA success rates increased substantially from **33%** at pass@1 to **92%** at pass@32, demonstrating latent competence within the policy's output.
- SVA consistently improved success rates across five VLA backbones on EmbodiedBench, achieving average gains of **+15.4 points** on EB-Habitat and **+13.2 points** on EB-Navigation.
- On robot manipulation benchmarks, SVA boosted average success rates from **38.5%** to **50.7%** on SimplerEnv (with π0) and from **36.0%** to **43.5%** on RoboTwin 2.0 (with π0.5), outperforming baseline and RoboMonkey methods.
- A Qwen3.5-9B model with SVA (N=4 or N=8) achieved a **7-point** higher performance with **27%** lower inference latency compared to a single-shot Qwen3.5-27B model, highlighting its cost-effectiveness in test-time scaling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA failures often stem from an "evaluation bottleneck," where high-quality actions are present in the model's output distribution, but the VLA lacks the mechanism to reliably identify and select them.
> - Distilling consequence-aware signals from simulation-based Monte-Carlo Tree Search into a lightweight Q-value model can effectively enhance VLA performance at deployment without the computational burden of online search.
> - Scaling test-time evaluation with a smaller VLA model and an evaluation framework can be more computationally efficient and cost-effective than simply scaling up the base VLA model size.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an emerging paradigm for developing generalist robotic agents by leveraging large-scale pretraining on diverse vision-language and robotic datasets. Models such as OpenVLA, π0.5, and GR00T have demonstrated capabilities in embodied domains. However, existing VLA models generally exhibit fragility, often failing on tasks that deviate even modestly from their training distributions, a limitation more pronounced than in their language model (LLM) or vision-language model (VLM) counterparts.

The prevailing approach to address VLA fragility is post-training, typically involving supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on curated demonstrations or reinforcement learning (RL) using environmental rewards. While these methods improve task-specific performance, they often require updating multi-billion-parameter VLA backbones, incurring substantial computational expense. Furthermore, fine-tuning can lead to specialist policies that narrow the generalist capabilities acquired during the initial pretraining phase, a trade-off that is particularly challenging given the already limited generalization of VLAs.

This paper positions its work as an alternative strategy that moves away from direct policy parameter modification. Instead, it aligns with a complementary line of research focused on action evaluation or verification, a concept that has proven effective in LLM reasoning through outcome- and process-reward models combined with search. Prior efforts in robotics include grounding LLM plans with affordance functions (SayCan), learning value functions via offline RL (V-GPS), training action preference models (RoboMonkey), or using value models to guide tree search at deployment (V-VLAPS). Other approaches involve training verifiers using synthetic data (VeGAS), contrastive learning (CoVer-VLA), or world model-based evaluations.

This research contributes to the action evaluation paradigm by proposing a method that distills simulation-based Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) into a lightweight Q-value model. Unlike methods relying on offline RL or synthetic data, this approach leverages MCTS rollouts to obtain consequence-aware signals and long-horizon estimates without the computational cost and accumulated error of world-model rollouts. The work aims to strengthen VLA performance while preserving the generalist capabilities of the frozen backbone, addressing the tension between task performance and generalization preservation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance and generalization of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models without modifying their pre-trained backbones. This objective is motivated by several identified challenges and observations:

1. **VLA Fragility and Generalization Limits:** Despite large-scale pretraining, VLA models frequently exhibit fragility and struggle to generalize to tasks only slightly outside their training distribution. This limitation hinders their deployment as robust generalist robotic agents.

2. **Drawbacks of Post-Training:** Current remedies, such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL), involve updating the VLA backbone parameters. This process is computationally expensive for multi-billion-parameter models and often results in specialist policies that sacrifice the broad generalist capabilities gained during pretraining. The authors seek a method that avoids these drawbacks.

3. **Identification of an "Evaluation Bottleneck":** The core motivation stems from the hypothesis that VLA failures are not solely due to an inability to *generate* competent actions, but also from an absence of effective *action evaluation*. VLAs are typically trained for imitation, producing locally plausible actions without explicit signals about their long-term consequences (e.g., leading to success, collision, or an unrecoverable state). They lack the capacity to foresee which alternative action might yield a better outcome.

4. **Empirical Evidence from Diagnostic Study:** To support this hypothesis, the authors conducted a diagnostic pass@k study. This study revealed that when a frozen VLA is allowed multiple independent attempts, its success rate increases substantially (e.g., from 33% at pass@1 to 92% at pass@32). This finding indicates that high-quality, task-completing actions often reside within the VLA's output distribution; the model simply lacks the mechanism to reliably distinguish these successful actions from suboptimal or harmful ones. This reframes the VLA failure mode as a deficiency in action evaluation rather than solely in action generation.

5. **Desire for Smarter Scaling Strategies:** The pass@k study also showed diminishing marginal returns for simply increasing the number of independent samples, suggesting the need for more efficient computational allocation strategies than naive independent sampling.

Based on these motivations, the key objective is to propose a framework that equips frozen VLA policies with "long-term consequence awareness." This framework should decouple action proposal from consequence evaluation, allowing for policy improvement while preserving the generalization capacity of the VLA backbone and offering a mechanism for cost-effective test-time scaling.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, named SVA (Search, Value, and Act), is a three-stage framework designed to improve the performance of frozen VLA policies by equipping them with the ability to evaluate candidate actions based on predicted long-term outcomes. The approach treats the pre-trained VLA as a fixed action proposal distribution and redirects selection towards higher-return regions via a learned Q-function.

#### 3.1. Search: Mining Evaluation Signals via MCTS

The first stage, Search, focuses on exploring the VLA's policy distribution to discover informative trajectories and assign credit. Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is employed in a simulated environment for this purpose. MCTS operates through an iterative process involving four steps:

- **Selection:** Starting from the root node (current state), actions are selected using the PUCT (Predictor Upper Confidence bound for Trees) rule. This rule balances exploitation (choosing actions with high estimated Q-values) and exploration (selecting actions with high prior probabilities that have not been visited often). The formula `a_t = argmax_a (Q(s_t, a) + c_puct * P(s_t, a) * sqrt(n(s_t)) / (1 + n(s_t, a)))` guides this selection, where `Q` is the action-value, `P` is the prior probability, `n(s_t, a)` is the visit count for action `a` from state `s_t`, `n(s_t)` is the total visit count for state `s_t`, and `c_puct` is an exploration constant.
- **Expansion:** When the traversal reaches a leaf node (a state not fully explored), the frozen VLA policy `π_θ` generates `N` candidate actions. Each candidate is added as a new edge to the tree, leading to a new leaf node corresponding to the successor state. The VLA's output probabilities for these candidates are stored as prior probabilities `P`.
- **Evaluation:** From the newly expanded leaf node, a simulation rollout is performed by executing the VLA policy forward for a fixed number of steps (`D`) or until task termination. The discounted return accumulated during this rollout `G(s_L)` serves as a Monte-Carlo estimate of the long-term consequence of reaching that state.
- **Backup:** The calculated rollout return `G(s_L)` is propagated back along the traversed path from the leaf to the root. For each visited edge `(s, a)`, its visit count `n(s, a)` is incremented, and its action-value `Q(s, a)` is updated as a running mean of all rollout returns that have passed through it. This update process refines the PUCT scores for subsequent MCTS iterations.

After a budget of MCTS iterations, diverse trajectories, including both successful and failed attempts, are collected. These trajectories, annotated with MCTS-backed empirical returns, provide the contrastive signals necessary for learning action quality.

#### 3.2. Value: Learning a Deployable Consequence Evaluator

The second stage, Value, addresses the computational expense of MCTS at deployment by distilling the knowledge acquired during the search phase into a lightweight, fast-to-evaluate Q-value model, denoted as `ˆQ_ϕ`. This model is designed to predict the expected consequence of candidate actions without requiring simulator access.

- **Architecture:** The `ˆQ_ϕ` model is built upon a lightweight VLM backbone (e.g., Qwen3.5-0.8B). It is augmented with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters and an ensemble of small Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) value heads.
- **Input Representation:** For each candidate action, a prompt is constructed including the textual instruction `l`, visual and proprioceptive inputs `s`, recent interaction history, and the candidate action `a`. For discrete, high-level skills (e.g., in EmbodiedBench), actions are serialized into the prompt. For continuous robot control vectors (e.g., in SimplerEnv), actions are first tokenized using a method like FAST and then appended to the prompt. A special `<VALUE>` token is appended to the prompt, and the hidden state at this token after self-attention serves as the state-action representation.
- **Output and Training:** The hidden state at `<VALUE>` is fed into the ensemble of value heads. The ensemble's mean provides the predicted Q-value `μ_ϕ`, while its standard deviation provides an uncertainty estimate `σ_ϕ`. The model is optimized using a Smooth-L1 loss function, which offers stable gradients for large errors and smooth optimization for small errors. Only the LoRA adapters and value heads are fine-tuned, preserving the pre-trained VLM backbone. Target values `Q(s, a; l)` are provided by the MCTS-collected data and normalized.

#### 3.3. Act: Evaluation-Guided Action Selection at Test Time

In the final stage, Act, the trained `ˆQ_ϕ` model guides action selection at deployment without requiring a simulator.

- **Candidate Generation:** At each decision step `t`, the frozen VLA policy `π_θ` generates `N` candidate actions `{a^(1), ..., a^(N)}`. The empirical prior probability `p(a | s_t; l)` for each candidate is estimated by its sampling frequency.
- **Action Selection:** The `ˆQ_ϕ` model scores all `N` candidates. The action `a_t` that maximizes an uncertainty-regularized Q-value is selected:
 `a_t = argmax_i (μ_ϕ(s_t, a^(i); l) - λ_1 * σ_ϕ(s_t, a^(i); l) + λ_2 * log p(a^(i) | s_t; l))`.
 Here, `μ_ϕ` is the Q-ensemble mean, `σ_ϕ` is the ensemble standard deviation (used for uncertainty regularization), and `p` is the empirical prior probability of the action. `λ_1` and `λ_2` are regularization coefficients. The selected action `a_t` is then executed.
- **Inference Latency and Scaling:** The "best-of-N" strategy requires `N` VLA forward passes for candidate generation and `N` Q-model passes for scoring. The Q-model's small size ensures negligible scoring overhead. Modern VLAs that decouple a heavy VLM backbone from a lightweight action expert enable sub-linear scaling of generation latency with `N` (the VLM runs once, the action expert `N` times). This makes test-time scaling a practical mechanism for improving performance by adaptively allocating compute.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of SVA spanned embodied reasoning and robot manipulation benchmarks, demonstrating its effectiveness across various VLA backbones.

#### 4.1. Diagnostic Pass@k Study

The initial diagnostic study, conducted on OpenVLA for SIMPLER and LIBERO, and π0.5 for ROBOTWIN, revealed:
- **Latent Competence:** The average success rate increased significantly from 33% at pass@1 to 92% at pass@32, indicating that successful behaviors are often present in the VLA's output distribution. This finding supports the hypothesis that the bottleneck lies in action evaluation rather than solely in generation.
- **Intermediate Difficulty Impact:** The benefits of action evaluation were most pronounced for tasks of intermediate difficulty, which saw substantial gains, whereas very easy tasks quickly saturated and very hard tasks showed limited improvement regardless of sampling budget.
- **Diminishing Returns:** Marginal gains in success rate decreased with increasing `k` (number of attempts), suggesting that naive independent sampling is inefficient and smarter scaling strategies are required.

#### 4.2. EmbodiedBench Performance

SVA was tested on EB-Habitat and EB-Navigation with five different VLA backbones (GPT-4o, Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.5-27B, and Gemma-4-E4B-it).
- **Consistent Improvements:** SVA consistently improved success rates across all tested backbones and benchmarks. Average gains were +15.4 points on EB-Habitat and +13.2 points on EB-Navigation.
- **Category-Specific Gains:** The most significant improvements were observed in categories requiring long-horizon planning (e.g., "Long Horizon" +23.34 points on Qwen3.5-27B) and visual grounding (e.g., "Common Sense" +28.33 points on Qwen3.5-27B). This indicates that the Q-model effectively corrects myopic action preferences by anticipating long-term consequences.

#### 4.3. Manipulation Benchmarks Performance

SVA was also evaluated on SimplerEnv (WidowX platform) and RoboTwin 2.0 (bimanual manipulation).
- **SimplerEnv:** π0 +SVA achieved an average success rate of 50.7%, outperforming both the base π0 (38.5%) and π0 +RoboMonkey (46.3%). Notable gains were seen on contact-rich, multi-step tasks such as Stack Cubes (+26.4 points) and Eggplant in Basket (+13.9 points), where precise action sequences are critical.
- **RoboTwin 2.0:** π0.5 +SVA improved the average success rate from 36.0% to 43.5% (+7.5 points), surpassing π0.5 +RoboMonkey (38.3%). Gains were observed across diverse manipulation primitives (e.g., Turn Switch +20.0, Press Stapler +16.7, Click Bell +15.0), suggesting the Q-evaluator's transferability across manipulation modalities.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

An ablation study on EB-Navigation (Qwen3.5-9B) isolated the contribution of SVA's components:
- **Importance of MCTS:** Training the Q-model without MCTS supervision (using only policy-rollout returns) resulted in a moderate decline in performance (50.83% vs. 56.11% for full SVA), indicating that MCTS provides richer, more structured evaluation signals.
- **Necessity of Q-model:** Replacing the Q-model with a majority voting mechanism led to the largest performance drop (43.33% vs. 56.11%), confirming that an explicit value estimator is essential for discriminating between plausible candidates.
- **Multi-Candidate Selection:** Eliminating multi-candidate selection (equivalent to single-shot execution) reduced performance to the baseline (39.17% vs. 56.11%), highlighting the value of providing diverse proposals for evaluation.
These results confirmed that all components of SVA contribute to its overall performance.

#### 4.5. Scaling SVA at Test Time: Performance, Latency, and Cost-Effectiveness

Analysis of SVA's scaling behavior by varying the number of candidate actions (N) revealed:
- **Monotonic Performance Gains:** Increasing N consistently led to higher success rates for SVA.
- **Sub-Linear Latency Growth:** Inference latency scaled sub-linearly with N. The Q-model's evaluation latency was negligible compared to action generation (≤ 1.33s vs. up to 11.2s at N=32 for Qwen3.5-9B), making test-time scaling practical.
- **Cost-Effectiveness:** A key finding was that scaling test-time evaluation can be more cost-effective than scaling model size. For example, Qwen3.5-9B + SVA (with N=4 or N=8) achieved a success rate of 51.1% (at 5.87s latency) or 53.6% (at 7.43s latency) respectively. This surpassed the performance of a single-shot Qwen3.5-27B model (46.7% success at 10.2s latency), demonstrating a 7-point performance gain with 27% lower inference latency using a 3x smaller backbone.

#### 4.6. Case Studies

Qualitative case studies demonstrated SVA's ability to correct VLA behaviors:
- **Distractor-Aware Instruction Following:** SVA successfully navigated a complex instruction with a salient but irrelevant distractor, selecting a lower-prior but higher-Q-value goal-directed plan, whereas the base policy was misled.
- **Spatial-Relation Grounding:** SVA accurately grounded a spatial relationship, selecting a spatially correct sequence of actions for object rearrangement where the base policy failed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of embodied AI by proposing an alternative paradigm for improving Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, shifting focus from solely refining action generation to enhancing action evaluation.

One of the primary implications is the **redefinition of VLA failure modes**. By demonstrating that high-quality actions often exist within a frozen VLA's output distribution, the work suggests that the critical bottleneck is often the model's inability to identify and select these competent actions. This reframing opens avenues for policy improvement that do not necessitate modifying the VLA's core parameters.

The **SVA framework provides a practical and effective alternative to conventional post-training methods** like supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, which often entail significant computational costs and risk narrowing the model's generalist capabilities. By keeping the VLA backbone frozen, SVA inherently **preserves the generalist capacity** acquired during pretraining, which is a valuable asset in broad embodied domains.

A notable finding is the demonstration that **scaling test-time evaluation can be more cost-effective than scaling model size**. The results showed a smaller VLA model (9B) combined with SVA outperforming a significantly larger VLA (27B) with lower inference latency. This suggests a paradigm shift where computational resources might be more efficiently allocated towards sophisticated test-time evaluation rather than solely increasing model scale, potentially making high-performing embodied agents more accessible.

The methodology's reliance on Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to generate high-quality, long-horizon evaluation signals, which are then distilled into a lightweight Q-value model, presents a robust approach to learning action consequences. This enables real-time action selection without requiring continuous access to a simulator during deployment.

**Potential impacts include:**
- **Accelerated VLA Development:** By offering an effective, less resource-intensive improvement strategy, SVA could accelerate the development and deployment of VLA models.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** The ability of the Q-model to override myopic or distractor-driven action proposals via long-horizon consequence evaluation leads to improved robustness in complex environments and under challenging instructions.
- **Adaptive Systems:** The "best-of-N" strategy within SVA offers a natural mechanism for adaptive computation, allowing agents to allocate more evaluation resources for more challenging decision points.

Despite its advancements, the research acknowledges limitations, including the decoupled nature of search and value learning, reliance on resettable simulators, and the need for real-robot validation. These limitations delineate clear directions for future work, such as unifying the search-and-learning loop, relaxing simulator assumptions through learned world models or sparse human feedback, and critically, bridging the sim-to-real gap through physical robot experiments. Overall, SVA establishes a foundation for scalable test-time reasoning in embodied agents, potentially paving the way for more general, scalable, deployable, and self-improving embodied systems.
