# PonderPounce: A Pretrained MLLM as an Episode Context Engine for Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> PonderPounce reuses a pretrained MLLM's native causal context as robot episode memory. Ponder (System 2, 9B) accumulates observations, demonstrations and prior cognition, emitting one continuous cognition token; Pounce (System 1 VLA) conditions each action chunk on the newest token plus its age. Jointly trained end to end, it reaches 60.83% on RoboMME versus 44.51% for the strongest purpose-built memory baseline, at 20 Hz playback.

## Problem

- VLA models inherit pretrained multimodal representations but do not carry the MLLM's contextual capacity into control, so a current-observation policy reaches only 17.93% versus 90.50% for humans.
- Memory-dependent policies add purpose-built mechanisms - frame samplers, segmentation banks, keyframe retrieval, external summary stores, fast-weight updates - each requiring history-specific design tuned to robot trajectories.
- Demonstration-conditioned methods process examples through the controller, task-specific representations, or parameter adaptation, coupling context capacity to the action model and its fast inference budget.

## Method

- **Ponder**, a 9B Qwen3.5 System 2, keeps an ==append-only causal context== of instruction, demonstrations, observations and past carriers; final carrier hidden states form cognition without decoding or pooling.
- The ==asynchronous cognition interface== passes only the newest ready carrier state plus a sinusoidally encoded age into **Pounce**'s prefix, using a learned null cognition when none is ready.
- Joint end-to-end training combines an ==action flow-matching loss== with ==LM-head grounding== on transition gates, subgoal text and demonstration reasoning, which stay internal to System 2; StaticCache and fused Triton kernels serve 20 Hz.

## Results

- RoboMME: **60.83%** at base data and **75.54%** at 9x, beating FrameSamp+Modul by **16.32** and **17.66** points; leads Permanence and Reference, trails on Imitation.
- Swapping Ponder from 0.8B to 9B with identical controller and interface lifts success from **50.04%** to **60.83%**; random initialization is unstable and yields **0.00%**.
- Removing LM-head grounding drops success to **27.96%**; holding cognition between transitions collapses it to **1.83%**; RoboCasa-DC reaches **12.5%** versus **8.6%** with null cognition.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pretrained MLLM context can serve as episode memory without a bespoke store or retrieval path, making System 2 capacity a scalable axis independent of the controller.
> - Continuous latent cognition was only accuracy-competitive with a subgoal-text interface; its real advantage is refresh cost, 78 ms versus 0.82 s per fire.
> - Staleness tolerance is trainable but trades against fresh-condition fit, so refresh interval must be chosen against the deployment latency budget.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation often necessitates access to information that is not immediately present in the current observation, such as previously seen objects, past events, identities of referents, or demonstrated procedures. Addressing this, the field of vision-language-action (VLA) models has explored various mechanisms to incorporate historical context into control policies.

Existing approaches to memory-dependent robotic policies typically involve purpose-built history mechanisms. These include:
- **Architecture-specific mechanisms:** Methods that sample, store, retrieve, or compress historical tokens, such as FrameSamp+Modul, SAM2Act+, and MemER.
- **Attention or learned representations:** Techniques that utilize cross-attention to prompt-video features or extract embodiment-agnostic skills from demonstrations, as seen in Vid2Robot and UniSkill.
- **Intermediate representations:** Approaches that generate maps or plans from context, like Voxposer or SeeTraceAct.
- **Dual-system architectures:** Recent efforts have explored separating "System 2" (slow reasoning) from "System 1" (fast action), with examples like Helix and RoboDual. However, many of these still rely on specific designs for context retention and routing.

While multimodal large language models (MLLMs) inherently possess the capacity to integrate long visual histories and reason from few examples, current VLA models generally inherit pretrained representations without explicitly leveraging an MLLM's native causal context as episode memory. Instead, memory is often handled by external or specially designed modules. The presented work positions itself by proposing to directly reuse an MLLM's native causal context for episode memory, thereby addressing the gap of integrating an MLLM's inherent contextual capabilities directly into robot control without bespoke memory architectures. It explores a decoupled, asynchronous dual-system design where a large MLLM serves as the context engine, providing continuous cognition to a separate action model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether the native causal context of a pretrained Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) can serve as an effective and scalable episode context engine for robot control, while a separate pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model remains responsible for generating actions.

The motivation for this objective stems from several observations in current robotic manipulation research:
- **Dependence on unseen evidence:** Many robotic tasks require information that is not available in the immediate observation, such as occluded objects, past events, or demonstrated procedures. Policies relying solely on the current observation often exhibit limited performance on such tasks. For instance, on the RoboMME benchmark, a current-observation policy (π₀.₅) achieves only 17.93% success, significantly lower than human performance (90.50%). This substantial gap highlights the necessity of robust memory mechanisms.
- **Underutilization of MLLM capabilities:** Pretrained MLLMs have demonstrated considerable capacity for integrating extensive visual histories and performing few-shot reasoning. However, existing VLA models frequently inherit pretrained representations without fully exploiting this inherent contextual capacity as episode memory. Instead, they often resort to purpose-built memory architectures, which can introduce design complexity and limit scalability.
- **Scalability and architectural decoupling:** Traditional memory-augmented policies often integrate memory mechanisms directly into the controller architecture. The authors hypothesize that decoupling the context processing from the action generation path could allow for independent scaling of the context engine (System 2) without requiring modifications to the fast controller (System 1) architecture. This approach aims to leverage the power of larger MLLMs for enhanced reasoning without impeding real-time control.
- **General-purpose memory substrate:** By reusing an MLLM's native context, the research seeks to determine if this can function as a general-purpose memory substrate, capable of handling diverse context-dependent control scenarios, including long-horizon memory tasks and test-time demonstration conditioning, without requiring task-specific memory designs.
- **Efficient and grounded context routing:** A specific objective is to determine what representation and interface can effectively and asynchronously transmit accumulated episode context from the slow reasoning system (PONDER) to the fast action system (POUNCE). The work also explores whether text-based grounding targets for the MLLM's internal cognition can improve downstream control performance, even if the text itself does not directly enter the action model.

In summary, the research aims to bridge the gap between the advanced contextual reasoning capabilities of large MLLMs and the real-time demands of robot control by developing an architecture that reuses MLLM context as memory, enabling scalable and effective context-dependent robotic policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PONDERPOUNCE framework integrates two pretrained models in an asynchronous dual-system design: PONDER (System 2, the MLLM context engine) and POUNCE (System 1, the VLA action model).

#### 3.1. Architecture

**PONDER (System 2):**
- **Role:** PONDER acts as the long-term memory and reasoning engine, accumulating and processing episode context.
- **Model:** It is initialized from Qwen3.5-9B, a 9-billion parameter MLLM.
- **Context Accumulation:** PONDER maintains an append-only causal context. At each query `t`, it appends the current observation `O_t` to a history that includes the instruction (`Ins`), optional demonstrations (`D_1:n`), internally generated subgoal text (`S_t`), demonstration reasoning (`DR`), and earlier cognition carriers. This design means all history is retained within the MLLM's native context window, removing the need for external memory modules.
- **Internal Reasoning:** PONDER predicts a transition token (`T_t ∈ {T_Yes, T_No}`). If `T_Yes` is predicted, it can generate subgoal text (`S_t`) and, for demonstration episodes, demonstration reasoning (`DR`). These textual outputs are primarily for internal grounding and do not directly enter POUNCE in the default configuration.
- **Cognition Output:** From the final hidden states of `K` designated carrier positions within its causal context, PONDER produces a continuous cognition vector `C_t ∈ R^(K×H)`. In the experiments, `K` is set to 1. This vector represents the distilled contextual information.

**POUNCE (System 1):**
- **Role:** POUNCE is the fast action model responsible for generating robot actions.
- **Models:** It is initialized from either the 3.6B π₀.₅ action model (for RoboMME) or the 3B GR00T N1.5 action model (for RoboCasa-DC).
- **Inputs:** At each invocation `j`, POUNCE receives the instruction (`Ins`), the current observation (`O_j`), and proprioception.
- **Cognition Interface:** It receives a prefix formed by the newest ready cognition (`eC_j`) from PONDER and its sinusoidally encoded age (`∆(j)`). This means only the most recent continuous cognition token and its temporal age are transmitted, keeping the interface minimal.
- **Action Generation:** The action head then predicts a chunk of actions (`A_1:h`), which are played back at 20 Hz. If no cognition is available, POUNCE uses a learned null cognition state (`C_∅`).

**Interface between PONDER and POUNCE:**
- The connection is asynchronous and transmits only a continuous cognition token and its age.
- The decoupling allows PONDER (System 2) to operate at a slower frequency while POUNCE (System 1) executes actions rapidly. This design allows System 2 capacity to scale independently without architectural changes to System 1.

#### 3.2. Training and Grounding

- **Initialization:** Both PONDER and POUNCE are initialized from their respective pretrained checkpoints. Random initialization for PONDER was attempted but led to unstable training.
- **Joint End-to-End Optimization:** The entire PONDERPOUNCE system is trained jointly end-to-end. There is no separate pretraining for a "bridge" between the two systems. Components of POUNCE frozen by its underlying recipe remain frozen.
- **Loss Function:** The total loss `L` is a weighted sum: `L = w_1 L_fm + w_2 L_ground`.
 - `L_fm`: Flow-matching Mean Squared Error (MSE) for action prediction, which is the primary objective for POUNCE.
 - `L_ground`: Token cross-entropy loss for PONDER's internal predictions, specifically for the transition target (`T*t`), subgoal text (`S_t`), and demonstration reasoning (`DR`) at annotated transitions. This loss grounds PONDER's internal cognition but is only applied where annotations are available (e.g., RoboMME). For tasks without such annotations (e.g., RoboCasa-DC), `w_2` is set to 0.
- **Gradient Scaling:** The action loss gradient from multiple POUNCE invocations can accumulate and dominate the LM-head grounding loss when backpropagating to PONDER. To mitigate this, the action gradient entering PONDER is scaled by 0.5.
- **Teacher Forcing:** During training, annotated transition targets and their associated text payloads are teacher-forced into PONDER's context before the carrier positions are inserted.

#### 3.3. Asynchronous Schedule

- **Independent Clocks:** PONDER and POUNCE operate on independent clocks.
- **Cognition Reference:** For each POUNCE invocation `j` at time `τ^(1)_j`, the scheduler identifies the latest available cognition `C_ref(j)` from PONDER whose generation and compute delay (`d_t`) were completed before `τ^(1)_j`.
- **Age Calculation:** The age of the cognition is `∆(j) = τ^(1)_j - τ^(2)_ref(j)`, where `τ^(2)_ref(j)` is the source observation time of the referenced PONDER query.
- **Training Schedule:** PONDER queries are sampled at log-normal intervals averaging 1 second, while POUNCE invocations average 100 ms, with an additional 300 ms compute delay. This exposes POUNCE to cognition of varying ages.
- **Evaluation Schedule:** During evaluation, both models run at a fixed 1 Hz, with a 300 ms delay, while action playback occurs at 20 Hz.

#### 3.4. Inference Optimization

To support real-time operation:
- **PONDER (StaticCache):** An append-only StaticCache is used to encode only new tokens, leveraging the native transformer KV cache. This avoids re-encoding the entire causal context with each PONDER query, significantly reducing latency for cognition refreshes.
- **POUNCE (Fused Kernels):** Fused Triton kernels are employed to reduce POUNCE's inference latency, enabling it to meet the required 20 Hz action playback rate.

#### 3.5. Experiments

- **Benchmarks:**
 - **RoboMME:** A suite of 16 memory-dependent tasks (Counting, Permanence, Reference, Imitation) requiring the robot to recall past information. Evaluated at base (1×) and 9× training data scales.
 - **RoboCasa-DC:** A benchmark for demonstration-conditioned control, featuring 5 held-out tasks and cross-embodiment demonstrations.
- **Baselines:** A range of memory-augmented and demonstration-conditioned policies, including FrameSamp+Modul, SAM2Act+, MemER, π₀.₅, Vid2Robot, UniSkill, ViVLA, and SeeTraceAct.
- **Ablation Studies:** Investigations into the impact of LM-head grounding, the nature of the context-to-control interface (continuous vs. sparse text), cognition refresh frequency (staleness), and the scale and initialization of the PONDER MLLM (9B vs. 0.8B, pretrained vs. random initialization).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across benchmark performance and analyses of the cognition channel.

#### 4.1. RoboMME Performance

- **Overall Success:** PONDERPOUNCE demonstrated an average success rate of 60.83% at the base (1×) data scale and 75.54% with 9× training data on RoboMME.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - At the 1× data scale, PONDERPOUNCE (60.83%) exceeded FrameSamp+Modul (44.51%) by 16.32 percentage points (pp). The baseline current-observation π₀.₅ achieved 17.93%, highlighting the impact of context.
 - With 9× data, PONDERPOUNCE (75.54%) surpassed FrameSamp+Modul (57.88%) by 17.66 pp.
- **Task-Specific Performance:** PONDERPOUNCE exhibited stronger performance on Permanence and Reference tasks across both data scales, suggesting effectiveness in hidden-state tracking and reference resolution. However, FrameSamp+Modul remained stronger on Imitation tasks and 9× Counting.
- **Impact of Data Scale:** Increasing training data from 1× to 9× resulted in a 14.71 pp improvement for PONDERPOUNCE, indicating its capacity to benefit from larger datasets.
- **Qualitative Example:** In the PickHighlight task, PONDERPOUNCE was able to retain a transient visual cue after its disappearance, enabling successful task completion, in contrast to a current-observation policy that timed out.

#### 4.2. RoboCasa-DC Performance

- **Demonstration Conditioning:** PONDERPOUNCE achieved a 12.5% success rate on the cross-embodiment demonstration-conditioned tasks in RoboCasa-DC.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** This performance was marginally higher than SeeTraceAct (11.6%), which was reported as the strongest published baseline.
- **Dependence on Cognition:** When cognition was replaced by a learned null state, the success rate for PONDERPOUNCE decreased to 8.6%. A control run without any demonstration achieved 9.0%. These results indicate that the transmitted cognition contributes to control, although the absolute success rates were modest overall.

#### 4.3. Analysis of the Cognition Channel

- **Impact of LM-Head Supervision:**
 - Removing only demonstration-reasoning targets while retaining joint training, the transition gate, and subgoal text, reduced RoboMME success from 60.83% to 48.21%.
 - Removing all LM-head grounding (subgoal text and demonstration reasoning) further lowered success to 27.96%. These findings suggest that LM-head grounding significantly contributes to the MLLM's ability to produce effective cognition, potentially by improving internal representation or gate/subgoal generation.
- **Continuous vs. Subgoal-Text Interface:** A separately adapted subgoal-text reference, which updated POUNCE only at predicted transitions, achieved 59.96%. This was competitive with the continuous cognition interface's 60.83%, indicating that continuous cognition is not necessarily superior in terms of accuracy but provides a comparable performance. Additionally, continuous cognition supports faster refresh rates compared to a forced text decode.
- **Cognition Refresh and Staleness:**
 - The reported PONDERPOUNCE checkpoint was found to depend on within-subgoal refreshes. When POUNCE received cognition only at predicted transitions (holding the last state between updates), success plummeted from 60.83% to 1.83%.
 - An offline diagnostic using teacher-forced loss demonstrated sensitivity to stale cognition. For a checkpoint trained with 1s refresh intervals, normalized loss increased 7.11× at 2.3s and 9.22× at 4.3s. Checkpoints trained with slower refresh intervals showed greater robustness to staleness but higher absolute loss in fresh conditions. This suggests a trade-off between robustness to staleness and performance with fresh cognition.
- **System 2 Pretrained Context Capacity:**
 - Replacing the 9B Qwen3.5 PONDER with a 0.8B Qwen3.5 model, while keeping POUNCE and the interface constant, resulted in a decrease in average RoboMME success from 60.83% to 50.04%. This 10.79 pp difference supports the hypothesis that a larger pretrained context engine improves control through the same interface.
 - Randomly initializing the 9B MLLM instead of using its pretrained weights led to unstable training and 0.00% success, underscoring the importance of pretrained MLLM capabilities for the proposed end-to-end training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that offers a distinct approach to incorporating memory into robot control, with several implications for the field:

- **Novel Memory Paradigm:** PONDERPOUNCE introduces a novel paradigm by directly leveraging the native causal context of a pretrained MLLM as the primary episode memory. Unlike many existing methods that rely on purpose-built memory modules (e.g., samplers, retrievers, compressors), this approach reuses an inherent capability of large language models, potentially simplifying architectural design for memory-augmented policies.
- **Scalability and Decoupled Design:** The asynchronous, dual-system architecture (PONDER as System 2, POUNCE as System 1) demonstrates that the capacity of the context engine (PONDER) can be scaled independently of the fast action model (POUNCE). The finding that a 9B MLLM context engine significantly outperforms a 0.8B counterpart, without changes to the controller or interface, highlights a path toward utilizing larger, more capable foundation models for richer contextual reasoning in robotics without compromising real-time action generation. This decoupling could enable more flexible and powerful system designs.
- **Enhanced Performance on Memory-Dependent Tasks:** PONDERPOUNCE achieves competitive and, in some cases, superior performance on benchmarks specifically designed to test memory-dependent and demonstration-conditioned control (RoboMME, RoboCasa-DC). Its notable gains on tasks requiring tracking hidden states and resolving references suggest the MLLM's context is effective for these challenges.
- **Insights into Context Routing:** The analysis of the cognition channel provides empirical insights into how information from a large context model influences a robot controller. The importance of LM-head grounding supervision, even for internal MLLM states, and the dependence on timely cognition refreshes underline critical design considerations for such systems. The competitiveness of continuous cognition with a discrete subgoal-text interface suggests that both channels can be effective, depending on training and implementation.
- **General-Purpose Memory Substrate:** The ability of the same interface to perform well across diverse memory demands on RoboMME and transfer to demonstration conditioning on RoboCasa-DC supports the notion of pretrained MLLM context as a strong general-purpose memory substrate. This could reduce the need for specialized memory architectures for different robotic tasks.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future exploration into optimizing dual-system VLA architectures, investigating the representations learned within the MLLM's cognition token, and expanding to real-world robot deployments. It opens avenues for studying the trade-offs between context model scale, computational cost, and performance, as well as refining asynchronous communication protocols.

In conclusion, PONDERPOUNCE demonstrates a viable and performant method for integrating the advanced contextual capabilities of MLLMs into robot control through a decoupled and asynchronously communicating dual-system. This contributes to the development of more intelligent and adaptable robotic agents by leveraging large-scale pretraining for robust memory and reasoning.
