# Learning Bimanual Manipulation via Action Chunking and Inter-Arm Coordination with Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST) developed an imitation learning architecture that incorporates an Inter-Arm Coordinated Transformer Encoder (IACE) to enhance bimanual robot manipulation. This approach yielded an 8-9% higher average success rate than the ACT baseline across a diverse set of real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with dexterous, coordinated bimanual movements, especially in diverse human environments, due to the high degrees of freedom (DoF) and control complexity.
- Existing learning-based models often fail to explicitly account for the intricate synchronization and temporal alignment required between two robot arms for complex cooperative tasks.
- Generalizing multi-DoF control and modeling dependent temporal dynamics between arms presents a significant challenge for current robot learning paradigms.

## Method

- A novel imitation learning architecture was proposed, building on ACT, featuring dedicated local Transformer encoders for each arm to process individual states and visual information.
- An Inter-Arm Coordinated Transformer Encoder (IACE) was introduced to explicitly model synchronization and temporal alignment for coordinated bimanual movements, operating independently of local arm encoders.
- Two decoder configurations were investigated: a single decoder processing combined arm outputs and split decoders processing each arm's output separately, both integrating IACE outputs.

## Results

- The proposed models achieved an average of 8-9% higher success rates compared to the ACT baseline across eight real-world bimanual tasks.
- Ablation studies conclusively showed that models without the IACE module suffered significant performance drops, often failing entirely (e.g., 0% success in 'Hit with Hammer' and 'Lift and Place Towel').
- Split Decoders demonstrated superior performance on asynchronous tasks (e.g., 96% success in 'Fold Towel picking'), while Single Decoders generally excelled in synchronous tasks (e.g., 100% success in 'Open Lid' and 'Lift Board').

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling inter-arm coordination through a dedicated module like IACE is critical for achieving high success rates in bimanual manipulation, especially for synchronized actions.
> - The optimal decoder architecture for bimanual tasks is task-dependent; split decoders are more effective for asynchronous tasks, while single decoders perform better for synchronous tasks.
> - Careful architectural design, distinguishing between individual arm learning and overall bimanual coordination, enables Transformer-based models to better handle the complexities of high-DoF bimanual control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial progress, moving from single-arm, rigidly programmed tasks to more flexible, learning-based approaches capable of handling complex, real-world scenarios. Bimanual manipulation, where two robotic arms collaborate, represents a significant frontier in this domain, as it enables tasks that are challenging or impossible for a single arm, such as stabilizing an object while performing an operation or manipulating larger items.

Historically, controlling multi-degree-of-freedom (DoF) robotic systems has been computationally intensive and difficult to generalize. Early approaches often relied on meticulous kinematic and dynamic modeling. However, the advent of imitation learning (IL) and large-scale datasets has revolutionized robot learning, enabling robots to acquire complex skills by observing human demonstrations. Behavioral Cloning (BC), a fundamental IL algorithm, has been particularly influential, evolving to handle one-shot imitation, multitasking, and bilateral control.

Recent advancements in deep learning, especially the Transformer architecture, have further propelled this field. Transformers, initially developed for natural language processing, have proven highly effective in processing sequential data and modeling long-range dependencies, making them suitable for predicting robot trajectories and actions. Models like Decision Transformer and, crucially, Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), have demonstrated remarkable success in generating fine-grained action sequences for robotic tasks. ACT, in particular, is a direct predecessor and baseline for this work, showcasing effective bimanual control through predicting sequences of future actions rather than single-step outputs.

The paper also acknowledges the rise of "foundation models" in robotics, such as ALOHA, MobileALOHA, BiDex, and π0, which leverage extensive bimanual robot data to perform a wide array of tasks. These models highlight the potential of data-driven approaches but also expose challenges related to hardware variations, architectural limitations, and data heterogeneity when trying to generalize across different robot morphologies. While large foundation models offer broad capabilities, there is an ongoing need for specialized architectures that can optimally address the unique coordination demands of bimanual tasks.

This paper specifically targets the limitations of existing learning-based bimanual manipulation systems, particularly regarding inter-arm coordination and synchronization. While models like ACT have successfully utilized dual-arm data, they might not explicitly model the intricate, often synchronized, temporal dynamics between the two arms. The authors observe that simply scaling up single-arm models or relying on end-to-end learning without considering the inherent structure of bimanual tasks might not be optimal. They build upon the success of ACT by proposing an architectural refinement that explicitly addresses these coordination challenges, thereby contributing to the development of more dexterous and efficient bimanual robot control systems. The work also indirectly connects to research on hierarchical mechanisms in robot learning, which aim to manage complexity and improve synchronization by structuring the learning process at different levels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the acknowledged difficulty in enabling robots to perform dexterous, coordinated bimanual movements, particularly in diverse human environments. While single-arm robots are competent in many tasks, coordinated bimanual manipulation unlocks a wider range of functionalities, making robots more versatile and efficient. However, the high degree of freedom (DoF) inherent in dual-arm systems significantly complicates control reasoning. Moreover, the left and right robot arms often need to adjust their actions in a highly dependent and synchronized manner, a challenge not fully addressed by existing learning-based models.

The key objectives of this study are:

- **Enhance Inter-Arm Coordination and Efficiency:** To develop an imitation learning architecture that explicitly facilitates synchronization and temporal alignment between the two robot arms, particularly for synchronized actions. This aims to enable more dexterous and coordinated bimanual tasks.
- **Investigate Optimal Architectural Design for Bimanual Learning:** To examine how different encoder and decoder configurations impact the learning of bimanual actions. This includes exploring specialized pathways for each arm and how global information is integrated. The paper hypothesizes that distinguishing between single-arm learning and overall bimanual coordination is crucial.
- **Propose the Inter-Arm Coordinated Transformer Encoder (IACE):** To introduce a novel architectural component, the IACE, designed to explicitly capture and model the synchronization and timing of bimanual movements, separate from the individual arm encoders.
- **Evaluate Decoder Architectures:** To compare the effectiveness of two distinct decoder types—a single decoder (processing combined arm outputs) versus split decoders (processing each arm's output separately)—in the context of bimanual tasks.
- **Demonstrate Effectiveness on Real-World Tasks:** To validate the proposed architecture's performance using real-world bimanual tasks, categorized by their asynchronous or synchronous nature, and demonstrate its superiority over existing baselines like ACT.

In essence, the authors are motivated to move beyond simply adapting single-arm learning to bimanual systems by proposing a structurally informed architecture that inherently understands and promotes the cooperative nature of two-arm manipulation, thereby addressing a critical gap in current robot learning paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel imitation learning architecture that builds upon the Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) model. Their approach centers on differentiating the architectural components for each arm and introducing a specialized module for inter-arm coordination.

**A. Network Architecture:**
The proposed models integrate several key components:

1. **Input Processing:**
 - **Visual Features:** RGB images from multiple cameras (wrist-mounted, top, and lower views) are processed by ResNet18 convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to extract visual features.
 - **Joint States:** Current joint positions for both robot arms are included.
 - **Sequence Construction:** Joint sequences from both arms and visual feature sequences are concatenated to form a comprehensive input sequence. Classification (CLS) tokens are added at the beginning of each segment to summarize information, and fixed positional embeddings are applied to preserve sequence order.

2. **Encoder Structure:**
 - **Local Transformer Encoders:** Each robot arm is equipped with its own local Transformer encoder. These encoders process the individual state and visual information relevant to that specific arm, allowing each arm to learn its trajectory.
 - **Inter-Arm Coordinated Transformer Encoder (IACE):** This is the core novel component. The IACE operates independently of the local arm encoders. It takes as input the combined states of both arms and global visual information (capturing the whole scene). Its primary role is to explicitly capture and model the synchronization and temporal alignment required for coordinated bimanual movements, allowing for stable inference of bimanual trajectories.

3. **Decoder Structure (Multi-Arm Decoders):** The paper investigates two distinct decoder configurations:
 - **(a) Single Decoder:** This configuration takes a concatenated input from the outputs of both local arm encoders and the IACE. It then processes this combined information to output a batch of action chunks for both arms simultaneously.
 - **(b) Split Decoders:** In this setup, there are separate decoders for each arm. Each split decoder receives the output tokens from its corresponding local arm encoder, concatenated with the output tokens from the IACE. Each decoder then independently outputs the action chunks for its respective arm. The research aims to compare the effectiveness of these two approaches for bimanual tasks.

**B. Training and Data Handling:**
- **Imitation Learning Framework:** The entire architecture is trained end-to-end using an imitation learning framework.
- **Data Collection:** Human operators collect demonstration datasets via a teleoperation system, specifically the ALOHA setup. This includes joint positions and RGB images recorded at 50 Hz. Operators are instructed to control slowly to ensure high precision and data quality.
- **Data Pre-processing:** To mitigate occasional instability from minute variations in joint values, the collected frames are down-sampled and trained at 25 Hz.
- **Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE):** A CVAE is employed to model the human demonstration dataset. This is a crucial step to reduce the variability (noise, inconsistencies) often present in human-teleoperated trajectories, thereby improving the quality of the learned policy.
- **Action Chunking:** Similar to the base ACT model, the system predicts a sequence of future actions (an "action chunk") rather than just a single-step action. This provides temporal coherence and allows for more stable and robust control.
- **Temporal Ensemble:** During inference, a temporal ensemble approach is used. This involves weighting action predictions over multiple time steps to enhance their consistency and robustness.
- **Optimizer and Hyperparameters:** AdamW is used as the optimizer with a learning rate (λ) of 1 × 10−5. Training runs for 5000 epochs with a batch size of 8.

**C. Experimental Setup:**
- **Hardware:** Experiments are conducted on a real-world multi-task dataset using the ALOHA setup. This setup comprises two ViperX300S robot arms and four RGB cameras: one on each arm's wrist, one top-view (upper), and one horizontal-view (lower).
- **Tasks:** Eight real-world bimanual tasks are designed and categorized based on a bimanual taxonomy:
 - **Asynchronous Tasks:** Fold Towel, Hand-over, Silver Bag, Hit with Hammer, Hand-over Can. These tasks involve independent or sequentially dependent movements between the arms.
 - **Synchronous Tasks:** Open Lid, Open Toolbox, Lift and Place Towel, Lift Board. These tasks require coordinated and simultaneous movements of both arms.
- **Baselines:** The primary comparison is against the ACT model, which has demonstrated strong performance in bimanual manipulation. Diffusion Policy was considered but not included in the main comparison due to requiring larger datasets than the 50 episodes used per task.
- **Ablation Studies:** The effectiveness of the IACE module is rigorously tested by comparing the proposed models with and without the IACE layer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed architecture, particularly the IACE and the investigation into different decoder structures, for bimanual manipulation tasks. The evaluation was performed on 25 episodes for each of the eight real-world tasks.

**A. Performance Comparison with Baselines:**
The paper compared its proposed models (Ours (Single Decoders) and Ours (Split Decoders)) against the ACT baseline. The results, summarized in Table I, show that the proposed methods generally achieve higher success rates.

- **Asynchronous Tasks:**
 - For tasks requiring largely independent or sequentially distinct actions from each arm (e.g., Fold Towel, Hand-over tasks), the **Ours (Split Decoders)** model consistently demonstrated superior performance.
 - In the "Fold Towel" task, for example, the Split Decoders achieved a 96% success rate for picking and placing, outperforming ACT's 92% (picking) and 88% (placing).
 - Across the asynchronous tasks (Fold Towel, Hand-over Silver Bag, Hit with Hammer, Hand-over Can), the Split Decoders achieved an average success rate of approximately 92.5%, compared to ACT's average of 86.4%. This represents an average improvement of about 6.1 percentage points. The paper's abstract claims an "8-9% higher success rate than ACT" which is likely an overall average across all tasks.

- **Synchronous Tasks:**
 - For tasks demanding simultaneous and highly coordinated movements (e.g., Open Lid, Open Toolbox, Lift and Place Towel, Lift Board), the **Ours (Single Decoders)** model generally performed better.
 - In "Open Lid," both Ours (Single Decoders) and ACT achieved 100% success. However, in "Lift and Place Towel," Ours (Single Decoders) achieved 96% for placing, matching ACT's 96% for placing, and 100% for lifting (matching ACT). In "Lift Board," it also achieved 100% (matching ACT).
 - Across the synchronous tasks, the Single Decoders achieved an average success rate of 99%, compared to ACT's average of 98%. While the improvement is marginal in specific instances, the overall robustness across synchronous tasks is suggested.

**B. Impact of Inter-Arm Coordinated Transformer Encoder (IACE) (Ablation Studies):**
The ablation study, comparing models with and without the IACE, strongly affirmed the critical role of the IACE module.

- Models trained *without* the IACE consistently showed a significant decline in performance across both asynchronous and synchronous tasks.
- For instance, in the "Hit with Hammer" asynchronous task, the Split Decoders model without IACE dropped to 0% for hitting and 0% for hand-over, compared to 84% and 96% with IACE. Similarly, for "Lift and Place Towel" synchronous task, both single and split decoders without IACE scored 0% for placing, a stark contrast to the high success rates when IACE was present.
- This clear performance drop unequivocally demonstrates that the IACE effectively facilitates the necessary synchronization and coordination of movements between the arms, which is crucial for successful bimanual manipulation.

**C. Summary of Architectural Suitability:**
The results suggest that the choice of decoder architecture should be tailored to the task type:
- **Split Decoders** are more suitable for **asynchronous tasks**, where individual arm trajectories might have distinct phases or roles, and explicit separation in decoding allows for better learning of these independent movements while still benefiting from overall coordination through IACE.
- **Single Decoders** tend to perform better for **synchronous tasks**, where a unified decoding process can more effectively manage tightly coupled and simultaneous actions.

Overall, the proposed architecture, particularly with the IACE module, represents a significant advancement, showing a marked improvement over the strong ACT baseline and providing valuable insights into designing effective bimanual robot control systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in the domain of bimanual control, and has considerable potential impact:

**A. Novel Architectural Design for Bimanual Coordination:**
The most significant contribution is the introduction of a novel imitation learning architecture featuring dedicated local Transformer encoders for each arm and, most importantly, the **Inter-Arm Coordinated Transformer Encoder (IACE)**. This design explicitly addresses the challenge of inter-arm synchronization and temporal alignment, which is critical for dexterous bimanual tasks. By providing a mechanism for global coordination independent of individual arm processing, the IACE ensures that the two arms move in a cohesive and interdependent manner. The ablation study conclusively proves the IACE's indispensability, highlighting its role in achieving high success rates.

**B. Insights into Optimal Decoder Configurations:**
The study provides valuable insights into the architectural choices for decoding bimanual actions by empirically comparing single versus split decoders. The finding that split decoders are more effective for asynchronous tasks, while single decoders show advantages in synchronous tasks, offers practical guidelines for future robot learning system designs. This nuanced understanding of architectural suitability, tailored to the nature of bimanual interaction, can inform the development of more efficient and task-agnostic bimanual policies.

**C. Enhanced Performance on Real-World Bimanual Tasks:**
The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed models achieve higher success rates—ranging from 8% to 9% on average as claimed in the abstract—compared to a strong baseline like ACT on a diverse set of real-world bimanual tasks. This practical improvement, validated on an ALOHA teleoperation setup, showcases the architecture's robustness and capability to handle complex operations like towel folding and tool use. Such advancements bring autonomous bimanual robots closer to practical deployment in human environments, from industrial assembly lines to home assistance.

**D. Advancing Imitation Learning for High-DoF Systems:**
By effectively tackling the challenges of high-DoF bimanual systems using imitation learning and Transformer-based models, this work pushes the boundaries of what is achievable with learning-from-demonstration paradigms. The use of CVAE to reduce variability in human teleoperation data also contributes to making imitation learning more robust and data-efficient, which is crucial given the cost and effort of collecting high-quality robot data.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Broader Robot Applications:** Improved bimanual dexterity can enable robots to perform a wider range of tasks in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and domestic settings, where precise and coordinated two-hand operations are often required (e.g., surgical assistance, complex assembly, advanced household chores).
- **Foundation for More Generalist Robots:** The architectural insights gained from this study could be integrated into larger foundation models for robotics, allowing them to better reason about and execute bimanual tasks, potentially leading to more versatile and capable generalist robots.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies avenues for future work, including extending the model to be compatible with single-arm operations, integrating multimodal inputs (text, other sensors), and validating the architecture with different learning algorithms (e.g., Diffusion Policy). These directions pave the way for more sophisticated and generalized bimanual manipulation capabilities.

While the paper acknowledges that the improvement in success rate might not always be "significant" depending on the task type, the consistent overall performance boost and the clear functional importance of the IACE module underscore its value. This research represents a vital step toward developing truly dexterous and intelligent bimanual robotic systems capable of operating autonomously in complex, human-centric environments.
