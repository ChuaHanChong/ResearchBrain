# Accessing GPT-4 level Mathematical Olympiad Solutions via Monte Carlo Tree Self-refine with LLaMa-3 8B

> [!summary] Summary
> MCT Self-Refine (MCTSr) couples an LLM with Monte Carlo Tree Search: tree nodes are candidate answers, edges are self-refine rewrites, and node values come from constrained LLM self-reward sampling, backpropagated through a modified UCT selection rule. With LLaMA3-8B, 8 rollouts lift GSM8K from 74.07% to 96.66% and MATH from 24.36% to 58.24%, approaching closed-source GPT-4-level scores on several math benchmarks.

## Problem

- LLMs hallucinate plausible but wrong steps in mathematical reasoning, where precision is paramount, making outputs untrustworthy for Olympiad-level problem solving.
- Single-pass self-refine rewriting relieves but does not eliminate erroneous outcomes, since it lacks systematic exploration over alternative answer revisions.
- Classic MCTS assumes discrete bounded action sets, misaligning with generative LLMs whose refinement actions form an unbounded continuous space, breaking expansion and value estimation.

## Method

- **MCTSr** builds a search tree where nodes are answer versions and edges are ==self-refine== edits, cycling selection, self-refine, self-evaluation, backpropagation, and ==UCT== update until termination.
- Self-evaluation samples an LLM self-reward in `[-100,100]` under strict-scoring prompts, full-score suppression above 95, and repeated sampling; Q averages the minimum and mean reward.
- A dynamic pruning rule defines full expansion via a child-count limit plus at least one child exceeding the parent's Q, then ranks candidates with a modified ==UCB-1== UCT formula.

## Results

- On GSM8K, accuracy rises from **74.07%** zero-shot CoT to **86.96%** one-turn self-refine, **93.03%** at 4 rollouts, and **96.66%** at 8 rollouts; GSM-Hard reaches only **45.49%**.
- MATH overall improves from **24.36%** to **58.24%** with 8 rollouts, but level-5 problems reach just **34.06%**, versus **90.16%** on level-1, showing difficulty-dependent gains.
- Olympiad sets gain consistently: AIME **2.36%** to **11.79%**, Math Odyssey **17.22%** to **49.36%**, OlympiadBench **1.25%** to **7.76%**, with Math Odyssey nearing GPT-4 Turbo's reported **49.1%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Search-time compute can substitute for parameters: an 8B open model with tree search reaches closed-source-model accuracy on several math benchmarks without any fine-tuning.
> - LLM self-reward is too smooth to rank answers; harsh-scoring prompts, full-score suppression, and min-plus-mean aggregation are needed to restore discriminative value signal.
> - Gains shrink sharply as problem difficulty rises, so rollout scaling mainly amplifies reasoning the model can already almost do, not genuinely absent capability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within Broader Research Landscape

The rapid evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 and LLaMA has fundamentally transformed the landscape of natural language processing (NLP), endowing systems with unprecedented language comprehension and generation abilities. These models exhibit emergent properties, including impressive reasoning and in-context learning, extending their applicability beyond traditional NLP to diverse domains such as mathematical problem-solving, recommendation systems, and even molecular generation.

However, despite these strides, LLMs face significant hurdles in tasks requiring robust strategic and logical reasoning. A critical challenge is the inherent inaccuracy and tendency to "hallucinate" in their outputs, particularly in mathematical contexts where precision is non-negotiable. While superficial plausibility may be achieved, factual incorrectness or irrelevance can undermine the integrity of the reasoning process. Existing techniques, such as Self-Refine, attempt to mitigate these issues by allowing models to iteratively refine their outputs based on self-generated feedback. However, for highly complex, multi-step mathematical problems typical of Olympiad-level challenges, these methods often fall short.

This research strategically positions itself at the intersection of LLM capabilities and traditional symbolic reasoning/search algorithms. Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), a well-established decision-making framework widely successful in domains like game AI (e.g., AlphaGo), offers a systematic approach to exploration and planning in vast state spaces. The broader research landscape has seen attempts to integrate MCTS with LLMs for mathematical reasoning (e.g., Chen et al., 2024; Xu, 2023). However, these prior works often lack a comprehensive framework that fully leverages the iterative self-refinement and self-evaluation capabilities inherent in modern LLMs within the MCTS loop.

The unique contribution of this paper lies in its innovative integration of MCTS with LLMs' self-refine and self-evaluation mechanisms. By abstracting the problem-solving process into a structured search tree, MCTSr aims to harness the LLM's generative and reflective strengths while mitigating its weaknesses in logical consistency and accuracy through systematic exploration and rigorous self-correction. This work addresses the specific gap of effectively combining the exploratory power of MCTS with the adaptive and reflective learning abilities of LLMs, pushing the frontier of complex reasoning in AI beyond mere pattern matching towards more robust, verifiable problem-solving. It directly tackles the challenge of achieving high-level mathematical reasoning performance, especially with smaller, more efficient open-source models, thereby making advanced AI capabilities more accessible.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the MCT Self-Refine (MCTSr) algorithm is to overcome the critical limitations of current Large Language Models (LLMs) in complex reasoning tasks, particularly in mathematical problem-solving. Despite their advanced language comprehension and generation abilities, LLMs frequently struggle with logical precision and factual accuracy in domains requiring rigorous reasoning, often producing "hallucinations" that undermine their trustworthiness. This issue is particularly pronounced in mathematics, where even minor errors can invalidate an entire solution. While self-refinement techniques have emerged to address some of these shortcomings, they often lack the systematic exploration and robust evaluation mechanisms needed for highly intricate, multi-step problems.

Against this backdrop, the authors set forth several key objectives:
1. **Develop a Novel Reasoning Algorithm:** To create and validate an innovative algorithm that integrates LLMs with the Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) framework, specifically adapting it for the unique characteristics of LLMs in mathematical contexts.
2. **Enhance LLM Performance in Complex Mathematical Reasoning:** To significantly improve LLMs' success rates and reliability in solving challenging mathematical problems, including those encountered in Olympiad-level competitions. This involves leveraging MCTS's systematic exploration to guide the LLM's generative process.
3. **Introduce Dynamic Pruning:** To propose and implement a dynamic pruning module within the MCTS framework. This module aims to optimize the exploration-exploitation balance and enhance the efficiency and accuracy of decision-making by intelligently managing the search space, which is critical given the potentially infinite action space of LLMs.
4. **Demonstrate Synergistic Potential:** To provide empirical evidence and insights into how the combination of LLMs and MCTS can create a synergistic effect, leading to improved performance in tasks that neither component can solve effectively on its own.
5. **Advance AI for Enhanced Decision-Making:** To contribute to the broader application of LLMs in sophisticated reasoning challenges and lay a foundational groundwork for future innovations in integrating AI technologies to improve decision-making accuracy and reliability in various LLM-driven applications.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the gap between LLMs' impressive generative capabilities and their current deficiencies in rigorous logical reasoning, thereby unlocking their potential for truly intelligent problem-solving in challenging domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MCT Self-Refine (MCTSr) algorithm is an innovative integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), designed to iteratively refine mathematical problem solutions within a structured search tree. The core idea is to treat different versions of an answer as nodes in a tree and attempts at improvement as edges, mimicking human trial-and-error learning.

The algorithm's operational workflow adheres to the general MCTS pattern but introduces LLM-specific adaptations across its four main phases, plus additional components:

1. **Initialization:** The process begins by establishing a root node. This node can be populated with an initial, naive answer generated by the LLM or even a dummy response (e.g., "I don't know.") to minimize potential model overfitting from an overly confident initial guess.

2. **Selection:** In this phase, the algorithm intelligently chooses the most promising node for further exploration and refinement. It employs a greedy strategy based on a calculated Q-value function, which estimates the worth of each answer node. The selection prioritizes nodes that have not been fully "expanded" according to specific criteria.

3. **Self-Refine:** Once a node (representing an answer `a` to problem `P`) is selected, the LLM is prompted to optimize it using a multi-turn dialogue feedback mechanism. First, the model generates a reflective or critical comment (`m`) about the current answer `a`. Subsequently, guided by this feedback `m`, the model modifies `a` to produce an improved version `a'`. This iterative process aims to enhance the quality of the response by leveraging structured self-correction.

4. **Self-Evaluation:** The newly refined answer `a'` is then scored to sample a reward value, which contributes to the calculation of its Q-value. The model utilizes a self-reward method where it assigns a score from -100 to 100. To counteract the LLM's tendency for overly smooth or optimistic self-rewards and ensure reliability, three critical constraints are imposed:
 - **Prompt Constraint:** The model is instructed to adhere to the strictest standards during reward scoring.
 - **Full Score Suppression:** Any reward exceeding 95 is reduced by a constant amount to prevent the model from consistently giving itself perfect or near-perfect scores.
 - **Repeated Sampling:** Each visit to a search tree node involves multiple samplings of the node's rewards to enhance reliability. Crucially, when child nodes are sampled, their parent node's reward is also sampled, increasing the statistical robustness of the parent's value.
 The Q-value of an answer `a` is then calculated using a specialized formula: `Q(a) = 1/2 * (min R_a + (Σ R_i_a) / |R_a|)`. This formula averages the minimum sampled reward and the mean of all sampled rewards, balancing worst-case and average outcomes.

5. **Backpropagation:** After the reward sampling and Q-value update for leaf nodes are completed, these changes are propagated backward up the tree to their parent and ancestor nodes. If the Q-value of any child node changes, the parent node's Q-value is updated to `Q'(a) = 1/2 * (Q(a) + max_{i∈Children(a)} Q(i))`. This formula refines the parent's Q-value by averaging its current value with the highest Q-value among its children, reflecting the potential for future improvement.

6. **UCT Update and Selection:** After all Q-values are updated, the algorithm prepares for the next selection round.
 - **Candidate Node Selection:** Leveraging the Markovian nature of the problem refinement (path-independent), candidate nodes for further expansion or selection are identified. These include all leaf nodes and those not "fully expanded."
 - **"Full Expansion" Criteria:** A node is considered "fully expanded" if its child count reaches a predefined limit AND at least one child node's Q-value exceeds the current node's Q-value. This dynamic criterion is crucial for LLMs, which can generate an infinite number of actions.
 - **UCT Update:** The Upper Confidence Bound (UCT) value, based on an improved UCB-1 formula, is calculated for each candidate node `a`: `UCT_a = Q(a) + c * sqrt((ln N(Father(a)) + 1) / (N(a) + ε))`. This balances exploration (visiting less-explored but potentially rewarding nodes) and exploitation (choosing known high-value nodes). `N(·)` represents visit count, `c` is an exploration constant, and `ε` is a small constant to prevent division by zero.
 - **Sorting and Selection:** The optimal node for the next refinement process is chosen from the candidate set based on its UCT value, typically through greedy or importance sampling.

7. **Termination Function:** The iterative process continues until a termination condition `T` is met. These conditions can include:
 - Early stopping when improvements diminish or results become repetitive.
 - Reaching a predetermined limit on the number of rollouts or maximum search depth.
 - Advanced criteria based on language model logits (e.g., confidence scores).
Once terminated, the best answers are extracted from the tree nodes based on their Q-values.

The foundational LLM used for experiments was LLaMA3-8B. The specific prompts for Self-Refine, Self-Evaluation, and Dummy Answers are detailed in the appendix, showcasing the careful engineering of the LLM's interaction within the MCTS framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

To evaluate the MCTSr algorithm's effectiveness, the authors conducted extensive experiments using LLaMA3-8B as the base model. They compared MCTSr's performance against several configurations: Zero-Shot CoT (Chain of Thought), One-turn Self-Refine, 4-rollouts MCTSr, and 8-rollouts MCTSr. The performance was also benchmarked against state-of-the-art closed-source models like GPT-4, Claude 3, and Gemini 1.5-Pro. The evaluations spanned a range of mathematical datasets, categorized into general benchmarks and Olympiad-level challenges.

**Experimental Settings:**
- **Base LLM:** LLaMA3-8B.
- **Comparison Baselines:** Zero-Shot CoT, Self-Refine (one-turn), and varying rollouts of MCTSr (4-rollouts, 8-rollouts).
- **External Benchmarks:** GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini 1.5-Pro.
- **Datasets:**
 - **General Math:** GSM8K, GSM-Hard, MATH (stratified into 5 difficulty levels).
 - **Olympiad-level:** AIME, Math Odyssey (notable for minimal pre-training overlap with LLaMa3-8B), and OlympiadBench (pure-text subset).

**Performance on GSM Benchmarks (Table 1):**
- **GSM8K:** The success rate dramatically improved with MCTSr rollouts. From 74.07% (Zero-Shot CoT) to 86.96% (One-turn Self-refine), it further jumped to 93.03% with 4-rollouts MCTSr and achieved an impressive **96.66%** with 8-rollouts MCTSr. This indicates a strong correlation between iterative refinement via MCTSr and improved accuracy for relatively less complex problems.
- **GSM-Hard:** For more challenging problems, 8-rollouts MCTSr reached **45.49%** success, a substantial improvement over Zero-Shot CoT (25.47%) and One-turn Self-refine (33.36%). However, the authors noted a performance ceiling, suggesting limitations against highly intricate problems, despite the clear benefits of MCTSr.

**Performance on MATH Benchmark (Table 2):**
The MATH dataset, with its five difficulty levels, showcased MCTSr's scalability.
- **Overall:** MCTSr consistently improved performance across all levels. The 8-rollouts MCTSr achieved an overall success rate of **58.24%**, a significant leap from Zero-Shot CoT's 24.36% and One-turn Self-refine's 35.74%.
- **Level-1 (Easiest):** 8-rollouts MCTSr attained a remarkable **90.16%** success rate.
- **Level-5 (Most Challenging):** Even at the highest difficulty, 8-rollouts MCTSr managed **34.06%** success, demonstrating its efficacy even in highly complex scenarios, though with expectedly lower absolute scores. The consistent trend of increased rollouts correlating with improved success rates validates the algorithm's effectiveness across varying mathematical complexities.

**Performance on Olympiad-level Benchmarks (Table 3):**
These datasets represent the pinnacle of mathematical reasoning challenges for LLMs.
- **AIME:** Success rate improved from 2.36% (Zero-Shot CoT) to **11.79%** with 8-rollouts MCTSr.
- **GAIC Math Odyssey:** Showed substantial improvement, rising from 17.22% to **49.36%** with 8-rollouts MCTSr. This dataset is particularly significant as it has minimal overlap with LLaMa3-8B's pre-training corpus, strongly indicating MCTSr's generalization capabilities to unseen problems.
- **OlympiadBench:** Improved from 1.25% to **7.76%** with 8-rollouts MCTSr.
The results unequivocally demonstrate that increasing rollouts consistently leads to higher success rates, underscoring the power of iterative refinement guided by MCTSr.

**Comparison with Closed-Source LLMs (Table 4 & Discussion):**
The most striking finding is the comparison of MCTSr (using LLaMa-3 8B) with top-tier closed-source models:
- **MATH:** While GPT-4 (73.4%), Gemini 1.5-Pro (67.7%), and Claude 3 Opus (60.1%) outperformed MCTSr's LLaMa-3 8B (58.24% with 8-rollouts) in absolute terms, the fact that an 8-billion parameter open-source model could achieve performance within a competitive range of models with vastly more parameters is highly significant.
- **Math Odyssey:** Remarkably, MCTSr with LLaMa-3 8B achieved **49.36%**, which is slightly *higher* than GPT-4's reported 49.1%, and significantly better than Gemini 1.5-Pro (45.0%) and Claude 3 Opus (40%). This result particularly highlights MCTSr's ability to boost smaller models to SOTA levels in challenging, generalization-heavy tasks.
- **GSM8K:** MCTSr's LLaMa-3 8B achieved **96.66%**, very close to GPT-4's 97.1%, and surpassing Claude 3 and Gemini 1.5-Pro.

In conclusion, the main findings demonstrate that MCTSr significantly enhances the mathematical reasoning capabilities of open-source LLMs, enabling LLaMa-3 8B to achieve performance levels comparable to, and in some cases even exceeding, cutting-edge closed-source models on complex mathematical benchmarks. This showcases the algorithm's robustness and its potential to push the boundaries of accessible AI.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MCT Self-Refine (MCTSr) algorithm represents a significant advancement in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly for complex mathematical problem-solving. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating that a structured search algorithm like Monte Carlo Tree Search, when synergistically integrated with an LLM's self-refine and self-evaluation capabilities, can effectively overcome the inherent limitations of LLMs in logical precision and accuracy.

**Key Significance:**
- **Bridging the Performance Gap:** The most impactful finding is that MCTSr enables a relatively small, open-source LLM (LLaMa-3 8B) to achieve performance levels in mathematical reasoning tasks that are comparable to, and in some instances even surpass, much larger and proprietary state-of-the-art models like GPT-4, Claude 3, and Gemini 1.5-Pro. This directly addresses the challenge of making high-level AI capabilities more accessible and efficient.
- **Enhanced Reliability and Accuracy:** By systematically exploring solution paths and iteratively refining answers through self-feedback and rigorous self-evaluation (including novel constraints like full score suppression and repeated sampling), MCTSr effectively mitigates the hallucination problem and significantly improves the trustworthiness of LLM outputs in critical reasoning tasks.
- **Robustness Across Difficulty Levels:** The consistent performance improvements observed across diverse datasets, from standard arithmetic problems (GSM8K) to highly complex Olympiad-level challenges (Math Odyssey, AIME), highlight the algorithm's robustness and adaptability to varying degrees of problem complexity. The strong generalization capability shown on Math Odyssey, with minimal pre-training overlap, is particularly noteworthy.
- **Novel LLM-MCTS Integration:** The proposed dynamic pruning strategy and the refined Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) formula, tailored to the generative nature of LLMs, represent innovative methodological contributions that can inform future research at the intersection of search algorithms and large generative models.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Democratization of Advanced AI:** By allowing smaller, open-source models to perform at near-GPT-4 levels for specific reasoning tasks, MCTSr can reduce the computational and financial barriers to deploying powerful AI systems. This fosters broader participation in AI research and development and enables the creation of more efficient, specialized AI applications.
- **Revolutionizing AI for Education:** The algorithm's proven ability to tackle Olympiad-level mathematical problems makes it a prime candidate for applications in intelligent tutoring systems, automated problem generation, and personalized learning platforms, potentially transforming how students engage with complex subjects.
- **Advancing AGI Research:** Improving an AI's capacity for multi-step, verifiable reasoning is a crucial step towards Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). MCTSr's methodical approach to problem-solving, mimicking human trial-and-error and self-correction, contributes valuable insights into building more capable and robust intelligent systems.
- **Foundation for Broader Applications:** While demonstrated for mathematical reasoning, the authors highlight MCTSr's potential as a general decision-making framework. Its principles could be extended to other complex domains requiring strategic planning, logical deduction, and iterative refinement, such as black-box optimization problems, scientific discovery, or even self-driven alignment of LLMs, paving the way for more sophisticated AI-driven solutions across various industries.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
The research is still in its preliminary stages, suggesting that the full potential of MCTSr remains to be explored. Its applicability in broader contexts beyond mathematical reasoning, such as black-box optimization or self-driven LLM alignment, needs further investigation. Additionally, the modular nature of MCTSr implies that ongoing development is necessary to identify and compare a wider range of component algorithms (e.g., different self-refinement strategies, reward functions, or tree traversal methods) to maximize its practical potential and effectiveness across diverse problem settings.

### 6. Conclusion

This paper convincingly demonstrates the efficacy of the MCT Self-Refine (MCTSr) algorithm in significantly enhancing the mathematical reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). By innovatively integrating the systematic exploration of Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with the self-refinement and self-evaluation mechanisms of LLMs, MCTSr directly addresses critical challenges related to accuracy, reliability, and trustworthiness in complex reasoning tasks.

The experimental results, particularly on challenging Olympiad-level mathematical benchmarks such as Math Odyssey and GSM8K, show that MCTSr can elevate the performance of a relatively small, open-source model like LLaMa-3 8B to a level comparable to, and in some cases even surpassing, that of larger, closed-source state-of-the-art models like GPT-4. This achievement is a testament to the synergistic potential of combining structured search algorithms with generative models, enabling more robust and verifiable problem-solving.

This research not only advances the application of LLMs in sophisticated reasoning challenges but also lays a crucial groundwork for future innovations in AI. The ability to achieve high performance with more efficient models opens avenues for broader accessibility of advanced AI technologies, with potential impacts across educational technology, scientific research, and the pursuit of more generally intelligent AI systems. While acknowledged limitations indicate areas for future exploration, the MCTSr algorithm marks a significant step forward in developing intelligent agents capable of tackling complex, real-world problems with enhanced accuracy and reliability.
