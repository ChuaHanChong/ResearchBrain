# Succeed or Learn Slowly: Sample Efficient Off-Policy Reinforcement Learning for Mobile App Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Huawei Noah's Ark Lab and University College London introduced Succeed or Learn Slowly (SoLS) with Successful Transition Replay (STR), an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm for sample-efficient fine-tuning of foundation models in mobile app control. This framework achieved a 51.3% success rate on a challenging AndroidWorld benchmark, outperforming GPT-4o-based and other RL methods while demonstrating 5-60x faster inference times.

## Problem

- Applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to mobile app control is challenging due to sparse/delayed rewards, large action spaces, and computationally expensive simulations (4-5 seconds per step).
- Existing solutions are either costly (large language model prompting) or struggle with generalization to out-of-distribution tasks (Supervised Fine-Tuning).
- Standard policy gradient updates from negative advantage values can inadvertently degrade model performance by increasing probabilities for semantically related but inappropriate tokens.

## Method

- Developed **SoLS**, a novel off-policy actor-critic algorithm that employs an ==asymmetric policy update== mechanism, learning aggressively from positive advantages while conservatively handling negative ones.
- Introduced Successful Transition Replay (STR), an experience replay buffer that selectively stores and prioritizes successful timesteps to maximize learning efficiency from rare successful interactions.
- Fine-tuned a Llama-3-8B-Instruct model using LoRA adapters, with text-only UI tree descriptions and action history as input for reduced token count and faster inference.

## Results

- SoLS-STR achieved the highest success rate of **51.3%** on an 80-task subset of AndroidWorld, outperforming all GPT-4o-based methods (e.g., UGround-**7B** at **43.8%**) and other RL baselines (e.g., DigiRL-STR at **38.8%**).
- The framework demonstrated significantly faster inference times of approximately **0.9** seconds per step, making it 5 to 60 times quicker than GPT-4o-based approaches.
- Both SoLS's asymmetric policy updates and STR independently contributed to performance gains, with STR proving crucial for sample efficiency across various RL algorithms.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An asymmetric policy update, reinforcing successful behaviors directly and constraining updates for unsuccessful ones, prevents policy degradation while enhancing learning in sparse reward settings.
> - Prioritizing and replaying successful individual transitions significantly boosts sample efficiency, especially in environments with high simulation costs and sparse rewards.
> - Smaller, fine-tuned foundation models, when combined with specifically designed RL algorithms, can achieve superior performance and significantly higher efficiency compared to large, proprietary model prompting approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of automated systems capable of interacting with mobile applications is an area of growing research interest, driven by potential applications in accessibility, automated testing, and personal assistants. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for training agents in such interactive environments, allowing systems to learn through trial and error without extensive manual labeling.

However, applying RL to mobile app control presents several significant challenges. Mobile environments are characterized by sparse or delayed reward signals, large and context-dependent action spaces, and computationally expensive simulations, where each interaction step can take several seconds.

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) and foundation models have led to approaches that leverage their strong language understanding and visual context capabilities. Some methods involve prompt engineering with LLMs like GPT-4o to guide agents through tasks. While these approaches can yield agents capable of following instructions, they often require multiple API calls per step and extensive in-context reasoning, leading to high operational costs and slow inference times. Conversely, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) methods, which train smaller models on human demonstrations, tend to struggle with generalization to new and online tasks, particularly when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) environments.

This paper addresses the gap between these two extremes: the high performance but high cost of large model prompting, and the efficiency but limited generalization of fine-tuned models. It specifically focuses on improving the sample efficiency of RL fine-tuning for foundation models, aiming to achieve competitive performance while maintaining practical computational and inference speed requirements. The work positions itself within the context of multi-turn tasks where policy approximations are derived from foundation models, facing the specific challenges of sparse rewards and policy gradient updates that can degrade model performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel RL algorithm that enables sample-efficient fine-tuning of foundation models for mobile app control tasks, specifically in settings characterized by sparse rewards and high simulation costs. The authors identify two main limitations in current RL approaches for this domain:

1. **Sparse Reward Settings:** Mobile app environments frequently provide only binary, terminal rewards (i.e., success or failure at the end of a task). This scarcity of feedback makes it difficult for traditional RL algorithms to learn effective policies, as most interactions do not yield immediate reward signals.
2. **Impact of Policy Gradient Updates:** In actor-critic RL, negative advantage values (indicating undesirable actions) lead the policy to decrease the probabilities of those actions. When applied to models with large output vocabularies, this can inadvertently increase probabilities for semantically related but inappropriate tokens across the entire vocabulary, potentially disrupting the model's learned representations and leading to performance degradation or "forgetting" of previously acquired skills. Standard policy regularization techniques, often used in RL from Human Feedback (RLHF) to prevent "reward model hacking," may not be optimally suited for environments with structured action formats.

Based on these observations, the authors formulate a core insight: updates derived from positive samples (those leading to high returns) typically do not necessitate policy regularization, as they reinforce desired behaviors directly. In contrast, updates from negative samples (reflecting undesirable behavior) require careful handling, as aggressive reduction of their probabilities can be detrimental to overall model performance.

The motivation for this work is multifold:

- **Improve Sample Efficiency:** The high computational cost of running mobile app simulations (4-5 seconds per step) limits the number of online interactions an agent can experience. This necessitates algorithms that can extract maximum learning signal from a limited number of generated samples.
- **Prevent Model Degradation:** Mitigate the negative consequences of aggressive policy updates from undesirable actions, which can harm the generalization capabilities and overall stability of fine-tuned foundation models.
- **Bridge Performance and Efficiency Gap:** Create a learning system that can match or exceed the performance of costly, large-model prompting methods (e.g., GPT-4o-based agents) while maintaining the computational efficiency and faster inference times characteristic of fine-tuned, smaller foundation models.
- **Address Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Challenges:** Enable agents to generalize effectively to new applications and tasks that are OOD relative to the initial Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) dataset, a common challenge for SFT-only approaches.

To achieve these objectives, the paper proposes a dynamic policy update mechanism and a selective experience replay strategy, aiming to create a robust and efficient learning system for mobile app control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors formulate the mobile app control problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where the goal is to learn a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected episode-terminal reward. The training pipeline consists of two phases: initial Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using an offline dataset, followed by online Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. Observation and SFT Step

The agent uses Llama-3-8B-Instruct as its base model. The input to the model includes the textual task goal, a processed UI tree (converted into a list of available UI elements with descriptions and attributes), and a history of past actions. Critically, visual input (screenshots) is omitted to reduce token count and improve inference speed, based on prior work suggesting competitive performance with text-only inputs.

The first training step involves fine-tuning this base model through SFT on the AndroidControl dataset. This phase familiarizes the model with the environment's action and observation space and output format, providing an initial policy that can solve some tasks before RL fine-tuning begins. The AndroidControl dataset is considered out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to the AndroidWorld benchmark used for RL and evaluation, posing a generalization challenge.

#### 3.2. Successful Transition Replay (STR)

To address the high computational cost of trajectory generation in Android emulators (4-5 seconds per step) and the sparsity of successful interactions, the authors introduce Successful Transition Replay (STR).

- **Mechanism:** STR is an experience replay buffer that selectively stores individual successful timesteps from completed episodes. It uses a hash table to map each task to a list of its 50 most recent successful timesteps.
- **Integration:** During RL fine-tuning, a specified number of timesteps (e.g., 50) are sampled from STR for each task and combined with newly generated on-policy data (e.g., 100 transitions) to form the training dataset for each update. Each parallel training process maintains its own instance of STR.
- **Benefit:** By focusing learning on proven effective actions, STR aims to maximize learning efficiency, reduce the need for extensive (and often fruitless) online exploration, and bridge the distribution gap between the initial SFT policy and the target environment by bootstrapping from rare successes.

#### 3.3. Succeed or Learn Slowly (SoLS)

SoLS is a novel off-policy actor-critic RL algorithm designed to address the observed issues of standard policy gradient updates, particularly concerning negative advantage samples.

- **Off-Policy Actor-Critic Foundation:** SoLS is based on the off-policy actor-critic framework, where data is sampled from a behavioral policy $\pi_b$. It uses importance sampling to correct for the off-policy nature of the data and reduce variance. Monte Carlo returns ($R$) are used instead of value function estimates for the action-value function ($Q$) to reduce bias, especially during early training phases.
- **Asymmetric Policy Update Mechanism (Core Innovation):** SoLS modifies the policy gradient update based on the advantage value $A(s, a) = R - V_{\pi_\theta}(s)$:
 - **Positive Advantage ($A > 0$):** When an action leads to a positive advantage, the policy is updated aggressively, reinforcing the successful behavior directly.
 - **Negative Advantage ($A < 0$):** When an action results in a negative advantage, the policy update is constrained. Specifically, the gradient is set to zero if the advantage is negative *or* if the importance sampling ratio ($\pi_\theta(a|s) / \pi_b(a|s)$) falls outside a symmetric clipping range $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$. This mechanism is inspired by PPO-like clipping but explicitly designed to prevent degradation caused by aggressively decreasing probabilities of "poor" actions, which might undesirably increase other irrelevant tokens' probabilities.
- **Critic Training:** The value function $V_{\pi_\theta}(s)$ is estimated by an affine layer with a sigmoid activation atop the final hidden layer of the transformer. The critic is trained by minimizing the squared TD-loss using Monte Carlo targets, avoiding the need for a separate target network.
- **Joint Loss:** The overall loss function is a weighted sum of the actor loss ($L_{ac}$) and the critic loss ($L_{cr}$), with a hyperparameter $\lambda$ balancing their contributions (set to 0.5).

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Model and Optimization:** Llama-8B-Instruct is fine-tuned using LoRA adapters. The AdamW optimizer is used.
- **SFT Phase:** Three epochs on AndroidControl, learning rate $10^{-4}$ (linearly decays to 0), batch size 64. LoRA adapters with 64 dimensions, lora-$\alpha$ of 32, and dropout rate of 0.05.
- **RL Fine-tuning Phase:** 15,000 episodes (~200,000 transitions), 8 concurrent processes. Each process collects 100 on-policy transitions, which are combined with 50 transitions from STR for training. Mini-batch gradient updates with batch size 64. Clipping parameter $\epsilon=0.2$. Learning rate $10^{-5}$ (constant). One training epoch for all algorithms except PPO (two epochs).
- **Evaluation Benchmark:** AndroidWorld, specifically an 80-task subset excluding Q&A, verification, and free-form drawing tasks to align with the agent's capabilities and action space. This subset results in a harder overall difficulty distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SoLS was conducted on the AndroidWorld benchmark, comparing its performance against various prompting-based and fine-tuned methods, as well as other RL algorithms.

#### 4.1. Overall Success Rate

SoLS-STR achieved an overall success rate of 51.3% on the AndroidWorld benchmark (80-task subset), which represents the highest performance among all evaluated methods. This outcome suggests that the proposed asymmetric update mechanism combined with prioritized replay contributes to more effective policy learning.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Prompting and Fine-tuned Methods

- **GPT-4o-based methods:** Methods relying purely on GPT-4o prompting (SeeAct, T3A, M3A) achieved success rates ranging from 22.5% to 40.0%. Hybrid methods combining GPT-4o with fine-tuned grounding models (UGround-2B, UGround-7B, AriaUI) performed slightly better, ranging from 38.8% to 43.8%.
- **SoLS-STR's advantage:** SoLS-STR consistently outperformed all these GPT-4o-based and hybrid methods. For example, it showed a significant improvement over the best UGround variant (UGround-7B at 43.8%) and T3A (40.0%). GPT-4o methods tended to perform better on "easy" tasks but struggled more with "medium" and "hard" tasks compared to SoLS-STR, indicating SoLS-STR's capacity for in-domain specific knowledge acquisition.
- **SFT-only and OS-Atlas:** An SFT-only model achieved a low success rate of 22.1%, primarily solving easy tasks. OS-Atlas-Pro, a vision-language model with GUI grounding pre-training, achieved 23.8%. These results highlight the limitations of purely supervised or pre-trained approaches for complex, OOD mobile control.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Other RL Methods

- **Existing RL approaches:** Among other RL methods, DigiRL-STR achieved the highest success rate at 38.8%, followed by A2C-STR at 32.1% and PPO at 28.3%.
- **SoLS-STR's advantage:** SoLS-STR significantly outperformed all existing RL baselines. It demonstrated a 32.5% relative improvement over DigiRL-STR and an approximate 60% relative improvement over A2C-STR (51.3% vs 32.1%). This directly validates the core hypothesis regarding the efficacy of conservative updates for negative-advantage actions while learning aggressively from positive experiences.

#### 4.4. Effect of Successful Transition Replay (STR)

Experiments showed that integrating STR significantly improved the success rates for PPO, DigiRL, and SoLS. This indicates that prioritizing and replaying successful interactions is a crucial component for sample efficiency in environments with costly simulations. The study also noted that SoLS, even without STR (training purely on on-policy data), outperformed PPO and DigiRL, confirming the independent contribution of the asymmetric policy updates.

#### 4.5. Inference Time and Efficiency

SoLS-STR achieved an inference time of approximately 0.9 seconds per step, making it significantly faster than GPT-4o-based methods, which ranged from 5 to 60 times slower (e.g., UGround-7B requiring 40-60 seconds per step). This efficiency gain is attributed to using a smaller, fine-tuned Llama-3-8B model and omitting visual inputs, positioning SoLS-STR favorably in the trade-off between success rate and computational cost.

#### 4.6. Training Evolution

Analysis of the success rate across task categories throughout training revealed that SoLS generally improved performance from early to late training phases. Categories where the initial SFT model already performed well (e.g., Audio, System) showed marginal improvements, while categories with significant OOD challenges (e.g., Files, Maps, Markor) demonstrated substantial increases in success rate, underscoring the importance of RL fine-tuning and the effectiveness of STR.

#### 4.7. Failure Case Analysis

Despite its strong performance, SoLS consistently failed on tasks belonging to four categories:
- **Memory-dependent tasks:** Requiring the agent to recall information from previous observations (e.g., numbers or text), which is not fully supported by the current action history input due to context length restrictions.
- **Visual input-dependent tasks:** Tasks where textual UI tree descriptions are inadequate for understanding critical visual information (e.g., maze layouts) or tasks requiring information extraction directly from images.
- **Unseen interactions:** Tasks involving device features or actions rarely encountered in the SFT training data (e.g., clipboard functionality, certain long-press actions).
- **Long-horizon tasks:** Tasks requiring a very long sequence of actions, where the probability of error accumulates and sparse rewards make learning challenging. Many difficult tasks combine multiple of these failure modes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Succeed or Learn Slowly (SoLS) as a significant step towards developing practical and efficient mobile app control agents.

**Practicality and Efficiency:** The work demonstrates that fine-tuned smaller language models, when combined with specifically designed RL algorithms, can achieve higher success rates than approaches relying on large, proprietary foundation models (like GPT-4o) while simultaneously requiring substantially fewer computational resources and offering significantly faster inference times (5-60x faster). This efficiency makes SoLS-STR a potentially practical solution for real-world deployment where response time and operational costs are critical considerations.

**Addressing Core RL Challenges:** SoLS directly addresses two fundamental challenges in applying RL to complex, open-world environments: sparse rewards and the potential for policy degradation during updates. The asymmetric policy update mechanism, which aggressively learns from successes and conservatively handles failures, combined with Successful Transition Replay (STR), effectively extracts maximum learning value from limited and costly interactions. STR's ability to selectively store and replay successful transitions mitigates the high simulation costs and sparse reward signals, enabling more robust learning.

**Methodological Contributions:** The introduction of SoLS and STR as novel components offers new strategies for fine-tuning foundation models in interactive environments. The findings regarding the benefits of asymmetric policy updates (SoLS) and prioritized successful experience replay (STR) provide insights for future RL algorithm design, particularly for multi-turn tasks with complex action spaces and sparse feedback.

**Narrowing the Performance-Efficiency Gap:** This research contributes to narrowing the gap between the high performance but high cost of large model prompting and the efficiency but often lower performance of fine-tuned models. It suggests that specialized RL techniques can unlock the potential of smaller foundation models to excel in specific, challenging domains.

**Future Research Directions:** The identified failure modes (memory, visual input, unseen interactions, long-horizon tasks) provide clear avenues for future work, such as integrating state summarization or external memory modules, exploring Vision-Language Models (VLMs) or OCR for richer visual understanding, expanding SFT data diversity, and investigating shaped rewards or more advanced exploration strategies.

**Societal Implications:** The authors acknowledge the broader societal implications of advanced mobile app control agents, highlighting potential risks related to user privacy, digital surveillance, and security vulnerabilities. This recognition underscores the importance of ethical considerations in the ongoing development and deployment of such autonomous systems.
