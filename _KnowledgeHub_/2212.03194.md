# DiffTune$^+$: Hyperparameter-Free Auto-Tuning using Auto-Differentiation

> [!summary] Summary
> DiffTune+ introduces hyperparameter-free optimization methods to enhance the efficiency and autonomy of controller parameter tuning within the DiffTune framework. The work develops a Line Search method that derives an optimal learning rate and a Gauss-Newton-like method for optimal parameter updates, demonstrating improved convergence and robustness over hyperparameter-dependent alternatives in robotic simulations.

## Problem

- Traditional controller tuning is a manual, iterative, and time-consuming process, often requiring expert experience or specific methods like Ziegler-Nichols.
- Existing auto-tune methods, including the predecessor DiffTune, rely on hyperparameters (e.g., learning rates for gradient descent) that require additional manual tuning, hindering full automation.
- Model-based auto-tune methods can be limited by model inaccuracies in real-world environments, while model-free methods can be computationally inefficient or lack stability guarantees.

## Method

- **DiffTune+** leverages the ==analytical gradients== provided by DiffTune's ==sensitivity propagation== to derive hyperparameter-free optimization methods for controller parameters.
- The approach approximates the closed-loop state and control actions using ==first-order Taylor expansions== to predict the change in the loss function when controller parameters are perturbed.
- Two primary hyperparameter-free methods are proposed: an optimal parameter update rule resembling ==Gauss-Newton (GN)== and an optimal learning rate via ==line search (LS)== for standard gradient descent direction.

## Results

- The **Line Search (LS)** method demonstrated superior loss reduction and convergence speed compared to hyperparameter-dependent first-order methods (GD, GDM) across Dubin's car and quadrotor simulations.
- The **Gauss-Newton (GN)** method achieved the minimum loss in quadrotor tasks but was associated with excessively large tuned gains; it also exhibited the minimum variance in loss for noisy systems.
- BFGS consistently terminated prematurely in quadrotor simulations due to negative curvature, and Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) performed worse than GN, highlighting sensitivity to its damping coefficient.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - First-order approximations of state and control sensitivities can effectively predict loss function changes, enabling the derivation of optimal, hyperparameter-free update rules.
> - The proposed Line Search (LS) method offers a robust and efficient hyperparameter-free alternative, outperforming traditional gradient descent methods in convergence speed and final loss.
> - While the Gauss-Newton (GN) method can achieve the lowest loss, it risks producing overly large control gains, potentially compromising system robustness in real-world applications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Controller design and tuning are fundamental processes in applying control techniques to dynamic systems. While controller design focuses on stability, parameter tuning aims to achieve desired performance in practical applications. Traditionally, controller tuning has been a manual, iterative process, often relying on expert experience or specific methods like Ziegler-Nichols for PID controllers. This manual approach is time-consuming, requires specialized personnel, and can be inefficient, particularly for complex systems with large parameter spaces or long operational loop times.

The need for improved efficiency and performance has driven the development of automatic tuning (auto-tune) methods. These methods integrate system knowledge, expert experience, and computational tools to identify optimal controller parameters. Existing auto-tune approaches are broadly categorized into model-based and model-free methods.

Model-based auto-tune methods leverage knowledge of the system's mathematical model to infer parameter choices, often employing gradient descent techniques. These methods can provide stability guarantees by incorporating system dynamics explicitly. However, their effectiveness can be limited by inaccuracies in the system model when applied to real-world environments. Examples include LQR tuning and gradient-based PID tuning.

Model-free auto-tune methods, conversely, do not rely on an explicit system model. They typically use approximate gradients or surrogate models to guide parameter updates, making them compatible with real-world data where models might be unavailable or imperfect. Approaches such as Markov chain Monte Carlo, Gaussian processes (GPs), and deep neural networks (DNNs) fall into this category. While adaptable to real data, model-free methods can be computationally inefficient in high-dimensional parameter spaces (e.g., GPs) and face challenges in establishing formal stability guarantees, often relying on empirical validation.

**DiffTune** (Cheng et al., 2022a), the predecessor to the work presented in this paper, was proposed as a model-based auto-tune method using auto-differentiation (AD). DiffTune addresses some limitations of prior methods by unrolling the dynamical system and controller into a computational graph, enabling the use of analytical gradients for parameter updates. Its key features include:
- **Stability:** Inherited from the underlying controller design with stability guarantees.
- **Real-data compatibility:** Achieved through "sensitivity propagation," which allows for incorporating measured or estimated states from real systems, bypassing the limitations of standard forward- or reverse-mode AD when the computational graph is "broken" by real-world data.
- **Efficiency:** Sensitivity propagation runs forward in time, in parallel with system evolution.
- **General applicability:** Can tune parameters for any differentiable system dynamics and controller, including those derived from optimization problems via implicit differentiation.

Despite these advantages, the original DiffTune utilized vanilla gradient descent for iterative parameter updates. A recognized limitation of vanilla gradient descent, and indeed many first-order optimization methods, is its reliance on a learning rate – a hyperparameter that itself requires careful tuning. The efficiency and convergence speed of the tuning process in DiffTune were significantly dependent on the selection of this learning rate.

This paper, **DiffTune+**, directly addresses this limitation. It aims to improve the efficiency and robustness of DiffTune by developing hyperparameter-free methods for updating controller parameters. This work integrates DiffTune's analytical gradient capabilities with advanced optimization strategies, positioning it as an evolution towards more autonomous and efficient controller tuning, reducing the need for manual intervention in the optimization process itself. The broader research landscape also includes advancements in learning for control, recurrent neural networks (due to their recurrent structure mirroring dynamical systems), and second-order optimization methods, all of which inform the choices and comparisons made in this work.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the tuning efficiency of the DiffTune framework by introducing hyperparameter-free parameter update methods. The motivation stems directly from a key limitation of the original DiffTune: its reliance on vanilla gradient descent, where the learning rate (a hyperparameter) significantly impacts convergence speed and overall tuning performance. This dependence necessitates additional manual tuning of the learning rate, counteracting the goal of fully automatic tuning.

Specifically, the paper seeks to:
1. **Develop a theoretical understanding:** Provide deeper insights into DiffTune by analyzing the first-order approximation of the predicted closed-loop state trajectory using sensitivity. This involves understanding how controller parameter changes affect the system state and control actions.
2. **Predict loss change:** Utilize the first-order state and control approximations to predict the change in the loss function when controller parameters are perturbed. This predicted loss forms the basis for deriving optimal parameter updates.
3. **Derive optimal parameter updates:** Based on the predicted loss, derive explicit formulas for optimal parameter updates (or optimal learning rates) that minimize the predicted loss reduction in each iteration. These derived methods are designed to be "hyperparameter-free," meaning they do not require external tuning of optimization parameters like learning rates.
4. **Propose and evaluate hyperparameter-free alternatives:** Introduce and implement two specific hyperparameter-free methods: one based on minimizing the predicted loss reduction by finding an optimal parameter update direction and magnitude, and another by finding an optimal learning rate via line search.
5. **Compare performance and robustness:** Systematically compare the proposed hyperparameter-free methods against traditional hyperparameter-based gradient descent variants (vanilla GD, GD with momentum, Levenberg-Marquardt) and another second-order hyperparameter-free method (BFGS) in simulation environments. This comparison aims to assess their respective tuning efficiency, convergence properties, and robustness.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to make DiffTune more autonomous, reducing the manual effort associated with the tuning process and potentially improving its applicability in real-world control systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of DiffTune+ builds upon the established DiffTune framework and introduces new methods for optimizing controller parameters without requiring the manual tuning of learning rates.

**Background of DiffTune:**
The original DiffTune method addresses controller tuning for discrete-time dynamical systems described by $x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k)$, where $x_k$ is the state and $u_k$ is the control input. The control input is generated by a feedback controller $u_k = h(x_k, \hat{x}_k, \theta)$, with $\theta$ representing the controller parameters. The objective is to minimize a loss function $L(\cdot)$ that evaluates performance over a time interval.
DiffTune computes the gradient of this loss with respect to $\theta$, $\nabla_{\theta}L$, by unrolling the system dynamics and controller into a computational graph. A key component is "sensitivity propagation" (Equations 4a and 4b), which calculates the sensitivities $\partial x_k / \partial \theta$ and $\partial u_k / \partial \theta$ forward in time, in parallel with the system's evolution. This propagation is crucial for real-data compatibility as it allows incorporating measured or estimated states $x_k$ directly, rather than relying solely on model evaluations. The overall gradient $\nabla_{\theta}L$ is then obtained via the chain rule (Equation 5). The original DiffTune used projected vanilla gradient descent: $\theta \leftarrow P_{\Theta}(\theta - \alpha \nabla_{\theta}L)$, where $\alpha$ is the learning rate and $P_{\Theta}$ is a projection operator to ensure stability.

**Hyperparameter-Free Optimization in DiffTune+:**
The core improvement in DiffTune+ lies in replacing the hyperparameter-dependent update rule with hyperparameter-free alternatives. The general idea is to determine an optimal parameter update $\epsilon$ (where $\theta \leftarrow P_{\Theta}(\theta + \epsilon)$) that maximizes the predicted loss reduction in each iteration.

1. **First-Order Approximation of State and Control:**
 The method begins by approximating the closed-loop state trajectory and control actions when the controller parameters are perturbed by a small $\epsilon$. Using Taylor expansion, the perturbed state $x_k(\epsilon)$ and control $u_k(\epsilon)$ are approximated as:
 $\tilde{x}_k(\epsilon) = x_k(0) + \frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta} \epsilon$
 $\tilde{u}_k(\epsilon) = u_k(0) + \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial \theta} \epsilon$
 where $x_k(0)$ and $u_k(0)$ are the current state and control, and $\frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta}$ and $\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial \theta}$ are the sensitivities obtained from the DiffTune sensitivity propagation.

2. **Predicted Loss Function:**
 Assuming a quadratic loss function, $L(\theta) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \|x_k - \hat{x}_k\|^2 + \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \lambda\|u_j\|^2$, the predicted loss function $\tilde{L}(\theta + \epsilon)$ is formulated by substituting the first-order approximations $\tilde{x}_k(\epsilon)$ and $\tilde{u}_k(\epsilon)$:
 $\tilde{L}(\theta + \epsilon) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \|\tilde{x}_k(\epsilon) - \hat{x}_k\|^2 + \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \lambda\|\tilde{u}_j(\epsilon)\|^2$
 This expanded form of $\tilde{L}(\theta + \epsilon) - L(\theta)$ is a quadratic function of $\epsilon$.

3. **Optimal Parameter Update ($\epsilon^*$):**
 To maximize the loss reduction (i.e., minimize $\tilde{L}(\theta + \epsilon) - L(\theta)$), the derivative of this difference with respect to $\epsilon$ is set to zero (Equation 8). Solving for $\epsilon$ yields the optimal parameter update $\epsilon^*$:
 $\epsilon^* = - \frac{1}{2} \left(\sum_{k=0}^{N} \left(\frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta}\right)^\top \frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta} + \lambda \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \left(\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial \theta}\right)^\top \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial \theta}\right)^{-1} \nabla_{\theta}L$ (Equation 9)
 This update rule resembles the Gauss-Newton method, where the inverse of the Jacobian product acts as an approximation of the inverse Hessian matrix, scaling the gradient $\nabla_{\theta}L$.

4. **Optimal Learning Rate via Line Search ($\alpha^*$):**
 Alternatively, a line search approach can be used to determine an optimal learning rate $\alpha^*$ for the standard gradient descent direction $\nabla_{\theta}L$. By minimizing $\tilde{L}(\theta - \alpha \nabla_{\theta}L) - L(\theta)$ with respect to $\alpha$, an explicit expression for $\alpha^*$ is derived:
 $\alpha^* = \frac{(\nabla_{\theta}L)^\top \nabla_{\theta}L}{2 \left( \sum_{k=0}^{N} \|\frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta} \nabla_{\theta}L\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \|\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial \theta} \nabla_{\theta}L\|^2 \right)}$ (Equation 10)
 This method is referred to as "Line Search (LS)" in the paper.

**Comparison Methods:**
The proposed LS method and the Gauss-Newton (GN) method (derived from $\epsilon^*$) are compared against several other optimization algorithms:
- **Vanilla Gradient Descent (GD):** A baseline first-order method requiring a tuned learning rate.
- **Gradient Descent with Polyak's Momentum (GDM):** An accelerated first-order method, also requiring tuned hyperparameters (learning rate and momentum coefficient).
- **Levenberg-Marquardt (LM):** A second-order method that introduces a damping term ($\mu I$) to the Jacobian product in the Gauss-Newton update, which itself is a hyperparameter requiring tuning.
- **Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS):** A quasi-Newton second-order method that approximates the inverse Hessian. It typically requires line search for step size, for which the derived $\alpha^*$ (Equation 10) is used.

**Simulation Environments and Evaluation:**
The methods are evaluated in simulations using two robotic models:
1. **Dubin's car model:** A planar vehicle model, tuned for circular trajectory tracking.
2. **Quadrotor model:** A 3D aerial vehicle model with geometric control, tuned for both 3D circular and 3D figure-8 trajectories. The figure-8 trajectory represents more dynamically aggressive conditions.

For both models, the loss function is the root-mean-square error (RMSE) in position tracking. Experiments are conducted under two conditions:
- **Noise-free system:** Ideal simulation environment.
- **Noisy system with Extended Kalman Filter (EKF):** Simulates sensor noise and the use of an EKF for state estimation, reflecting real-world conditions. This setup uses Monte Carlo trials (100 trials) to assess robustness.

The partial derivatives required for sensitivity propagation (∇f, ∇h) are automatically generated using CasADi. The authors also provide an open-source toolset, DiffTuneOpenSource, to facilitate the application of DiffTune for various systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation experiments compared the proposed hyperparameter-free methods (Line Search (LS), Gauss-Newton (GN)) with hyperparameter-based methods (Gradient Descent (GD), Gradient Descent with Momentum (GDM), Levenberg-Marquardt (LM)) and another hyperparameter-free method (BFGS).

#### 4.1. Dubin's Car Model

- **First-Order Methods:** The proposed LS method demonstrated superior performance among the first-order methods (GD, GDM, LS) in terms of loss reduction.
- **Second-Order Methods:** BFGS exhibited performance comparable to that of LS, sometimes achieving termination in fewer iterations, indicating benefits from Hessian approximation. GN showed performance similar to GDM. LM's performance was inferior to GN, which the authors attributed to the chosen damping coefficient. Further testing with different damping coefficients for LM reportedly led to worse performance.

#### 4.2. Quadrotor Model (3D Circle and 3D Figure 8 Trajectories)

- **Convergence:** All methods achieved loss reduction and exhibited convergence, with the difference in loss between consecutive iterations falling below 7e-3 by the end of 100 iterations.
- **First-Order Methods:** LS consistently showed faster loss reduction and converged to smaller final losses compared to GD and GDM. The performance of GD and GDM was noted to be dependent on their chosen hyperparameters (learning rate, momentum coefficient).
- **Second-Order Methods:**
 - **Gauss-Newton (GN):** Achieved the minimum loss among all compared methods. However, this minimum loss was associated with "overly large" tuned gains (as reported in Table A.1 and A.2 in the Appendix), which the authors suggest could compromise robustness in real systems, especially with delays. Despite this, GN exhibited the minimum variance in loss for noisy systems.
 - **Levenberg-Marquardt (LM):** Performed slightly worse than GN, which was attributed to the influence of its damping term.
 - **BFGS:** This method consistently terminated prematurely (in less than three iterations) due to the curvature turning negative. This issue prevented it from providing meaningful parameter updates or performance improvements in the quadrotor simulations.
- **Impact of Noise:** Comparison between noise-free and noisy systems (with EKF for state estimation) revealed that the overall trend of loss reduction remained largely consistent. This indicates that the presence of sensor noise, when processed by an EKF, did not significantly impact the effectiveness of the tuning process for these methods.

#### 4.3. Discussion and Overall Assessment

The findings generally favor the Line Search (LS) method as the most suitable choice for DiffTune+ based on two primary considerations:
1. **Hyperparameter-Free and Robustness:** LS is hyperparameter-free, simplifying its implementation compared to methods like GD, GDM, and LM, which require careful tuning of learning rates or damping coefficients. Furthermore, LS demonstrated greater robustness compared to other hyperparameter-free methods. GN, while achieving the lowest loss, led to excessively large control gains that could negatively affect system robustness. BFGS faced issues with negative curvature, leading to early termination and limited applicability.
2. **Performance Superiority:** In most scenarios, LS outperformed hyperparameter-based methods like GD, GDM, and LM in terms of loss reduction and convergence efficiency. This superiority, combined with its hyperparameter-free nature, significantly reduces the workload for the user, as they do not need to tune the optimization algorithm's parameters in addition to the controller's gains. The effectiveness of LM was particularly sensitive to the proper tuning of its damping term.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of automated control system tuning by enhancing the DiffTune framework with hyperparameter-free optimization methods. The significance and potential impact of DiffTune+ are multifaceted:

1. **Improved Efficiency and Accessibility of Auto-Tuning:** By eliminating the need to tune optimization hyperparameters (like the learning rate), DiffTune+ streamlines the auto-tuning process. This directly reduces the effort and specialized knowledge required from an operator, making advanced controller tuning more accessible to a broader range of engineers and researchers. This is particularly impactful for complex systems where manual tuning of optimization parameters would add a significant computational and cognitive burden.

2. **Enhanced Robustness and Practicality:** The proposed Line Search method, identified as the most robust performer among the hyperparameter-free options, offers a practical approach for controller tuning. Unlike Gauss-Newton, which could lead to overly aggressive controller gains, or BFGS, which encountered stability issues with negative curvature, LS provided consistent performance without these drawbacks. This robustness is crucial for deploying auto-tuning methods in real-world applications where system safety and stability are paramount. The finding that tuning performance is largely unaffected by sensor noise (when an EKF is used) further underscores its practical viability for real systems with inherent measurement uncertainties.

3. **Advancement of Model-Based Auto-Tuning:** DiffTune+ builds upon the strengths of the original DiffTune – its analytical gradient computation via sensitivity propagation, real-data compatibility, and inherent stability guarantees from well-designed controllers. By improving the optimization step, this work strengthens the overall utility of model-based auto-tuning, offering a robust and efficient alternative to model-free and traditional manual tuning approaches.

4. **Broader Applicability in Robotics and Control:** The simulation results on a Dubin's car and a quadrotor demonstrate the method's applicability to common robotic systems. The general framework, being applicable to any differentiable system dynamics and controller, suggests a wide potential impact across various domains requiring precise and adaptive control, from industrial automation and autonomous vehicles to aerospace systems.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies areas for future work, specifically improving second-order methods within the DiffTune+ framework. This indicates that the insights gained from this research, particularly regarding the challenges with GN's aggressiveness and BFGS's curvature issues, can serve as a valuable foundation for developing even more sophisticated and robust hyperparameter-free auto-tuning algorithms.

In summary, DiffTune+ represents a significant step towards truly autonomous and efficient controller tuning. By mitigating the hyperparameter tuning burden, it enhances the practicality, robustness, and accessibility of gradient-based auto-tuning for a wide array of dynamic systems.
