# IntervenGen: Interventional Data Generation for Robust and Data-Efficient Robot Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A new data generation system, IntervenGen, autonomously creates diverse corrective interventions for robot imitation learning from a small set of human examples. This method improves policy robustness to perception errors, enabling up to a 39x increase in task success rates and successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with minimal human input.

## Problem

- Robot imitation learning policies exhibit brittleness and poor generalization due to distribution shift, particularly from perception errors in real-world environments.
- Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL), while effective for robustness, demands extensive and continuous human supervision to provide corrective interventions.
- Generating a sufficiently diverse dataset of recovery behaviors to cover the wide spectrum of potential policy failures with human effort alone is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.

## Method

- Introduce **IntervenGen** (I-Gen), a system that autonomously generates diverse corrective intervention trajectories from a small seed of human-provided interventions.
- Employ closed-loop policy execution to actively generate genuine policy mistakes under varied, corrupted environmental observations.
- Adapt and replay source human recovery segments via object-centric transformations to guide the robot from generated mistake states back to task completion.

## Results

- Policies trained with IntervenGen achieved up to a **39x** increase in success rate over base policies across various tasks, utilizing only 10 initial human interventions.
- IntervenGen consistently outperformed policies trained on either equivalent amounts of full human demonstrations or MimicGen-generated full demonstrations, by **34%** to **68%**.
- Demonstrated successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for a block grasping task on a physical Franka robot, achieving **90%** success for robust policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Automating the generation of corrective interventional data is more impactful for policy robustness to distribution shift than simply expanding datasets of successful task demonstrations.
> - The use of closed-loop policy execution to induce and identify genuine policy mistakes in varied, corrupted environments is essential for producing relevant and effective recovery data.
> - Achieving robustness to simulated perception errors through intervention data allows for successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for state-based policies, mitigating reliance on perfect real-world sensing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in robot imitation learning (IL): the inherent brittleness of policies due to **distribution shift**. When a robot encounters conditions during deployment that differ from its training data, its performance can degrade significantly. This problem is particularly acute in real-world scenarios where perception errors (e.g., inaccurate object pose estimation) are common.

The existing research landscape for tackling distribution shift in IL can be broadly categorized:

- **Large-scale Offline Data Collection:** Efforts like collecting thousands of demonstrations via human teleoperation aim to cover diverse conditions. However, this approach is extremely expensive, time-consuming, and resource-intensive, requiring hundreds of human operator hours.
- **Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL) / DAgger Variants:** Methods like DAgger involve human operators providing "corrective interventions" during policy rollouts. This allows the robot to learn recovery behaviors from its own mistakes, theoretically and practically improving robustness. While effective, IIL imposes a heavy burden on human supervisors who must continuously monitor and intervene, often over multiple rounds of data collection and training. Crucially, collecting enough *diverse* interventions to cover the vast space of potential policy mistakes remains a significant challenge.
- **Other Robustness Techniques:** These include injecting noise during data collection, designing experts in simulation, using planners to return to seen states, and domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer. While valuable, many of these either still require significant data, engineering effort, or may not directly address the challenge of generating targeted *recovery* data for specific policy failures.

IntervenGen carves out a unique niche by directly addressing the **data burden of interactive imitation learning**. It recognizes that while human interventions are powerful for teaching robustness, relying solely on human operators to cover every possible mistake is impractical. The paper builds upon the core insight of **MimicGen**, a previous system from some of the same authors, which demonstrated that large datasets of *full task demonstrations* could be synthetically generated from a small number of human demonstrations by adapting trajectories based on object poses.

IntervenGen extends this concept to *interventional data*. Instead of generating full successful trajectories, it focuses on autonomously generating *corrective intervention segments*. This is a crucial distinction because policy mistakes often lead to states far from the training distribution, requiring specialized recovery behaviors that are rarely present in successful full demonstrations. The key differentiators from MimicGen are:
1. Generating *interventional* data rather than full demonstrations.
2. Relaxing the assumption of precise object pose knowledge, which is critical in real-world deployments.
3. Integrating **closed-loop policy execution** for mistake generation, allowing the robot's *current policy* to genuinely make mistakes in new configurations, leading to more relevant recovery data.
4. Allowing variation not just in object poses but also in the robot's *erroneous beliefs* about these poses.

By tackling the scalability of interactive IL and the specificity of recovery data generation, IntervenGen significantly pushes the boundary towards more data-efficient, robust, and deployable robot learning policies in environments characterized by uncertainty and perception challenges. It offers a method to automate a labor-intensive part of the training pipeline, making advanced robot skills more attainable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a system that can effectively increase the robustness and data-efficiency of robot control policies trained via imitation learning. This is driven by several key motivations stemming from the limitations of current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Addressing Distribution Shift and Brittleness:** A primary motivation is the inherent fragility of imitation learning policies when faced with conditions that deviate from their training data, a phenomenon known as distribution shift. This is a pervasive issue in real-world robotics, where factors like sensor noise, occlusion, network delay, or model inaccuracies can lead to erroneous object pose estimations. Such errors cause the robot to venture into unfamiliar states, often resulting in task failure. The paper specifically highlights the need to create policies robust to perception errors.

2. **Reducing Human Intervention Burden in Interactive IL:** While interactive imitation learning (e.g., DAgger) has shown promise in improving policy robustness by incorporating human-provided corrective interventions, it places a substantial and continuous burden on human operators. They must actively monitor robot execution and intervene whenever a mistake occurs. The paper poses a fundamental question: "do we actually need to have a human operator collect corrections every single time a policy makes a mistake?" This question directly motivates the search for automated solutions to scale intervention data collection.

3. **Generating Diverse Recovery Behaviors:** Policies need to learn how to recover from a wide array of potential mistakes. Manually collecting sufficient human interventions to adequately cover the broad distribution of possible policy errors is prohibitively time-consuming and effort-intensive. The objective is to find a way to autonomously generate a large and diverse set of recovery interventions.

4. **Leveraging Prior Insights for Scalability:** Inspired by the success of MimicGen in generating full task demonstrations from a small human dataset, the authors aim to apply a similar data generation paradigm to the more specialized domain of *interventional data*. The goal is to adapt the concept of object-centric behavior transformation to synthesize corrective actions rather than complete successful tasks.

In summary, the key objectives are:
- To propose **IntervenGen (I-Gen)**, a novel data generation system that can autonomously produce a large, diverse set of corrective interventions.
- To achieve this from **only a small number of initial human interventions**, thereby dramatically reducing human operator burden.
- To use this synthetically generated data to train robot policies that are **significantly more robust** to real-world challenges, particularly object pose estimation errors (sensor noise, geometry error).
- To explore the system's utility in various applications, including improving task success rates, enhancing perception robustness, and facilitating **sim-to-real transfer** through a form of data-driven domain randomization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

IntervenGen (I-Gen) is a novel data generation system designed to autonomously create a large and diverse dataset of corrective interventions from a minimal set of human-provided examples. The core idea is to transform and replay human recovery behaviors in varied simulated environments where the robot policy is actively making mistakes.

The methodology is encapsulated in Algorithm 1, outlining an iterative process:

1. **Initialize and Train Base Policy ($\pi_\theta$):**
 The process begins by training an initial robot policy $\pi_\theta$ using a base dataset of demonstrations (e.g., a small set of human demonstrations and/or MimicGen-generated demonstrations). This policy serves as the starting point for robot execution during data collection and generation.

2. **Human Interventional Data Collection ($m$ episodes):**
 In an initial phase, the trained policy $\pi_\theta$ is deployed, and a human operator monitors its execution. When the robot policy makes a mistake (i.e., enters a state outside the desired distribution or fails to progress), the human intervenes. The human takes control, demonstrates a *recovery behavior*, and guides the robot back to a successful path or completes the task. These human-gated interventions are recorded as short "mistake" followed by "recovery" segments and aggregated into a small source dataset of human interventions ($D_{src}$). This is the *only* part of the process that requires significant human-in-the-loop effort after initial policy training.

3. **Synthetic Interventional Data Generation ($n$ trajectories):**
 This is the core innovation of I-Gen, where a large number of synthetic intervention trajectories are generated autonomously from the small $D_{src}$. For each synthetic trajectory:
 - **Sample New Task and Error:** A new initial state is sampled, including a new scene configuration (e.g., object initial pose) and a novel **observation corruption** (e.g., a different magnitude or direction of sensor noise applied to object pose observations). This ensures diversity in the *conditions leading to a mistake*.
 - **Mistake Generation (Closed-Loop Policy Execution):** Unlike MimicGen, which primarily replays full demonstrations open-loop, I-Gen executes the *current robot policy* ($\pi_\theta$) in this newly sampled, corrupted environment. This is crucial because it allows the system to generate *genuine mistakes* that the actual policy would make under the specific new conditions. The policy execution continues until a mistake is detected (e.g., via contact detection, a learned classifier, or other robot-gated criteria). This step generates diverse "mistake states" where recovery is needed.
 - **Sample Source Intervention:** A human recovery segment (a sequence of end-effector poses) is randomly selected from the small $D_{src}$ collected earlier.
 - **Recovery Generation (Open-Loop Trajectory Replay):** The selected human recovery segment is adapted to the current mistake state. This adaptation involves:
 - **Trajectory Transformation:** The poses in the source human recovery segment are transformed using an SE(3) transform. This transform ensures that the *relative poses* between the robot's end-effector and the relevant object frame are preserved from the source demonstration to the current (potentially erroneous) object pose in the new scene. This is similar to the object-centric adaptation in MimicGen.
 - **Interpolation:** A linear interpolation segment is added to smoothly transition the robot's end-effector from its current mistake configuration to the starting pose of the transformed recovery segment.
 - **Execution:** The combined interpolation and transformed recovery segment is then executed by the robot's end-effector controller in an open-loop manner.

4. **Output Filtering and Dataset Aggregation:**
 - **Success Filtering:** After executing the synthetic recovery trajectory, the system checks if the task was successfully completed. Only successfully completed synthetic trajectories are retained. This prevents the generation of "bad" recovery data.
 - **Intervention Filtering:** Similar to interactive IL algorithms like DAgger, the initial "mistake" segment (the portion where $\pi_\theta$ was executing poorly) is filtered out. Only the "recovery" segment, corresponding to the expert's corrective action (or its synthetic counterpart), is added to the aggregated dataset $D$. This ensures the robot learns from corrections, not from its own mistakes.
 - **Retraining:** The new, expanded dataset $D$ (containing original base data, human interventions, and synthetic interventions) is then used to retrain a new policy $\pi_\theta$. This iterative process of data generation and policy improvement can be repeated for multiple rounds.

**Assumptions:** I-Gen relies on several assumptions, some inherited from MimicGen:
- The action space consists of delta-pose commands in Cartesian end-effector space.
- The task can be broken down into a known sequence of object-centric subtasks.
- Object poses are observable at the beginning of each subtask *during data collection* (to enable trajectory transformation), but not necessarily during policy deployment.
- Crucially, demonstrated recovery behavior can be explained by *some* component of the robot's observations during human intervention, even in the presence of observation corruption. This could be coarse signals like force-torque sensing, tactile feedback, or implicit cues like a closed gripper indicating a missed grasp.

**Experimental Setup:** The methodology was validated across four simulated environments (Nut Insertion, 2-Piece Assembly, Coffee, Nut-and-Peg Assembly) and one physical environment (Block Grasp). The sources of observation error included sensor noise and object geometry error. A BC-RNN policy was used as the learning algorithm. Critical baselines included deploying the base policy, using raw human interventions, MimicGen-generated full demonstrations, and an ablation of I-Gen without closed-loop policy execution for mistake generation. The effectiveness was also evaluated for sim-to-real transfer and under varying degrees of observability during recovery.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments presented in the paper demonstrate that IntervenGen (I-Gen) significantly enhances the robustness and data-efficiency of robot imitation learning policies. The key findings are summarized below:

- **Vastly Improved Policy Robustness with Minimal Human Input:**
 - I-Gen substantially boosts policy performance, achieving up to a **39x increase** in success rate over the base policy in tasks like Coffee, with similar significant improvements (3.5x, 10.7x) in Nut Insertion and 2-Piece Assembly (Table I). This remarkable gain is achieved with the collection of **only 10 human interventions**.

- **Superiority over Alternative Uses of Human Data Budget:**
 - **Outperforms Naive Human Demonstrations:** I-Gen consistently surpasses policies trained on an equivalent amount (10 episodes) of *full human task demonstrations* (Source Demo) by 56%-68%. This highlights that successful full demonstrations often lack the specific recovery behaviors needed for distribution shift.
 - **Outperforms MimicGen-Generated Full Demonstrations:** Even when 10 human demonstrations are expanded to 1000 synthetic full demonstrations using MimicGen (MG Demo), I-Gen still outperforms these policies by 34%-62%. This confirms that generating *interventional recovery data* is more effective for robustness than simply generating more successful task completions.
 - **Outperforms Raw Human Interventions:** Policies trained on only the 10 source human interventions (Source Int) perform poorly compared to I-Gen (58%-70% lower success), indicating that a small number of interventions are insufficient for broad coverage. Even weighting these interventions higher (Weighted Src Int) does not close the gap (48%-74% lower success), suggesting the need for *diversity* in mistakes and recoveries.

- **Crucial Role of Novel Components:**
 - The "Policy Execution" component (generating mistakes by running the current policy, Section IV-B) is critical. The ablation study (I-Gen - Policy), which generates synthetic interventions without this closed-loop mistake generation, performs 12%-38% worse than full I-Gen (Table I). This demonstrates the value of actively exploring the policy's failure modes in diverse, corrupted environments to generate relevant recovery data, rather than passively transforming a fixed set of human-made mistakes.

- **Generality Across Diverse Environments and Error Types:**
 - I-Gen's effectiveness is consistent across various contact-rich manipulation tasks, from simple insertions to more complex multi-stage assemblies (Nut Insertion, 2-Piece Assembly, Coffee, Nut-and-Peg Assembly).
 - It is robust to different sources of observation error: sensor noise (positional inaccuracies, Table I) and **object geometry error** (model misspecification, Table II). In Nut-and-Peg Assembly, I-Gen achieves 90% average success across two geometries, whereas baselines either overfit to one geometry or struggle significantly (50-62% for mixture).

- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer with Robustness:**
 - Policies trained with I-Gen in simulation successfully transfer **zero-shot** to a physical Franka robot for a block grasping task (Table IV). These policies retain their robustness to erroneous state estimation in the real world, achieving 90% success compared to 0% for the base policy. This highlights I-Gen's potential to bridge the sim-to-real gap, especially for state-based policies reliant on accurate perception.

- **Sufficiency of Partial Observability for Recovery:**
 - Even with partially improved observability upon contact (e.g., mean contact position or direction unit vector) instead of full ground truth pose, I-Gen's performance only degrades by a small margin (4%-8%, Table III vs. Table I). This indicates that practical, real-world contact sensors (like force-torque sensing) can provide sufficient information to ground recovery behaviors learned via I-Gen.

- **Scalability of Synthetic Data Generation:**
 - Performance scales positively with the amount of synthetically generated interventions. In 2-Piece Assembly, increasing synthetic interventions from 200 to 1000 to 5000 (all from the same 10 human interventions) led to success rates of 34%, 70%, and 88% respectively. This allows for fine-tuning the trade-off between computation time and desired policy performance.

- **Data-Efficiency Advantage:**
 - A policy trained on 1000 I-Gen interventions (derived from 10 human interventions) achieved 70% success in 2-Piece Assembly. This significantly outperforms a policy trained on 100 *direct human interventions* (46% success), while requiring only 12% of the human data collection time and effort. This showcases a substantial gain in efficiency and performance.

These findings collectively establish IntervenGen as a highly effective and efficient system for training robust robot manipulation policies, particularly in the face of real-world perception uncertainties, by intelligently leveraging and expanding upon a small set of human corrections.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

IntervenGen represents a significant step forward in making robot imitation learning more practical, scalable, and robust for real-world deployment. Its potential impact spans several critical areas in robotics and AI:

1. **Breaking the Data Bottleneck in Interactive IL:** The most immediate and profound impact of I-Gen is its ability to drastically reduce the human data collection burden in interactive imitation learning. By autonomously generating a vast and diverse set of corrective interventions from just a handful of human examples, I-Gen overcomes a major obstacle to scaling IIL. This makes the powerful DAgger-like approaches accessible for a much wider range of tasks and deployments, where continuous human supervision is impractical.

2. **Enhanced Policy Robustness to Perception Errors:** Real-world robot operation is inherently uncertain, with perception errors (e.g., in object pose estimation) being a constant challenge. I-Gen directly addresses this by systematically generating recovery data for diverse and specific policy mistakes caused by such errors. This leads to policies that are significantly more robust and reliable, capable of recovering gracefully from unforeseen circumstances, a critical characteristic for safe and effective autonomous systems.

3. **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The paper demonstrates successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for policies trained with I-Gen. By making state-based policies robust to inaccurate state estimations in simulation, I-Gen inherently prepares them for the noise and imperfections of real-world sensors. This is particularly impactful for state-based policies, which are often easier to transfer across visual domain gaps than image-based policies but traditionally rely heavily on accurate perception. I-Gen offers a data-driven approach to mitigate this reliance, bridging a crucial gap in robotics research.

4. **Foundation for Advanced Robot Autonomy:**
 - **Domain Randomization for IL:** I-Gen can act as a sophisticated form of domain randomization specifically for imitation learning policies. While reinforcement learning algorithms can learn adaptations to randomized dynamics, IL typically requires new human demonstrations for such variations. I-Gen's ability to generate diverse recovery behaviors under varied conditions could dramatically reduce the human effort required to make IL policies resilient to broad domain shifts, paving the way for more general-purpose robot skills.
 - **Personalized Robot Behavior:** The framework's flexibility could be extended to adapt policy behavior to individual human preferences. A small number of human-provided interventions reflecting specific styles or recovery strategies could be expanded by I-Gen to generate a personalized robust policy without extensive data collection.
 - **Handling Complex Failure Modes:** The framework's extensibility to "inter-subtask recovery" and "offline mode" (where humans intentionally demonstrate mistakes) further broadens its applicability, indicating a versatile tool for addressing more severe and complex robotic failure scenarios.

5. **Catalyst for Future Research:**
 - The work opens avenues for integrating I-Gen with advanced sensing modalities like force-torque or tactile sensing. These sensors can provide richer, contact-rich feedback during recovery, potentially enabling even more nuanced and precise corrective behaviors for high-precision tasks.
 - Further exploration into learned mistake detection mechanisms (beyond contact detection) or advanced robot-gated intervention criteria could enhance the autonomy and efficiency of the data generation process.

In conclusion, IntervenGen represents a significant step towards enabling robots to learn robust and generalizable skills with substantially less human effort. By intelligently automating the generation of corrective interventions, it moves the field closer to developing truly autonomous robotic systems that can operate reliably in complex, unpredictable real-world environments, accelerating their adoption across industries from manufacturing and logistics to service robotics.
