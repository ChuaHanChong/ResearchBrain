# PAMAE: Phase-Aware-MoE Action Experts Towards Reliable Flow-Matching Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> PAMAE enhances flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies for robotic manipulation by introducing a phase-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module. This approach improves task success rates by up to 9.2% on simulated multi-stage tasks through dynamic, phase-consistent expert allocation without requiring explicit phase labels during inference.

## Problem

- Current flow-matching VLA policies rely on a single shared action expert, which struggles to adapt to distinct control patterns required across different execution phases of multi-stage manipulation tasks.
- Existing Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures in robotics often do not explicitly leverage fine-grained, low-level execution phase structure for action generation, leading to suboptimal control in phase-dependent scenarios.
- Achieving reliable and precise low-level action generation for multi-stage robotic tasks necessitates a mechanism for consistent allocation of specialized action experts that can adapt to phase-dependent control demands.

## Method

- Introduces **PAMAE**, a plug-and-play module that replaces the single action expert in pretrained flow-matching VLA policies with a sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) coordinated by a ==phase-aware router==.
- The router uses a composite feature comprising multimodal context, flow time, and a lightweight execution descriptor (gripper state, action magnitude, progress cue) to dynamically allocate experts at inference time without explicit phase labels.
- Employs a two-stage training pipeline that first warms up the experts under a flow-matching objective and then introduces phase-supervised routing objectives (phase prediction, route alignment, smoothness, load balancing) with pseudo phase labels, gradually annealing auxiliary losses.

## Results

- PAMAE(π0) increased the average task success rate from **73.8%** to **83.0%** (a **9.2%** absolute improvement) over the baseline π0 across five simulated multi-stage manipulation tasks.
- PAMAE(π0.5) improved the average task success rate from **85.8%** to **91.4%** (a **5.6%** absolute improvement) over the baseline π0.5.
- The learned routing behavior demonstrated high Phase-Conditioned Dominance Purity (**89.0%**), indicating a strong and stable correlation between the dominant expert and the underlying execution phase, and an average dominant-expert run length of 8 action chunks, suggesting temporally coherent transitions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Specializing action experts for distinct execution phases significantly improves control precision and robustness in multi-stage robotic manipulation.
> - A two-stage training strategy, starting with expert warm-up and then applying phase-consistent routing supervision with annealing, is crucial for stable expert specialization and robust policy learning.
> - Coarse, rule-based pseudo phase labels combined with specific routing alignment objectives are sufficient to guide phase-aware expert allocation without requiring explicit phase labels during inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied intelligence is actively pursuing the development of reliable action generation for multi-stage robotic manipulation. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a prominent paradigm, offering a unified approach to visual perception, language understanding, and action generation for general-purpose robot control. These models, often benefiting from large-scale pretraining, exhibit strong multimodal grounding, flexible task specification, and broad generalization across various manipulation tasks.

Within the VLA landscape, flow-matching VLA policies, exemplified by models such as $\pi^0$ and $\pi^{0.5}$, have demonstrated strengths in multimodal grounding and continuous action generation. They represent actions as conditional velocity fields, suitable for high-frequency control in dexterous and long-horizon manipulation. However, a key limitation of these systems is their typical reliance on a single, shared action expert to predict control signals across all stages of task execution. This monolithic architecture struggles to accommodate the distinct control patterns, transition dynamics, and precision requirements inherent in different execution phases (e.g., approach, contact, transport). Consequently, phase-specific behaviors can be diluted, potentially reducing action precision and task robustness in complex, contact-rich scenarios.

Another area of related research involves Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, which aim to increase model capacity and specialization by routing inputs to different experts. In robotics and VLA systems, MoE designs have been explored for various purposes, including model scaling, handling embodiment heterogeneity, skill specialization, and multimodal fusion. However, current MoE-based VLA methods often organize experts around high-level task semantics or auxiliary modalities, rather than explicitly structuring them around low-level execution phases. This presents a gap, as low-level control variations in flow-matching manipulation policies frequently arise across distinct execution phases. Different tasks might share similar control behaviors within the same phase, while different phases of a single task can demand substantially different control patterns. Therefore, effective control in flow-matching VLA models requires not only increased expert capacity but also a mechanism for phase-consistent allocation of these low-level action experts.

This work, PAMAE, positions itself by directly addressing these identified limitations. It aims to enhance existing flow-matching VLA policies by integrating phase-aware expert allocation, thereby enabling low-level action generation to better capture phase-dependent control patterns and transition dynamics in multi-stage manipulation, without requiring explicit phase labels at inference time.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenge of achieving reliable action generation for multi-stage robotic manipulation using existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly those based on flow-matching techniques.

**Motivation:**

1. **Limitation of Single Shared Expert:** Current flow-matching VLA policies typically employ a single action expert to generate control signals throughout an entire task. This design is suboptimal for multi-stage tasks because different execution phases (e.g., approaching an object, making contact, manipulating it, transporting it) often demand distinct control patterns, velocity fields, and action sensitivities. A single expert struggles to capture these phase-specific nuances, potentially leading to blurred behaviors, reduced responsiveness to stage-dependent control demands, and decreased precision and robustness, especially in long-horizon or contact-rich tasks.
2. **Inadequate Exploitation of Execution-Phase Structure in MoE:** While Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures offer a way to increase model capacity and specialization, existing MoE implementations in robotics and VLA systems are not primarily designed to leverage fine-grained execution-phase structure for low-level action generation. Experts are often organized based on broader task semantics, scaling objectives, or embodiment differences. The observation that low-level control variation in flow-matching policies frequently correlates with execution phases suggests that explicitly aligning expert allocation with these phases could significantly improve control.
3. **Need for Phase-Consistent Expert Allocation:** Effective low-level control in multi-stage manipulation requires not only specialized expert capacity but also a mechanism to consistently allocate the most appropriate expert for the current execution phase. This would allow the policy to adapt its control strategy dynamically as the task progresses through different stages.

#### 2.1. Key Objectives

1. **Develop a Plug-and-Play Phase-Aware MoE Action Module (PAMAE):** To create a modular component that can replace the single action expert in existing pretrained flow-matching VLA policies, preserving the VLA backbone while introducing specialized action experts.
2. **Enable Phase-Consistent Action Generation:** To design a mechanism that allows low-level action generation to capture and leverage phase-dependent control patterns and transition dynamics within multi-stage manipulation.
3. **Implement Phase-Aware Routing:** To introduce a router that leverages execution-phase cues to dynamically allocate action generation across specialized experts. This router should be supported by a lightweight phase prediction head and a routing alignment objective.
4. **Achieve Stable Expert Specialization:** To develop a robust training strategy, specifically a two-stage optimization scheme, that first warms up the expert module and then optimizes for phase-consistent routing, gradually relaxing auxiliary constraints in later training stages to refine action quality.
5. **Operate Without Inference-Time Phase Labels:** To ensure that the learned phase-consistent expert allocation mechanism does not require explicit phase labels or additional supervision during inference, maintaining the autonomy of the original flow-matching generation process.
6. **Improve Task Success and Reliability:** To demonstrate that the proposed PAMAE framework leads to measurable improvements in task success rates and overall reliability in multi-stage robotic manipulation tasks compared to strong VLA baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PAMAE framework is designed as a plug-and-play module to enhance pretrained flow-matching VLA policies. It achieves this by replacing the original single shared action expert with a sparse mixture of specialized experts coordinated by a phase-aware router.

**A. Core Architecture of PAMAE:**

1. **VLA Backbone Preservation:** PAMAE maintains the pretrained VLA backbone (denoted as $f_{enc}$), which is responsible for encoding multimodal inputs (visual observations $o_t$, language instruction $l$, and robot states $s_t$) into a contextual representation $h_t = f_{enc}(o_t, l, s_t)$.
2. **Shared Action Trunk:** A shared action trunk $f_{shared}(z_{\tau t}, h_t)$ takes the intermediate noisy action state $z_{\tau t}$ and the multimodal context $h_t$ to produce a common latent representation $q_{\tau t}$. This trunk serves as an initial processing layer before expert specialization.
3. **Multiple Action Experts:** On top of the shared trunk, $M$ individual action experts, denoted as $e_{\tau t,i} = f^{expert}_i(q_{\tau t})$ for $i = 1, \dots, M$, are instantiated. Each expert is responsible for predicting a candidate velocity-field component for the current noisy action state.
4. **Phase-Aware Router:** This component is central to PAMAE.
 - **Routing Feature:** The router takes a composite routing feature $r_{\tau t} = [h_t, \phi_t, \tau]$ as input. Here, $h_t$ is the multimodal context, $\tau$ is the flow time, and $\phi_t$ is a lightweight execution descriptor constructed from low-dimensional trajectory signals. $\phi_t$ includes the gripper state ($g_t$), gripper variation ($\Delta g_t = g_t - g_{t-1}$), recent action magnitude ($\|a_{t-1}\|^2$), and a coarse normalized progress cue ($\rho_t = t/T$, clipped at 1). These signals are available at inference time and provide cues about the current execution status.
 - **Expert Allocation:** The router outputs a distribution over experts $\pi_{\tau t} = \text{Softmax}(W r_{\tau t})$. Sparse top-k routing is then applied to obtain $\tilde{\pi}_{\tau t}$, selecting the top $k$ experts based on their weights.
5. **Weighted Aggregation:** The final velocity prediction $\hat{v}_{\tau t}$ is computed as a weighted sum of the outputs from the selected experts: $\hat{v}_{\tau t} = \sum_{i=1}^M \tilde{\pi}_{\tau t,i} e_{\tau t,i}$. This aggregated output directly replaces the single expert's prediction in the original flow-matching VLA policy.

**B. Pseudo Phase Label Definition (Training Only):**

For training purposes, each timestep $t$ in a demonstration trajectory is associated with a pseudo phase label $y^{phase}_t \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, corresponding to pre-contact, contact/manipulation, and post-contact stages. These labels are defined using a simple rule-based system based on gripper state ($g_t$) and end-effector velocity ($v_t = \|p_t - p_{t-1}\|^2$).
- Contact onset ($t_c$): when gripper closes rapidly (e.g., $\Delta g_t < -\delta_g$).
- Transport onset ($t_m$): when end-effector moves with a closed gripper after contact (e.g., $v_t > \delta_v$ and $g_t < \delta_c$).
- Phases are assigned as: $y^{phase}_t = 0$ for $t < t_c$, $1$ for $t_c \le t < t_m$, and $2$ for $t \ge t_m$. This provides a coarse phase abstraction for supervision.

**C. Two-Stage Training Pipeline:**

PAMAE is trained using a two-stage optimization strategy to stabilize expert specialization and prevent premature routing collapse.

1. **Stage 1 (Expert Warm-up):**
 - The sparse action experts are initially warmed up primarily under the standard flow-matching objective ($L_{fm}$).
 - A very small load-balancing regularizer ($L_{lb}$) is included to weakly prevent expert collapse without enforcing uniform usage.
 - $L^{(1)} = L_{fm} + \lambda^{(1)}_b L_{lb}$
 - $L_{fm} = E_h \|\hat{v}_{\tau t} - u^\star(z_{\tau t} | a_t)\|_2^2$, where $u^\star$ is the target velocity.

2. **Stage 2 (Phase-Consistent Routing Supervision):**
 - After warm-up, phase-supervised routing objectives are introduced to encourage structured expert specialization.
 - The full loss function combines $L_{fm}$ with three auxiliary objectives and the load-balancing regularizer:
 - $L^{(2)} = L_{fm} + \lambda_1 L_{phase} + \lambda_2 L_{route} + \lambda_3 L_{smooth} + \lambda_4 L_{lb}$
 - **Phase Prediction Loss ($L_{phase}$):** A lightweight MLP head on the routing feature $r_{\tau t}$ predicts phase probabilities $p^{phase}_t$. A Cross-Entropy loss ($L_{phase} = \text{CE}(p^{phase}_t, y^{phase}_t)$) encourages the routing feature to retain explicit phase information. This head is used only during training.
 - **Route Alignment Objective ($L_{route}$):** A KL divergence loss ($L_{route} = \text{KL}(\pi_{\tau t} \| p^{target}_t)$) aligns the router's expert distribution $\pi_{\tau t}$ with phase-specific target distributions $p^{target}_t$, which are predefined priors over experts for each coarse phase.
 - **Routing Smoothness Loss ($L_{smooth}$):** An $L_2$ loss ($L_{smooth} = \sum_t \|\pi_{\tau t} - \pi_{\tau t-1}\|_2^2$) is applied to reduce routing jitter between neighboring timesteps.
 - **Load Balancing Regularizer ($L_{lb}$):** A KL divergence loss ($L_{lb} = \text{KL}(\bar{\pi} \| u)$) between the batch-level average expert utilization $\bar{\pi}$ and a uniform expert distribution $u$ prevents experts from remaining unused.
 - **Auxiliary Loss Annealing:** During the final 30% of Stage 2, the weights of $L_{phase}$, $L_{route}$, and $L_{lb}$ are linearly annealed to 10% of their initial values. This shifts optimization emphasis from enforcing expert separation to refining action quality under the main $L_{fm}$ objective, while retaining weak structural regularization.

**D. Inference Time Execution:**

- At inference, PAMAE maintains the original flow-matching generation process.
- Crucially, it does not require explicit phase labels.
- The VLA backbone produces $h_t$. An initial noisy action state $z^0_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is iteratively refined.
- At each flow time $\tau$, the learned router uses $r_{\tau t} = [h_t, \phi_t, \tau]$ to predict sparse expert weights $\pi_{\tau t}$.
- The top-k experts are aggregated to form the velocity field $\hat{v}_{\tau t}$, which updates the action state $z_{\tau t}$.
- The final refined state after multiple integration steps is the predicted action chunk $\hat{a}_t$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments focused on evaluating PAMAE's ability to improve task success, achieve phase-consistent expert allocation, and the importance of its specific components through ablation studies, all conducted on simulated multi-stage manipulation tasks.

**A. Main Results on Task Success:**

- **Consistent Improvements:** PAMAE consistently improved task success rates across all five evaluated multi-stage simulation tasks: Table-Cleaning (T.C.), Drawer-Cycle (D.C.), Lid-Open (L.O.), Shelf-Insert (S.I.), and Cup-Upright (C.U.).
- **Performance Gains over Baselines:**
 - Instantiated on a $\pi^0$ backbone, PAMAE($\pi^0$) increased the average task success rate from 73.8% (baseline $\pi^0$) to 83.0%, representing an absolute improvement of 9.2%.
 - Instantiated on a $\pi^{0.5}$ backbone, PAMAE($\pi^{0.5}$) improved the average task success rate from 85.8% (baseline $\pi^{0.5}$) to 91.4%, an absolute improvement of 5.6%.
- **Comparison to $\pi^0$ (labeled):** A control baseline, $\pi^0$ (labeled), which used the same coarse phase supervision as PAMAE during training but without MoE routing or expert specialization, achieved an average success rate of 74.6%. PAMAE($\pi^0$)'s superior performance (83.0%) indicates that merely exposing phase information is insufficient; both phase-supervised routing and expert specialization are necessary for improved action generation.
- **Comparison to ProgressVLA:** PAMAE also outperformed ProgressVLA (an 78.2% average success rate), suggesting that the observed gains stem from phase-consistent expert allocation within the action expert, rather than solely from coarse progress cues.

**B. Analysis of Learned Routing Behavior:**

- **Temporally Coherent Allocation:** PAMAE($\pi^0$) demonstrated an average dominant-expert run length of 8 action chunks. This metric indicates that the expert allocation is stable over time, rather than exhibiting erratic switches, suggesting coherent behavioral transitions.
- **Phase-Consistent Allocation:** The Phase-Conditioned Dominance Purity (PCP) for PAMAE($\pi^0$) was 89.0%. This high purity score signifies a strong correlation between the selected dominant expert and the underlying execution phase, confirming that the routing mechanism effectively aligns with phase-specific control structures without explicit inference-time phase labels.

**C. Ablation Studies:**

Ablation studies were conducted using the $\pi^0$-based backbone to isolate the contributions of different PAMAE components:

- **Full PAMAE Model (Full):** Achieved the best average task success at 83.0%.
- **Without Stage 1 Warm-up (-S1):** Removing the initial expert warm-up stage reduced average success to 78.2%. This result highlights the importance of Stage 1 in stabilizing early training and preventing premature collapse of routing or expert specialization.
- **Without Routing Alignment Loss (-R):** Removing the $L_{route}$ objective led to the most significant performance drop, reducing average success to 76.2%. This finding confirms that the phase-conditioned routing alignment is a critical mechanism for translating coarse phase structure into effective expert allocation and improved low-level action generation.
- **Without Phase Prediction Loss (-P):** Removing the auxiliary $L_{phase}$ objective resulted in a decrease in average success to 79.2%. This suggests that explicit phase supervision on the routing feature provides valuable complementary guidance that enhances the routing alignment process.

**Summary of Ablations:** The ablation studies collectively demonstrate that both the phase-supervised routing design (incorporating $L_{route}$ and $L_{phase}$) and the two-stage optimization strategy (particularly the Stage 1 expert warm-up) are essential for PAMAE to achieve its full performance potential. The routing objectives facilitate learning phase-consistent expert specialization, while staged optimization contributes to stable expert formation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of PAMAE represents a significant step towards more reliable and adaptable robotic manipulation policies, particularly for tasks involving multiple distinct execution phases.

- **Enhanced Reliability and Precision in Multi-stage Tasks:** The most direct impact of PAMAE is its demonstrated ability to consistently improve task success rates in complex, multi-stage manipulation scenarios. By allowing the policy to specialize control patterns for different phases (e.g., approach, contact, transport), PAMAE enables more precise and robust action generation where a single, undifferentiated expert might falter. This is critical for real-world robotic applications that demand high reliability.

- **Addressing a Key Limitation of Flow-Matching VLA Policies:** PAMAE directly addresses the inherent limitation of existing flow-matching VLA models that rely on a single shared action expert. By introducing a phase-aware Mixture-of-Experts architecture, it significantly enhances the policy's capacity to model diverse control requirements across temporally structured tasks without requiring a complete overhaul of the VLA backbone.

- **Plug-and-Play Adaptability:** The design of PAMAE as a plug-and-play module is highly impactful. It allows for the augmentation of existing pretrained VLA backbones (such as $\pi^0$ and $\pi^{0.5}$) without extensive re-training of the entire model. This modularity reduces computational costs and accelerates the adoption of phase-aware control in various VLA systems.

- **Autonomous Phase-Consistent Control:** A notable contribution is achieving phase-consistent expert allocation without requiring explicit phase labels during inference. This maintains the autonomy and simplicity of the original flow-matching generation process while leveraging phase information learned during training. This avoids the need for external phase detectors or manual annotations at deployment, which would otherwise complicate real-world application.

- **Improved Interpretability and Diagnostics:** The explicit specialization of experts for different phases could offer improved interpretability of policy behavior. Analyzing expert activation patterns could provide insights into how the robot perceives and responds to different execution stages, potentially aiding in debugging and policy refinement.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. The concept of leveraging explicit phase structure for expert routing could be extended to more complex tasks, different robot embodiments, or even to learning more granular or adaptive phase definitions beyond the rule-based approach used in this study. The intention to evaluate PAMAE on real robots and explore adaptive phase discovery further underscores its potential for broader impact.

In summary, PAMAE offers a practical and effective mechanism for improving the reliability and action quality of VLA policies by integrating phase-aware expert allocation. Its plug-and-play nature and independence from inference-time phase labels make it a promising advancement for multi-stage robotic manipulation.
