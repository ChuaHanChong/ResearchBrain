# Learning Action-Conditional and Object-Centric Gaussian Splatting World Models for Rigid Objects

> [!summary] Summary
> The Multi Rigid Object Gaussian World Model (MRO-GWM) from the University of Augsburg learns action-conditional 3D rigid object dynamics by employing an object-centric Gaussian splatting representation and a novel spatio-temporal transformer architecture. It predicts multi-object interactions, enabling model-predictive control for non-prehensile manipulation tasks with success rates up to 77% for single object pushing in simulation.

## Problem

- Existing world models struggle to predict action-conditional 3D dynamics for multiple rigid objects, especially when objects have arbitrary shapes, are closely positioned, or are partially observed due to occlusions.
- Many current 3D world models are computationally intensive (e.g., NeRF-based) or rely on explicit physics simulations that may not accurately capture all real-world interaction dynamics.
- Integrating learned neural dynamics models, particularly those using explicit 3D representations like Gaussians, into effective robotic control frameworks like Model Predictive Control (MPC) remains a challenge.

## Method

- An object-centric Gaussian splatting representation, extending ObjectGS, is used to model arbitrary object shapes and their rigid transformations, handling partial observations by associating Gaussians with specific object IDs.
- A novel spatio-temporal transformer architecture processes these object-aware Gaussian representations, incorporating temporal, spatial, and a new temporal-spatial attention layer to capture complex multi-object dynamics across time.
- The model predicts relative changes in object position and orientation between consecutive timesteps, trained with L2 losses on normalized transformations, and is integrated into an iCEM-based ==MPC== framework for control.

## Results

- MRO-GWM, integrated into an MPC framework, achieved a **0.77** success rate for pushing a single object to a target position (within 1cm) and a **0.66** success rate for a multi-object 'clear middle' task in simulation.
- Prediction errors are primarily determined by the total prediction horizon (total time), showing comparable performance across different timestep granularities when evaluated at the same total time horizon.
- Ablation studies revealed that removing multiscale downsampling or the spatio-temporal attention layer led to the most significant performance degradation, underscoring their importance in the model's architecture.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The multi-scale downsampling and aggregation of points within the transformer architecture are crucial for effectively processing complex object geometries and significantly improving prediction accuracy.
> - A diverse set of training objects is essential for the model to generalize effectively to unseen objects, while the model demonstrates robustness to limited camera views, implicitly handling missing shape information.
> - The newly introduced temporal-spatial attention layer plays a notable role in modeling complex interactions between objects, highlighting its contribution to accurately predicting their relative motions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

World models are a fundamental component in intelligent agents, enabling them to forecast the outcomes of potential actions and plan effectively. Recent research in machine learning, robot learning, and computer vision has explored various approaches to learning world models.

Existing methods include action-conditional video prediction models, which often operate in 2D or learn latent dynamics. Other approaches focus on 3D world models, with some utilizing implicit Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) representations for object shape, as seen in models for multi-object interactions. However, these NeRF-based methods can be computationally intensive or struggle with real-time requirements.

Explicit 3D representations, such as Gaussian splatting, have gained attention for their efficiency and quality in scene rendering. Within this domain, several works integrate physics models or learn neural physical dynamics for Gaussian representations:
- **Physics-integrated Gaussian models:** Approaches like PIN-WM and ContactGaussian-WM use differentiable rendering and physics engines to infer physical parameters or propagate objects. Embodied Gaussians couple visual Gaussians with particles for point-based dynamics. Related methods integrate material point methods for deformables into 3D Gaussian reconstructions. A common limitation of these physics-based approaches is their reliance on known or fully observed scene and object shapes, and their adherence to the constraints of the underlying physics simulator. They may not effectively learn dynamics that deviate from pre-defined physical models.
- **Neural dynamics for Gaussians:** Models such as 3DGSim train transformers for single-object dynamics without action conditioning. Others employ graph neural networks to learn single-step, action-conditional dynamics for single rigid or deformable objects, applied to planning non-prehensile manipulation. ManiGaussian and related works learn single-step prediction models for Gaussians, often used for imitation learning or failure detection. Latent diffusion transformers have also been applied for single-step Gaussian motion prediction for model-based reinforcement learning. GNNs have been used for granular media manipulation, and Gaussian Action Fields (GAF) predict future states and scene flow for imitation learning. Neural Gaussian Force Fields learn object-centric dynamics by predicting forces with neural interaction networks and using ODE solvers, but they typically do not model robot actions as end-effector motions and may rely on shape completion for occlusions.

The presented work, Multi Rigid Object Gaussian World Model (MRO-GWM), addresses several limitations of prior research by focusing on action-conditional, multi-object dynamics within complex rigid-body scenes. Unlike many existing Gaussian-based dynamics models, MRO-GWM is designed for multi-object scenarios where objects interact, are closely placed, and can be partially observed due to occlusions. It distinguishes itself by employing an object-centric Gaussian splatting representation to model arbitrary object shapes and by developing a spatio-temporal transformer architecture capable of predicting multi-step rigid body motion conditioned on a history of object and end-effector Gaussians and future actions. This approach implicitly handles occlusions and does not rely on explicit physics simulations, allowing it to learn dynamics directly from interactions. The integration of this world model with model-predictive control (MPC) for non-prehensile manipulation further demonstrates its practical applicability in robotic tasks, an area where many neural dynamics models for Gaussians are limited to single-step prediction or do not explicitly incorporate robot actions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel action-conditional world model, termed Multi Rigid Object Gaussian World Model (MRO-GWM), capable of learning and predicting the 3D dynamics of rigid objects in multi-object scenes. A key aspect is the ability to handle arbitrary object shapes and scenarios where objects are closely positioned and may occlude one another.

The motivation for this work stems from the utility of world models in empowering intelligent agents with the ability to foresee the outcomes of their actions, facilitating proactive planning and enabling generalization across diverse tasks. A challenging domain for such generalization is environments characterized by rigid-body dynamics, particularly in everyday household scenes. These environments frequently involve numerous objects of varied shapes placed in close proximity, where interaction with one object can cascade effects onto others, and objects are often partially observable due to occlusions.

Specifically, the research aims to address the following challenges and integrate specific capabilities:
- **Modeling Multi-Object Rigid Body Dynamics:** To learn the complex interactions and dynamics of multiple rigid objects within a 3D environment, including contact-rich scenarios.
- **Handling Diverse Object Shapes:** To represent arbitrary object geometries effectively without explicit mesh or voxel models, using an object-centric Gaussian splatting representation.
- **Coping with Partial Observations:** To enable the model to infer dynamics even when objects are partially occluded from certain viewpoints, which is a common occurrence in cluttered scenes.
- **Action-Conditional Prediction:** To predict future object trajectories based on a history of object states and explicit robot end-effector actions, thereby forming a predictive model for robot manipulation.
- **Integration with Control:** To demonstrate the practical utility of the learned world model in model-predictive control for robotic manipulation tasks, specifically non-prehensile manipulation.

The core contributions proposed to achieve these objectives are:
1. **Object-Centric Gaussian Splatting Representation:** The adoption of object-centric Gaussian splatting as a scene representation, combined with per-object rigid transformations to encode the history of pose observations. This enables a compact, explicit 3D representation that is amenable to rigid motion.
2. **Novel Spatio-Temporal Transformer Architecture:** The introduction of a transformer architecture designed to operate on this representation, incorporating both temporal and spatial attention blocks, alongside a new spatio-temporal attention layer to effectively capture both the temporal evolution of states and spatial relationships, including contacts between objects.
3. **Model-Predictive Control Integration:** The demonstration and evaluation of the world model's effectiveness within a model-predictive control framework for non-prehensile manipulation tasks in simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of MRO-GWM encompasses a specific scene representation based on Gaussian splatting, a spatio-temporal transformer architecture for dynamics prediction, and a tailored experimental setup for training and evaluation.

#### 3.1. Scene Representation with Object-Aware Gaussian Splatting

The scene representation builds upon ObjectGS, an extension of ScaffoldGS, which uses object-aware Gaussian splatting. Instead of optimizing 3D Gaussians directly, the scene is represented by a grid of voxels, termed "anchors," each possessing latent features. An MLP decodes a fixed number of Gaussians from each anchor. Crucially, each anchor and its decoded Gaussians are associated with a unique object ID, enabling rigid-body transformations per object.

- **Input Data:** The model assumes access to multiple posed RGB-D images with corresponding camera poses and ground-truth object segmentation masks. During robot interaction, ground-truth object poses and end-effector poses are also assumed. The input for prediction consists of a history of object poses ($\tau^{obj}_{1:h}$) and end-effector poses ($\tau^{ee}_{1:h}$), along with a trajectory of future end-effector poses ($\tau^{ee}_{h+1:h+p}$).
- **Anchor Initialization:** To initialize anchors, an extended voting scheme is used. Points from an initial point cloud are projected into camera views, and if within the object segmentation mask and with a depth mismatch smaller than 1 cm, they contribute a vote for the object ID. This extension specifically addresses scenes with occlusions, ensuring votes only come from observed parts of objects. The 2D Gaussian splatting variant is employed, which was found to better represent surfaces in this context.
- **Latent Space and Multi-Scene Adaptation:** The model can operate either on explicit 2D Gaussian properties (position, rotation, scale, opacity) or on the neurally compressed anchor features. For consistency across multiple training scenes, the MLPs for scale, rotation, and opacity are shared, while a distinct MLP is used for color encoding per scene. The model processes only the 16 spatial features of the 32-dimensional anchor features, discarding color. For inference on unseen scenes, the trained shared MLPs are reused.
- **Robot End-Effector Representation:** The robot end-effector is also represented by splatted Gaussians, generated using distinct MLPs to maintain reconstruction quality, and spatially integrated into the scene.
- **View-Independence:** The scene representation is designed to be view-independent, which is critical for dynamics modeling, by using only the view-independent components of the Gaussian decoding process.
- **Background Filtering:** To focus computational resources on relevant areas, background anchors or Gaussians beyond a 10 cm threshold from any object or end-effector anchor are filtered out.

#### 3.2. Spatio-Temporal Transformer

The core of the dynamics prediction is a novel spatio-temporal transformer architecture designed to process the transformed scene representations.

- **Input Encoding:** For each timestep in the history and prediction horizon, all anchors or Gaussians corresponding to objects or the end-effector are rigidly transformed by their respective poses. This results in $h+p$ spatially distinct scene representations. Anchor features include spatial location, rotated offsets, scaling parameters, latent features, and a quaternion for rotation. Gaussian features comprise rotation, opacity, and scaling. Both types of inputs are augmented with an embedded object index and a Boolean indicating if they belong to a history or future timestep.
- **Architecture Stages:** The model consists of multiple stages, each processing the transformed representations across all timesteps concurrently. Each stage (`s_i`) involves a pooling operation (`pool_i`), a positional embedding (`pe`), and several residual blocks (`b_i,j`).
 - **Spatial Grid Pooling:** The pooling operation `pool_i` aggregates features from points within the same spatial grid cell, similar to PTv2, and averages their coordinates. This pooling is performed independently for each object (and background). Critically, pooling is calculated once for all grid cell sizes and objects at the first timestep, then applied identically across all timesteps to maintain point correspondences over time.
 - **Positional Embedding:** After pooling, a standard sinusoidal positional embedding `pe` encoding the time index is added to all features.
 - **Residual Blocks:** Each residual block `b_i,j` contains four types of layers, inspired by DiT and PTv2:
 1. **Temporal Attention:** Standard attention over time, allowing points to exchange information with their counterparts at different timesteps, without spatial information flow.
 2. **Spatial Vector Attention:** A layer from PTv2 where information is exchanged between neighboring points within the same timestep, without temporal flow.
 3. **Temporal-Spatial Attention:** A newly introduced layer that employs a mechanism similar to PTv2's spatial attention, but its "neighbors" are the point counterparts at different timesteps. This provides direct information about the relative motion of each point for attention calculation.
 4. **Pointwise MLP Layer:** A standard Multi-Layer Perceptron applied independently to each point's features.
 - **Output Layer:** The final stage uses a grid cell size that spans the entire scene, resulting in a single pooled point per object. After discarding background and end-effector points, a linear layer maps the features of the object points to the prediction.
- **Prediction Representation and Losses:** The model is trained to predict the relative change in position and orientation between consecutive timesteps for each object.
 - **Rotation:** Rotations are represented as 9D rotation matrices, and orthogonalization is applied to ensure valid 3D rotations. A normalization scheme is used where the identity matrix is subtracted from the ground-truth relative rotation matrix, and components are divided by their training set standard deviations. An L2 norm loss is computed between the predicted and normalized ground-truth matrices.
 - **Position:** Relative position changes are normalized by their mean norm over the training set, and a squared L2 norm loss is applied.

#### 3.3. Experiment Setup

- **Simulator and Dataset Generation:** Experiments are conducted using the Maniskill simulator. Scenes contain 1 to 5 household objects from the YCB model dataset, arranged to be closely positioned. A specific sampling procedure is used to generate varied and interacting object configurations, including diverse orientations (e.g., "standing," "box-like," "cylindrical"). Occlusion filtering ensures each object is sufficiently visible from multiple views.
- **Dataset Splits:** To evaluate generalization, disjoint object pools are used for training, validation, and testing (YCB-V, full YCB set excluding test/val). Training datasets vary in the number of original scenes and augmented layouts (e.g., YCB-100-100, YCB-50-200, YCB-200-50, YCBV-100-100 with a smaller object pool).
- **Scene Capture and Splatting:** Scenes are captured with 32 RGB-D images from varying viewpoints (elevation 30°/60°, radius 0.4m, 360° azimuth, with an ablation for 180°). Object-aware Gaussian splatting is performed with a 1 cm anchor resolution for 5,000 steps.
- **Interaction Data:** A Franka Panda robot with a stick end-effector interacts with the scene. A heuristic targets points around objects to generate interaction data. Linear interpolation creates end-effector trajectories, recorded at 20 Hz, which serve as actions. Corresponding object poses are recorded as ground truth.
- **Model Training:** The model is implemented in PyTorch and trained with AdamW for 60,000 steps using a half-period cosine learning rate schedule. Training examples are chunks of interaction trajectories with a fixed history size ($h=3$) and a default prediction horizon ($p=4$) at a 5 Hz step resolution.
- **Prediction Performance Evaluation:** Performance is assessed on a test set (100 scenes, 20 per object count, 3 trajectories each) by comparing predicted poses to ground truth using Euclidean distance for positions and geodesic angle for rotations. Ablation studies examine different model variants and architectural components.
- **Planning Performance Evaluation:** The world model is integrated into a model-predictive control (MPC) framework, specifically a variant of iCEM, for sampling-based optimization of keypoint-parametrized end-effector trajectories. Two non-prehensile manipulation tasks are evaluated: "push object to position" and "clear middle." Planning is repeated at 2 Hz, with each iteration updating the mean of the sample distribution. Success rates and goal achievement metrics are reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MRO-GWM was conducted on both prediction performance and its utility in model-predictive control (MPC) within simulated tabletop scenes.

#### 4.1. Prediction Results

- **Effect of Prediction Horizon and Granularity:** Analysis of prediction errors over time revealed that the prediction horizon (total time) is the primary determinant of median prediction error in both position and rotation. Model variants with different timestep granularities (5 Hz vs. 10 Hz) and prediction horizons (4, 8, 12 steps) performed comparably when evaluated at the same total time horizon. For instance, a 4-step prediction at 10 Hz (0.4s) showed similar error to an 8-step prediction at 5 Hz (1.6s) but not to a 4-step prediction at 5 Hz (0.8s).
- **Error Distribution:** A significant observation was the large spread between the 25th and 75th percentiles of errors, particularly in "moving" scenarios where objects underwent ground-truth movement. This is attributed to the nature of multi-object interactions: an action might not affect all objects, or small changes in robot interaction can lead to substantial, rapid object motion (e.g., an object falling over).
- **Model Variants and Ablations (Table 1):**
 - **Training Data Size and Augmentations:** The main training set (YCB-100-100, 100 original scenes, 100 augmented layouts) achieved slightly superior position and rotation prediction performance compared to variants with different ratios of original scenes and augmented layouts (YCB-50-200, YCB-200-50).
 - **Object Pool Diversity:** A training set derived from a smaller object pool (YCBV-100-100, 16 objects) exhibited the strongest performance degradation, indicating that a diverse set of training objects is beneficial for generalization to unseen objects.
 - **Scene Representation Details:**
 - **Anchor Resolution:** Using anchor resolutions of 0.005m (finer) or 0.02m (coarser) resulted in small performance losses compared to the default 0.01m resolution. The finer resolution's drop was hypothesized to be due to the transformer's spatial aggregation combining too many anchors without considering their fine-grained spatial layout, while the coarser resolution loses geometric detail.
 - **Explicit Gaussians vs. Anchors:** Operating the transformer directly on decoded Gaussians instead of the compressed anchor features also led to small performance drops, suggesting benefits of the anchor's compressed geometric information.
 - **Limited Camera Views:** Surprisingly, a model trained with limited camera views (azimuths 0-180°) performed almost as well as the model trained with a full 360° range, suggesting the model implicitly learned to handle missing or imprecise shape information effectively.
 - **Architectural Ablations:**
 - **Spatio-Temporal Attention:** Removing the spatio-temporal attention layer resulted in a noticeable performance drop, particularly in position prediction, highlighting its contribution to modeling complex interactions.
 - **Multiscale Downsampling:** The most significant performance degradation was observed when multiscale downsampling and aggregation of points were removed, emphasizing the importance of processing complex object geometries through hierarchical spatial aggregation.
- **Qualitative Examples (Figure 3):** The qualitative analysis showcased varying prediction accuracy. For interactions leading to the smallest prediction errors, the model closely replicated the ground-truth motion of single objects. For median and worst-case scenarios, small initial deviations in robot-object contact led to larger discrepancies in the predicted object trajectories, especially for elongated objects (e.g., screwdriver rotation) or in complex multi-object interactions.

#### 4.2. Planning Results

- **Model-Predictive Control (MPC) Performance (Table 2):** MRO-GWM (using a prediction horizon of 8 at 5 Hz) demonstrated successful application in an MPC setting for non-prehensile manipulation tasks.
 - **Task 1 (Push Object to Position):** Achieved a success rate of 0.77 (at a 1 cm distance threshold) and 0.80 (at 2 cm), with a mean final goal distance of 2.87 cm, a notable reduction from the initial mean distance of 14.17 cm. The task was successful in the majority of episodes.
 - **Task 2 (Clear Middle):** This multi-object task, requiring up to 5 objects to be pushed out of a central area, yielded a success rate of 0.66 (all objects at 0 cm threshold) and 0.72 (all objects at 2 cm threshold). Success rate per object was higher at 0.82 (0 cm threshold). The mean final goal distance was 3.12 cm, significantly reduced from the initial 30.4 cm.
- **Observed Failure Cases:** Visual inspection of failed MPC episodes revealed several common issues:
 - **Kinematic Limits:** The robot sometimes got stuck at its kinematic workspace limits, particularly when pushing objects towards the edge of the table.
 - **Object Geometry Challenges:** The robot struggled to manipulate elongated objects like screwdrivers and spoons, suggesting difficulties in accurately predicting the motion of such geometries under various contacts.
 - **Planning Strategy Limitations:** In some cases, the robot switched between approaching different target objects in Task 2, eventually running out of time, indicating potential for refinement in the planning cost functions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MRO-GWM represents a significant advancement in the development of action-conditional world models for complex robotic manipulation tasks involving rigid objects. Its primary significance lies in its ability to learn multi-object rigid body dynamics directly from interactions using a novel object-centric Gaussian splatting representation and a specialized spatio-temporal transformer. This approach enables the model to handle challenging aspects of real-world scenes, such as arbitrary object shapes, close-proximity interactions, and partial observations due to occlusions, without relying on explicit physics engines or full shape information. The demonstrated capability to generalize to unseen objects, albeit with performance sensitivity to training object diversity, further underscores its potential for practical application.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:
- **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation:** By providing an effective world model for predicting action consequences, MRO-GWM can empower intelligent agents with superior planning capabilities for non-prehensile manipulation tasks. This is particularly relevant for tasks in unstructured environments, such as household robotics, where objects are diverse and interactions are complex.
- **Advancements in 3D Scene Understanding and Dynamics Modeling:** The use of object-centric Gaussian splatting for dynamics modeling pushes the boundaries of explicit 3D representations beyond rendering, demonstrating its utility for predictive tasks. The novel spatio-temporal transformer architecture, with its dedicated attention mechanisms, contributes to the field of sequence modeling for physically-grounded tasks.
- **Foundation for Generalization in AI:** World models are key to achieving generalization in AI agents. MRO-GWM's ability to learn task-agnostic dynamics from visual data contributes to building more general-purpose robots that can adapt to varying scenarios.
- **Future Research Avenues:** The identified limitations, such as the reliance on ground-truth object masks and pose estimates, highlight clear directions for future work. Integrating segmentation foundation models and real-time pose trackers could transition this approach from simulation to real-world robotic systems. Furthermore, extending the model to predict object deformations beyond rigid-body motion would broaden its applicability to a wider range of physical interactions. Improving real-time control capabilities is also a crucial step towards practical deployment.

In summary, MRO-GWM provides a robust framework for learning and utilizing action-conditional dynamics in multi-object 3D environments, laying groundwork for more capable and adaptive robotic systems.
