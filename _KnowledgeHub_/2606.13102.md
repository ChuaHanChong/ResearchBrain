# FTP-1: A Generalist Foundation Tactile Policy Across Tactile Sensors for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A new generalist foundation tactile policy, FTP-1, enables robots to learn and transfer tactile manipulation skills across various tactile sensors and robot platforms. It achieved an average success rate improvement of +17.2% on seen sensor setups and +31.6% on unseen tactile sensors in contact-rich tasks, showcasing robust generalization and reactive control.

## Problem

- Generalist robotic policies often lack the fine-grained tactile perception necessary for robustly performing contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- Existing tactile policies are largely sensor-specific, failing to generalize across the wide variety of tactile hardware due to heterogeneous signal formats, resolutions, and morphologies.
- The absence of a unified, transferable tactile policy baseline hinders systematic development and comparison of tactile manipulation research.

## Method

- Introduced **FTP-1**, a multi-expert foundation policy, integrating language, RGB vision, proprioception, and diverse tactile sensor inputs into a unified action space.
- Developed a ==Morphology-Aware Tactile Token Space== (MTTS) with sensor-specific heterogeneous encoders to abstract and standardize diverse tactile signals (image, array, state-type) into common token representations.
- Employed an independent, pretrained 300M-parameter Transformer as a dedicated tactile expert, trained on the new 3,000-hour FTP-1-Dataset, to process and fuse tactile tokens effectively.

## Results

- FTP-1 demonstrated superior performance on seen tactile sensor setups, achieving an average success rate improvement of **+17.2%** in contact-rich manipulation tasks compared to existing baselines.
- The policy exhibited strong transferability to previously unseen tactile sensor setups, showing a **+31.6%** higher average success rate than baselines without FTP-1's pretraining.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the learned transferable tactile knowledge from pretraining was responsible for FTP-1's robust adaptation, leading to more stable actions and improved reactive control during critical manipulation phases.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A unified, morphology-aware token space can effectively abstract and standardize heterogeneous tactile signals, enabling a single policy to interpret data from diverse sensor types and anatomies.
> - Utilizing an independent, pretrained tactile expert within a multi-expert architecture is crucial for learning transferable tactile skills across varied hardware and prevents interference with pre-existing vision-language knowledge.
> - Large-scale pretraining on a diverse, heterogeneous tactile dataset is essential for acquiring generalizable tactile manipulation knowledge, leading to robust adaptation to unseen tactile sensors and contact-rich tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic learning has seen a shift towards generalist policies, particularly in vision-based systems. These policies, such as π0.5, leverage large-scale heterogeneous datasets, computation, and model capacity to enable efficient adaptation to diverse embodiments, tasks, and environments. Such models provide robust initialization for various downstream manipulation tasks.

However, this generalist paradigm has not been extensively applied to tactile-based policy learning. Tactile sensing is recognized as essential for contact-rich, fine-grained manipulation tasks, which are prevalent in many real-world robotic applications. Existing pretrained tactile policies have typically been confined to specific sensor configurations, observation formats, and robot embodiments. This limitation stems from the inherent heterogeneity of tactile signals across different hardware; tactile sensors vary significantly in modality (e.g., image, array, state), resolution, physical morphology, and their response to contact. This diversity makes cross-sensor generalization challenging.

This work, FTP-1, addresses this gap by aiming to develop a generalist foundation policy for tactile manipulation. It investigates whether a single tactile policy can assimilate heterogeneous tactile experiences and transfer learned skills to new sensors and embodiments not encountered during its initial training. The paper positions FTP-1 as an initial unified foundation baseline for tactile manipulation, offering a shared model-level starting point for future tactile policy development, analogous to foundation models in other modalities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop FTP-1, a generalist foundation tactile policy that is pretrained to acquire transferable tactile manipulation abilities across diverse sensors and embodiments. The motivation for this work is rooted in several critical challenges and observations within tactile robotics:

- **Addressing Tactile Signal Heterogeneity:** Tactile sensors exhibit substantial variations in their output signals. These differences include:
 - **Modality:** Some sensors produce image-like data (e.g., GelSight), others provide an array of force/pressure readings (e.g., Contactile), and some output state-based signals (e.g., force-torque sensors).
 - **Resolution and Morphology:** Sensors differ in the density and arrangement of their sensing elements, as well as their physical shape and placement on a robot.
 - **Contact Response:** The way different sensors respond to and measure contact can vary considerably.
 These heterogeneities make it difficult to create a single, unified policy that can interpret and utilize data from various tactile sensors interchangeably.
- **Overcoming Sensor-Specific Limitations of Existing Policies:** While tactile feedback has been demonstrated to improve contact-rich manipulation, current tactile-based policies, including those that use pretraining, are typically designed for or confined to specific hardware setups. They lack the generalizability to adapt to new or different tactile sensors without substantial retraining or redesign. This "sensor-specific" design approach limits the scalability and broader applicability of tactile robotic skills.
- **Enabling Transferable Tactile Skills:** The research is motivated by the question of whether it is possible for a tactile policy to learn reusable manipulation skills from a diverse range of tactile experiences that can then generalize to novel sensor configurations and robot embodiments. This capability is critical for moving beyond task- and hardware-specific solutions towards more versatile and robust robotic systems.
- **Establishing a Foundation Model for Tactile Manipulation:** The broader motivation is to establish a foundational model for tactile perception and manipulation, akin to the success of vision-language foundation models in other robotic domains. Such a foundation model could provide a powerful, pretrained initialization that significantly reduces the data and computational resources required for developing new tactile manipulation skills on various platforms.
- **Improving Contact-Rich Manipulation:** Ultimately, the objective is to enhance the performance, robustness, and data efficiency of robots in contact-rich manipulation tasks by effectively integrating diverse tactile feedback through a generalist policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FTP-1 is designed as a multi-expert foundation policy built upon the π0.5 framework. It processes heterogeneous inputs—language instructions (ℓ), multi-view RGB observations (It), proprioception (st), and diverse tactile observations (Xt)—to predict a chunk of actions (Ât:t+H−1) within a Unified Action Space (UAS).

#### 3.1. Morphology-Aware Tactile Token Space (MTTS)

To address the heterogeneity of tactile signals, FTP-1 introduces the Morphology-Aware Tactile Token Space (MTTS).
- **Unified Interface:** MTTS defines 24 functional areas that serve as a standardized representation for tactile signals across various sensors. Each functional area is represented by a single token.
- **Functional Area Definition:** For in-hand tactile signals, slots 0-14 correspond to different regions of a hand. For force/torque signals from wrists and fingers, slots 15-20 are used. For parallel grippers, the two gripper-side sensors are mapped to the thumb-tip (slot 0) and index-fingertip (slot 1) functional areas, reflecting their grasping function. Slots 21-23 are reserved.
- **Embedding:** To provide positional and functional context, a learnable functional-area embedding, shared across all sensors, is added to each token. Separate embeddings are used for left and right hands.

#### 3.2. Heterogeneous Tactile Encoders for MTTS Tokenization

Despite the unified MTTS interface, raw tactile inputs still vary in shape and modality. FTP-1 employs heterogeneous, sensor-specific tactile encoders to project these varied inputs into the MTTS. For each sensor, signals are first grouped by their MTTS functional area, and then processed based on their observation type:
- **Image-type Inputs (e.g., GelSight):** Resized to 224x224, these inputs are processed by a lightweight sensor-specific Vision Transformer (ViT) (depth=3, width=768, head=12). The output is then passed to a shared pretrained T3 Transformer tactile encoder (depth=9, width=768, head=12). The final [CLS] token from this shared module is used as the tactile token.
- **Array-type Inputs (e.g., Contactile):** For inputs of shape (H, W, D), Fourier encoding is applied to the signal dimension. This is concatenated with the original input and processed by a 3-layer Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), followed by a 2-layer ReLU Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to generate the token.
- **State-type Inputs (e.g., force-torque):** For inputs of shape (D,), Fourier encoding is applied, concatenated with the original state, and processed by a 3-layer ReLU MLP.
- **Parameter Sharing:** Encoders are shared across functional areas of the same observation shape within a single sensor to minimize sensor-specific parameters and promote consistent modeling of tactile dynamics. All generated tactile tokens are LayerNorm-normalized, augmented with functional-area embeddings, and projected by a 2-layer GELU MLP to match the tactile expert input dimension.

#### 3.3. Shared Tactile Expert for Modality Fusion

FTP-1 uses a multi-expert architecture to fuse tactile tokens with vision-language (VL) perception for action generation.
- **Core Experts:**
 - A pretrained vision-language Transformer expert (initialized from π0.5) processes image observations and language instructions.
 - An independent shared tactile Transformer expert (300M parameters, width 1024, depth 18, MLP dim 4096, 8 attention heads, head dim 256) processes the unified tactile tokens.
 - A flow-matching Transformer action expert attends to the outputs of both the vision-language and tactile experts. The tactile expert does not attend to the action expert.
- **Proprioception Integration:** Proprioceptive state is Fourier-encoded, processed by a 3-layer ReLU MLP, LayerNorm-normalized, and then injected into the attention blocks via adaptive RMSNorm, fused with flow-matching timestep features from π0.5.
- **Design Rationale:** This independent tactile expert design is intended to: (1) facilitate the reuse of the pretrained tactile expert on unseen sensors during finetuning, (2) avoid perturbing the existing pretrained vision-language knowledge, and (3) enhance tactile processing efficiency and manipulation performance compared to adapter-based methods.

#### 3.4. FTP-1 Pretraining Datasets

FTP-1 is pretrained on a new large-scale, heterogeneous tactile manipulation dataset, termed FTP-1-Dataset.
- **Aggregation:** The dataset aggregates approximately 3,000 hours of human and robot demonstrations from 26 data sources.
- **Sensor Diversity:** It encompasses data from 21 distinct tactile sensors: 7 image-type, 5 array-type, and 9 state-type sensors. This includes existing datasets and a newly collected Sharpa North-FTP-1 dataset (4,000 long-horizon dexterous demonstrations).
- **Standardization:** All tactile annotations are organized according to the MTTS functional areas. Language instructions were rewritten using GPT-4o for increased linguistic diversity.
- **Data Balancing:** To manage data imbalance, dataset-specific sampling ratios are applied during training, resulting in a mixture of approximately 20% human-hand data, 30% dexterous-hand data, and 50% gripper data.
- **Training Details:** Pretraining was conducted on 48 NVIDIA H20 GPUs for 50k steps with a global batch size of 768. Finetuning used 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs for 20k steps. Z-score normalization was applied for state and action normalization.

#### 3.5. Unified Action Space (UAS)

FTP-1 utilizes a Unified Action Space (UAS) to manage the varying control signals across different robot embodiments. This space represents all robot commands as fixed-length sparse vectors (a = [aL, aR, aego, asup]). For hand joint actions, a Function–Actuator–Aligned Space (FAAS) is adopted, unifying action spaces by mapping functionally similar joints to consistent action slots. Unsupported control slots for a given embodiment are masked during training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FTP-1 involved finetuning experiments on both seen and unseen tactile sensor setups, along with ablation studies to determine the source of performance gains.

#### 4.1. Finetuning on Seen Tactile Sensor Setups

Experiments were conducted on three hardware configurations: UniVTAC (simulation), Sharpa North (real-world, dexterous hand), and Sharpa&Dexmate (real-world, dexterous hand), covering GelSight-Mini and Sharpa DTC sensors that were part of the pretraining data.

- **Overall Performance:** FTP-1 achieved the highest average success rate across all evaluated seen setups, reaching 62.5% (Table 2). This represented a 17.2 percentage point improvement over the next best baseline, π0.5 (45.3%).
- **UniVTAC Simulation Benchmark:** FTP-1 obtained an overall success rate of 66.66% and 59.5% when excluding two "Lift" tasks (which π0.5 largely solved without tactile input). This performance exceeded the second-best method by approximately 17.5 percentage points in both metrics (Table 1).
- **Real-World Sharpa North and Sharpa&Dexmate:** In these real-world dexterous manipulation tasks, FTP-1 demonstrated robust performance (62.5% average success rate). Notably, π0.5, a vision-language-action (VLA) model without tactile input, performed better (45.3%) than the other tactile-based baselines (Tactile-VLA and FTP-π0.5). This observation suggests that incorporating tactile input without an appropriate modality fusion strategy can negatively affect performance, particularly in long-horizon tasks. In contrast, FTP-1 generated more stable and smoother actions, exhibiting reactive adjustments in tasks involving contact force control, which the baselines struggled to maintain.

#### 4.2. Transfer to Unseen Tactile Sensor Setups

To assess FTP-1's transfer capabilities, experiments were performed on two setups featuring tactile sensors not included in the pretraining dataset: FlexivXense (with Xense image tactile sensors) and TactileUMI (with Contactile array sensors). For these setups, only the sensor-specific tactile encoder was trained from scratch, while other pretrained components were reused.

- **Significant Transfer Gains:** FTP-1 achieved an average success rate of 46.6% on the unseen sensor setups (Table 3). This marked a 31.6 percentage point increase compared to FTP-π0.5 (15%). This finding indicates that large-scale heterogeneous tactile pretraining enables effective finetuning performance under novel sensor conditions.
- **Comparison with Baselines:** π0.5 performed similarly to FTP-π0.5 (both 15% average success rate on unseen sensors), implying that merely adding a tactile branch without a suitable fusion prior and pretraining knowledge does not inherently improve performance or transferability.
- **Qualitative Observations:**
 - **Insert Hanoi:** FTP-1, along with π0.5, exhibited recovery behaviors. FTP-1 additionally demonstrated reactive insertion control, adjusting motion based on tactile feedback during misalignment, a capability absent in π0.5.
 - **Insert USB:** On this data-efficient task (100 demonstrations), FTP-1 showed greater stability and fewer undesired shaking motions during insertion compared to other models.
 - **Wipe Board:** Similar to tasks on seen sensors, FTP-1 maintained stable pressing force and consistent contact, while other models exhibited difficulties.
These results collectively indicate that FTP-1's pretraining provides a robust foundation for generalization, data efficiency, and reactive control even in novel tactile sensor environments.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study: Improvements from Pretrained Tactile Knowledge

An ablation study was conducted to differentiate whether performance gains originated from the similarity of the pretraining data distribution to downstream tasks (Hypothesis 1) or from transferable tactile manipulation knowledge learned during pretraining (Hypothesis 2). A "No-Tactile-Pretraining" (NTP-1) model was created, using the same pretraining setup and data as FTP-1 but without tactile inputs or architecture during pretraining. Tactile architecture was added to NTP-1 during finetuning.

- **Results:** On UniVTAC (seen sensor), NTP-1 outperformed FTP-π0.5 (50.0% vs. 45.16% average success rate), suggesting some benefit from the overall data distribution of FTP-1-Dataset. However, FTP-1 still significantly outperformed NTP-1 (66.66% vs. 50.0%). On FlexivXense (unseen sensor), FTP-1 substantially outperformed NTP-1 by 37.5 percentage points (Table 6, Fig. 7).
- **Conclusion:** This ablation supports Hypothesis 2, confirming that the pretrained tactile branch in FTP-1 acquires general tactile manipulation knowledge that is transferable and beneficial for downstream contact-rich tasks, particularly for generalization to unseen tactile sensor setups.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FTP-1 represents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing the long-standing challenge of tactile signal heterogeneity. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Establishment of a Generalist Foundation Tactile Policy:** FTP-1 is presented as the first generalist foundation tactile policy that can acquire and transfer tactile manipulation abilities across a diverse range of sensors and embodiments. This shifts the paradigm from sensor-specific tactile policies to a more unified and scalable approach.
- **Unified Interface for Tactile Signals:** The introduction of the Morphology-Aware Tactile Token Space (MTTS) provides a structured and functional interface for integrating disparate tactile data modalities (image, array, state). This approach addresses a fundamental hurdle in cross-sensor generalization, enabling a single policy to interpret and utilize inputs from various tactile hardware.
- **Demonstrated Transferability to Unseen Sensors:** A key finding is FTP-1's ability to transfer learned skills to tactile sensors not encountered during pretraining, achieving a significant performance gain of over 31% on unseen setups. This empirical evidence supports the notion that general tactile manipulation knowledge can be acquired through large-scale, heterogeneous pretraining and effectively generalized to novel hardware.
- **Improved Contact-Rich Manipulation:** The consistent performance improvements demonstrated across various finetuning tasks, especially in contact-rich scenarios, indicate that FTP-1 enables more stable, smoother, and reactive control. This enhancement is crucial for tasks requiring precise force application, fine-grained adjustments, and robust interaction with objects.
- **Shared Model-Level Starting Point:** By aggregating a substantial and diverse dataset (approximately 3,000 hours from 21 sensors) and providing a pretrained model, FTP-1 establishes a foundational baseline for future research in tactile manipulation. This shared starting point can reduce the effort and resources required for developing new tactile policies, accelerating innovation in the field.
- **Avenues for Future Research:** The work opens several promising directions. The authors note limitations in the current scale and diversity of the pretraining dataset, suggesting that further aggregation could yield additional benefits. Moreover, extending the heterogeneous encoding framework to incorporate tactile prediction and prediction-based low-level control represents a logical next step to enhance active tactile sensing and servoing capabilities.

Overall, FTP-1 provides a foundational framework for generalist tactile intelligence in robotics, demonstrating that the challenge of tactile heterogeneity can be systematically addressed to enable transferable manipulation skills across diverse robotic platforms.
