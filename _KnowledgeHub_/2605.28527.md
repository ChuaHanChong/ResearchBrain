# What Frozen VLAs Already Know About Success: A Probing Study of Value-Like Structure in Foundation Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Peking University and China Agricultural University demonstrated that frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model representations implicitly encode value-like information correlated with task success. This signal can be linearly decoded and leveraged at test time through a simulator-backed action selection process, improving robotic manipulation success rates by up to 17.67 percentage points on certain challenging tasks without requiring policy retraining.

## Problem

- Imitation-trained VLA policies typically lack explicit internal mechanisms for estimating rewards, progress, or future task success.
- Traditional test-time action selection methods often rely on external reward models or world models, which require separate training or complex computational overhead.
- It was unknown if frozen VLA internal representations implicitly contained sufficient information about task outcomes to influence action selection beneficially.

## Method

- Developed a "probing-to-selection" protocol to systematically investigate value-like signals within frozen VLA representations.
- Trained linear ridge probes offline to decode a discounted Monte-Carlo "value-like" target (reflecting proximity to success) from various frozen VLA/VLM features, including Pi0.5, OpenVLA, DINOv2, and CLIP.
- Implemented a simulator-backed, best-of-K candidate action selection procedure at test time, where the decoded value-like signal guides action choices for a frozen Pi0.5 policy.

## Results

- Offline probes achieved R² values of approximately 0.51-0.55 for task-split prediction of value-like targets using VLA/VLM features, significantly surpassing scalar baselines (e.g., progress R² of ~0.03).
- Same-step matched-pair controls showed the Pi0.5 probe achieved **94.22%** pairwise ordering accuracy, confirming the robustness of the value-like signal against temporal and task-identity confounds.
- Value-guided action selection improved success rates by **17.67** percentage points on 'push-plate' (to **44.3%**) and **8.33** percentage points on 'wine-rack' (to **44.0%**) compared to greedy decoding on hard LIBERO tasks, with statistical significance.
- The improvements came with increased computational cost, approximately doubling wall-clock time per episode compared to random selection and over twenty times that of greedy decoding.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frozen VLA and VLM representations, despite being trained solely for action imitation, robustly encode a decodable, value-like signal indicative of task success, which is not attributable to simple shortcuts like task identity or elapsed time.
> - This implicitly encoded value-like signal can be effectively leveraged at inference time to enhance robotic task success rates without modifying the underlying policy or requiring external reward models.
> - The proposed probing-to-selection framework provides a systematic diagnostic tool for uncovering and utilizing emergent properties within robot foundation model representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of robot foundation models, representation learning diagnostics, and test-time action selection. Recent advancements in robotics have seen the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, such as RT-1, RT-2, PaLM-E, OpenVLA, and Pi0.5, which are primarily trained through imitation learning. These policies are designed to generate actions based on visual observations and language instructions, typically without explicit supervision for estimating rewards, progress, or future success. The current study investigates whether, despite this training objective, the internal representations of these frozen VLA models implicitly encode information related to task outcomes.

The work draws inspiration from the field of probing studies, where linear classifiers are used to reveal the semantic content of hidden representations in various domains, including natural language processing and computer vision. By extending this diagnostic approach to embodied control, the authors aim to characterize the representational capabilities of VLA models beyond their direct action generation function.

Furthermore, the paper connects to methods for test-time action selection, such as sampling-based control and model-predictive control, which typically rely on external reward models, cost functions, or world models to evaluate candidate actions. A key distinction of this research is its exploration of whether an *internal*, representation-derived score, extracted from the *frozen* VLA policy itself, can guide action choices without requiring a separately trained reward model or any policy updates. This contrasts with existing approaches by seeking to leverage an inherent capability within the policy's representation rather than introducing an external scoring module. The study thus addresses an underexplored aspect of VLA models: their implicit understanding of task success and its potential for improving robotic behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are twofold: first, to ascertain whether frozen representations within imitation-trained VLA policies encode information about task outcomes, such as success or progress, even though their training objective does not explicitly demand this; and second, to determine if any such decodable information can be utilized to influence action selection at test time to improve policy performance without requiring policy retraining.

The motivation for the first objective stems from a fundamental question about the nature of VLA representations. If these representations, trained solely for action imitation, also implicitly capture outcome-relevant state information, it would imply that they possess a more comprehensive understanding of the task environment than previously acknowledged. This would position VLA representations as more than just conditional action generators, suggesting they also carry an intrinsic evaluative capacity. Such a finding could change how VLA models are conceptualized and utilized in robot learning.

For the second objective, the motivation is to explore a novel pathway for improving robotic decision-making. If outcome-related information is indeed embedded in frozen VLA features, it could potentially be read out and used to rank candidate actions. This approach would offer an alternative to methods that rely on separately trained reward models or complex predictive models, providing a way to influence policy decisions at inference time without incurring the computational expense or data requirements of retraining the core policy. The ability to enhance performance via an internal signal from a frozen model would be a significant contribution to practical robot deployment.

A crucial aspect of the research's motivation is to rigorously deconfound any observed signals from mere "surface-level shortcuts." The authors acknowledge that simple factors such as task identity, elapsed time, or coarse visual statistics could correlate with success in collected trajectory data, potentially leading probes to appear successful without truly encoding action-relevant information. Therefore, a significant motivation is to design stringent controls that explicitly rule out these spurious correlations, ensuring that any detected value-like signal genuinely reflects an understanding of progress or outcome within the policy's internal state. By addressing these potential confounding factors, the study aims to provide robust evidence for the inherent informational content of VLA representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a staged "probing-to-selection" protocol designed to systematically investigate the existence, robustness, and actionability of value-like signals within frozen VLA representations. This protocol consists of three main stages: offline probing, deconfounding with matched controls, and test-time candidate selection.

**Problem Setup and Value-Like Signals:**
The study considers language-conditioned manipulation episodes, where a policy observes an image, a language instruction, and proprioceptive state, producing actions or action chunks. Each trajectory is associated with a binary task outcome (success or failure). To create a "value-like" target (`v_t`), a discounted Monte-Carlo approach is used: for successful trajectories of length `T`, `v_t = γ^(T-1-t)` where `γ = 0.99`. This assigns higher values to states closer to the successful completion of an episode. For failed trajectories, `v_t = 0` for all timesteps. This target is designed to reflect information correlated with eventual success, explicitly differentiated from Bellman-consistent value functions typically learned in reinforcement learning, as it avoids bootstrapped estimates and relies directly on observed trajectory outcomes. Nuisance variables such as scalar progress (`t/T`), time-to-go (`(T-t)/T`), and task identity are also recorded as baselines for comparison.

**Offline Probing of Frozen Representations:**
In the first stage, frozen features `ϕ_m(o_t, g)` are extracted from various models (`m`), including VLA/VLM layers (e.g., Pi0.5, OpenVLA, SmolVLA), general-purpose visual encoders (DINOv2, CLIP), proprioceptive state, random projections, and scalar nuisance features. For each feature family, a linear ridge probe `v̂_t = w^⊤~ϕ_m(o_t, g) + b` is trained to predict the value-like target `v_t`. The features `~ϕ` are standardized using training-set statistics, and the ridge penalty is determined via cross-validation. Performance is evaluated using metrics such as R², Spearman correlation, RMSE, and MAE on held-out examples.

Two splitting regimes are employed:
1. **Demo split:** Trajectories are grouped by suite, task, and source demonstration, with train/test splits sampled within each task. This assesses the probe's ability to capture value-like structure within known tasks.
2. **Task split:** Groups are defined by suite and task, meaning held-out examples come from entirely unseen tasks. This represents a stricter test of generalization.
Crucially, task identity, progress, and time-to-go are not provided as privileged covariates to the VLA probes but are trained as separate baselines to control for their explanatory power.

**Deconfounding with Matched Controls:**
To address potential shortcuts, a "same-step matched-pair control" is implemented. After training, probes are evaluated by grouping data points by the same task name and timestep. Within each group, pairs are formed where one data point has a significantly higher value-like label (difference of at least 0.20) than the other. A probe is considered correct for a pair if it assigns a higher score to the data point with the higher value-like label. A "label-shuffle control," where the positive member of each pair is randomly swapped, serves as a negative control to establish chance performance. This rigorous control aims to demonstrate that the probe captures within-task, within-timestep ordering information, rather than merely exploiting task identity or elapsed time.

**Test-Time Candidate Selection:**
The third stage assesses the behavioral utility of the decoded signal. A best-of-K selection procedure is applied using the frozen Pi0.5 policy. At each replan step, K=16 action chunks are sampled using deterministic seeds, and a short prefix (five actions) of each chunk is considered. The core of this stage is a "simulator-backed teacher selector":
1. For each candidate prefix, the simulator is restored to the current snapshot, the prefix is rolled out, and the resulting observation, accumulated reward (`r_k`), and the probe score (`s_k`) extracted from the final observation `ϕ(o^(k)_t+h, g)` are recorded.
2. The selector prioritizes successful candidate prefixes: if any candidate leads to task success during its short rollout, the one with the highest probe score `s_k` is chosen.
3. If no candidate achieves success in the short rollout, the candidate maximizing a combined score of normalized reward `z(r_k)` and normalized probe score `z(s_k)` is chosen.
4. The simulator is then restored to the original snapshot, and only the selected prefix is executed.
This procedure is contrasted with greedy decoding (executing the policy's direct output) and random selection (choosing a candidate uniformly at random). The use of simulator rollouts highlights that this is a diagnostic intervention to test the signal's actionability, acknowledging the associated computational cost.

**Experimental Setup:**
The main benchmark for experiments is LIBERO-Goal, utilizing a dataset of 311,719 frame-level rows from 1,400 mixed successful and failed trajectories. CALVIN-D and RobotWin datasets are used for cross-benchmark checks. Online experiments are performed with the official Pi0.5 LIBERO policy, focusing on "hard non-ceiling" tasks where greedy decoding success rates are below approximately 40%. Statistical analysis includes Wilson 95% confidence intervals for success rates and McNemar exact p-values for paired strategy contrasts. Reproducibility is ensured through multiple random seeds for probe training and distributed online rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings across the three stages of its protocol, providing evidence for the decodability, robustness, and actionability of value-like signals in frozen VLA representations.

**Representation Evidence and Deconfounding (Tables 1 and 2):**
Offline probing on LIBERO-Goal demonstrated that value-like targets are decodable from a wide range of frozen representations. OpenVLA-OFT, OpenVLA, Pi0.5, SmolVLA (VLA/VLM families), DINOv2 (self-supervised vision), and CLIP (contrastive vision-language) features consistently yielded R² values for the task split between approximately 0.51 and 0.55. These results significantly surpassed those obtained from scalar nuisance baselines: progress and time-to-go features achieved an R² of about 0.03, and task identity features were near zero. Proprioception features also exhibited low predictive power (R² ≈ 0.11). Even when compared to random projections, which showed non-trivial R² values around 0.39 (suggesting some signal from dataset geometry alone), the learned VLA and visual features performed substantially better. An informative exception was Pi0, a flow-matching action head, which yielded a task-split R² of only 0.07, suggesting that heavy specialization for action generation might reduce the retention of outcome-related structure. Cross-benchmark checks on CALVIN-D and RobotWin showed weaker or less generalizable signals, indicating context-dependency.

To rule out shortcuts, the same-step matched-pair controls were applied. The primary Pi0.5 probe achieved a pairwise ordering accuracy of 94.22% on the combined dataset, while a label-shuffled negative control remained at 50.05% (chance). Similar high accuracies (92.31% to 95.06%) were observed for individual tasks such as open middle drawer, push plate, and wine rack. Across multiple runs and probe configurations, the mean pairwise accuracy remained above 92%, with shuffled controls consistently around 50%. This robust gap indicates that the probe's ability to order outcomes is not merely a consequence of encoding task identity or elapsed time.

**Online Positive Evidence (Table 3):**
The study then investigated whether the decoded value-like signal could influence action choice. Using a simulator-backed selection procedure with the frozen Pi0.5 policy, the value-guided strategy demonstrated improved success rates on specific "hard non-ceiling" tasks from the LIBERO-Goal benchmark.

On the `push-plate` task, value-guided selection achieved a success rate of 44.3%, marking a 10.67 percentage point (pp) improvement over random selection (33.7%) and a 17.67 pp improvement over greedy decoding (26.7%). The McNemar p-value for the contrast between value-guided and random selection was 0.00294, indicating a statistically significant gain.
For the `wine-rack` task, value-guided selection yielded 44.0% success, an 8.00 pp improvement over random selection (36.0%) and an 8.33 pp improvement over greedy decoding (35.7%). The McNemar p-value for this task was 0.0264, also suggesting a significant improvement.
In contrast, on the `open middle drawer` task, value-guided selection showed only marginal gains (42.4% vs. 36.9% for random, +5.56 pp), which were not statistically significant (p=0.0614 for the aggregate hard-3 suite against random). This result helps delineate the boundary conditions under which the selection strategy provides benefits, indicating that improvement is not universal and depends on task structure and the diversity of sampled candidates.

The observed improvements came with an increased computational cost. Value-guided selection typically required approximately twice the wall-clock time per episode compared to random selection, and over twenty times the time of greedy decoding (Table 8). This cost is attributed to the simulator rollouts required for evaluating multiple candidate action prefixes.

**Goal-Conditioning Diagnostics (Tables 9 and 10):**
Goal-swap experiments confirmed that the probe's operation is not purely visual but is influenced by the language goal. While a vision-only control showed no feature or score change when the goal text was altered, Pi0.5 VLM features exhibited measurable shifts under language swaps. Probe scores also moved, although the direction of the change was not uniformly aligned with preferring the original goal across all tasks, suggesting that the probe inherits a goal-conditioned representation from the VLM stack but does not function as a perfectly calibrated reward model.

In summary, the findings indicate that frozen VLA representations contain a decodable, value-like signal robust against task and temporal shortcuts. This signal can be harnessed to improve robotic manipulation performance in certain challenging tasks by guiding action selection at test time, albeit with additional computational resources.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of emergent properties within robot foundation models and offers a novel approach to enhancing their performance.

**Fundamental Understanding of VLA Representations:** The primary significance lies in the demonstration that imitation-trained VLA policies implicitly encode more information than their explicit training objective demands. The presence of a robust, decodable "value-like" signal related to future success within frozen VLA, VLM, and general-purpose visual encoders suggests that these models develop an internal, evaluative understanding of their environment without explicit reward supervision. This expands the conceptualization of VLA representations beyond mere action generators, indicating an inherent capacity to represent task progress and outcome.

**Enhancing Test-Time Performance without Retraining:** The study's behavioral findings show that this implicitly encoded signal can be leveraged to improve robotic manipulation success rates on specific challenging tasks (e.g., push-plate, wine-rack). Crucially, this improvement is achieved at test time, without any policy updates, additional training data, or external reward models. This capability provides a practical avenue for improving the robustness and success of deployed robotic policies where retraining might be infeasible or costly. It offers a method to extract more value from existing, frozen foundation models.

**Diagnostic Framework:** The staged probing-to-selection protocol itself serves as a valuable diagnostic tool. It provides a systematic and rigorous method for analyzing the informational content of hidden representations and for testing the actionability of any signals extracted. This framework can be applied to other foundation models to understand their internal mechanisms and uncover under-exploited capabilities.

**Potential Impact and Future Directions:**
- **Reduced Development Cycle:** By enabling performance improvements without policy retraining, the approach could potentially shorten development cycles for robotic applications, making it faster to adapt or refine behaviors.
- **Informed Policy Design:** The findings might inspire future VLA architectures or training objectives that explicitly encourage or better utilize these implicit value-like signals, potentially leading to more sample-efficient or robust learning.
- **Beyond Action Selection:** The detected value-like signal could have broader applications, such as enabling more informed failure detection, assisting in human-robot collaboration by providing an internal measure of progress, or guiding exploration in reinforcement learning scenarios.
- **Computational Efficiency:** A key limitation identified is the computational cost associated with simulator-backed candidate evaluation. Future work could focus on replacing the simulator-backed teacher selector with a learned scorer that can predict outcomes or value-like scores more efficiently. Initial explorations into teacher-student amortization (Appendix A) suggest this is a plausible extension.
- **Generalizability of Signals:** Further research is needed to understand what factors determine the availability of useful candidate alternatives within a policy's sampling distribution and why the gains are task-dependent. Investigating the specific characteristics of tasks where this method yields significant improvements versus those where it does not will be crucial for broader applicability.
