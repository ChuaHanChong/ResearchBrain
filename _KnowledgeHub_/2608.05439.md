# SCP-NL2TL: Selective Conformal Prediction with Semantic Verification for Natural Language to Temporal Logic Specifications

> [!summary] Summary
> SCP-NL2TL presents a selective translation framework that equips black-box Natural Language to Temporal Logic (NL2TL) systems with an accept-or-abstain decision layer, statistically guaranteeing a user-defined bound on the rate of accepted incorrect specifications. The framework achieved consistent risk control across various logic languages and improved discrimination of errors with a fusion score achieving 0.706-0.920 AUROC, while an Out-of-Distribution (OOD) filter enhanced robustness to distribution shifts.

## Problem

- Existing Natural Language to Temporal Logic (NL2TL) translators always produce a specification, lacking a mechanism to signal unreliability, which poses significant risks in safety-critical autonomous systems.
- Standard evaluation metrics like exact-match accuracy measure average performance but do not provide trustworthiness for individual translations, crucial for downstream planning that requires a single, executable output.
- The black-box nature of many advanced NL2TL models prevents access to internal confidence scores for reliability assessment, and statistical guarantees can be invalidated by distribution shifts in real-world environments.

## Method

- Introduces **SCP-NL2TL**, a model-agnostic, black-box selective translation framework that adds an accept-or-abstain decision layer to any NL2TL translator, controlling the joint risk of accepting incorrect specifications.
- Develops a ==semantic consistency score== by fusing back-translation fidelity (semantic agreement between original instruction and back-translated formula) and self-consistency (stability of repeated translations).
- Integrates ==Conformal Risk Control (CRC)== to statistically bound the joint risk and an instruction-level ==conformal anomaly detector== to filter out-of-distribution inputs, enhancing robustness to deployment drift.

## Results

- The fusion score (S_fu) consistently achieved the highest AUROC (**0.706** for STL, **0.920** for SpaTiaL), demonstrating superior ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect translations compared to individual scoring channels.
- Conformal Risk Control consistently satisfied the target risk budget `alpha` in **all 18** evaluated settings for a high-error GPT-5.2 translator, effectively utilizing the budget, while a coverage-calibrated baseline violated it in **13 out of 18** settings.
- The instruction-level OOD filter successfully reduced joint risk in **5 out of 6** cross-tier shift scenarios, bringing **4** of them back below the target budget, highlighting its effectiveness in maintaining robustness against distribution shifts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing back-translation fidelity and self-consistency provides complementary signals, significantly improving the discrimination between correct and incorrect temporal logic specifications, achieving AUROC values up to 0.920.
> - Conformal Risk Control (CRC) effectively controls the joint risk of accepting erroneous specifications, providing distribution-free statistical guarantees that reliably adhere to user-specified error budgets for individual outputs.
> - An instruction-level out-of-distribution (OOD) filter serves as a crucial upstream safeguard, maintaining the robustness of risk guarantees under distribution shifts by proactively deferring atypical inputs before translation, also offering computational benefits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is positioned within the field of Natural Language to Temporal Logic (NL2TL) translation, a critical area for enabling autonomous systems (such as robots and cyber-physical systems) to interpret human instructions and execute formally verifiable behaviors. Formal specification languages like Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), Signal Temporal Logic (STL), and geometric Spatio-Temporal Logic (SpaTiaL) offer precise means to describe system requirements, but their formulation typically demands specialized expertise. Recent advancements in semantic parsing, sequence models, and large language models (LLMs) have aimed to bridge this gap by automating the translation process from natural language to formal specifications.

However, a significant limitation of existing NL2TL systems is their tendency to always produce a specification, irrespective of its reliability or semantic accuracy. This presents substantial risks in safety-critical applications, where an incorrect formal specification could lead to unintended or hazardous system behavior. Standard evaluation metrics, such as exact-match accuracy, quantify average model performance but do not provide a measure of trustworthiness for individual translations.

This research addresses this gap by integrating principles from conformal prediction (CP), a statistical framework for quantifying prediction reliability with finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees. Traditional CP methods typically generate prediction sets, which are not directly applicable to scenarios where a single executable formula is required for downstream planning. The paper builds upon recent extensions of CP, specifically conformal risk control, which enables calibration against arbitrary bounded monotone losses, thereby allowing for accept-or-abstain decisions. It also draws from selective conformal procedures that incorporate a mechanism to withhold a response if the calibration data is insufficient or the input is deemed out-of-distribution (OOD).

Unlike some related works that modify the internal architecture of the translator or use conformal prediction during the formula generation process (e.g., via conformal QA steps), this work develops a model-agnostic, black-box selection layer that operates on the output of any existing translator. Furthermore, it distinguishes itself from other LLM-based conformal prediction applications by not relying on internal model confidences or token likelihoods, which are often inaccessible in black-box systems, instead employing semantic signals derived solely from the input instruction and the generated specification. This approach aims to establish a foundation for developing trustworthy natural language interfaces by equipping AI systems with the capacity to identify when their generated specifications might be unreliable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the trustworthiness and reliability of Natural Language to Temporal Logic (NL2TL) translation systems. This is achieved by developing a selective translation framework that not only generates formal specifications but also determines when these specifications can be confidently accepted for execution in safety-critical applications.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in current NL2TL systems:

1. **Risk in Safety-Critical Applications**: Autonomous systems, such such as robots and cyber-physical systems, increasingly rely on natural language instructions. Translating these instructions into formal specifications is crucial for automated planning, reasoning, and formal verification. However, if a translation is semantically incorrect—even if syntactically valid—it can lead to unintended or unsafe actions, posing significant risks.
2. **Inherent Unreliability of Current Translators**: Existing NL2TL models, including those leveraging advanced large language models, typically produce a formal specification for every input natural language instruction. These models do not inherently possess a mechanism to signal when a translation might be unreliable or when it fails to accurately capture the user's intent.
3. **Limitations of Standard Evaluation Metrics**: Conventional metrics like exact-match accuracy assess the average performance of a translator over a dataset. They do not provide insights into the trustworthiness of an individual translation, which is critical for real-world deployment where each decision carries potential consequences.
4. **Requirement for a Single, Executable Specification**: Downstream planning and verification systems typically require a single, definitive formal specification to execute. Traditional conformal prediction, which often yields a *set* of candidate predictions, is therefore not directly suitable for this application. A mechanism for making an "accept-or-abstain" decision on a single output is necessary.
5. **Black-Box Nature of Advanced Translators**: Many high-performing NL2TL translators are black-box models, meaning their internal parameters, confidence scores, or token likelihoods are not accessible. This prevents the use of internal model signals for assessing translation reliability.
6. **Vulnerability to Distribution Shift**: The statistical guarantees offered by conformal prediction rely on the assumption of exchangeability between calibration and test data. In dynamic real-world environments, distribution shifts in user instructions can invalidate these guarantees, leading to reduced reliability.

To address these motivations, the research establishes the following key objectives:

- To design and implement a *selective translation framework* that can augment any black-box NL2TL translator with an accept-or-abstain mechanism.
- To statistically *bound the rate* at which incorrect temporal logic specifications are accepted for execution (joint risk) at a user-specified confidence level `alpha`.
- To introduce a novel *semantic consistency score* that combines external signals from back-translation fidelity and internal signals from semantic agreement across repeated translations, improving the discrimination between correct and incorrect specifications.
- To integrate an *instruction-level conformal anomaly detector* to identify and filter out-of-distribution inputs before translation occurs, thereby enhancing the robustness of the framework under potential distribution shifts.

These objectives aim to enable AI systems to self-assess the reliability of their outputs, thereby paving the way for more robust and trustworthy natural language interfaces in safety-critical autonomous applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a selective translation framework, SCP-NL2TL, designed to augment any black-box Natural Language to Temporal Logic (NL2TL) translator with an accept-or-abstain decision layer. The framework operates in three main stages: an instruction-level filter, semantic consistency scoring, and risk-calibrated selection. The overarching objective is to maximize the acceptance rate of translations while maintaining a user-defined budget `alpha` for the joint risk, defined as the probability that a translation is both accepted and incorrect.

#### 3.1. Formal Logic Languages and Problem Formulation

The framework is presented as general for any formal logic language adhering to a compositional grammar (e.g., LTL, STL, SpaTiaL). Given a natural language instruction `x`, a black-box translator `f` produces a formal specification `hat_phi = f(x)`. A selection function `g(x, hat_phi)` then decides whether to accept (`g=1`) or abstain (`g=0`). Translation error `z` is defined as 1 if `hat_phi` is not semantically equivalent to the reference `phi_star`, and 0 otherwise. The goal is to control the joint risk `R_joint = E[g(x, hat_phi) * z(x, hat_phi, phi_star)]` to be less than or equal to `alpha`.

#### 3.2. Semantic Consistency Scoring

To assess the reliability of a translation without access to internal model confidences, the framework employs two complementary black-box semantic signals that are combined into a nonconformity score `S(x, hat_phi)`:

1. **Back-translation Fidelity (S_bt)**:
 - A large language model (LLM) acts as a back-translator `B`, converting the generated formal specification `hat_phi` back into natural language (`hat_u = B(hat_phi)`).
 - Another LLM, serving as a judge, evaluates the semantic consistency between the original instruction `x` and the back-translated `hat_u`. This evaluation is performed across four dimensions: logical structure, temporal operators, time constraints, and overall meaning, yielding an average agreement score `A(x, hat_u)` between 0 and 1.
 - The back-translation score `S_bt(x, hat_phi)` is computed as `1 - A(x, B(hat_phi))`. Invalid or unparsable formulas are automatically assigned the maximum score of 1. This approach localizes logic-specific knowledge within the back-translator, while the judge operates in the natural language domain.

2. **Self-consistency (S_sc)**:
 - For stochastic translators, this score measures the stability of `k` repeated translations of the same instruction `x`.
 - The score `S_sc(x)` is defined as `1 - (1/k) * max_j |{m : hat_phi^(m) = hat_phi^(j)}|`, where `hat_phi^(m)` are the `k` samples and ` = ` denotes semantic equivalence under logic-specific canonicalization. This extends the concept of self-consistency to formal specifications where exact semantic equivalence can be checked.

3. **Fusion Score (S_fu)**:
 - The default reliability score `S_fu` is an equal-weight fusion of the two complementary signals: `S_fu = 0.5 * (S_bt + S_sc)`. This fusion aims to leverage the strengths of `S_bt` (detecting semantic mismatches) and `S_sc` (capturing translation uncertainty).

#### 3.3. Risk-Calibrated Selection

The nonconformity score `S` is then used with a calibration dataset `D_cal = {(x_i, hat_phi_i, phi_star_i)}` to select an acceptance threshold `hat_tau` using Conformal Risk Control (CRC).

- For each calibration example, `S_i = S(x_i, hat_phi_i)` and `z_i = 1[hat_phi_i != phi_star_i]` are computed.
- The empirical joint risk for a candidate threshold `tau` is `R_hat(tau) = (1/n) * sum(1[S_i <= tau] * z_i)`.
- The threshold `hat_tau` is chosen as the largest `tau` such that `(n/(n+1)) * R_hat(tau) + (1/(n+1)) <= alpha`. This calibration rule provides a distribution-free guarantee that the expected joint risk `R_joint` will not exceed `alpha` for future exchangeable test inputs (Theorem 1).
- The final decision rule is `g(x, hat_phi) = 1[S(x, hat_phi) <= hat_tau]`.
- A "feasibility floor" `alpha_l = (L_0 + 1) / (n + 1)` exists, below which no valid threshold can be certified (leading to full abstention), where `L_0` is the minimum number of calibration errors in any non-empty acceptance region.

#### 3.4. Filtering Out-of-Distribution Instructions

To enhance robustness against distribution shifts that might invalidate the exchangeability assumption of CRC, an instruction-level conformal anomaly detector is introduced as an upstream filter.

- This filter assesses the typicality of an instruction `x` based on its embedding `e(x)`.
- A `k`-nearest-neighbor (kNN) distance `D(x)` to a fixed reference set of training-sourced instruction embeddings `X_ref` is computed: `D(x) = (1/k) * sum_{x_j in N_k(x)} ||e(x) - e(x_j)||_2`.
- A conformal p-value `p(x)` is then calculated, indicating how typical `x` is compared to a set of deferral-calibration instructions.
- If `p(x) < delta` (where `delta` is a user-defined deferral level), the instruction is deferred *before* any translation attempt, meaning the system abstains from generating a specification for it. This guarantees that in-distribution instructions are deferred with probability at most `delta` (Theorem 2). This pre-screening step aims to remove inputs for which the statistical guarantee cannot be provided, thus improving the reliability of the subsequent CRC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the SCP-NL2TL framework was conducted across three formal specification languages: Signal Temporal Logic (STL), Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), and geometric Spatio-Temporal Logic (SpaTiaL). Each language included three difficulty tiers (D2–D4). Two types of LLM translators were evaluated: a fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B model for SpaTiaL (with a raw error rate of approximately 15%) and a few-shot GPT-5.2 model for STL and LTL (with a raw error rate of approximately 48%). A fine-tuned T5 model (~2% error) was also used for self-consistency score applicability verification. The evaluation involved 100 random calibration-test resplits, with thresholds calibrated per language and tier.

#### 4.1. Scoring Ablation

The quality of the nonconformity scores was evaluated using Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), which measures the ability of a score to rank incorrect translations higher than correct ones.

- **Fusion Score Performance**: The fusion score (S_fu), combining back-translation fidelity (S_bt) and self-consistency (S_sc), consistently achieved the highest AUROC values across both STL and SpaTiaL (0.706 for STL and 0.920 for SpaTiaL). This indicates its superior ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect translations compared to individual scoring channels.
- **Complementary Information**: The analysis revealed that S_sc and S_bt provide complementary information. S_sc detects "uncertain" failures (e.g., inconsistent translations across multiple samples), while S_bt effectively identifies "confident" semantic mismatches where the translator generates a plausible-looking but incorrect formula. For instance, on STL, while 77.8% of inconsistent translations were erroneous, S_bt still provided discriminative power (AUROC 0.600) among self-consistent translations, where 37.5% remained incorrect.
- **Score Resolution and Feasibility**: Beyond AUROC, score resolution significantly impacted the feasibility of calibration. For STL, S_sc alone struggled to certify risks below 0.270 due to tied scores concentrating calibration errors, leading to complete abstention at tighter risk budgets (e.g., alpha = 0.10). The fusion score, by inheriting the more continuous resolution of S_bt, reduced the certifiable floor (alpha_l) to 0.048 and substantially increased the acceptance rate.

#### 4.2. Risk Control

The core mechanism of Conformal Risk Control (CRC) was compared against a coverage-calibrated baseline (Split CP).

- **CRC Compliance**: For the high-error GPT-5.2 few-shot translator on STL (raw error rates of 0.423-0.551), CRC consistently satisfied the target risk budget `alpha` in all 18 evaluated settings. The mean joint risk typically approached `alpha`, demonstrating effective utilization of the budget. In cases where the budget `alpha` fell below the feasibility floor `alpha_l` (e.g., STL D2 at `alpha=0.05`), CRC appropriately abstained on all inputs rather than providing an uncertifiable guarantee.
- **Split CP Violations**: The Split CP baseline, designed for coverage rather than joint risk, violated the specified risk budget in 13 out of 18 settings. Its behavior was also observed to be less intuitive, with risk sometimes decreasing as the coverage target (and thus `alpha`) increased, due to the different calibration objective.
- **Fine-tuned Translator Performance**: On the lower-error SpaTiaL LLaMA translator (raw error rates of 0.037-0.309), CRC still effectively controlled risk, particularly on the more challenging tiers. For example, on SpaTiaL D4 at `alpha=0.10`, CRC reduced the joint risk from an unconditional 0.309 to 0.088 by abstaining on approximately 23% of inputs.

#### 4.3. Deployment Drift

The instruction-level out-of-distribution (OOD) filter was evaluated under cross-tier shifts for STL, where the system was calibrated on one difficulty tier and tested on another without recalibration.

- **In-Distribution Behavior**: The filter, set at a deferral level of `delta=0.05`, deferred a small proportion of in-distribution inputs (0.6% to 5.4%), consistent with its design to control deferral rates for typical inputs.
- **Shifted Input Robustness**: Under cross-tier shifts, which naturally increase joint risk, the filter successfully reduced the joint risk in 5 out of 6 shifted settings, bringing 4 of them back below the target budget. The most substantial reduction was observed when a deep-tier stream (D4) was tested against an intermediate-tier (D3) calibration, where joint risk decreased from 0.123 to 0.076.
- **Complementary Protection**: The filter effectively rejected inputs that the subsequent CRC threshold might have accepted, including incorrect translations that appeared plausible. This highlights the distinct evidence used by the two components: instruction typicality by the filter and translation reliability by the CRC.
- **Efficiency**: Screening inputs before translation also provided computational benefits by avoiding downstream model calls for deferred instructions (e.g., preventing 31-474 model calls per 150 shifted instructions).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SCP-NL2TL framework presents a significant advancement in the development of trustworthy natural language interfaces for autonomous systems. Its primary significance lies in moving beyond merely generating formal specifications to enabling AI systems to reliably assess the trustworthiness of their own outputs, a critical capability for real-world deployment.

**Enhanced Safety and Reliability:** By bounding the rate at which incorrect formal specifications are accepted for execution (joint risk) with distribution-free guarantees, the framework directly contributes to improved safety and reliability in safety-critical domains such as robotics and cyber-physical systems. This allows developers to set an explicit, statistically verifiable risk budget, providing a quantifiable level of assurance that was previously unavailable.

**Generality and Modularity:** The model-agnostic and black-box nature of SCP-NL2TL is a key strength. It can be integrated with any existing NL2TL translator without requiring modifications to the underlying model's architecture or access to its internal confidence scores. This broad applicability across diverse formal logic languages (demonstrated for LTL, STL, and SpaTiaL) and various translator types makes it a versatile tool for enhancing trustworthiness in a wide range of applications.

**Robustness to Distribution Shift:** The incorporation of an instruction-level conformal anomaly detector addresses a fundamental challenge in real-world AI deployment: the failure of exchangeability assumptions under distribution shift. By screening out-of-distribution inputs before translation, the framework enhances the robustness of the reliability guarantees, ensuring that the system can recognize when it is operating outside its calibrated domain. This proactive abstention mechanism is crucial for maintaining performance and safety in dynamic environments.

**Improved Error Detection:** The proposed semantic consistency score, which combines back-translation fidelity and self-consistency, demonstrates improved discrimination between correct and incorrect translations. By leveraging complementary failure modes—detecting explicit semantic mismatches through back-translation and identifying translation uncertainty through self-consistency—the framework achieves a more comprehensive assessment of translation quality than either signal alone.

**Practical Implications:** The framework offers practical implications for the design and deployment of AI-powered control systems. It shifts the paradigm from focusing solely on average translation accuracy to providing actionable, per-instance reliability judgments. This allows for informed decision-making: a system can either execute a trusted command or abstain and potentially seek human intervention or clarification, thereby preventing potentially harmful actions stemming from misunderstood instructions.

In summary, this work establishes a foundation for more accountable and verifiable AI systems interacting with humans through natural language, fostering a higher degree of trust and enabling the safer deployment of autonomous technologies in complex and sensitive operational environments.
