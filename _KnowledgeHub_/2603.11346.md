# Learning to Assist: Physics-Grounded Human-Human Control via Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> AssistMimic is a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid agents to learn physics-grounded controllers for complex, high-contact human-human assistive tasks. It is the first method to successfully track these interactions, achieving success rates up to 85.8% on established benchmarks and demonstrating robustness to varying partner dynamics.

## Problem

- Prior physics-based humanoid control lacked robust solutions for multi-agent interactions involving close physical contact and force exchange, typically focusing on single-agent or contact-less scenarios.
- Existing kinematic replay strategies for multi-agent interaction failed to model bidirectional dependencies and continuous co-adaptation required in tightly coupled physical assistive scenarios, leading to physical inconsistencies.
- Training Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) for complex, high-contact humanoid behaviors was unstable due to large state-action spaces, difficult exploration, and noisy motion capture data.

## Method

- The research formulates human-human assistive ==motion imitation== as a fully coupled Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) problem, jointly training partner-aware policies for both a supporter and a recipient agent.
- It utilizes a goal-conditioned policy architecture, building on single-human motion priors for initial skills and augmented with 'assistive state' features to incorporate partner information and contact data.
- The framework introduces dynamic reference retargeting to adapt supporter hand targets to the recipient's real-time pose deviations and incorporates contact-promoting rewards to prioritize functional physical contact over strict kinematic adherence.

## Results

- AssistMimic successfully tracked assistive human-human motions, achieving success rates of **74.9%** on Inter-X and **85.8%** on HHI-Assist benchmarks for specialist policies, significantly outperforming kinematic and decoupled baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that motion prior initialization, dynamic reference retargeting, and contact-promoting rewards were individually critical for achieving convergence and robustness.
- The framework demonstrated strong robustness to unseen recipient dynamics (e.g., increased mass, reduced joint torques) and successfully converted diverse generated kinematic interactions into physically plausible motions, showing generalization across various support behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A fully coupled multi-agent reinforcement learning formulation, allowing bidirectional adaptation between agents during training, is crucial for effectively learning complex, force-exchanging assistive interactions, outperforming decoupled or kinematic approaches.
> - Bootstrapping MARL with single-agent motion priors is essential for stabilizing exploration and enabling convergence in high-contact humanoid tasks, preventing training failures or non-functional reward hacking behaviors.
> - Dynamic reference retargeting and contact-promoting rewards are critical components that enhance the learning of robust, physically valid interactions and improve generalization to unseen partner dynamics by prioritizing functional support.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "Learning to Assist: Physics-Grounded Human-Human Control via Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning" addresses a gap in physics-based humanoid control, specifically concerning multi-agent interactions involving close physical contact and force exchange.

Prior research in physics-based human motion synthesis has primarily focused on single-agent motion tracking. Frameworks such as DeepMimic, AMP, and PHC have enabled virtual characters and robots to reproduce diverse human movements within physics engines, leading to agile and robust behaviors. However, these methods are generally limited to isolated movements or contact-less social interactions.

For multi-agent interactions, existing approaches often rely on kinematic replay strategies, where one agent's motion is pre-defined or generated in isolation, and another agent learns to react to it. Examples include methods integrating diffusion planners with physics-tracking policies (e.g., Human-X) which provide reactive, open-loop control. The limitation of these approaches is their inability to model the bidirectional dependencies and continuous co-adaptation required in tightly coupled physical interactions, especially when both agents simultaneously influence each other. Decoupling the learning of partner policies in such scenarios can lead to physical inconsistencies.

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has shown promise for interactive behaviors, but within humanoid control, MARL has largely been applied to competitive or cooperative games rather than explicit embodied physical contact and support tasks.

This paper differentiates itself by formulating the imitation of closely interacting, force-exchanging human-human motion sequences as a fully coupled multi-agent reinforcement learning problem. It aims to jointly train partner-aware policies for both a supporter (assistant) and a recipient agent, thereby enabling continuous bidirectional adaptation and maintaining physical consistency in scenarios where one agent requires physical assistance from the other. This contrasts with previous works that either avoid close contact, decouple agents kinematically, or do not model the reactive forces and mutual dependencies inherent in caregiving and assistive tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework, termed AssistMimic, capable of learning physics-grounded controllers for humanoid agents to perform complex, high-contact human-human assistive interactions. This includes tasks where one agent (supporter) physically assists another (recipient) through force exchange and continuous adaptation to the partner's evolving state.

The motivation for this work stems from the limitations of current humanoid robotics and character animation in addressing real-world service and caregiving applications. While significant advancements have been made in general motion tracking (GMT) for single-person movements and contact-less social interactions, these capabilities are insufficient for assistive scenarios. Assistive tasks necessitate:
1. **Continuous awareness of a human partner:** Agents must perceive and understand their partner's real-time posture and dynamics.
2. **Rapid adaptation to evolving states:** The assistant needs to adjust its actions instantly based on the recipient's movements and stability.
3. **Physically grounded interaction:** These scenarios involve close physical contact and the exchange of forces, which must be accurately simulated and controlled to ensure stability and safety.

The authors identify several challenges that motivate their specific methodological choices:
- **Decoupled learning issues:** Existing kinematic replay strategies for multi-agent interaction fail in tightly coupled assistive motions because the recipient's behavior cannot be computed independently. Decoupling learning breaks physical consistency and prevents meaningful functional support from emerging.
- **Complexity of contact-rich behaviors:** Assistive scenarios require precise spatial alignment and timed force application. Minor errors can destabilize the recipient, making reinforcement learning for reactive control in physically entangled interactions highly challenging.
- **Noisy reference data:** Close-range human-human motion capture often suffers from occlusion, leading to noisy and inaccurate reference trajectories, which complicates learning through direct imitation.
- **Exploration stability in MARL:** Training MARL policies for these complex, high-contact settings is inherently unstable due to the large state-action spaces and the need for coordinated, physically consistent behaviors.

To address these motivations and challenges, the specific objectives for AssistMimic include:
- Formulating the imitation of close-proximity, force-exchanging human-human motion as a multi-agent reinforcement learning problem.
- Jointly training partner-aware policies for both supporter and recipient agents in a physics simulator to track assistive motion references.
- Introducing mechanisms to make the MARL problem tractable, such as leveraging single-person motion priors, implementing recipient-adaptive reference retargeting, and incorporating contact-promoting rewards.
- Demonstrating the first physics-based controller capable of successfully tracking assistive interaction motions on established benchmarks.

The ultimate aim is to enable the development of more socially aware and physically grounded humanoid control systems for practical assistive and caregiving applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AssistMimic formulates the problem of human-human interactive motion imitation as a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) problem. The framework involves two distinct humanoid agents: a Supporter (assistant) and a Recipient. The recipient's dynamics are intentionally constrained (reduced maximum joint torques and lowered Proportional-Derivative (PD) gains) to simulate physical impairment, thereby necessitating assistance and making the supporter's role critical to prevent falls. Both agents learn tracking policies optimized for reference motion fidelity under these physics constraints.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation: Multi-Agent MDP with Asymmetric Dynamics

The interaction is modeled as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). Each agent *m* ∈ {S, R} (Supporter, Recipient) samples an action *a*t from its policy π*m*(· | *s*t; ϕ*m*), where ϕ*m* are policy parameters and *s*t is the agent-centric observation. The environment transition dynamics *Pκ* incorporate physical interactions, with the recipient's dynamics parameterized by *κ* = (*k*p, *k*d, τmax) to represent impairment. The objective is to learn optimal policy weights (ϕ∗S, ϕ∗R) that maximize the expected discounted return of a joint reward function *J*(πS, πR).
The base per-agent reward *r*track encourages fidelity to a reference motion sequence {^q(m)t}Tt=1. For the recipient, the task reward includes tracking, power, and assist stability terms. For the supporter, the objective is modified by dynamic reference retargeting and contact-promoting rewards to prioritize functional support.

#### 3.2. Goal-conditioned Tracking Policy Architecture

AssistMimic utilizes a symmetric, goal-conditioned policy architecture, building upon the single-human motion tracking controller PHC. The core idea is to leverage single-human motion priors to bootstrap learning, providing basic locomotion skills. The PHC architecture is augmented by expanding its input layer to include interaction-aware features, termed the "assistive state" (*s*assist).
The policy input for each agent *m* is a tuple (*s*prior, *s*assist, *g*), where:
- *s*prior,t: The ego agent's proprioceptive state (joint rotations, positions, angular/linear velocities, root height).
- *g*t: A set of reference delta states calculated between the current state and the reference motion state in the next timestep.
- *s*assist,t: Encodes partner-aware information, including the partner's observation (root rotation, joint states, joint positions relative to ego-agent's wrists), partner's hand contact state, ego-agent's contact state, self-force (3D contact forces on elbows, wrists, fingertips), and the ego-agent's previous action.

#### 3.3. Weight Initialization from Single Person Motion Prior

To accelerate learning and stabilize exploration, both supporter and recipient policies are initialized using parameters from a pre-trained single-person tracking controller (πprior). Since AssistMimic's policy architecture includes additional assistive state inputs, a zero-padding initialization strategy is employed. Weights corresponding to the original proprioceptive state are copied from the prior, while weights for the new assistive features are initialized to zero. This preserves the prior's basic motor skills while allowing new interaction-specific behaviors to emerge.

#### 3.4. Dynamic Reference Retargeting

To ensure effective assistance in close-contact scenarios, the supporter's hand targets must adapt to the recipient's real-time pose deviations from the reference motion. This mechanism dynamically adjusts the assistant's hand targets to maintain their intended relative position with respect to the recipient's body.
The retargeting is activated when the supporter's and recipient's root positions are within a predefined distance threshold (*τ*dist). For each supporter wrist, the nearest recipient joint (anchor joint) in the canonical reference space is identified. The desired relative offset between the supporter's hand and this anchor joint is then computed from the reference motion and transferred to the *simulated* recipient state. This updates the supporter's tracking target, ensuring its hands track a physically meaningful interaction point relative to the recipient's current pose, rather than a fixed global location.

#### 3.5. Contact-Promoting Rewards

Due to noise and occlusions in motion capture data, strict hand trajectory tracking can be unreliable for effective support. To address this, contact-incentivizing rewards are introduced when the recipient's body is in close proximity to the assistant's hands.
When the supporter's hands are distant from the recipient, the standard tracking reward is used. When in close proximity (distance *d*i,t ≤ *d*th), the tracking penalty is suppressed, and a contact-promotion term is activated. This term combines a bonus for establishing contact (*b*contact) with a continuous reward based on the measured finger-contact forces (*f*i,t) and an exponential decay based on proximity. The force term uses a safety-aware soft saturation function, encouraging appropriate force exertion without overshooting. This prioritizes functional physical contact over strict kinematic adherence.

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

- Policies are trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- Specialist policies are trained per cluster of motions, then distilled into a generalist policy using DAgger.
- Tight early termination is applied to episodes based on pose-deviation thresholds.
- Physical State Initialization (PSI) is used, seeding episodes from states collected in recent rollouts to avoid noisy reference trajectories.
- The critic network takes the same input as the actor, augmented with a role label.
- For the HHI-Assist bed setting, the final 15 frames are discarded during evaluation to account for periods where support is no longer needed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated AssistMimic on two datasets: Inter-X (diverse "Help-up" motions) and HHI-Assist (caregiving motions on a bed or chair), focusing on close-range, contact-rich assistive interactions. Evaluation scenarios included specialist and generalist policies, robustness to unseen recipient dynamics, and tracking of generated kinematic trajectories. Metrics included Success Rate (SR), Mean Per Joint Position Error (MPJPE), and Center of Mass (COM) Stability.

#### 4.1. Baseline Comparisons and Joint Training

- **Kinematic Baselines (Phys-Reaction, Kinematic-Recipient):** These approaches, which rely on a fixed kinematic replay of the recipient, largely failed in assistive tasks. The recipient would "stand up" independently, ignoring support, or severe interpenetration would occur, leading to restorative forces that pushed characters apart. This demonstrated that kinematic replay is unsuitable for the recipient in tightly coupled assistive scenarios, highlighting the necessity of a fully coupled MARL formulation.
- **Frozen-Recipient (Decoupled Sequential Learning):** This baseline, where a pre-trained recipient policy is frozen while the assistant learns to react, was significantly outperformed by AssistMimic's joint MARL formulation (e.g., AssistMimic achieved 74.9% SR on Inter-X vs. 62.4% for sequential training). This finding supports the argument that bidirectional adaptation, where both agents co-adapt during training, is crucial for handling complex, force-exchanging interactions.

#### 4.2. Contributions of AssistMimic Components (Ablation Studies)

- **Motion Prior Initialization:** Bootstrapping from a single-agent motion prior was found to be essential for convergence. Without it, the reinforcement learning process either failed completely (0% success rate on Inter-X) or led to reward hacking behaviors that produced unnatural, non-functional motions (e.g., 19.1% SR on HHI-Assist with reward hacking). This highlights the importance of providing initial locomotion skills to stabilize exploration.
- **Dynamic Reference Retargeting:** This module was critical for maintaining valid physical contact, particularly for the HHI-Assist dataset, where it improved SR from 49.1% to 67.8% under unseen dynamics (Mass x1.5) and from 62.9% to 73.2% under reduced hip torque. Without it, the supporter often failed to adjust its hands to the recipient’s actual position, leading to missed contacts and inadequate support. However, it did not consistently improve performance on the Inter-X dataset, possibly due to the wider range and instability of recipient movements in that dataset, which made relative goal positions less reliable.
- **Contact-Promoting Rewards:** This reward term significantly enhanced robustness to unseen recipient dynamics. By prioritizing physical contact over strict kinematic tracking, it enabled policies to adapt to unexpected physical changes (e.g., increased mass, reduced PD gains, or torque limits). Removing this reward substantially degraded robustness (e.g., on HHI-Assist with Mass x1.5, SR dropped from 67.8% to 56.4%; with Max hip torque x0.5, SR dropped from 73.2% to 27.7%). It encourages the learning of active, force-aware interaction strategies.

#### 4.3. Performance of Specialist and Generalist Policies

- **Specialist Policies:** AssistMimic was the only method capable of successfully tracking closely interacting, force-exchanging human motions on established benchmarks, achieving success rates of 74.9% on Inter-X and 85.8% on HHI-Assist in seen dynamics settings.
- **Generalist Policies:** When trained directly on a diverse subset of 30 Inter-X clips, AssistMimic achieved a 77.3% success rate. Distilling specialist policies into a single generalist agent using DAgger further improved performance, reaching 94.7% success rate and demonstrating the ability to reproduce a wide variety of support behaviors with one unified controller.

#### 4.4. Robustness and Generalization

- **Robustness to Unseen Recipient Dynamics:** AssistMimic exhibited strong robustness to recipient constraints not encountered during training (e.g., halved PD gains, increased body mass, reduced hip torque), demonstrating its ability to generalize across varying degrees of impairment.
- **Tracking Generated Interactions:** The framework successfully converted dense interaction kinematics from a text-conditioned motion diffusion model into physically plausible motions, even when the generated data contained physical artifacts like foot sliding or penetrations. This indicates broad applicability beyond real MoCap data.
- **Generalization to Unseen Supportive Actions:** AssistMimic showed generalization to unseen interaction categories from Inter-X, such as "support-by-arm," successfully placing a supportive hand on the partner’s arm while avoiding collisions, even if this specific pattern was not in the training data.

#### 4.5. Failure Cases and Limitations

- Failures primarily occurred in motions requiring precise grasping and lifting of recipient's arms. These were attributed to limited hand dexterity in the current model, as fine, coordinated finger control is challenging to learn from noisy demonstrations and exacerbated by recipient weight.
- The authors suggest that tighter integration between motion planning and tracking, along with incorporating visual observations, would be necessary to improve robustness to dynamic partner states and enhance dexterity.
- Transferring the learned behaviors from simulation to real humanoid systems remains an important open challenge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces AssistMimic, a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework that significantly advances the field of physics-based humanoid control for interactive scenarios. Its primary significance lies in being the first method capable of successfully learning physics-grounded controllers to track complex, high-contact, force-exchanging human-human motion sequences in a simulation environment.

The potential impact of AssistMimic is substantial, particularly in several key areas:

1. **Assistive Robotics and Caregiving:** The framework provides a foundational step toward developing autonomous humanoid robots that can physically assist humans in daily tasks. By learning robust and adaptive support behaviors, robots could potentially aid in mobility, transfers, and other caregiving functions, enhancing independence for individuals with mobility impairments. This could lead to practical applications in healthcare, elder care, and personal assistance.

2. **Collaborative Manipulation and Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability of AssistMimic to model and learn physically consistent, co-adaptive interactions is relevant beyond caregiving. It lays groundwork for robots to engage in more sophisticated collaborative manipulation tasks with humans, where physical contact and mutual force exchange are critical for task success. This could improve the safety and efficiency of human-robot co-working environments.

3. **Embodied Simulation of Social Interactions:** AssistMimic offers a method for creating more realistic and physically plausible virtual characters that can interact in close proximity. This has implications for virtual reality, gaming, character animation, and the development of socially intelligent AI, allowing virtual agents to exhibit more natural and responsive physical interactions.

4. **Advancement in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning:** The methodological innovations—specifically, weight initialization from single-human motion priors, dynamic reference retargeting, and contact-promoting rewards—address core challenges in MARL for high-contact settings. These strategies improve exploration efficiency and stability, making otherwise intractable problems solvable. These contributions may inform future MARL research in other complex, physically coupled domains.

5. **Generalizable Framework:** The success of AssistMimic in extending single-agent imitation learning to physically grounded multi-agent domains demonstrates a generalizable paradigm. This suggests that the principles developed could be adapted to a broader range of complex multi-agent tasks requiring reactive coordination and force feedback.

Overall, AssistMimic contributes to the development of physically grounded, socially aware humanoid control, moving closer to the goal of deploying robots that can safely and effectively interact with humans in real-world assistive and collaborative contexts.
