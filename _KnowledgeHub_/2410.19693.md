# MILES: Making Imitation Learning Easy with Self-Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> MILES is a self-supervised imitation-learning framework that turns a single human demonstration and one environment reset into a dense training set. From each demonstration waypoint the robot moves to a random nearby pose and returns, recording wrist-camera and force-torque data; valid trajectories are fused with the demonstration and cloned by an LSTM policy, reaching 87% mean success across seven real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Behavioral cloning needs hundreds or thousands of demonstrations per task, so collecting a sufficiently diverse dataset costs prohibitive human labor for every new skill.
- RL-based imitation learning trains autonomously but explores randomly, learns unstably, and demands frequent manual environment resets that often cost more human time than extra demonstrations.
- Replay methods that estimate object pose then replay one demonstration compound small pose errors open-loop, and collisions or object perturbation break the replay entirely.

## Method

- **MILES**'s ==self-supervised augmentation trajectories==: from each demonstration waypoint the robot moves to a random pose within 4 cm and 4 degrees, then returns under ==impedance control==, auto-labeling actions from end-effector deltas.
- Two validity conditions gate every trajectory: ==proprioceptive reachability== of the target waypoint, and an ==environment-disturbance check== comparing ==DINO== feature cosine similarity of wrist images against a 0.94 threshold.
- Valid trajectories are fused with the demonstration suffix into new demonstrations; a per-task ==LSTM behavioral-cloning policy== takes RGB plus force-torque input, excluding proprioception to gain spatial generalization.

## Results

- Across seven real-world tasks at 20 trials each, MILES averaged **87%** success versus **71%** for pose estimation plus replay, **10%** for reset-free FISH and **7%** for residual RL.
- Ablations dropped mean success to **43%** without sequential waypoint ordering, **47%** without the disturbance check, **73%** without the reachability check, and **74%** without LSTM memory.
- Vision-only policies kept **70-95%** on contact-rich tasks while force-only collapsed to **0-5%**; halving data dropped lock-with-key from **90%** to **10%**, though lid opening held **100%** at 25% data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured self-labeling beats random exploration: returning to a known demonstration yields dense, correctly labeled supervision near the expert manifold without reward design or repeated resets.
> - Order of data collection matters as much as quantity; collecting recovery data out of waypoint sequence halves performance because later waypoints assume earlier states stay undisturbed.
> - Force sensing helps only some contact-rich tasks and can hurt others; wrist vision carries most of the signal, and low-tolerance tasks scale worst with reduced data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Teaching robots to perform manipulation tasks by watching human demonstrations has long been a central goal in robotics. However, current imitation learning methods face a fundamental trade-off: they either require extensive human supervision through multiple demonstrations or frequent environment resets, or they suffer from poor generalization when learning from limited data. This paper introduces MILES (Making Imitation Learning Easy with Self-Supervision), a framework that enables robots to learn complex manipulation tasks from just a single human demonstration and a single environment reset.

MILES addresses a critical bottleneck in making imitation learning practical for real-world deployment. The framework combines the simplicity of behavioral cloning with autonomous data augmentation, allowing robots to expand their training data without additional human effort. This approach is particularly valuable for contact-rich manipulation tasks like locking mechanisms, USB insertion, and appliance operation, where precise control and adaptability to variations are essential.

### 2. The Challenge with Current Imitation Learning

Existing imitation learning approaches face several fundamental limitations that MILES seeks to overcome:

**Behavioral Cloning (BC)** learns policies directly from demonstration data but suffers from covariate shift - when the robot encounters states not seen in the limited demonstration data, it fails to generalize appropriately. Traditional BC approaches require many demonstrations to cover the state space adequately.

**Reinforcement Learning-based methods** can improve generalization but require frequent environment resets and can be unstable. They also demand extensive exploration, which is inefficient and potentially unsafe for manipulation tasks.

**Demonstration replay methods** rely on precise pose estimation and struggle with environment disturbances or collisions that deviate from the original demonstration path.

The core problem is that single demonstrations, while minimizing human effort, create a narrow data distribution that leads to poor generalization. Existing solutions to this problem typically require either more human supervision or computationally expensive exploration strategies.

### 3. The MILES Framework

MILES introduces a self-supervised data collection strategy that bridges the gap between minimal human supervision and robust policy learning. The framework operates in three main phases:

1. **Single Demonstration Collection**: A human provides one demonstration of the target task
2. **Self-Supervised Data Augmentation**: The robot autonomously collects additional training data
3. **Policy Learning**: A neural network policy is trained on the combined dataset

The key insight behind MILES is that robots can systematically explore variations around demonstration waypoints and learn to return to the demonstrated path. This creates a rich dataset of state-action pairs that covers the variations the robot might encounter during deployment.

### 4. Self-Supervised Data Collection Process

The self-supervised data collection process is the core innovation of MILES. For each waypoint in the original demonstration, the robot generates multiple "augmentation trajectories" through the following procedure:

**Waypoint Sampling**: The robot identifies key states from the human demonstration that serve as target waypoints for data collection.

**Random Perturbation**: For each waypoint, the robot moves to a random pose near the demonstration state, introducing controlled variations in position and orientation.

**Return Trajectory Generation**: The robot attempts to return to the target waypoint using a straight-line trajectory in Cartesian space, recording RGB observations, force-torque data, and corresponding actions.

**Data Validation**: Each collected trajectory is evaluated using reachability and environment disturbance criteria to ensure data quality.

This process generates diverse training data that captures how the robot should behave when starting from various positions near the demonstrated path. The autonomous nature of this data collection means no additional human supervision is required beyond the initial demonstration.

### 5. Validation Conditions for Quality Control

MILES employs two critical validation conditions to ensure the quality of self-collected data:

**Reachability Condition**: This verifies that the robot can successfully reach the target demonstration waypoint after executing the augmentation trajectory. If collisions or other factors prevent the robot from reaching the target, the trajectory is discarded as invalid. This condition ensures that only feasible recovery behaviors are learned.

**Environment Disturbance Detection**: This condition uses DINO (self-supervised vision transformer) features to compare RGB observations before and after trajectory execution. The cosine similarity between DINO features must exceed a threshold (typically 0.93) to ensure the robot hasn't significantly disturbed the environment. This prevents the robot from learning behaviors that rely on moving objects to unintended positions.

These validation conditions are crucial for maintaining data quality and ensuring that the learned policy generalizes appropriately to real deployment scenarios.

### 6. Policy Learning and Deployment

MILES trains task-specific policies using behavioral cloning on the augmented dataset. The policy architecture consists of an LSTM network that processes RGB images and force-torque sensor data to predict robot actions.

**Training Process**: The policy is trained on the combined dataset of the original demonstration and valid augmentation trajectories. This creates a rich training distribution that covers variations the robot might encounter during execution.

**Inference Strategy**: During deployment, the policy operates in a closed-loop manner when no environment disturbances occurred during data collection. If disturbances were detected during data collection (indicating the task requires moving objects), the policy executes until the disturbance point, then switches to open-loop replay of the remaining demonstration actions.

This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of both closed-loop control (adaptability) and open-loop replay (reliability for object manipulation) depending on the task requirements.

### 7. Experimental Results

MILES was evaluated on seven real-world manipulation tasks requiring precise control and contact interactions:

- **Lock with key**: Inserting and turning a key in a lock mechanism
- **Insert USB**: Precise insertion of a USB connector
- **Plug into socket**: Electrical plug insertion task
- **Insert power cable**: Cable connection task
- **Twist screw**: Screwdriver operation requiring rotational control
- **Bread in toaster**: Appliance operation task
- **Open lid**: Container manipulation requiring coordinated motion

The results demonstrate that MILES significantly outperforms baseline methods across all tasks. Success rates ranged from 70% to 95% across different tasks, substantially higher than traditional single-demonstration behavioral cloning approaches.

### 8. Analysis and Insights

Several key insights emerge from the experimental analysis:

**Vision is Critical**: Ablation studies show that RGB vision is the most important modality for MILES' performance. Force feedback provides additional benefits but is secondary to visual information for most tasks.

**Data Efficiency**: The amount of self-supervised data required varies with task complexity. High-tolerance tasks like "open lid" require less augmentation data, while precision tasks like "insert USB" benefit from more extensive data collection.

**Progressive Improvement**: Performance generally improves with larger augmentation datasets, but the rate of improvement varies by task. Some tasks achieve maximum performance with relatively small augmentation datasets.

**Environment Disturbance Detection**: The DINO feature similarity metric effectively distinguishes between acceptable and problematic environment changes, enabling appropriate switching between closed-loop and open-loop execution strategies.

### 9. Significance and Future Directions

MILES represents a significant advancement in making imitation learning more practical and accessible. The framework's key contributions include:

**Reduced Human Effort**: By requiring only a single demonstration and environment reset, MILES dramatically reduces the human supervision needed for robot learning.

**Scalability**: The self-supervised data collection approach can be applied to diverse manipulation tasks without task-specific modifications.

**Robustness**: The validation conditions ensure data quality and prevent the learning of unsafe or ineffective behaviors.

**Practical Deployment**: The hybrid inference strategy adapts to different task requirements automatically.

Future research directions include extending MILES to more complex environments, developing more sophisticated environment disturbance detection methods, and exploring integration with other learning paradigms. The framework opens possibilities for autonomous skill acquisition in domestic and industrial robotics applications.

The work demonstrates that carefully designed self-supervision can bridge the gap between minimal human input and robust robot learning, making imitation learning more viable for real-world deployment across a wide range of manipulation tasks.
