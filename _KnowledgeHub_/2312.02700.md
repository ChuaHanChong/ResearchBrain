# Revisit Human-Scene Interaction via Space Occupancy

> [!summary] Summary
> This work reframes static human-scene interaction as human-occupancy interaction: any scene acts on motion mainly through the space it occupies. Negating voxelized human motion yields pseudo-scene occupancy, turning 13 motion-only datasets into a 98k-instance paired database (MOB). A single auto-regressive transformer controller with occupancy-field regulation, trained without real 3D scans, reaches 77.91% success versus 15.73% for the prior state of the art.

## Problem

- Paired human-plus-3D-scene capture is expensive, so existing interaction datasets stay small, narrow in object range, and geometrically simple, capping model diversity.
- End-to-end scene-to-motion pipelines are inflexible and cannot react to dynamic scenes, since the scene is consumed once as a static conditioning input.
- Two-stage designs split locomotion from interaction, ignoring their integrated nature, and rely on rigid-cylinder path planners that fail in cramped, geometrically complex layouts.

## Method

- **Motion Occupancy Base (MOB)** voxelizes each motion's swept body volume, then negates it to produce ==pseudo-scene occupancy==, pairing pure motion with stringent implied geometry at scale.
- A ==transformer== encoder-decoder controller auto-regressively predicts the next canonicalized pose from motion history, target pose or end-effector trajectory, and a local binary occupancy grid.
- **Field Regulation** applies a potential-field-style corrective velocity that grows sharply near occupied voxels, trained jointly with penetration and occupancy-field losses to curb collisions.

## Results

- On MOB goal-reaching the controller hits **77.91%** success and **15.21** penetration, against **15.73%** and **123.12** for the strongest prior method, using one policy not several.
- Path planners underpinning prior work fail outright on most MOB layouts: NavMesh finds no feasible path for **69.43%** of samples, A* for **70.29%**.
- Ablations confirm each part: dropping penetration loss costs **3.77** points of success and doubles penetration; removing field regulation raises penetration by **18.28** while cutting foot sliding **1.56%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing a data-scarce task around its underlying physical constraint can convert abundant unpaired data into paired supervision, sidestepping expensive joint capture entirely.
> - Training on artificially over-constrained geometry transfers downward to easy real scenes, so difficulty-inflated synthetic data can substitute for realistic-but-simple data.
> - Per-step egocentric occupancy sensing gives dynamic-scene generalization for free from static training, though mismatch in obstacle speed still causes residual collisions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of realistic and diverse Human-Scene Interaction (HSI) motions is an active area of research with applications in fields such as animation and embodied artificial intelligence. A significant challenge in this domain is the limited availability of high-quality training data that simultaneously captures human motion and detailed 3D environments. Existing datasets, such as PROX and CIRCLE, are either constrained in terms of diversity, complexity, or rely on costly data acquisition methods like VR applications or synthetic data generation. This data scarcity often limits the generalizability of models trained on them.

Current HSI generation approaches typically fall into two categories:
1. **End-to-end pipelines:** These methods directly generate motion from a scene but often lack the flexibility to adapt to dynamic environments. Examples include approaches by CIRCLE and HUMANISE.
2. **Two-stage paradigms:** These decouple HSI into separate interaction and locomotion stages, often involving initial key-frame generation followed by interpolation or pathfinding algorithms (e.g., DIMOS, GAMMA). However, these methods can oversimplify the integrated nature of human movement and interaction, frequently relying on simplified human representations (e.g., rigid cylinders) for path planning, which can lead to failures in complex or cluttered scenes.

Prior work has also explored human motion generation conditioned on various inputs (e.g., action categories, natural language, audio) but often without grounding the motion in a 3D environment. Other efforts focus on specific human-object interactions (HOI) with single target objects.

This work introduces a re-conceptualization of static HSI (interactions that do not alter the scene) as "Human-Occupancy Interaction." By focusing on the abstract physical concept of space occupancy rather than explicit scene semantics or detailed meshes, the authors propose a novel approach to alleviate data scarcity and develop a unified motion controller. This perspective aims to bridge the gap between motion-only datasets and HSI generation, and to enable robust motion synthesis in complex and dynamic environments, a capability largely unaddressed by previous methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the significant data bottleneck in Human-Scene Interaction (HSI) generation. The acquisition of paired data, which includes synchronized human motion and high-fidelity 3D scene scans, is an expensive and labor-intensive process. This cost contributes to the limited scale, diversity, and complexity of currently available HSI datasets, hindering the development of robust and generalizable HSI models.

The core argument put forth in this paper is that static HSI is fundamentally an interaction with the *space occupancy* of a scene. From an abstract physical perspective, a human interacts with the physical space that a scene occupies, rather than its semantic labels or detailed visual properties. For instance, sitting on a chair involves interacting with the chair's solid form and its occupied volume, which dictates the possibilities for interaction (affordances) and imposes geometric constraints (e.g., preventing penetration). This perspective suggests that the requirement for highly realistic and explicitly captured 3D scenes for HSI learning might be amenable to re-evaluation.

Based on this foundational argument, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Reconceptualize HSI:** To propose and establish "Human-Occupancy Interaction" as a primary conceptual framework for understanding and modeling static HSI. This involves shifting focus from explicit scene representation to abstract space occupancy.
2. **Address Data Limitations:** To leverage the new conceptualization to create a large-scale, paired human-occupancy interaction database. This objective involves converting existing vast repositories of motion-only data into a format that implicitly contains scene interaction information, thereby substantially alleviating the reliance on costly paired motion-scene datasets. This database, named Motion Occupancy Base (MOB), is intended to offer complex and stringent geometric constraints, pushing models to learn robust interaction behaviors.
3. **Develop a Versatile Controller:** To design and implement a single, auto-regressive motion controller capable of generating flexible and stable HSI motions. This controller should be able to:
 - Learn from the challenging geometric constraints present in the MOB dataset.
 - Generalize effectively to a wide range of scenarios, from cramped, complex environments to more open, regular scenes.
 - Unify locomotion and interaction generation, eliminating the need for separate models or stages for different types of movement.
 - Exhibit adaptability to dynamic scene changes, a feature that many existing static-scene-trained models lack.
4. **Demonstrate Efficacy:** To empirically validate the proposed methodology through extensive experiments, showcasing its ability to generate realistic and stable HSI motions in diverse static and dynamic scenarios, even without direct training on real 3D scene data.

In essence, the motivation is to simplify the input requirements for HSI generation, expand the available training data, and create a more flexible and robust generation system that can handle complex and evolving environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on two main components: the creation of a novel dataset called Motion Occupancy Base (MOB) and the development of a versatile, auto-regressive motion controller.

**Motion Occupancy Base (MOB) Construction:**
The foundational idea is to view static human-scene interaction as interaction with "space occupancy." To address the data scarcity of paired motion-scene data, the authors convert existing large-scale motion-only datasets into a human-occupancy interaction database.
1. **Data Aggregation:** The MOB aggregates motion data from 13 different motion-only datasets (e.g., AMASS, GTAHuman, CIRCLE, GRAB), totaling 98,000 motion instances. For datasets that originally contained scene or object information (e.g., CIRCLE, GRAB), only the human motion data is utilized.
2. **Motion to Occupancy Conversion:** For each SMPL (or SMPL-X) motion sequence, the following steps are performed:
 - The human mesh sequence is generated from the SMPL parameters for each frame.
 - A bounding box encompassing the entire motion sequence is determined.
 - This bounding box region is voxelized into a binary "motion occupancy volume" (ˆO). Voxels intersected by the human mesh at any point in the sequence are marked as occupied.
 - The "pseudo-scene occupancy" (O) is then derived by negating the motion occupancy: `O = 1 - ˆO`. This conceptually treats the space *not* occupied by the human during a specific movement as the "scene" with which the human is interacting.
3. **Paired Data:** The result is a paired (Motion, Pseudo-Scene Occupancy) dataset, where the "scene" is implicitly defined by the vacant space around the moving person. This process generates data with stringent geometric constraints, as the human moves within a volume precisely defined by its own inverse.

**Versatile Motion Controller:**
An auto-regressive controller is designed to generate future motion given historical motion states, current pose, and control signals.

1. **Motion State Representation:**
 - **Current Pose State (`x_t`):** Represents the human's state at time `t`, including canonicalized root location, root orientation, limb orientations, and their first derivatives (velocities). Canonicalization aligns the input with respect to the root's position and facing direction at `t`.
 - **Historical Motion State (`h_t`):** Captures motion from `t-w` to `t`, including canonicalized trajectories of the root, hands, and feet, along with their velocities and root angular velocity.
 - **Future Prediction (`ˆy_t`):** Comprises the predicted next frame's pose (`ˆx_{t+1}`) in the egocentric canonical view of `t`, and a comprehensive set of future trajectories, facing directions, and their rates of change (`ˆu_t`) for a window size `f`.

2. **Control Signals (`c_t`):**
 - **Canonical Occupancy (`c_t^o`):** A voxel-based (binary) representation of the scene occupancy in the human's canonical space. This grid is centered with an offset in the human-facing direction. Its real-time calculation allows the controller to adapt to dynamic scene changes.
 - **Target Pose (`c_t^t`):** Flexible signal, which can include target positions for the root, hands, and feet relative to the current canonical space, or future end-effector trajectories. Partial or blank targets are permissible.

3. **Controller Architecture:**
 - A lightweight Transformer-based architecture is employed. Input features are projected into a latent space (`z`).
 - A multi-layer Transformer encoder (`E`) processes `z` to distill dynamic information (`ˆz`).
 - A Transformer decoder (`D`), using a learnable query and random noise for diversity, generates the final predictions (`ˆy_t`).
 - **Field Regulation Module:** This module enhances collision avoidance. Inspired by potential fields, it introduces an "Occupancy Field" that applies a corrective velocity (`∆˙p_j`) to human joints. This field exerts negligible force at a distance but increases repulsion as proximity to occupied voxels increases, prompting deceleration and redirection to avoid collisions. This module is integrated into the training process.

4. **Training Objectives:**
 - The controller is trained in a fully-supervised manner using a combination of losses:
 - `L_mix`: A combination of L1 loss for rotation-related outputs and L2 loss for position-related outputs.
 - `L_pen`: Penetration loss, which penalizes predicted joint locations that occupy scene voxels, based on the distance to the closest unoccupied voxel center.
 - `L_field`: Occupancy field loss, which penalizes excessive field effects on root velocity and high velocities, ensuring smooth adaptation to the field regulation.
 - The total loss is `L = L_mix + αL_pen + βL_field`, where `α` and `β` are coefficients.
 - During training, control signals are randomly masked for augmentation, and Scheduled Sampling is used to progressively replace ground truth inputs with model predictions.

5. **Auto-regressive Inference:**
 - Motion generation proceeds auto-regressively, tracking global root position and orientation.
 - At each time step, control signals (target, canonical occupancy) are computed based on the current global state.
 - The model predicts the next motion frame, and the global and historical states are updated for the subsequent prediction.

**Implementation Details:**
The Transformer encoder has two layers and the decoder one layer, both with 512 latent dimensions and 8 heads. The canonical occupancy grid is (25,25,25) with an 8cm unit size. The controller operates at 10 FPS, with history and future window sizes of 1 frame. Training takes 75,000 iterations over approximately 6 hours on a single NVIDIA Titan Xp.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents both quantitative and qualitative results across various interaction scenarios to validate the proposed Human-Occupancy Interaction framework and the versatile motion controller.

**Evaluation on Motion Occupancy Base (MOB) - Goal-Reaching Task:**
A goal-reaching task was designed on the MOB test set, requiring the model to generate a 10-second motion from a starting pose to a target end-effector pose within a given occupancy volume. Metrics included: Success Rate (Suc. %), average minimum distance to target (DT), time to reach target (Time), Foot Sliding (FS. %), Penetration (PEN, voxels penetrated per frame), and Edit distance for real penalty (ERP) for trajectory similarity.

- **Quantitative Results (Table 1):** The proposed method demonstrated a success rate of 77.91% with a penetration of 15.21 voxels per frame. This significantly surpasses state-of-the-art methods like DIMOS (15.73% Suc., 123.12 PEN), NSM (11.76% Suc.), and SAMP (13.94% Suc.). The model trained without explicit occupancy input ("Ours w/o Occupancy") achieved a lower success rate (71.22%) and substantially higher penetration (46.05), indicating the importance of the canonical occupancy signal. Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of `L_pen` and `L_field` loss terms for effective penetration avoidance and stable motion. The Field Regulation module improved penetration avoidance but introduced a slight increase in foot sliding (from 6.57% to 8.13%), representing a trade-off.
- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 5, 7):** The controller successfully generated feasible motions in complex and cramped occupancy scenarios, reaching targets with minimal penetration. Previous methods, such as DIMOS, often failed due to limitations in their pathfinding algorithms (e.g., NavMesh treating the human as a rigid cylinder), leading to an inability to find paths in 69.43% of MOB samples (Fig. 6). The unified controller also demonstrated versatility, producing diverse motions like sitting, crawling, and getting up from the same model.

**Evaluation on Static Scenes (Realistic Rooms and Scans):**
The controller, trained primarily on the complex MOB, was tested for generalization to more conventional and realistic scenes.

- **Quantitative Results (Table 2 - CIRCLE dataset):** When evaluated on the CIRCLE dataset for a hand-reaching task, the MOB-only trained controller (73.01% Suc., 9.56 cm collision depth) performed competitively with a controller trained only on CIRCLE data (75.52% Suc., 13.74 cm collision depth). Combining MOB with CIRCLE data for training yielded the best performance (78.97% Suc.), suggesting MOB acts as an effective auxiliary data source and its inherent complexity aids robustness.
- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 8, 1):** The model produced convincing and long-term motions (up to 30 seconds) in synthetic and scanned rooms, navigating obstacles (e.g., circumnavigating a cabinet) and performing interactions such as sitting on various objects (sofas, beds, stools) by recognizing their occupancy as potential seats. This demonstrated the model's ability to operate effectively in both sparse and cluttered environments.

**Evaluation on Human-Object Interaction (HOI):**
The flexibility of target pose control signals was utilized for HOI motion synthesis.

- **Quantitative Results (Table 4 - OMOMO dataset):** After fine-tuning on the OMOMO dataset, the model showed lower root translation (8.83) and root rotation (0.17) errors compared to OMOMO (18.44 and 0.50), indicating accurate global path planning. Hand JPE and MPJPE were higher, attributed to the model's lightweight nature compared to OMOMO's diffusion model.
- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 9):** The model synthesized plausible full-body motions for interacting with objects (e.g., moving a floor lamp) when provided with hand trajectories.

**Evaluation on Dynamic Scenes:**
Despite being trained on essentially static data from MOB, the controller's real-time calculation of canonical occupancy allowed for adaptation to dynamic environments.

- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 10):** In a custom automatic revolving door scenario, the model successfully navigated around the moving obstacles, adapting its speed and pausing when necessary to avoid collision before reaching the target. This showcased its ability to produce realistic and contextually appropriate motion in dynamic settings, a novel capability compared to previous work.

**Ablation Studies:**
Ablations confirmed the importance of penetration loss, occupancy field loss, and the field regulation module for maintaining high success rates and low penetration. While the voxel-based occupancy representation proved effective, an alternative Basis Point Set (BPS) representation led to degradation in performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a paradigm shift in understanding and generating Human-Scene Interaction (HSI), primarily by re-conceptualizing it as Human-Occupancy Interaction (HOI). This perspective has several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Alleviating Data Scarcity:** The most immediate impact is the substantial mitigation of the "data hunger" problem in HSI generation. By demonstrating that pure motion-only data can be transformed into a paired human-occupancy interaction database (MOB), the need for expensive and difficult-to-acquire paired motion-3D scene datasets is significantly reduced. This unlocks vast existing motion capture repositories for HSI research, enabling the training of models on unprecedented scales of data diversity and complexity.

2. **Unified and Versatile Motion Generation:** The development of a single, auto-regressive motion controller capable of unifying locomotion and interaction generation represents an advancement. Unlike previous approaches that often decouple these aspects or rely on multiple policies, this work provides a more holistic and flexible framework. This unification simplifies the HSI generation pipeline and enhances the model's ability to produce coherent, long-term motions across various interaction types.

3. **Robustness in Complex and Dynamic Environments:** The controller's ability to learn from the stringent geometric constraints of the MOB dataset enables it to handle highly complex and cramped scenes more effectively than prior methods that often fail in such scenarios due to simplified pathfinding assumptions. Furthermore, its demonstrated capacity to generalize to and operate within dynamic environments, such as automatic doors, is a notable step forward, addressing a limitation of many existing static-scene-trained HSI models. This adaptability is crucial for applications in real-world settings.

4. **Advancements in Embodied AI and Robotics:** The improved ability to generate realistic and collision-free human-scene interactions can significantly benefit embodied AI and robotics research. This includes enhancing human-robot collaboration, developing more natural navigation behaviors for intelligent agents in complex environments, and facilitating realistic simulations for training autonomous systems.

5. **Applications in Digital Content Creation:** In animation, virtual reality, and gaming, this work can lead to the generation of more realistic, natural, and diverse character-scene interactions, reducing the manual effort involved in animation and improving immersion. For fields like interior design and architecture, the ability to simulate how humans might interact with proposed spaces could offer valuable insights during the design phase.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge certain limitations, including sub-optimal performance with dynamic scenes due to a "space occupancy changing rate gap" between training and testing, and the persistent challenge of foot sliding artifacts. Future work could explore methods to mitigate these gaps, improve foot contact modeling, and extend the human-occupancy view to more complex human-object interaction scenarios. Expanding the controller's input signals beyond target poses and trajectories could also enhance its utility.

In summary, this research offers a foundational re-evaluation of HSI, provides a scalable data generation strategy, and introduces a robust controller capable of operating in diverse and dynamic settings. These contributions have the potential to significantly advance the field of HSI and its practical applications.
