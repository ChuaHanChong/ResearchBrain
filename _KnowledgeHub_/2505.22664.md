# Zero-Shot Vision Encoder Grafting via LLM Surrogates

> [!summary] Summary
> A new method, "Zero-Shot Vision Encoder Grafting via LLM Surrogates," efficiently trains vision encoders for Vision-Language Models by leveraging smaller surrogate LLMs that replicate the target LLM's early processing layers. This approach reduces VLM training costs by approximately 45% and enables direct, zero-shot integration of the encoder into large, untuned language models.

## Problem

- Training Vision-Language Models (VLMs) is computationally expensive, primarily due to the large language model (LLM) component.
- Existing methods like LoRA often do not sufficiently accelerate convergence for the demanding task of aligning giant LLMs with vision features.
- VLM training frequently leads to a degradation of the LLM's original language understanding capabilities.

## Method

- Construct =="surrogate models"== by preserving the original parameters of the target LLM's early, feature-extraction layers and condensing its later layers into a single "translator" transformer.
- Train vision encoders and vision adapters using these smaller, specially designed surrogate models.
- Employ a ==multi-stage training strategy==: pre-training the surrogate's adapter and translator, ==fine-tuning the vision encoder with the frozen surrogate==, and optionally fine-tuning the full VLM decoder with a warm start from the surrogate-trained encoder.

## Results

- Achieved an approximate **45%** reduction in overall VLM training cost for models using Llama-70B as the decoder.
- Surrogate-trained encoders enabled strong zero-shot grafting, with grafted VLMs often matching or surpassing fully fine-tuned baselines on various VLM benchmarks (e.g., MME, POPE, SEED-Bench).
- The method mitigated language degradation in the fine-tuned LLM decoder, maintaining its language understanding capabilities on par with or slightly better than the original Llama-70B.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models exhibit distinct "early-phase" (feature extraction) and "late-phase" (prediction convergence) layers, identifiable by analyzing the layer-wise prediction trajectory using KL divergence.
> - Preserving the original parameters of the LLM's early-phase layers in a surrogate model is crucial for a vision encoder's ability to transfer its learned visual features to the full target LLM.
> - Vision encoders trained with these surrogates can be directly "zero-shot grafted" into much larger target LLMs, effectively activating complex visual understanding without additional fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses one of the most pressing challenges in the development of Vision-Language Models (VLMs): the prohibitive computational cost of training and fine-tuning these models, particularly when scaling to large language models. The work positions itself at the intersection of VLM efficiency, mechanistic interpretability of LLMs, and zero-shot learning.

**VLM Training and Efficiency:** Modern auto-regressive VLMs typically pair a vision encoder (e.g., CLIP) with a large language model (e.g., Llama-70B). The paper highlights that the LLM decoder constitutes the primary computational burden during training. Existing methods to mitigate this include:
- **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA):** While LoRA reduces trainable parameters and improves efficiency, the paper argues it often does not accelerate convergence speed for the demanding task of aligning LLMs with vision features, especially for giant models or complex tasks. It's designed for fine-tuning already aligned models, not for the "text-to-vision-language space" transformation required by VLMs.
- **Model Pruning:** Approaches that prune redundant layers (often deep layers) in LLMs aim to create smaller, more efficient models. While sharing the insight that certain layers might be less critical, pruning's objective is to reduce model size *while preserving performance*, whereas this work's objective is *encoder transferability* and *cost reduction* through a surrogate.
- **Progressive Multi-stage Training:** Some VLMs use multi-stage training to scale model size and refine image processing. However, this paper provides a more specific and well-defined framework for constructing efficient *surrogate* models tailored for *any* target LLM, focusing on direct transferability and cost reduction.

**Mechanistic Interpretability of LLMs:** The core of the methodology relies on analyzing the "prediction trajectory" within LLM layers. This ties into the growing field of mechanistic interpretability, which seeks to understand the internal workings of neural networks. Previous works have used linear classifiers or "logit lens" techniques to probe intermediate layer dynamics. This paper extends such analysis by identifying distinct "early-phase" (feature extraction) and "late-phase" (prediction convergence) layers, using this understanding to inform architectural design choices for surrogate models. This approach demonstrates how interpretability insights can directly lead to practical efficiency gains.

**Zero-Shot Transfer and LLM Steering:** A significant contribution is the concept of "zero-shot grafting," where a vision encoder trained on a smaller surrogate can be directly plugged into a much larger target LLM without any additional fine-tuning, enabling complex visual understanding. This aligns with research in "steering LLMs," where lightweight methods are used to guide LLMs to perform specific tasks without extensive fine-tuning. In this context, the surrogate-trained image features act as "steering tokens," allowing the target LLM to interpret visual content effectively.

In summary, this work provides a novel and highly effective solution to the VLM training cost problem by intelligently leveraging insights from LLM interpretability to construct efficient surrogate models. It offers a practical alternative or complementary approach to existing efficiency methods, setting a new benchmark for cost reduction and faster convergence in VLM development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind this research stems from the observation that training Vision-Language Models (VLMs) is excessively expensive, primarily due to the large language model (LLM) component. The vision encoder, while critical, typically has a modest parameter count compared to the LLM decoder (e.g., CLIP with ~400M parameters versus Llama-70B with 70B parameters). Training the entire VLM end-to-end, which is necessary to align visual features with the LLM's representation space, incurs a massive computational burden dominated by the LLM.

Given this bottleneck, the authors set out with the following key objectives:

1. **Reduce Overall VLM Training Costs:** The overarching goal is to significantly decrease the computational and time resources required to train and fine-tune VLMs, particularly those incorporating giant LLMs. This addresses a major barrier to VLM research and deployment.

2. **Develop Small, Aligned "Surrogate Models":** To achieve cost reduction, the authors aim to construct smaller language models, termed "surrogates," that maintain the same embedding space and representation language as their much larger target LLMs. The motivation here is to enable vision encoder training with a less resource-intensive language model.

3. **Enable "Zero-Shot Grafting" of Vision Encoders:** A crucial objective is to demonstrate that a vision encoder trained with a surrogate model can be directly transferred and used by a full-size target LLM *without any additional fine-tuning*. This "zero-shot grafting" capability would signify that the surrogate-trained encoder can produce image features directly interpretable by the target LLM, effectively converting it into a VLM instantly.

4. **Accelerate Full VLM Decoder Training:** Beyond zero-shot grafting, the authors aim to show that encoders trained on surrogates can provide a "warm start" for subsequent fine-tuning of the full-size target LLM decoder. This would lead to faster convergence and substantial reductions in the final training stage's cost, which is the most expensive part of VLM development.

5. **Understand and Leverage LLM Internal Dynamics:** A foundational objective is to analyze the internal prediction dynamics of LLMs, specifically how predictions evolve across layers. By identifying critical transition points and distinct processing phases within LLMs, the authors aim to inform the design of effective surrogate models that accurately mimic the target LLM's early-phase behavior essential for vision-language alignment.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to democratize VLM development, making advanced multimodal AI more accessible and efficient for a wider range of researchers and applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology revolves around the intelligent construction and utilization of "surrogate models" to efficiently train vision encoders for large target LLMs. The approach can be broken down into three main parts: surrogate model construction, a multi-stage training strategy, and comprehensive evaluation.

#### 3.1. Surrogate Model Construction: "Layer Grafting" based on Prediction Trajectory Analysis

The most novel aspect of the methodology is how surrogate models are built. This is guided by a deep analysis of LLM internal dynamics:

1. **Analyzing the Prediction Trajectory:**
 - For a given target LLM and text input, the authors trace the evolution of hidden states across all transformer layers.
 - They compute intermediate probability distributions ($q_ℓ$) for the next token at each layer $ℓ$, and compare these to the final probability distribution ($p$) of the model using KL divergence ($D_{KL}(q_ℓ || p)$).
 - This analysis reveals a "transition point" where the layer-wise predictions abruptly coalesce and then monotonically converge to the final output. This point is hypothesized to separate an "early phase" (feature extraction, combining simple representations into higher-order concepts) from a "late phase" (convergence towards final token prediction). For example, in Llama-70B, this point occurs around layer 40.

2. **Studying the Transition Phases and Transferability:**
 - To validate their hypothesis, the authors experiment with Llama-3B (and later Llama-8B), constructing small models by replacing blocks of layers with a single "translator" transformer layer.
 - They test two scenarios: replacing early-phase layers (discarding initial feature extraction) and replacing late-phase layers (preserving initial feature extraction).
 - **Key Finding:** Preserving the *early-phase* layers and their original parameters is crucial for the vision encoder's transferability and zero-shot grafting capability to the target LLM. Replacing early-phase layers leads to poor performance and grafting failure. Conversely, replacing late-phase layers with a translator maintains strong transferability.
 - **Optimizing Layer Preservation:** Further ablations determine that retaining all early-phase layers *before* the identified transition point yields the best zero-shot grafting performance.

3. **Surrogate Model Design:**
 - Based on these findings, a surrogate model is constructed by:
 - **Inheriting Shallow Layers:** Directly taking the original parameters of the target LLM's initial layers up to the identified transition point (the "early phase"). This ensures the shared embedding space and representation language.
 - **Replacing Deep Layers:** Condensing all subsequent layers (the "late phase") into a single, identical transformer layer, referred to as a "translator." This translator is initialized from the shallowest original layer it replaced.
 - For Llama-70B (with 79 layers, transition at layer 40), the surrogate T(40, 78) preserves layers 0-40 and replaces layers 41-78 with a translator, resulting in a 37B parameter surrogate.

#### 3.2. Multi-Stage Training Strategy

The overall training process for VLMs using surrogate models involves three stages:

1. **Stage 1: Surrogate Pre-training (Adapter + Translator):**
 - A vision adapter (a two-layer MLP) and the *translator* component of the surrogate model are simultaneously pre-trained.
 - Data: A mix of vision-language instructions (e.g., LLaVA-1.5-665K) and text-only instructions (e.g., GenQA-500K). The vision adapter only receives gradients from vision-language data.
 - Objective: To partially align the vision adapter and train the translator to process features in the surrogate's context.

2. **Stage 2: Vision Encoder Fine-tuning (with Surrogate):**
 - The vision encoder (e.g., CLIP-L/14) and the vision adapter are fine-tuned.
 - The *surrogate model* acts as the frozen decoder.
 - Data: LLaVA-1.5-665K instructions.
 - Objective: To align the vision encoder's output features with the surrogate's (and thus the target LLM's) embedding space. This is where the encoder gains its "zero-shot grafting" capability. For Llama-70B experiments, only the last eight layers of the encoder are trained for efficiency.

3. **Stage 3: Full VLM Decoder Fine-tuning (Optional, for optimal performance):**
 - The *surrogate-trained encoder* from Stage 2 is plugged into the *full-size target LLM*.
 - The entire target LLM decoder (or a subset of its layers, if LoRA is used) is fine-tuned.
 - Data: LLaVA-1.5-665K instructions.
 - Objective: To further refine the VLM's performance, leveraging the strong warm start provided by the already aligned encoder. This stage significantly benefits from the prior surrogate training, requiring less data and time.

#### 3.3. Evaluation

The methodology includes extensive evaluation using:
- **Text Benchmarks:** To assess the quality of the surrogate models (e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag).
- **VLM Benchmarks:** To evaluate the performance of encoders:
 - When paired with the surrogate.
 - In a *zero-shot grafted* configuration (surrogate-trained encoder directly with target LLM).
 - After *full decoder fine-tuning* (Stage 3).
 - Benchmarks include MME, POPE, SEED-Bench, MM-Vet, LLaVA-Wild, MMBench, TextVQA, GQA, DocVQA, ChartQA, InfoVQA, AI2D, Viz-Wiz, and CV-Bench.
- **Cost Analysis:** Direct comparison of training hours and data utilization between the proposed surrogate approach and traditional full-decoder training methods.
- **Qualitative Analysis:** Examples of VLM responses to complex visual questions, creativity tasks, and OCR to illustrate the quality of zero-shot grafting.

The authors also discuss dynamic loss weighting (in the appendix) to address performance issues for short-answer tasks when using smaller LLMs, by balancing loss contributions based on response length. This highlights a nuanced consideration for training stability across different response styles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, validating the proposed methodology for efficient VLM training:

1. **Consistent Identification of LLM Transition Points:** The KL divergence analysis of layer-wise prediction trajectories consistently revealed a clear "transition point" across various LLM families and sizes (Llama-3B, 8B, 70B; Gemma-2B; Qwen families). This point effectively separates an early phase (critical for feature extraction and representation) from a late phase (focused on final token prediction convergence).

2. **Critical Role of Early-Phase Layers for Encoder Transferability:**
 - Experiments robustly demonstrated that preserving the *original parameters* of the early-phase layers is paramount for an encoder's ability to transfer its learned visual features to a target LLM.
 - Surrogate models built by discarding early-phase layers (e.g., T(1,11) for Llama-3B) resulted in encoders that failed to generate interpretable image features for the full target LLM, leading to poor zero-shot grafting performance.
 - Conversely, surrogates that preserved early-phase layers and replaced late-phase layers (e.g., T(16,26) for Llama-3B, T(17,30) for Llama-8B, T(40,78) for Llama-70B) enabled strong encoder transferability.

3. **Strong Zero-Shot Grafting Capability:**
 - Encoders trained on these carefully constructed surrogate models (e.g., surrogate-37B for Llama-70B) could be directly "grafted" into the full-size target LLM *without any additional fine-tuning*.
 - The grafted VLM demonstrated remarkable visual understanding capabilities, often outperforming the encoder-surrogate pair and, on some VLM benchmarks (e.g., MME, POPE, SEED-Bench, MM-Vet, LLaVA-Wild, MMBench), even matching or surpassing the performance of full decoder training with the target LLM. This highlights that surrogate-trained encoders effectively "trigger" the giant LLM to perform complex visual tasks.

4. **Significant Reduction in Training Costs:**
 - The surrogate training approach drastically reduced the computational cost of full VLM decoder training. For Llama-70B as the decoder, the method achieved approximately a **45% reduction in overall training cost**.
 - This reduction is primarily attributed to accelerated convergence in the final decoder fine-tuning stage. The surrogate-trained encoder provides such a strong "warm start" that the full Llama-70B decoder achieved performance comparable to a baseline trained on 100% of the data, using only **10-20% of the training data**.
 - The total training time for the entire process (pre-training surrogate, fine-tuning encoder, fine-tuning decoder) was 19.17 hours, compared to 34.79 hours for the baseline, demonstrating substantial time savings.

5. **Mitigation of Language Degradation:** The surrogate approach helped to mitigate the common problem of language performance degradation in LLMs after VLM training. The fine-tuned Llama-70B decoder from their method retained its language understanding capabilities, performing on par with or slightly better than the original Llama-70B on text benchmarks, whereas the baseline method suffered a significant performance drop.

6. **Generalizability Across LLM Families:** The methodology proved generalizable across different LLM architectures and scales, including Llama, Gemma, and Qwen families, suggesting its broad applicability.

7. **Limitations Identified:** While highly effective, the authors noted a limitation: the constructed surrogate models, though smaller, can still be relatively large (e.g., 37B for Llama-70B). This highlights the need for further research into more efficient compression methods for surrogates. Additionally, distilled models (e.g., Llama-1B as a surrogate for Llama-8B) were found to be less ideal due to embedding dimension mismatches, underscoring the importance of inheriting shallow layers directly.

These findings collectively demonstrate a highly efficient and effective strategy for training VLMs, significantly lowering the resource demands while maintaining or even improving performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Zero-Shot Vision Encoder Grafting via LLM Surrogates" carries profound significance and holds the potential for substantial impact across various facets of AI research and application.

1. **Democratization of VLM Development:** The most immediate and impactful contribution is the significant reduction in VLM training costs (approximately 45% for Llama-70B). This lowers the barrier to entry for developing and experimenting with powerful Vision-Language Models. Researchers and institutions with limited computational resources, who might otherwise be priced out of working with giant LLMs, can now participate more actively in cutting-edge VLM research. This can accelerate innovation and foster a more diverse research landscape.

2. **Faster Research and Development Cycles:** By enabling a "warm start" and accelerating convergence during full VLM decoder fine-tuning, the methodology allows for quicker iteration in VLM development. This will enable researchers to test new ideas, architectures, and datasets much faster, leading to a more rapid pace of progress in multimodal AI.

3. **Enhanced VLM Performance and Stability:** Beyond just cost reduction, the surrogate-trained encoders facilitate strong zero-shot grafting capabilities, often matching or surpassing fully fine-tuned baselines on various VLM benchmarks. Furthermore, the approach helps mitigate language degradation, a common issue where VLM training compromises the LLM's pure language understanding. This leads to more robust and versatile VLMs that excel in both visual and linguistic tasks.

4. **New Avenues in Mechanistic Interpretability:** The methodology is rooted in a deeper understanding of LLM internal dynamics. The discovery of distinct early and late processing phases and their roles in transferability contributes valuable insights to the field of mechanistic interpretability. This work demonstrates a concrete application of interpretability research to improve practical AI system design, potentially inspiring further exploration into how internal model states can be leveraged for efficiency and performance.

5. **Foundation for Future VLM Architectures:** The concept of "zero-shot grafting" through aligned surrogate models could become a standard technique for VLM pre-training. It suggests a paradigm shift where vision encoders are not just paired, but intelligently "grafted" onto LLMs, optimized for direct interpretability from the outset. This could influence the design of next-generation multimodal foundation models.

6. **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated for vision encoders, the authors hint that the surrogate model concept might extend to other types of encoders (e.g., audio, tabular data) in multimodal contexts. This could lead to a more generalized framework for efficient multimodal AI.

7. **Industry Adoption:** With Meta's involvement, the practical applicability and scalability of this method are underscored. Its potential for significant cost savings makes it highly attractive for industry players developing and deploying large-scale multimodal AI solutions.

In conclusion, this paper presents a groundbreaking approach to VLM training that simultaneously addresses critical challenges in cost, efficiency, and model stability. By intelligently leveraging LLM internal dynamics, it offers a pathway to more accessible, faster, and higher-performing multimodal AI systems, with wide-ranging implications for both academic research and real-world applications.
