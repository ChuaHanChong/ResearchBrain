# Speech2Grasp: Data-Efficient Transfer of Text-Conditioned Grasp Detection to Speech in Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at multiple institutions, including the University of California San Diego and VinMotion, developed Speech2Grasp, a data-efficient framework enabling humanoid robots to perform text-conditioned grasp detection directly from speech commands. This approach achieves grasp rates comparable to text-only methods, significantly reduces inference latency, and demonstrates robustness to real-world acoustic variations with a modest training dataset of 15K speech samples.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots predominantly rely on textual inputs for vision-language models, despite speech being a more natural and intuitive communication modality for humans.
- Existing methods for integrating speech into robotic systems either require extensive data collection and computational resources (adapting large foundation models) or suffer from high latency and error propagation (cascaded ASR pipelines).
- A data-efficient approach was needed to transfer the semantic understanding of established text-conditioned grasp detection models to direct speech inputs.

## Method

- **Speech2Grasp** was developed, a framework that leverages a lightweight ==Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) projector== to map speech embeddings into the latent space of a pre-trained vision-language model (==ALBEF==).
- The projector is trained using a ==knowledge distillation loss== to align speech-conditioned representations with existing image-text representations, minimizing the need for large, task-specific speech datasets.
- A ==frequency-aware visual encoder==, integrating ==Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)==, processes input images to enhance robustness against camera noise by preserving object boundaries while discarding noise-dominant components.

## Results

- Speech2Grasp achieved grasp detection success rates (harmonic mean **0.35**) comparable to text-only and ASR-based baselines in simulations, while reducing inference latency **threefold** (**36.6 ms** vs. **102.2 ms**) by eliminating the transcription step.
- Real-world experiments on a Unitree humanoid robot showed Speech2Grasp achieved higher grasp detection rates than ASR-based pipelines (e.g., **0.70** vs. **0.59** for one speaker) in uncontrolled acoustic environments with non-native speakers, mitigating ASR mistranscription errors.
- The framework proved data-efficient, requiring only **15K** speech samples for training while maintaining a high representation alignment (cosine similarity **0.94**) and overall grasp performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A lightweight MLP-based projector is sufficient to effectively align image-speech and image-text representations within a VLM's latent space, preserving semantic discrimination with an average cosine similarity of 0.968.
> - The speech projector demonstrates robustness to unseen speakers and acoustically perturbed speech, suggesting that data-efficient adaptation for speech transfer is feasible without prohibitively large datasets.
> - Integrating DWT into the visual pipeline improves robustness against camera noise by selectively processing image subbands, which is critical for maintaining grasp localization accuracy in noisy environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots require advanced multimodal understanding to facilitate natural interaction with humans. Vision-language models (VLMs) have emerged as foundational components for enabling language-conditioned perception in robots. However, a prevalent characteristic of these models is their reliance on textual inputs, despite speech being a more intuitive and natural communication modality for humans.

Existing approaches to integrate speech into robotic systems typically involve either adapting large-scale foundation image-speech models or utilizing cascaded pipelines that employ Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems. Adapting foundation image-speech models, such as ImageBind or LanguageBind, to specific downstream robotic tasks like grasp detection often necessitates extensive data collection with task-specific annotations and significant computational resources for architectural redesigns and fine-tuning. Alternatively, cascaded ASR-based pipelines convert speech to text before feeding it to text-conditioned VLMs. This approach, while seemingly straightforward, introduces increased latency and is susceptible to error propagation, particularly mistranscriptions by the ASR system, which can lead to complete failure in the downstream robotic task.

The current research positions itself by addressing the challenge of transferring the capabilities of well-established *text-conditioned* robotic systems to operate directly with *speech inputs* in a *data-efficient* manner. It builds upon previous works in cross-modal transfer, which have demonstrated the feasibility of distilling knowledge from large pre-trained text models to speech with relatively small datasets. Specifically, prior studies have shown that lightweight neural networks can align text and speech representations while preserving semantic content. For robotic grasp detection, existing language-conditioned methods, such as Grasp-Anything, Grasp-Anything++, and Grasp-SAM, predominantly assume textual language inputs. This paper extends these capabilities by exploring direct speech-guided grasp detection, thereby bridging a gap in the application of multimodal understanding for human-robot interaction. It aims to develop a practical paradigm that leverages the semantic knowledge already embedded in text-conditioned VLMs without incurring the substantial data and computational overhead typically associated with training new speech-aware models from scratch or relying on error-prone cascaded ASR systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-efficient framework for transferring the semantic understanding of established text-conditioned grasp detection models to direct speech inputs for humanoid robots. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and specific objectives:

1. **Enhance Natural Human-Robot Interaction**: Speech is identified as a more natural and intuitive mode of communication for human-robot interaction compared to textual input. The current reliance of most vision-language models (VLMs) on text limits the fluidity and naturalness of human-robot communication. The work aims to overcome this limitation by enabling robots to directly interpret spoken commands.

2. **Overcome Limitations of ASR-based Pipelines**: Existing cascaded ASR-based pipelines for speech-to-text conversion suffer from two main drawbacks:
 - **Increased Latency**: The sequential nature of transcription followed by VLM processing introduces delays, which can be critical in real-time robotic applications.
 - **Error Propagation**: ASR mistranscriptions, particularly in noisy or unconstrained environments, can lead to complete failure of downstream tasks (e.g., grasping the wrong object if the ASR misinterprets the target). The research seeks to bypass this discrete transcription step to improve robustness.

3. **Address Data and Computational Demands of Foundation Models**: While foundation image-speech models exist, adapting them to specific robotic tasks often requires collecting large, task-specific speech datasets with corresponding annotations, followed by complex architectural redesigns and computationally expensive fine-tuning processes. A motivation is to achieve speech capability without these prohibitive requirements.

4. **Investigate Text-Speech Representation Gap**: A foundational objective is to diagnostically analyze the differences between text and speech representations within a pre-trained VLM's latent space. Specifically, using ALBEF (ALign BEfore Fuse) as a case study, the research aims to understand the representation gap introduced when text inputs are replaced by corresponding speech inputs in the joint embedding space.

5. **Develop Data-Efficient Adaptation Mechanisms**: Based on the diagnostic findings, a key objective is to design and evaluate a lightweight adaptation mechanism (e.g., an MLP-based projector) that can effectively bridge the representation gap between speech and text within the VLM's latent space. This mechanism should preserve semantic discrimination and robustness to variations in acoustics and speakers, while requiring only a modest amount of paired image-speech data.

6. **Introduce Speech2Grasp Framework**: The research aims to integrate these findings into a practical framework, named Speech2Grasp, for transferring text-conditioned grasp detection to speech. This framework should demonstrate performance comparable to, or exceeding, ASR-based pipelines in terms of grasp detection rates, while simultaneously reducing inference latency and maintaining data efficiency.

7. **Demonstrate Real-World Deployability**: A final objective is to validate the developed framework through real-world experiments on a humanoid robot, assessing its feasibility and robustness under uncontrolled acoustic conditions and speaker variations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research can be broadly divided into diagnostic analyses of vision-language model representations and the development of the Speech2Grasp framework.

#### 3.1. Diagnostic Analyses of ALBEF Text–Speech Representations

The initial phase involved a diagnostic study using ALBEF, a pre-trained vision-language model designed for learning semantically aligned image-text representations. The goal was to understand the representation gap when text is replaced by speech.

1. **ALBEF Module as a Case Study**: ALBEF’s fusion module combines image and text features into a joint [CLS] embedding. The study investigated if speech-conditioned representations could be aligned with this existing shared latent space.
2. **Data Generation**: Image-text pairs were sourced from the Grasp-Anything++ dataset. Corresponding speech instructions were synthesized using Kitten-TTS, resulting in 15K aligned image-text-speech samples for diagnostic analysis.
3. **Speech Embedding and Projection**: A pre-trained Whisper encoder extracted speech embeddings. These embeddings were then passed through a trainable lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)-based projector. The projector's role was to map speech embeddings into ALBEF’s latent space.
4. **Knowledge Distillation (KD) Objective**: The projector was trained using a knowledge distillation loss ($L_{KD}$), which minimized the cosine similarity between the student’s (image-speech) and teacher’s (image-text) [CLS] embeddings. This encouraged the image-speech branch to reproduce the joint representation generated by the frozen image-text branch.
5. **Evaluation of Representation Gap**:
 - **Cosine Similarity (CS)**: Kernel Density Estimates (KDE) of CS between student and teacher [CLS] embeddings were used to quantify alignment. Variants included using speech without the projector, with the projector using matched speech, and with the projector using randomly paired speech.
 - **Semantic Discrimination**: The ability of the projector to distinguish between matched and mismatched speech inputs was assessed by comparing the CS for matched versus randomly paired speech.
 - **t-SNE Visualizations**: t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) was used to visualize the high-dimensional [CLS] embeddings, showing the spatial relationship and overlap between teacher, unprojected student, and projected student embeddings.
6. **Generalization Study**: To evaluate robustness, an additional 21K speech samples were synthesized using seven unseen voices from Kitten-TTS. These samples were further acoustically perturbed using a model incorporating room impulse responses (RIRs) and environmental noise at 8 dB Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), resulting in 84K unseen and corrupted speech samples. The projector's performance was evaluated under these varied conditions using KDE curves and t-SNE visualizations.

#### 3.2. Speech2Grasp Framework Development

Motivated by the diagnostic findings, the Speech2Grasp framework was developed for data-efficient transfer of grasp detection to speech.

1. **Problem Statement**: The goal was to transform a text-conditioned grasp detector $f(I, T) \rightarrow G$ into a speech-conditioned one $g(I, S) \rightarrow G$, using minimal paired image-speech data.
2. **Architecture Initialization**: The student model $g(I, S)$ was initialized from a pre-trained text-conditioned grasp detector, LGD (Language-driven Grasp Detection).
3. **Visual Branch**: The grasp head of the visual branch was kept frozen to preserve existing grasp capabilities. The U-shaped encoder-decoder was lightly fine-tuned to adapt to the new input stream and fused features.
4. **Speech Branch**: This branch incorporated the pre-trained Whisper encoder and the MLP-based projector, as validated in the diagnostic studies, to map speech embeddings into the ALBEF latent space.
5. **Fusion Mechanism**: The projected speech embeddings were injected into the visual semantic bottleneck for vision-language fusion, leveraging ALBEF's original fusion strategy.
6. **Training Objective**: The total training loss was a combination of a task-specific grasp loss ($L_{grasp}$) and the knowledge distillation loss ($L_{KD}$) from the diagnostic phase: $L = L_{grasp} + \lambda_{KD} L_{KD}$.
 - $L_{grasp}$: Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth grasp maps.
 - $\lambda_{KD}$: A balancing term with an off-ramp linear scheduler, initially emphasizing representation alignment and gradually shifting focus to the task loss.
7. **Speech Encoding and Augmentation**:
 - **On-the-fly Acoustic Augmentation**: During training, speech inputs were augmented dynamically using various RIRs and noise samples to simulate diverse acoustic conditions without increasing dataset storage.
 - **Feature Extraction**: Augmented speech signals were converted into log-mel spectrograms, which are the expected inputs for Whisper.
 - **Tokenization**: Whisper outputs were converted into a BERT-like token sequence, with a [SUMMARY] token from global mean pooling and [UTTER] tokens from adaptive pooling.
 - **Projector Architecture**: The MLP projector incorporated a skip connection to mitigate overfitting.
8. **Frequency-aware Encoder and Decoder**:
 - **Motivation**: To improve robustness against camera noise and emphasize high-frequency (HF) components (object boundaries) crucial for grasp localization.
 - **Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)**: The input RGB image was processed by DWT, decomposing it into LL (coarse), LH (horizontal), HL (vertical), and HH (diagonal) subbands.
 - **Input Modification**: The noise-dominant HH subband was discarded. The LL, LH, and HL subbands were concatenated to form the input to the visual encoder-decoder (R H/2xW/2x9). This preserved object boundaries while suppressing noise.
 - **Encoder-Decoder Optimization**: Only the initial layers of the encoder-decoder were optimized to adapt to the wavelet subbands, maintaining grasp capability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Diagnostic Analyses of ALBEF Text–Speech Representations

- **Effective Alignment**: A lightweight MLP-based projector was found to be sufficient for aligning image-speech and image-text [CLS] embeddings within the ALBEF latent space. The average cosine similarity (CS) between projected student and teacher embeddings was 0.968 ± 0.027, a substantial improvement compared to 0.346 ± 0.088 when no projector was used.
- **Semantic Discrimination Preservation**: The projector maintained semantic discrimination, as evidenced by a significantly higher CS for matched speech pairs (0.968) compared to randomly paired speech (0.835). The matched model outperformed the shuffled model in 98.2% of test instances after projection.
- **Robustness to Unseen Conditions**: The projector demonstrated graceful degradation and high alignment preservation even when faced with unseen speakers and acoustically perturbed speech (noise and reverberation). For the worst-case scenario (specific voice with point-source interference), the average CS remained high at 0.915 ± 0.070, and t-SNE visualizations showed structural similarity to clean embeddings. These findings suggested data-efficient adaptation for speech transfer was feasible without prohibitively large datasets.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

- **Grasp Performance and Latency (Table I)**:
 - Speech2Grasp achieved grasp detection success rates (Seen: 0.36, Unseen: 0.33, Harmonic Mean: 0.35) comparable to the text-only teacher model (LGD: S: 0.36, U: 0.33, H: 0.35).
 - It slightly outperformed the cascaded LiteASR-based pipeline (S: 0.34, U: 0.33, H: 0.34), which suffered from occasional mistranscriptions of clean, synthesized speech.
 - Speech2Grasp significantly reduced inference latency by threefold (36.6 ms) compared to the ASR-based pipeline (102.2 ms) by eliminating the transcription step.
 - The framework was data-efficient, requiring only 15K speech samples for training, in contrast to the approximate 1M samples implicitly used by ASR models or LGD's pre-training. The final CS for speech-text alignment with 15K samples was 0.94.
- **Robustness to Camera Noise (Table II)**:
 - Integration of the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) into Speech2Grasp improved robustness against simulated Poisson-Gaussian camera noise.
 - Under "mild" noise conditions, DWT-integrated Speech2Grasp maintained a harmonic mean grasp rate of 0.34, similar to its noiseless performance. Without DWT, performance degraded.
 - Under "severe" noise conditions, DWT-integrated Speech2Grasp achieved a harmonic mean of 0.29, outperforming the non-DWT version of LGD and the ASR-pipeline. This was attributed to discarding the noise-dominant HH subband while preserving object boundary information in LH and HL.
- **Effect of Number of Speech Samples (Table III)**: Increasing the number of speech training samples (from 7.5K to 15K) improved both representation alignment (CS from 0.83 to 0.94) and overall grasp detection performance (Harmonic Mean from 0.30 to 0.35).

#### 4.3. Real-world Robotic Experiments

- **Improved Grasp Detection Rate**: Experiments on a Unitree humanoid robot using speech commands recorded in an uncontrolled office environment with non-native English speakers demonstrated that Speech2Grasp achieved higher grasp detection rates compared to the cascaded ASR-based pipeline. For example, for Person #1 in the single-object scenario, Speech2Grasp achieved 0.70 ± 0.02, whereas the ASR pipeline achieved 0.59 ± 0.03.
- **Mitigation of ASR Mistranscriptions**: The ASR pipeline frequently suffered from mistranscriptions of short, context-lacking real-world speech commands, leading to complete task failures. Speech2Grasp, being natively trained on speech and robust to acoustic variations as shown in diagnostic studies, avoided these discrete transcription errors, contributing to its improved real-world performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a practical and data-efficient paradigm for extending the capabilities of existing text-conditioned vision-language models to handle speech inputs directly, particularly within the domain of humanoid robot manipulation.

The **diagnostic analyses** are significant because they demonstrate that the semantic knowledge embedded in pre-trained VLMs like ALBEF can be effectively transferred to speech through a lightweight MLP-based projector. This finding suggests that a complete architectural overhaul or extensive retraining with large speech datasets may not be necessary to endow existing text-based robotic systems with speech understanding. This has the potential to accelerate the development of speech-enabled robotic applications.

The **Speech2Grasp framework** offers several impacts:

1. **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction**: By enabling direct interpretation of speech commands, Speech2Grasp facilitates more natural, intuitive, and fluid human-robot interaction. This is a critical step towards creating robots that can communicate and operate seamlessly within human environments, moving beyond cumbersome text-based interfaces.
2. **Increased Robustness and Reliability**: The framework's ability to operate directly from speech bypasses the error propagation inherent in cascaded ASR pipelines. Its demonstrated resilience to unseen speakers, acoustic variations (noise and reverberation), and camera noise makes it more robust for deployment in real-world, uncontrolled environments, where such factors are prevalent.
3. **Efficiency Gains**: Reduced inference latency, achieved by eliminating the ASR transcription step, is crucial for real-time robotic operations. Furthermore, the data-efficient adaptation requiring only modest paired speech data (15K samples) significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing speech-controlled robotic tasks, making it a more accessible solution for researchers and developers.
4. **Leveraging Existing Investments**: The approach allows for the re-use of well-established, high-performing text-conditioned models, thereby maximizing the utility of existing research and development in vision-language processing for robotics. This avoids the costly and time-consuming process of developing entirely new image-speech models for every robotic application.
5. **Broader Applicability**: While demonstrated specifically for language-driven grasp detection, the findings regarding data-efficient cross-modal transfer suggest that this paradigm could be generalized to other text-conditioned robotic tasks (e.g., navigation, object manipulation, instruction following). This could foster a wider adoption of speech interfaces across various robotic capabilities.

In summary, this work provides a method for integrating speech understanding into robotic systems that is both effective and practical, addressing key challenges related to naturalness, robustness, and resource efficiency. It suggests a viable path for the widespread adoption of speech-enabled control in future humanoid robots.
