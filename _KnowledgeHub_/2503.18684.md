# Efficient Continual Adaptation of Pretrained Robotic Policy with Online Meta-Learned Adapters

> [!summary] Summary
> Online Meta-Learned Adapters (OMLA) proposes an online meta-learning framework for continuously adapting pretrained robotic policies, using meta-learned adapters to proactively transfer knowledge across tasks. This approach achieves superior forward transfer (e.g., 0.86 average FWT on LIBERO-OBJECT compared to LoRA's 0.71) and prevents catastrophic forgetting in both simulated and real-world robotic control tasks.

## Problem

- Existing continual learning methods in robotics suffer from catastrophic forgetting and conservative learning, hindering efficient adaptation to new tasks.
- Current adapter-based approaches for robotic policy fine-tuning treat each new task's adapter learning independently, limiting knowledge transfer from past experiences.
- Traditional meta-learning frameworks can be computationally intensive for high-dimensional robotic observations and large models, making them impractical for online continual adaptation.

## Method

- OMLA integrates parameter-efficient LoRA adapters with an online meta-learning objective to learn a task-agnostic prior for adapter parameters.
- A two-stage learning paradigm is introduced: an online meta-learning phase updates adapter initialization using past tasks, followed by a fine-tuning phase on the current new task.
- A novel similarity-based sampling strategy is employed for meta-learning, selecting relevant observation-action pairs from past episodes to reduce computational overhead.

## Results

- OMLA achieved consistently higher Forward Transfer (FWT) compared to LoRA and Experience Replay (ER) across LIBERO task suites, for example, 0.86 FWT on LIBERO-OBJECT versus 0.71 for LoRA.
- Both OMLA and LoRA demonstrated a Backward Transfer (BWT) of 0, effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting, unlike ER which showed negative BWT values.
- The method proved robust across varying dataset sizes (5 to 20 demonstrations) and different policy architectures (ViT and ResNet-18 backbones), and achieved higher success rates in real-world pick-and-place tasks (e.g., 84.0% with 20 demonstrations vs. LoRA's 60.0%).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning an informed prior for adapter parameters through online meta-learning enables more efficient and effective adaptation to new tasks by facilitating knowledge transfer.
> - A targeted sampling strategy for meta-learning can significantly reduce computational costs in robotics while retaining sufficient information for effective adaptation.
> - Continually incorporating newly learned task data into the meta-learning pool is crucial for enriching the prior and maintaining high adaptation performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses a critical challenge in the development of general autonomous agents: the efficient and effective continual adaptation of pretrained robotic policies. This area resides at the intersection of continual learning, meta-learning, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) for robotic control.

In recent years, large pretrained models have demonstrated strong generalization capabilities across various domains, including natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision. This paradigm has extended to robotics, where pretrained policies exhibit zero-shot generalization to new initial conditions. However, real-world robotic tasks often involve novel objects, unseen instructions, or dynamic environments, necessitating mechanisms for continual adaptation beyond simple generalization.

Traditional continual learning methods in robotics frequently encounter two primary issues: catastrophic forgetting, where performance on previously learned tasks degrades upon learning new ones, and conservative learning, which limits the model's ability to adapt sufficiently to new tasks. Prior works, inspired by PEFT techniques like LoRA in language models, have explored using lightweight, task-specific adapters to modify pretrained policies. These adapters introduce a small number of additional parameters, mitigating catastrophic forgetting by keeping the core pretrained model frozen.

However, a limitation of these existing adapter-based approaches is their tendency to treat each new task's adapter learning as an independent process. This separation restricts knowledge transfer from previously acquired tasks to new ones, hindering efficient adaptation. The current work aims to bridge this gap by integrating meta-learning principles with adapter-based fine-tuning, specifically in an online continual learning setting, to facilitate proactive knowledge transfer and improve adaptation efficiency. This places the work as an advancement in leveraging inter-task relationships for more robust and efficient continual adaptation in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the necessity for autonomous agents, such as household robots, to continually adapt to novel tasks and environments specific to their deployment context, even after extensive pretraining. While pretrained robotic policies offer a strong foundation, they struggle with truly novel scenarios, highlighting the need for efficient adaptation mechanisms.

The key objectives of this paper are:
1. **Address the limitation of existing adapter-based approaches:** Prior methods utilize task-specific adapters to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning. However, they typically train these adapters from scratch for each new task, thereby limiting knowledge transfer from previously learned tasks. The objective is to overcome this by enabling adapters to leverage past experience.
2. **Facilitate knowledge transfer between tasks:** The core goal is to develop a mechanism that allows lightweight adapters to effectively transfer knowledge from a history of learned tasks to a current learning task. This is hypothesized to lead to faster and more effective adaptation.
3. **Propose an online meta-learning objective for adapter initialization:** To achieve knowledge transfer, the authors aim to use an online meta-learning framework. Instead of random initialization, this framework would learn a prior over adapter parameters from previous tasks, providing a more informed starting point for new task adaptation.
4. **Develop a computationally efficient meta-training strategy for robotic policies:** Conventional meta-learning in robotics, especially with high-dimensional visual observations and large models, can be computationally intensive, often requiring processing entire trajectories. An objective is to devise a sampling strategy that reduces this overhead while retaining essential information for meta-learning.
5. **Validate the proposed method in diverse robotic settings:** The research seeks to demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed approach across various simulated robotic tasks (object, spatial, and goal variations) and in real-world pick-and-place scenarios, with varying dataset sizes and policy architectures.
6. **Evaluate performance using established continual learning metrics:** Quantify the benefits of the proposed approach in terms of forward transfer (adaptation speed/performance on new tasks) and backward transfer (prevention of catastrophic forgetting) compared to baseline methods.

In essence, the motivation is to evolve adapter-based continual adaptation from a task-isolated approach to a knowledge-sharing paradigm, leading to more general, adaptable, and robust robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Online Meta-Learned Adapters (OMLA), is designed for the continual adaptation of pretrained robotic policies. It integrates parameter-efficient adapters with an online meta-learning objective to facilitate knowledge transfer.

**A. Vision-Language Robotic Policy Architecture:**
The foundation is a pretrained vision-language robotic policy. The policy takes three inputs:
- **Task Description:** Encoded into a task embedding using a pretrained text encoder (e.g., BERT).
- **Visual Observations:** RGB images from agent-view and on-gripper view, processed by a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone.
- **Proprioceptive States:** Joint and gripper states, mapped into the same embedding space as the task description via MLPs.

A sequence of C consecutive observations is used to infer the underlying state. The encoded features from ViT, along with the task embedding, are fed into a temporal transformer. An action prediction token ([ACT]) is prepended to this sequence. The output corresponding to the [ACT] token is then passed to a policy head. The policy head models multi-modal action distributions using a Mixture of Gaussian Distributions (implemented with a two-layer MLP) and predicts actions sampled from this distribution. The training objective is Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

**B. Adapting Pretrained Robotic Policy with Adapters:**
The method employs lightweight adapters to fine-tune the pretrained policy for new tasks. Specifically, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is utilized. LoRA modifies the original weight matrix W₀ by adding a low-rank decomposition (BA), where B and A are small matrices. This significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to fine-tuning the entire model. The key advantage of task-specific adapters in continual adaptation is their ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting, as the original pretrained model parameters remain frozen, and each task has its own adapter.

**C. Online Meta-Learned Adapters (OMLA):**
The core innovation is OMLA, which addresses the limitation of adapters being trained from scratch for each new task. OMLA introduces a two-stage learning paradigm:

1. **Online Meta-Learning Phase:**
 - Instead of assuming a fixed task distribution like traditional meta-learning, OMLA uses an *online meta-learning* approach, suitable for continual settings where tasks arrive sequentially.
 - The goal is to learn a prior for adapter parameters (ϕ) using knowledge from previously encountered tasks.
 - During this phase, for a current task, a previous task `k` is uniformly sampled from the set of previously learned tasks.
 - From task `k`'s dataset, a meta-train dataset `D_tr_k` and a meta-validation dataset `D_val_k` are constructed.
 - The adapter parameters ϕ are updated using a meta-gradient that involves an inner-loop adaptation to `D_tr_k` and an outer-loop evaluation on `D_val_k`. This gradient (Eq. 3) effectively learns an initialization that enables faster adaptation.
 - Crucially, the pretrained model parameters remain frozen during this entire process.

2. **Fine-tuning Phase:**
 - After the online meta-learning phase, the adapter parameters (ϕ) are no longer randomly initialized but have learned a task-agnostic prior.
 - These pre-initialized adapters are then fine-tuned using the specific dataset of the current new task, leveraging the standard MLE loss.

**D. Proposed Sampling Strategy for Online Meta-Learning:**
To overcome the computational burden of applying meta-learning objectives to entire trajectories (especially with high-dimensional observations), OMLA introduces an efficient sampling strategy for constructing meta-train and meta-validation data:
- Two episodes (i and j) are sampled from a selected previous task's dataset.
- A query data point (observation-action pair) is sampled from episode j (meta-validation episode).
- An "anchor" data point in episode i (meta-train episode) is identified by finding the observation with the highest visual feature similarity to the query data point using Euclidean distance in the encoded visual feature space (Eq. 4).
- A window of `b` observations around this anchor in episode i is sampled to form the meta-train data. This ensures relevance to the query without processing entire trajectories.

This strategy significantly reduces memory and computational overhead while still providing relevant data for effective online meta-learning. After a task is learned, its dataset is added to the pool of "previously learned tasks" `D` for subsequent online meta-learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in both simulated (LIBERO benchmark) and real-world environments, evaluating OMLA against baselines like LoRA and Experience Replay (ER). The key metrics were Forward Transfer (FWT, success rate on new tasks) and Backward Transfer (BWT, success rate change on previous tasks).

**1. Simulation Results (Q1: Better Adaptation Performance):**
- **Superior FWT:** OMLA consistently outperformed LoRA and ER across all three LIBERO task suites (LIBERO-OBJECT, LIBERO-SPATIAL, LIBERO-GOAL). For example, on LIBERO-OBJECT, OMLA achieved an average FWT of 0.86 compared to LoRA's 0.71 and ER's 0.59. Similar trends were observed in LIBERO-SPATIAL (OMLA: 0.69, LoRA: 0.57, ER: 0.37) and LIBERO-GOAL (OMLA: 0.58, LoRA: 0.52, ER: 0.43).
- **Effective Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** As OMLA and LoRA both use task-specific adapters while keeping the pretrained model frozen, their BWT values were 0, indicating no catastrophic forgetting. In contrast, ER exhibited negative BWT values (e.g., -0.43 for LIBERO-OBJECT), signifying degradation in performance on previous tasks.
- **Representation Analysis:** Visualizations using t-SNE showed that after the online meta-learning phase, observation representations became more structured and well-separated compared to their highly entangled state before meta-learning. This suggests that OMLA's meta-learning objective effectively organizes the feature space, contributing to better adaptation.

**2. Impact of Dataset Size (Q2: Scalability with Dataset Size):**
- OMLA consistently outperformed LoRA across different demonstration numbers (5, 10, and 20 demonstrations per task).
- The performance gap between OMLA and LoRA was maintained, with OMLA showing approximately a 20% higher average success rate. This indicates OMLA's ability to leverage prior knowledge is effective even with limited task-specific data.

**3. Impact of Policy Architecture (Q3: Robustness across Architectures):**
- OMLA was evaluated with an alternative policy architecture (A1) using a ResNet-18 backbone for visual encoding, instead of the primary ViT-based architecture (A0).
- While the overall performance of A1 was lower due to reduced expressivity, OMLA continued to outperform LoRA across all tested demonstration sizes and task suites with architecture A1. This demonstrates the robustness of OMLA to variations in the underlying policy architecture.

**4. Impact of Previously Learned Tasks (Q4: Knowledge Transfer Dynamics):**
- **Similarity of Tasks (S1):** When pretraining tasks were significantly dissimilar from adaptation tasks (e.g., pretraining on LIBERO-GOAL for LIBERO-OBJECT adaptation), OMLA's performance decreased by 14.6% compared to its original setting. This highlights that the similarity between previously learned tasks and current learning tasks is crucial for effective knowledge transfer.
- **Dataset Incorporation (S2):** When task datasets from the continual adaptation phase were excluded from the online meta-learning process (only using pretraining data), OMLA's performance decreased by 7.5%. This finding emphasizes the importance of continually incorporating newly learned task datasets into the meta-learning pool to enrich the prior.
- **Continued Superiority:** Even under these suboptimal conditions (S1 and S2), OMLA still outperformed the standard LoRA baseline, indicating that its meta-learning mechanism provides benefits even when the available prior knowledge is less ideal.

**5. Real Robot Experiments:**
- **Improved Real-World Robustness:** In real-world pick-up tasks with varying objects and distractors, policies adapted with OMLA generally performed tasks smoothly and exhibited robust behavior in unseen scenes.
- **Qualitative Difference:** LoRA-adapted policies were observed to fail in recognizing target objects and exhibit jittery motions in some unseen scenes.
- **Quantitative Success:** OMLA consistently achieved higher success rates than LoRA with both 10 and 20 demonstrations. For example, with 20 demonstrations, OMLA achieved an average success rate of 84.0%, while LoRA achieved 60.0%. This confirms OMLA's effectiveness and practical applicability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of continual robot learning by addressing a critical limitation in current adapter-based approaches: the lack of systematic knowledge transfer between tasks. By proposing Online Meta-Learned Adapters (OMLA), the authors introduce a method that enables lightweight, task-specific adapters to leverage prior experience, moving beyond a purely episodic view of adaptation.

The **significance** of this work can be summarized as follows:
- **Enhanced Adaptation Efficiency:** OMLA consistently demonstrates superior forward transfer compared to baseline methods like LoRA and Experience Replay, indicating faster and more effective learning of new tasks, even with limited demonstrations. This is particularly valuable in robotics where data collection can be expensive and time-consuming.
- **Robust Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** By retaining the core principle of task-specific adapters and freezing pretrained model parameters, OMLA inherently prevents catastrophic forgetting, a longstanding challenge in continual learning.
- **Principled Knowledge Transfer:** The integration of an online meta-learning objective provides a structured way to learn a prior over adapter parameters. This allows knowledge from past tasks to inform the initialization and learning process for new tasks, which is a key step towards true lifelong learning in robots. The observed improved structuring of feature representations after meta-learning supports this.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The proposed similarity-based sampling strategy for meta-learning effectively mitigates the high computational and memory costs typically associated with meta-learning on high-dimensional robotic data, making the approach practical for complex vision-language policies.
- **Generalizability and Robustness:** The method's consistent outperformance across diverse simulated task suites, varying demonstration sizes, and different policy architectures (ViT vs. ResNet-18) indicates its general applicability and robustness. Its validation in real-world scenarios further strengthens its practical relevance.

The **potential impact** of OMLA is significant for the development of general-purpose autonomous agents:
- **More Adaptable Robots:** Robots equipped with OMLA could adapt more seamlessly to new household layouts, object variations, or user preferences without needing extensive retraining or suffering from performance degradation on previously mastered skills. This moves closer to the vision of truly versatile and generalist robots.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By accelerating new task learning with fewer demonstrations, OMLA can reduce the data collection and training overhead, making robotic deployment more efficient and accessible.
- **Foundation for Lifelong Learning:** OMLA represents a step towards lifelong robot learning systems that can continuously acquire and refine skills over extended periods in dynamic environments, rather than being limited to a fixed set of pretrained behaviors.
- **Advancement in PEFT for Robotics:** The work extends the utility of parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, demonstrating their effectiveness not just for mitigating forgetting but also for actively facilitating knowledge transfer in a continual learning context.

Future directions, such as incorporating context understanding for anchor identification and leveraging advanced policy heads like diffusion policies, suggest avenues for further enhancing the capabilities of such adaptive robotic systems.
