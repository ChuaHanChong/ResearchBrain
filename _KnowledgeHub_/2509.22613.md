# Benefits and Pitfalls of Reinforcement Learning for Language Model Planning: A Theoretical Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Microsoft Research Asia, Peking University, Harvard University, and the University of Southern California present a theoretical framework examining reinforcement learning (RL) for Large Language Model (LLM) planning, comparing policy gradient (PG) and Q-learning against supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The study identifies SFT's memorization limitations, reveals a diversity collapse in PG which KL regularization can address, and demonstrates Q-learning's capacity to recover graph structures and preserve output diversity when utilizing appropriate process rewards.

## Problem

- Despite empirical successes of RL in enhancing LLM planning, a comprehensive theoretical framework explaining its advantages over SFT and its limitations was largely absent.
- Understanding the specific learning dynamics of different RL algorithms like Policy Gradient (PG) and Q-learning in the context of LLM planning was needed to explain observed behaviors such as memorization, generalization, and diversity collapse.
- Existing research empirically noted SFT's tendency to memorize training data and its struggles with inferring transitive relationships, highlighting the need for a structural characterization of its stable points.

## Method

- LLM planning was abstracted as a tractable ==path-finding== problem on an unknown directed graph, using a one-layer, single-head Transformer as the model backbone and a key assumption that logits are a function of the (target, current) node pair.
- Theoretical analyses were conducted to characterize SFT's stable points and to investigate the convergence properties and output diversity of PG (with and without KL regularization) and Q-learning (with outcome vs. process rewards).
- Empirical validations were performed on synthetic Erd{u}s-R{e}nyi graphs and the Blocksworld domain, comparing training and test accuracy, as well as output diversity, across different learning paradigms.

## Results

- SFT empirically demonstrated struggles in capturing all adjacency relationships and generalizing to unseen pairs, consistent with its theoretical characterization as a memorization-based approach.
- PG without KL regularization achieved **100%** training accuracy but showed a continuous decline in output diversity and test accuracy, whereas KL regularization maintained higher diversity and test accuracy.
- Q-learning with outcome rewards resulted in near-zero accuracy due to Q-value bias, but with process rewards, it achieved high training and test accuracy, successfully recovering graph structures and maintaining output diversity, even with off-policy data collection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) primarily memorizes co-occurrence patterns in training data, failing to infer transitive relationships crucial for robust planning and resulting in limited generalization.
> - Policy Gradient (PG) benefits from on-policy data generation for exploration but inherently suffers from 'diversity collapse,' converging to a single correct path unless KL regularization is applied, which then trades off some accuracy gains for diversity.
> - Q-learning, particularly with well-designed process rewards, effectively recovers underlying graph structures and preserves output diversity by converging to multiple feasible paths, and naturally supports efficient off-policy learning, unlike outcome rewards which lead to Q-value bias and model collapse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Planning constitutes a fundamental aspect of intelligent behavior, involving the decomposition of complex goals into actionable steps and the anticipation of challenges. Large Language Models (LLMs) have increasingly adopted planning mechanisms to improve performance on structured and long-horizon tasks. Initially, LLMs, relying on next-token prediction, exhibited limited planning capabilities. However, the integration of reinforcement learning (RL) objectives, exemplified by models such as o1, has demonstrably enhanced LLMs' ability to perform multi-step reasoning, including task decomposition for tool use, spatial navigation, and robotics. This empirical success of RL-based methods often surpasses that of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) counterparts.

Despite these advancements, a comprehensive theoretical framework explaining RL's advantages and limitations in the context of LLM planning has remained largely unestablished. Existing research has empirically noted that SFT tends to memorize training data, while RL appears to promote generalization. Some studies have highlighted the suboptimal nature of verification-free approaches like SFT and identified entropy mechanisms influencing RL training.

This paper addresses this theoretical gap by providing an analytical treatment of RL learning dynamics for language model planning. It adopts a tractable graph-based abstraction, modeling planning as a path-finding problem. This abstraction allows for a granular analysis of how different learning algorithms—SFT, policy gradient (PG), and Q-learning—interact with underlying graph structures.

The work differentiates itself from other paradigms of analyzing LLMs for graph problems. While mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer model weights (e.g., showing transformers implement spectral algorithms for shortest paths) and expressiveness analysis constructs ideal weight configurations for algorithm simulation, this paper focuses on the *gradient dynamics* of learning. By investigating how SFT, PG, and Q-learning update model parameters in response to data and rewards, the authors aim to explain observed behaviors such as memorization, generalization, and diversity collapse. The study connects with existing findings regarding SFT's inability to learn transitive relationships and RL's exploration-driven improvements, while also introducing novel insights into the specific behaviors of PG and Q-learning, particularly regarding diversity and off-policy learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a theoretical basis for understanding the benefits and limitations of reinforcement learning methods when applied to language model planning. This involves a detailed investigation into the learning dynamics of prominent RL algorithms, specifically policy gradient and Q-learning, in comparison to supervised fine-tuning.

The motivations driving this study are multi-faceted:

1. **Elucidating RL's Effectiveness**: Despite the demonstrable empirical success of RL in augmenting LLM planning capabilities across various domains (e.g., tool use, gaming, robotics), the theoretical underpinnings of its superiority over SFT have remained largely unarticulated. The paper aims to provide a principled explanation for this observed performance gap.
2. **Characterizing SFT's Limitations**: Previous work has indicated that transformer-based LLMs trained with SFT struggle with inferring complete reachability structures and transitivity in planning tasks. A key motivation is to provide a structural characterization of SFT's stable points, specifically demonstrating how SFT tends to memorize co-occurrence relationships within its training data, thereby leading to spurious solutions and limited generalization. This characterization serves as a foundational benchmark for comparison with RL approaches.
3. **Analyzing Policy Gradient (PG) Dynamics**: PG is a widely adopted algorithm for training LLMs. The research seeks to dissect its learning dynamics to understand how it achieves planning capabilities. This includes identifying its mechanisms for exploration-driven data augmentation that contribute to generalization, as well as uncovering potential drawbacks such as output diversity collapse during training. The role of KL regularization in mitigating such issues is also a motivational aspect.
4. **Investigating Q-Learning for LLM Planning**: Q-learning is a prominent RL paradigm in game playing but has been less frequently applied to LLMs. The paper is motivated to analyze Q-learning's behavior in the planning context, specifically focusing on its sensitivity to reward design, its capacity for preserving output diversity, and its potential for off-policy learning, which offers practical advantages in terms of data efficiency.
5. **Providing a Tractable Theoretical Framework**: To enable rigorous analysis of complex gradient dynamics, the research is motivated to adopt a simplified yet representative graph-based abstraction of planning. This abstraction allows for mathematical analysis of model convergence, stable points, and learning behaviors, which can then be empirically validated in both synthetic and real-world planning benchmarks like Blocksworld.
6. **Guiding Future Research and Algorithm Design**: By theoretically clarifying the advantages and pitfalls of different learning approaches, the study aims to offer principled insights that can inform the design of more robust, scalable, and generalizable RL methods for language model planning, particularly by highlighting the potential of Q-learning.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond empirical observations to provide a theoretical understanding of *why* RL works, *how* different RL algorithms behave, and *what* their fundamental limitations are in the context of LLM planning, thereby contributing to the development of more effective and reliable planning agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combination of theoretical analysis and empirical validation to investigate the learning dynamics of SFT, Policy Gradient (PG), and Q-learning for language model planning.

#### 3.1. Planning Abstraction

The core methodology relies on abstracting LLM planning as a path-finding problem on an unknown directed graph G = (V, E).
- **Nodes (V)**: Represented by unique tokens, forming the LLM's vocabulary.
- **Edges (E)**: Represent directed connections between nodes, signifying valid transitions.
- **Planning Task**: Identifying a valid path from a source node (s) to a target node (t) in G.
- **Running Example**: The Blocksworld domain is used to connect this abstraction to a real-world LLM planning scenario. Block configurations map to nodes, and valid moves map to edges.

#### 3.2. Data Generation and Datasets

The set of all reachable source-target pairs (s, t) is partitioned into a training set (`D_Train`) and a test set (`D_Test`).
- **SFT Training Data (`D_SFT`)**: Generated by sampling `K` random paths for each reachable pair in `D_Train`. Each sample is a sequence "s t s a b c t \n", where `s a b c t` forms a valid path. The model trained with SFT is termed the "base model."
- **RL Training Data (`D_RL`)**: Sampled (s, t) pairs from `D_Train`. The model (on-policy) or the base model (off-policy) generates the remaining tokens. Rewards are then assigned based on sequence validity or intermediate steps.
- **Test Data (`D_Test`)**: Consists of (s, t) pairs never encountered during SFT or RL training, used to evaluate generalization.
- **Graph Details**: For main empirical validation, an Erd˝os-R´enyi graph with `|V|=100` nodes and `p=0.15` edge probability is used. The Blocksworld graph with 73 nodes (four blocks) is also utilized.

#### 3.3. Model Architecture

A one-layer, single-head Transformer is used as the backbone model. The embedding size `d` is set to 120. `Assumption 3.1` is introduced for theoretical tractability, stating that the model's predicted logits for the next token are a function of the (target, current) node pair. This assumption is empirically validated by analyzing attention maps, which show strong focus on target and current nodes across SFT, PG, and Q-learning. A two-layer, one-head Transformer is also tested to confirm consistency of this attention pattern.

#### 3.4. Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

- **Policy Gradient (PG)**:
 - **Reward**: An outcome reward `R(u) = r δ_u∈P_ + p` is used, where `r > 0` and `p` are constants, and `δ` is an indicator for a valid path.
 - **Loss Function**: Includes a policy gradient term `(-R(u) log ˆu_m_[u_m+1_])` and an optional KL divergence regularization term `(λ log ˆu_m_[u_m+1_] log (ˆu_m_[u_m+1_] / ˆu_base_m_[u_m+1_]))`. The KL term is detached to prevent gradient flow to the base model's probability.
 - **Theoretical Analysis**: Examines the connection between PG and SFT, the convergence properties of PG without KL regularization (`Theorem 4.2`), and the evolution of output diversity (`Theorem 4.3`). It also analyzes the effect of KL regularization on stable points (`Theorem 4.4`).

- **Q-Learning**:
 - **Objective**: Approximating the Q-function `Q_θ_(s_m_, a_m_)` using model logits.
 - **Loss Function**: A squared difference `(˜u_m_[u_m+1_] - R(u, m) - max_k ˜u_m+1_[k])^2` based on the Bellman equation.
 - **Reward Designs**: Two scenarios are studied:
 - **Outcome Reward**: `R(u, m) = δ_u∈P_ δ_um+1=utarget`. Reward is given only if the entire path is valid and the target is reached.
 - **Process Reward**: `R(u, m) = δ_um+1=utarget - δ_(um,um+1)∉E`. Intermediate rewards are given for reaching the target and penalties for non-adjacent transitions.
 - **Theoretical Analysis**: Analyzes stable points under `Assumption 3.1` for both outcome and process rewards (`Theorem 5.1` and `Theorem 5.2`). It introduces `Assumption 5.1` (Persistent exploration) to ensure convergence. The analysis for process reward is further extended to a simplified one-layer, single-head linear Transformer without `Assumption 3.1` (`Assumption 5.2`, `Theorem 5.3`).

#### 3.5. Empirical Validation

Experiments are conducted on both Erd˝os-R´enyi and Blocksworld graphs.
- **Metrics**: Training accuracy, test accuracy (under greedy decoding), and output diversity (average number of distinct correct paths generated over multiple sampling trials for a given source-target pair).
- **Visualizations**: Heatmaps of learned adjacency structures (Figure 1), normalized logits (Figure 4), and attention weights (Figure 5, 6) are used to illustrate theoretical findings.
- **Comparison**: PG with varying KL regularization strengths is compared against continual SFT. Q-learning with different reward designs (outcome vs. process) and sampling policies (on-policy vs. off-policy) is compared against PG.

This comprehensive approach combines theoretical rigor with empirical verification to systematically analyze and explain the behaviors of different learning paradigms for LLM planning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several key findings derived from both theoretical analysis and empirical validation, addressing the benefits and limitations of SFT, Policy Gradient (PG), and Q-learning in the context of language model planning.

#### 4.1. Limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

- **Theoretical Finding (Theorem 3.1)**: SFT primarily memorizes co-occurrence relationships present in the training dataset (`D_SFT`). The optimal solution for SFT ensures that the model's output probabilities match the empirical frequencies of next-token occurrences given the target and current nodes. This mechanism means SFT fails to exploit transitivity information, which is crucial for true graph connectivity required in path planning.
- **Empirical Observation**: Experiments on the Blocksworld dataset (Figure 1b) show that SFT struggles to capture all adjacency relationships, especially those with low frequency in the training data, despite their presence. This supports the notion that SFT relies on memorization rather than inferring underlying structural rules.

#### 4.2. Path Planning Capacities of Policy Gradient (PG)

- **Connection to SFT and Exploration (Theorem 4.1, Takeaway 2)**: When PG uses a 0-1 outcome reward and no KL regularization, its loss function is equivalent to performing SFT exclusively on the *correct paths generated during the current RL training step*. This implies that PG's advantage over static SFT stems from its iterative, on-policy data generation process, which enables exploration and discovery of new correct paths, effectively expanding the training dataset.
- **Convergence and Diversity Collapse (Theorem 4.2, 4.3, Takeaway 3)**:
 - PG without KL regularization converges such that the probability of outputting wrong paths in `D_Train` goes to zero.
 - However, `Theorem 4.3` reveals a "diversity collapse" phenomenon: the model's output diversity continuously declines throughout training, even after achieving 100% training accuracy. This means the model eventually produces only one specific correct path for each source-target pair.
- **Empirical Confirmation of Diversity Collapse**: Figure 2b and 2c demonstrate that PG without KL regularization achieves and maintains 100% training accuracy while its output diversity steadily decreases. This decline in diversity is associated with a degradation in test accuracy (Figure 2a).
- **Role of KL Regularization (Theorem 4.4, Takeaway 4)**:
 - KL regularization explicitly acts as a diversity-preserving mechanism by constraining the trained model to remain close to the base model's probability distribution. This can mitigate overfitting and improve generalization by preventing the model from collapsing to a single optimal path.
 - However, this comes at a cost: stronger KL regularization can limit potential accuracy gains, particularly when the base model's prior is suboptimal.
- **Empirical Confirmation of KL Regularization's Role**: Figure 2a, 2b, 2c, and 2d illustrate this trade-off. PG with KL regularization maintains higher output diversity and exhibits better generalization (higher test accuracy) compared to PG without KL regularization. However, it achieves slightly lower training accuracy, indicating that the regularization prevents it from fully optimizing on the training data.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Q-Learning-Based Planning Mechanism

- **Q-Value Bias with Outcome Rewards (Theorem 5.1, Takeaway 5)**:
 - When Q-learning is trained solely with an outcome reward, it suffers from Q-value bias. At a stable point, for any fixed target node `i` and any next node `k` that is not the target node `i`, all logits `f(i, j)[k]` collapse to the same constant value, independent of the current node `j` or the specific next node `k`. This causes the model to lose structural information.
- **Empirical Confirmation of Outcome Reward Failure**: Figure 3a shows that Q-learning with outcome rewards collapses to near-zero accuracy on both training and test sets, validating the theoretical prediction of Q-value bias.
- **Benefits of Process Rewards (Theorem 5.2, 5.3, Takeaway 5, 6)**:
 - Introducing *process rewards* (which account for both adjacency and target conditions at each step) mitigates the Q-value bias.
 - `Theorem 5.2` demonstrates that with persistent exploration, process rewards enable the Q-learning model to converge to well-defined limits that correctly capture the underlying graph structure (adjacency and reachability). Specifically, logits for correct adjacent and reachable nodes converge to higher values (e.g., 1), while incorrect ones converge to lower values (e.g., 0 or -1).
 - `Theorem 5.3` extends this to a linear Transformer model (without `Assumption 3.1`), showing that the model learns to encode adjacency information in feed-forward weights and reachability information in attention value weights.
 - Q-learning with process rewards offers two key advantages over PG: it converges to a solution that preserves output diversity (as all feasible next nodes converge to a similar high logit value, allowing multiple correct paths), and it naturally supports off-policy learning.
- **Empirical Confirmation of Process Reward Success**: Figure 3a demonstrates that Q-learning with process rewards achieves high training and test accuracy, significantly outperforming Q-learning with outcome rewards and comparable to or better than PG. Figure 4 visually confirms that logits for valid next nodes consistently increase and converge to similar high values, reflecting structural recovery and diversity preservation.
- **Off-Policy Learning (Takeaway 6)**: Figure 3a illustrates that off-policy Q-learning with process rewards achieves performance comparable to its on-policy counterpart, confirming its ability to leverage data collected from different policies.
- **Diversity Preservation**: Figure 3b further highlights that Q-learning with process rewards maintains higher output diversity compared to PG models across various accuracy levels.

#### 4.4. Validation of Attention Mechanisms

- **Empirical Validation (Figure 5, 6)**: Attention maps across SFT, PG, and Q-learning consistently show that the Transformer model primarily focuses its attention on the target node (and, implicitly, the current node via residual connections). This empirically validates `Assumption 3.1`, which forms the basis for much of the theoretical analysis.

In summary, the findings indicate that while RL offers significant advantages over SFT through exploration, the choice of RL algorithm and reward design is critical. PG suffers from diversity collapse, which KL regularization can partially address at a cost. Q-learning, particularly with well-designed process rewards, emerges as a promising approach due to its ability to recover correct graph structures, preserve diversity, and support off-policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the understanding of Large Language Models and reinforcement learning, with implications for both theoretical foundations and practical applications.

1. **Theoretical Foundation for RL in LLM Planning**: The work provides a rigorous theoretical framework to explain why reinforcement learning methods enhance LLM planning capabilities, moving beyond empirical observations. By abstracting planning as a graph path-finding problem, the authors offer a tractable way to analyze complex learning dynamics, which is crucial for developing robust and reliable LLM agents.
2. **Clarification of SFT Limitations**: The study theoretically characterizes SFT's tendency to memorize co-occurrence relationships and its inherent inability to infer transitive properties of underlying planning graphs. This explains why SFT-trained LLMs often struggle with generalization in planning tasks and provides a clear baseline against which RL's advantages can be measured.
3. **Insights into Policy Gradient Behavior**: The identification and theoretical explanation of "diversity collapse" in policy gradient methods is a notable contribution. This phenomenon, where the model's output diversity diminishes even after achieving high accuracy, has practical implications for generalization and robustness. The analysis of KL regularization's role in mitigating this, while also highlighting its trade-offs with accuracy, offers valuable guidance for hyperparameter tuning and algorithm design in PG-based LLM fine-tuning (e.g., PPO).
4. **Highlighting Q-Learning's Advantages**: The research positions Q-learning as a potentially superior paradigm for LLM planning compared to policy gradient methods. Its theoretical advantages in preserving output diversity at convergence and its natural support for off-policy learning represent significant findings. Off-policy learning is particularly impactful for practical LLM training, as it allows for more efficient use of data collected from various (potentially older or exploratory) policies, which can reduce computational costs and accelerate learning.
5. **Importance of Reward Design**: The stark contrast between Q-learning with outcome rewards (leading to collapse) and process rewards (leading to structural recovery and robust planning) underscores the critical importance of careful reward engineering in RL for LLMs. This finding provides direct, actionable guidance for practitioners developing reward functions for complex LLM planning tasks.
6. **Bridging Theory and Practice**: The empirical validation across both synthetic Erd˝os-R´enyi graphs and the Blocksworld benchmark consistently supports the theoretical predictions. This strong alignment between theory and practice enhances the credibility and applicability of the findings, suggesting that the graph-based abstraction captures fundamental aspects of LLM planning behavior.
7. **Future Research Directions**: The work opens promising avenues for future research, particularly in exploring the full potential of Q-learning for LLM planning. It encourages further investigation into reward design strategies that can leverage Q-learning's strengths, as well as the development of novel RL algorithms that specifically address diversity preservation and sample efficiency.

In conclusion, this paper provides a principled foundation for understanding the mechanisms underlying reinforcement learning in LLM planning. Its detailed theoretical analyses, supported by empirical evidence, not only explain previously observed phenomena but also offer clear guidance for the development of more effective, generalizable, and robust AI planning agents.
