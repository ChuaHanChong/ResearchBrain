# Model-Based Inverse Reinforcement Learning from Visual Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> A model-based inverse reinforcement learning framework that learns cost functions from visual human demonstrations alone. Self-supervised 2D keypoints form a low-dimensional latent state, a learned dynamics model predicts keypoint and joint transitions, and cost parameters are optimized by differentiating through the inner action-optimization loop. Learned costs then drive visual model-predictive control, reproducing reaching and bottle-placing behavior on a Kuka iiwa.

## Problem

- Model-based IRL typically assumes a known transition model of robot and environment, which real manipulation platforms with unknown dynamics do not provide.
- Prior IRL methods require demonstrations pairing actions with states, which are costly to collect, and mostly ignore high-dimensional visual feature spaces.
- Feature-matching outer loops optimize cost parameters via hand-designed distance metrics disconnected from policy optimization, causing unstable, oscillating cost learning.

## Method

- ==Self-supervised keypoint detector== (autoencoder with structural bottleneck, mini **ResNet-18**) yields K keypoints of pixel position plus intensity as the visual latent state.
- Learned ==latent dynamics model== combines an MLP keypoint predictor with joint-state integration of desired joint-angle displacements, enabling differentiable gradient-based ==visual MPC== rollouts.
- ==Bi-level gradient IRL== differentiates the squared-keypoint IRL loss through the inner action-descent step via the higher library, over weighted, time-dependent, and ==RBF-kernel== cost parametrizations.

## Results

- On simulated reaching and placing, the apprenticeship-learning baseline oscillates between good and bad solutions across outer-loop iterations while the gradient-based method converges stably.
- On **15** hardware reaching demonstrations, all three learned costs cut final relative distance to goal keypoints below both baselines, with **one** demonstration matching **ten**.
- For bottle placing from a single human video, RBF and time-dependent costs reach mean squared keypoint distances of **4.26** and **6.12** versus **26.96** for the default cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Coupling the outer cost update to the inner policy optimization through differentiation stabilizes IRL where decoupled feature-matching schemes oscillate and need hand-tuned constraints.
> - Compact self-supervised keypoints make visual demonstrations usable as cost signals, and learned weights become interpretable, downweighting static or low-variance keypoint dimensions.
> - Time-dependent cost parametrization generalizes better on multi-phase tasks; the main bottleneck remains learning an accurate, viewpoint-robust visual dynamics model.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of learning from demonstrations (LfD) for robotics, specifically focusing on Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL). LfD aims to enable robots to acquire skills by observing human examples, with IRL being a prominent approach where the robot infers the underlying reward or cost function that explains the demonstrator's behavior.

Historically, many IRL approaches have required demonstrations that couple both action and state measurements, which can be costly and impractical to acquire in real-world robotic scenarios. Furthermore, much of the foundational work in model-based IRL has assumed a known transition model of the environment and robot dynamics, primarily operating in simulation or simplified settings. In practice, robots typically operate in environments with unknown dynamics, necessitating the learning of these models.

While model-free IRL counterparts have seen success in real-world robotic manipulation tasks, often relying on proprioceptive state measurements, scaling model-based IRL to real-world tasks with unknown dynamics, especially from high-dimensional visual input, remains a significant challenge. Key difficulties include:
1. **Learning robust dynamics models:** Accurately predicting how actions affect the environment, particularly from visual observations.
2. **Scalability to high-dimensional state spaces:** Processing and utilizing rich visual information effectively.
3. **Learning from diverse demonstration types:** Moving beyond proprioceptive data to incorporate more accessible visual-only demonstrations.

Prior model-based IRL methods, such as apprenticeship learning, often approximate the optimization of cost functions by minimizing a hand-designed distance metric between demonstrations and policy rollouts. This approximation can lead to instabilities in cost function learning, as the inner loop (policy optimization) and outer loop (cost function optimization) are typically treated as separate steps without explicit gradient-based communication.

This paper addresses these challenges by integrating recent advancements in unsupervised keypoint representations, visual dynamics modeling, and gradient-based bi-level optimization. It aims to bridge the gap by developing a model-based IRL framework capable of learning cost functions directly from visual human demonstrations, leading to more stable and effective optimization for robotic manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a gradient-based inverse reinforcement learning framework that can infer cost functions for robotic manipulation tasks solely from visual human demonstrations, even when the robot's dynamics are initially unknown. This framework then uses the learned cost functions to reproduce the demonstrated behavior through visual model predictive control.

The motivation behind this work stems from several challenges in current IRL methodologies:
1. **Enabling model-based IRL for real-world robotics:** Despite promises of sample efficiency and better generalization, model-based IRL has lagged behind model-free methods in real-world applications, largely due to the difficulty of learning accurate dynamics models for complex, high-dimensional visual inputs and the computational demands of the nested optimization problems.
2. **Overcoming the need for action-state coupled demonstrations:** Most IRL methods require demonstrations that include both state observations and corresponding expert actions, which are difficult and expensive to collect. The goal is to move towards learning from more easily obtainable visual-only demonstrations.
3. **Addressing instability in cost function learning:** Traditional model-based IRL approaches often decouple the inner loop (policy optimization given a cost function) and the outer loop (cost function optimization based on policy performance). This separation can lead to optimization instabilities and reliance on hand-designed distance metrics. The authors motivate a more principled approach using gradient-based bi-level optimization to establish an explicit connection between these two loops.
4. **Leveraging visual representations for control:** Recognizing the utility of low-dimensional visual features, the research aims to incorporate unsupervised keypoint detectors to process high-dimensional visual data into a compact, learnable state representation suitable for dynamics modeling and control.
5. **Validation on hardware:** The paper aims to evaluate the practical applicability and effectiveness of the proposed framework on actual robotic hardware for basic object manipulation tasks, moving beyond purely simulated environments.

In summary, the core motivation is to enhance the practicality and performance of model-based IRL by enabling learning from visual demonstrations, improving optimization stability through a gradient-based bi-level approach, and validating the system on physical robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates several components: a keypoint detector for visual feature extraction, a visual dynamics model, a gradient-based visual model predictive control (MPC) system, and a novel gradient-based inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) algorithm.

#### 3.1. Keypoints as Visual Latent State and Dynamics Model

- **Keypoint Detector ($g_{key}$):** An autoencoder with a structural bottleneck is utilized to detect 2D keypoints. These keypoints correspond to pixel positions or areas with high variability in RGB image inputs. The architecture is a mini-version of ResNet-18. It is trained in a self-supervised manner on visual data collected by moving the robot arm while gripping an object, emphasizing features on the object rather than the arm. The detector outputs a Kx3 vector for K keypoints, with (x,y) pixel coordinates and an intensity (confidence) score.
- **Dynamics Model ($f_{dyn}$):** This model predicts the next state ($\hat{s}_{t+1}$) given the current state ($s_t$) and action ($u_t$). The state $s_t$ combines the low-dimensional visual keypoints ($z_t$) from the keypoint detector and the robot's joint state ($\theta_t$, $\dot{\theta}_t$). Actions $u_t$ are desired joint angle displacements. The dynamics model has two components: a neural network (two hidden layers with ReLU activations) for keypoint prediction, and a direct integration component for joint state prediction. The model is trained on data collected through self-supervised sine motions of the robot joints (for simpler tasks) or a combination of self-supervised, expert data, and data augmentation (for complex tasks) by minimizing a normalized mean squared error (NMSE).

#### 3.2. Gradient-Based Visual Model Predictive Control (MPC)

- The MPC component aims to optimize a sequence of actions ($u = (u_0, \ldots, u_{T-1})$) to minimize a task-specific cost function $C_\psi(\hat{\tau}, z_{goal})$, where $\hat{\tau}$ is the predicted trajectory and $z_{goal}$ are goal keypoints.
- It leverages the learned visual dynamics model $f_{dyn}$ to simulate forward trajectories given an action sequence.
- Action optimization is performed via gradient descent: $u_{new} = u - \eta\nabla_u C_\psi(\hat{\tau}, z_{goal})$. This requires the entire process, from dynamics model forward pass to cost computation, to be differentiable.

#### 3.3. Gradient-Based Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)

- The core contribution is a novel IRL algorithm that connects the inner loop (action optimization via MPC) and the outer loop (cost function parameter $\psi$ optimization) using gradient-based bi-level optimization.
- The objective is to learn a cost function $C_\psi$ such that the actions optimized by MPC using $C_\psi$ result in a predicted trajectory ($\hat{\tau}$) that minimizes an IRL loss $L_{IRL}(\tau_{demo}, \hat{\tau})$ with respect to expert demonstrations ($\tau_{demo}$).
- **Bi-level Optimization:** The algorithm computes the gradient of $L_{IRL}$ with respect to $\psi$ by differentiating through the entire inner loop action optimization process. This explicitly accounts for how changes in cost parameters $\psi$ affect the optimized actions $u_{opt}$ and, consequently, the predicted trajectory $\hat{\tau}$. The `higher` library for generalized inner-loop meta-learning is used for this differentiation.
- **IRL Loss ($L_{IRL}$):** This loss measures the discrepancy between the predicted latent trajectory and the demonstrated latent trajectory. It is defined as the squared distance between predicted and demonstrated keypoints at each time step: $L_{IRL}(\tau_{demo}, \hat{\tau}) = \sum_t (\mathbf{z}_{t,demo} - \hat{\mathbf{z}}_t)^2$.
- **Cost Function Parametrizations ($C_\psi$):** Three parametrizations are explored:
 1. **Weighted Cost:** Assigns constant weights ($\psi_{x,k}$, $\psi_{y,k}$) to the squared distance of each keypoint's (x,y) coordinate from its goal across the entire trajectory.
 2. **Time Dependent Weighted Cost:** Extends the Weighted Cost by assigning different weights ($\psi_{x,t,k}$, $\psi_{y,t,k}$) for each keypoint at each time step, allowing for temporal importance.
 3. **RBF Weighted Cost:** Employs a set of J time-dependent RBF kernels to weight the squared distances, offering flexibility while limiting parameter growth compared to the Time Dependent Weighted Cost (J < T).

#### 3.4. Illustrative Comparison with Feature-Matching IRL (Simulation)

- A preliminary comparison is conducted in a simulated Kuka robot environment against a feature-matching IRL algorithm based on apprenticeship learning. The simulation assumes ground truth 3D keypoints and a differentiable dynamics model. This comparison aims to demonstrate the improved convergence behavior and stability of the proposed gradient-based approach due to the explicit connection between inner and outer loops.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both a simulation-based comparison and hardware experiments to evaluate the proposed gradient-based IRL framework.

#### 4.1. Illustrative Comparison with Feature-Matching IRL (Simulation)

- **Convergence Behavior:** In simulated reaching and placing tasks, the proposed gradient-based IRL algorithm demonstrated more stable convergence during cost training compared to a feature-matching IRL baseline. The baseline exhibited oscillations between good and suboptimal solutions, whereas the new algorithm converged to a stable solution.
- **Reason for Stability:** This improved stability is attributed to the explicit gradient-based connection between the inner (policy optimization) and outer (cost function learning) loops. This connection allows the cost function parameters to be updated with direct awareness of their impact on policy performance, unlike feature-matching methods that often decouple these steps.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments (Quantitative Analysis on Automatically Generated Visual Demonstrations)

- **Task:** Moving an object along the X-axis using a Kuka arm, with visual demonstrations generated automatically.
- **Keypoint Interpretation:** The keypoint detector successfully identified the object. Notably, one keypoint was fixed in the background, serving as a reference.
- **Cost Function Performance:** All three learned cost functions (Weighted, Time Dependent Weighted, RBF Weighted) performed considerably better than two baselines: the apprenticeship learning algorithm and a naive "Default" cost (which simply measures squared distance to goal). This was measured by the final relative distance to the goal keypoint positions on 5 test demonstrations.
- **Demonstration Quantity:** The learned costs performed similarly whether trained on 1 or 10 demonstrations, suggesting potential for sample efficiency.
- **Learned Parameters Interpretation:** Analysis of the learned cost parameters (Figure 5) showed that Y-axis dimensions and the background-fixed keypoint received relatively smaller weights. This indicates that the IRL algorithm successfully identified the relevant degrees of freedom and features from the demonstrations (predominantly X-axis movement).
- **Baseline Instability:** The apprenticeship learning baseline experienced significant instability during optimization, requiring careful tuning of learning rates and regularization in previous work. The proposed method demonstrated robust performance without such additional tuning, reinforcing the benefits of the gradient-based bi-level optimization.

#### 4.3. Learning Cost Functions from Visual Human Demonstrations

- **Task:** Placing a bottle on a shelf, demonstrated by a human user via video data, involving both X-axis movement and subsequent Y-axis (downward) movement.
- **Demonstration Data:** A single human demonstration (downsampled to 5 Hz) was used, and tests were conducted from two different robot starting configurations (close to initial demo position and closer to target).
- **Training Convergence:** The IRL loss converged approximately around 2,000 gradient steps (Figure 6a).
- **Learned Parameters (Time-Dependency):** The time-dependent cost functions (Time Dependent Weighted and RBF Weighted) learned to emphasize X-direction distance to the goal during the first half of the motion and Y-direction distance in the latter half (Figure 6b, c, d). This correctly reflected the two-stage nature of the human demonstration (moving horizontally, then vertically down).
- **Robot Performance:**
 - **Mean Squared Distance to Goal:** Table 1 shows that both Time Dependent and RBF Weighted costs significantly outperformed the "Default" baseline in minimizing the mean squared distance to the goal keypoint on the Kuka robot.
 - **Generalization:** The simple Weighted Cost performed well in one starting configuration but showed reduced performance in another, suggesting that the time-dependency component of the Time Dependent and RBF Weighted costs contributes to better generalization across different initial states for multi-stage tasks.
 - **Qualitative Results:** Visualizations (Figure 7 and Appendix A.6, Figure 9) illustrate the robot's ability to reproduce the demonstrated placing behavior using the learned cost functions, with detected keypoints closely matching the goal keypoints at the final state.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a model-based Inverse Reinforcement Learning framework that makes several contributions to the field of robotic learning and control, demonstrating its significance and potential impact:

1. **Enabling Model-Based IRL from Visual-Only Demonstrations:** The framework successfully addresses the challenge of learning cost functions for robotic manipulation solely from high-dimensional visual human demonstrations. This capability reduces the burden of collecting costly action-state coupled data, making IRL more accessible and applicable in real-world settings.
2. **Improved Optimization Stability and Efficiency:** By employing a gradient-based bi-level optimization approach, the framework establishes an explicit differentiable connection between the inner (action optimization) and outer (cost function learning) loops. This design leads to more stable and effective cost function learning, as evidenced by the simulation results, and reduces the need for heuristic tuning or additional constraints often required by traditional feature-matching IRL methods.
3. **Leveraging Low-Dimensional Visual Representations:** The use of unsupervised keypoint detectors to extract compact, task-relevant visual features from raw images is a practical approach to handle high-dimensional visual inputs. This low-dimensional representation simplifies the learning of visual dynamics models and enables efficient planning.
4. **Hardware Validation for Robotic Manipulation:** The successful evaluation on a physical Kuka iiwa robot for both simple reaching and complex placing tasks demonstrates the practical viability of the proposed system. This move from simulation to hardware is crucial for validating the robustness and effectiveness of novel control and learning paradigms.
5. **Interpretability of Learned Cost Functions:** The analysis of learned cost parameters, particularly the time-dependent weights, provides insights into which visual features and temporal segments of a task are considered important by the inferred cost function. This interpretability can aid in understanding and refining robotic behaviors.
6. **Potential for Broader Application:** The framework's ability to learn from visual demonstrations and reproduce complex, multi-stage tasks suggests its potential applicability to a wider range of robotic manipulation problems where human demonstrations are readily available but detailed state-action data is not.

While the work acknowledges remaining challenges, such as robust dynamics model learning under varying viewpoints and generalizing demonstrations across contexts, it represents a step towards making model-based IRL a more practical tool for real-world robotics. The explicit connection between policy optimization and cost function learning through gradients offers a promising avenue for future research in robust and scalable imitation learning.
