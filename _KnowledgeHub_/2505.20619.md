# Gait-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning with Multi-Phase Curriculum for Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> University College London researchers present a unified, reference-free reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid robots to perform diverse locomotion behaviors, including standing, walking, running, and smooth transitions, using a single recurrent policy. This approach successfully transferred to a Unitree G1 robot without real-world fine-tuning, demonstrating stable and natural movements.

## Problem

- Reference-based locomotion methods are constrained by the availability and quality of motion capture data, often leading to morphological mismatches and limited interpretability.
- Integrating multiple distinct gaits (e.g., standing, walking, running) into a single controller is complex due to reward interference and architectural overhead, typically requiring separate expert policies or intricate switching mechanisms.
- Reference-free reinforcement learning often struggles to naturally enforce human-like biomechanical traits crucial for balance and energy efficiency without meticulous reward tuning.

## Method

- A unified, recurrent actor-critic policy based on LSTMs is developed to control standing, walking, running, and transitions, conditioned by a one-hot gait ID.
- A gait-conditioned reward routing mechanism dynamically activates only relevant reward terms for the current locomotion mode, mitigating interference between conflicting objectives.
- A multi-phase curriculum progressively introduces locomotion complexity, starting with walking, then adding standing/transitions, and finally running/transitions, to facilitate stable skill acquisition.

## Results

- The learned policy accurately tracks commanded velocities up to 3 m/s and performs stable, smooth transitions between standing, walking, running, and their intermediate states in simulation.
- Analysis confirms that the policy maintains low total angular momentum in the Z-axis (yaw) through coordinated anti-phase motions of the arms and legs, reflecting human-like balance mechanisms.
- The policy, trained solely in simulation, successfully transferred to a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating stable standing, walking, and smooth walk-to-stand transitions on real hardware without any fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically routing rewards based on the current gait mode is critical for enabling a single policy to effectively co-learn and coordinate diverse and potentially conflicting locomotion behaviors.
> - A biologically inspired, multi-phase curriculum is essential for the stable acquisition of complex, multi-gait skills, preventing convergence failures seen in simultaneous multi-gait training.
> - Integrating biomechanically grounded reward terms, such as those encouraging straight-knee support and anti-phase arm-leg coordination, can effectively promote natural, dynamically stable, and efficient motions without requiring external motion capture data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of natural, efficient, and versatile locomotion strategies for humanoid robots represents a fundamental challenge in robotics. Current research in this domain broadly explores two main paradigms: reference-based learning and reference-free reinforcement learning (RL).

Reference-based methods, such as those employing Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), leverage large-scale motion capture (MoCap) datasets or analytically generated trajectories to teach robots human-like movements. While these methods have achieved agile locomotion, they are constrained by the availability and quality of reference data, often struggle with morphological mismatches between human demonstrators and robotic platforms, and can lack interpretability due to implicit imitation objectives. Furthermore, integrating multiple gaits typically requires switching between pre-recorded clips or managing diverse expert policies.

Conversely, reference-free RL approaches aim to discover locomotion skills by optimizing handcrafted reward functions that promote desired characteristics such as energy efficiency, symmetry, or stability. These methods offer greater generalization and design freedom but often demand meticulous reward tuning, exhibit slower convergence, and can struggle to learn complex maneuvers or agile transitions.

Addressing the integration of multiple skills, existing multi-skill and modular architectures often rely on policy distillation, mixture-of-experts, or compositional policies. These frameworks enhance versatility but typically incur increased architectural complexity, require pre-training of expert policies, and can suffer from interference when coordinating different skill domains. A common challenge in multi-behavior RL is mitigating reward interference across heterogeneous skills, which some approaches tackle through task-specific critic heads or phase-conditioned policies. While prior work like DeepMimic used one-hot skill identifiers for policy conditioning, they did not extend this to dynamically altering the reward structure.

This research positions itself by proposing a unified, reference-free RL framework that aims to overcome the limitations of both reference-based and modular methods. It distinguishes itself by employing a single recurrent policy for multi-gait control (standing, walking, running, and transitions) without requiring MoCap data. The key differentiating factors include a novel gait-conditioned reward routing mechanism that explicitly mitigates reward interference and a biologically inspired multi-phase curriculum for progressive skill acquisition. This approach seeks to provide a scalable and robust alternative to existing methods, emphasizing unified end-to-end training and naturalistic locomotion without the architectural overhead or data dependency associated with many current solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid robots to perform a diverse set of locomotion behaviors, specifically standing, walking, running, and smooth transitions between these modes, all managed by a single recurrent policy.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and observations in the field of humanoid locomotion:

1. **Demand for Natural and Efficient Locomotion:** Humanoid robots operating in dynamic real-world environments require not only stable balance but also adaptable and natural locomotion strategies. Human locomotion exhibits specific biomechanical traits, such as straight-knee support, coordinated anti-phase arm swings, and smooth heel-to-toe contact, which are crucial for enhancing balance, reducing energy expenditure, and regulating angular momentum. Existing RL methods often fail to enforce these biomechanical traits directly or rely on external motion capture data for imitation, which limits their applicability and interpretability.

2. **Limitations of Reference-Based Learning:** Many successful locomotion controllers are reference-based, imitating pre-recorded human motion. This dependency on motion capture (MoCap) datasets is restrictive, as it often leads to morphology mismatch issues between human demonstrators and robotic platforms. Furthermore, the reliance on implicit imitation objectives can limit interpretability, flexibility in reward design, and the ability to modulate tasks. A reference-free approach is motivated by the desire to overcome these data and generalization limitations.

3. **Complexity of Multi-Skill Integration:** Integrating multiple gaits or skills into a single controller is a complex task. Prior approaches, which typically involve multi-policy distillation, mixture-of-experts, or skill fusion, often necessitate expert pretraining, intricate switching mechanisms, and careful coordination of rewards across sub-policies. These architectures contribute to increased system complexity, higher training costs, and potential interference between skill domains during deployment. The motivation is to achieve multi-gait control with a simpler, unified architecture.

4. **Mitigation of Reward Interference:** When learning multiple distinct behaviors within a single policy, different task objectives can conflict, leading to unstable training and degraded performance. For instance, rewards encouraging stillness for standing might conflict with those promoting forward velocity for walking. This research is motivated by the need for a mechanism to effectively manage these conflicting reward signals.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives articulated in the paper's contributions include:
- Developing a **unified, reference-free RL framework** for standing, walking, running, and transitions within a single recurrent policy.
- Implementing a **gait-conditioned reward routing scheme** to mitigate interference among diverse locomotion objectives.
- Integrating **biomechanically grounded reward shaping** to promote natural, efficient, and human-like motions without relying on MoCap data.
- Designing a **progressive multi-phase curriculum** to facilitate stable skill acquisition and expand the command space, drawing inspiration from biological motor development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates the problem of learning unified multi-gait control as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). The objective is to train a policy that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted return.

**Policy Architecture:**
The system utilizes an **asymmetric actor-critic architecture** based on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks followed by Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). LSTMs are employed to capture temporal dependencies crucial for balance recovery, handling contact effects, and enabling smooth transitions.
- **Actor:** The actor, designed for deployment, receives proprioceptive observations including base angular velocity, gravity orientation, commanded velocities, joint position offsets, joint velocities, the previous action, a sine-cosine gait phase encoding, and a one-hot gait ID. It outputs a 23-dimensional joint position offset vector, which is then tracked by a joint-level proportional-derivative (PD) controller.
- **Critic:** During training, the critic augments the actor's inputs with privileged information. This includes foot contact states, ground reaction forces, contact friction/restitution, binary contact indicators at hip/knee joints, and externally applied disturbances. This additional information aids in more accurate value estimation and improves training stability.

**Gait-Conditioned Reward Routing:**
A central component is the gait-conditioned reward routing mechanism.
- **Gait Mode Inference:** A gait mode ID (e.g., standing, walking, running, transition) is inferred at each timestep based on the commanded velocity and the robot's dynamic state.
- **Policy Conditioning:** This gait ID is encoded as a one-hot vector and incorporated into the policy's observation, conditioning both the actor and critic networks.
- **Reward Masking:** During training, a "gait mask" is applied to the reward vector. This mask selectively activates only the reward terms relevant to the current gait mode, while shared task-level and regulation rewards remain active across all gaits. For example, specific rewards for push-off dynamics and short contact durations are activated only for running, while standing focuses on upright posture and stillness. This mechanism is designed to mitigate reward interference and ensure dedicated optimization for each gait within a unified policy.

**Curriculum Learning Strategy:**
A multi-phase curriculum is implemented, inspired by human motor development, to progressively introduce gait complexity and expand the commanded velocity space:
- **Phase 1 (Walking only):** The policy is initially trained solely on walking (Gait ID = 1), with the gait ID fixed and only walking-specific rewards active. This phase establishes stable, periodic locomotion.
- **Phase 2 (Standing and Walk-to-Stand):** Gait ID switching is introduced. When commanded speed drops below 0.1 m/s, the system enters Walk-to-Stand (W2S, ID = 2). After 1.5 seconds of low speed and double support, it transitions to Standing (ID = 0). Hysteresis prevents premature switching.
- **Phase 3 (Running and Run-to-Walk):** Running (ID = 3) is enabled based on the Froude criterion (Fr > 0.5). When speed reduces from a running state, it enters Run-to-Walk (R2W, ID = 4) for a fixed duration of 2.5 seconds to ensure smooth deceleration and contact reshaping. All five modes (Stand, Walk, W2S, Run, R2W) are co-trained with full reward routing.

**Human-Inspired Gait Design and Reward Shaping:**
To promote natural and efficient motions, biomechanical principles are integrated into the reward function:
- **Straight-Knee Support:** Rewards encourage extended knees during stance phases to improve support efficiency.
- **Anti-Phase Arm-Leg Coordination:** A key reward term (Equation 2) directly penalizes residual total angular momentum, especially in the yaw direction, and promotes anti-phase yaw swing between arms and legs. This is designed to mimic the human mechanism for balancing whole-body angular momentum and stabilizing the upper body, without requiring MoCap data.
- **Gradual Transitions:** Explicit transition gaits (W2S, R2W) are included to encourage gradual, biomechanically necessary momentum reduction, reflecting human motor strategies during gait changes.
- **Gait-Specific Rewards:** A table of representative gait-specific reward terms is provided, covering contact patterns, foot clearance, push-off dynamics, base stability, and smooth deceleration, tailored for walking, running, standing, and transitions.

**Training Platform and Domain Randomization:**
Training is conducted in **Isaac Gym**, a GPU-accelerated physics simulator, enabling parallel simulation of multiple environments. The **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm is used for policy optimization. To enhance robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, **domain randomization** is applied, perturbing physical parameters (e.g., ground friction, robot mass, joint properties) and introducing external disturbances (randomized pushes).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed gait-conditioned reinforcement learning framework through simulation experiments and real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

**Simulation Results:**

1. **Gait Switching and Velocity Tracking:**
 - The learned policy demonstrates the ability to accurately track a commanded forward velocity profile that ramps up and down between 0 and 3 m/s.
 - The robot exhibits stable and smooth transitions between all defined gait modes: standing, walking, running, walk-to-stand (W2S), and run-to-walk (R2W). These transitions occur without abrupt changes in contact patterns or requiring external resets. The policy naturally slows down from running into walking and settles into a balanced standing posture. This indicates robust internal gait modulation by the unified controller.

2. **Angular Momentum Coordination:**
 - Analysis of Z-axis angular momentum (yaw) across different gait modes reveals that the policy maintains a low total angular momentum of the body.
 - This is achieved through coordinated anti-phase motion of the arms and legs. Specifically, during walking and running, the angular momenta of the arms and legs show opposite signs and comparable magnitudes, leading to effective cancellation.
 - This coordination is maintained smoothly even during the R2W and W2S transitions, with segment amplitudes adapting to the changing velocity and contact modes. This result supports the effectiveness of the biomechanically inspired reward shaping in promoting dynamically stable and efficient behaviors.

3. **Ablation Studies:**
 - **No Curriculum:** Training all five gait modes (Standing, Walking, Running, W2S, R2W) simultaneously from the beginning, without the multi-phase curriculum, resulted in a significant failure to converge. The mean episode length was 31.26 steps, and the mean return was 2.45, in contrast to the full model's 972.6 steps and 89.3 return. This indicates that the progressive curriculum is critical for stable acquisition of complex, multi-gait skills.
 - **No Reward Routing:** Disabling the gait-conditioned reward routing mechanism, even with the curriculum in place, led to unstable gradient updates and a collapse in performance. The mean episode length was 11.59 steps, and the mean return was 0.00. This outcome confirms that dynamically activating mode-specific reward terms is essential to mitigate conflicts between heterogeneous gait objectives (e.g., stillness vs. locomotion) and ensure effective learning.

**Real-World Transfer:**

- The policy, trained exclusively in simulation, was directly deployed onto a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot without any additional fine-tuning or sim-to-real adaptation.
- The robot successfully demonstrated stable standing, walking, and smooth walk-to-stand transitions on real hardware.
- The transferred behaviors maintained coherence and robustness under moderate command perturbations. The robot exhibited natural whole-body coordination, including extended knees during stance and coordinated arm-leg motions, consistent with the simulated behavior and the human-inspired reward design.
- These results validate the generalizability of the proposed framework and the effectiveness of its components (human-inspired reward shaping, gait-conditioned training, and domain randomization) in achieving robust real-world humanoid locomotion. Dynamic running behaviors are currently stable in simulation and are targeted for future real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a unified gait-conditioned reinforcement learning framework that offers a scalable and reference-free approach to versatile and naturalistic humanoid locomotion control. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Unified and Reference-Free Control:** The development of a single recurrent policy capable of controlling standing, walking, running, and smooth transitions without reliance on motion capture data is a notable advancement. This removes the dependency on expensive, difficult-to-acquire, and morphology-specific datasets, thereby lowering the barrier to developing complex humanoid behaviors. The unified policy simplifies system architecture by eliminating the need for multiple expert controllers, complex switching logic, or hierarchical planners.

2. **Mitigation of Reward Interference:** The introduction of a gait-conditioned reward routing mechanism directly addresses a significant challenge in multi-skill reinforcement learning: conflicting reward signals. By dynamically activating only relevant reward terms for the current gait mode, the framework stabilizes training, promotes cleaner credit assignment, and enables the co-learning of diverse behaviors within a single policy. This approach could be influential in other domains requiring a single agent to master multiple distinct tasks.

3. **Promotion of Biomechanically Natural Motions:** The incorporation of human-inspired reward terms (e.g., straight-knee support, anti-phase arm-leg coordination) without explicit trajectory references encourages the emergence of energy-efficient and biomechanically plausible locomotion patterns. This contributes to the development of robots that move more naturally, which can be important for human-robot interaction and aesthetic appeal. The method's success in achieving angular momentum compensation through arm-leg coordination demonstrates a deeper understanding of dynamic balance principles.

4. **Robustness and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The combination of a multi-phase curriculum and extensive domain randomization during simulation training results in policies that are robust to variations and capable of direct transfer to physical hardware. The successful deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid, without any fine-tuning, validates the practical applicability and reliability of the proposed framework, which is crucial for real-world robotics deployment.

5. **Foundation for Future Complex Behaviors:** This work provides a foundation for extending humanoid locomotion capabilities to more challenging scenarios. While the current approach relies on manually defined gait IDs and requires extensive reward tuning, future work can build upon this by exploring more autonomous gait classification, emergent gait patterns, and integration with vision-guided navigation for uneven or deformable terrains. The framework's ability to handle diverse locomotion modes also opens avenues for incorporating complex whole-body behaviors such as climbing, object manipulation, and advanced human-robot collaboration.

The limitations, such as the manual definition of gait IDs and the labor-intensive tuning of reward terms, indicate directions for further research. However, the current findings represent a step towards developing versatile and autonomous humanoid robots capable of navigating and interacting with unstructured environments with human-like proficiency.
