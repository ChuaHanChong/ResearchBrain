# AHA-WAM:Asynchronous Horizon-Adaptive World-Action Modeling with Observation-Guided Context Routing

> [!summary] Summary
> AHA-WAM introduces an asynchronous, horizon-adaptive architecture for robot manipulation, decoupling the temporal scales of visual world planning and action execution. This approach achieves a 4.59x inference speedup and a 24.17 Hz control frequency (up to 56.95 Hz with distillation), while maintaining a 92.80% success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and 78.33% on real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Existing World-Action Models (WAMs) rigidly couple world prediction and action execution at the same temporal resolution, forcing the video world model to focus on short-horizon, often redundant frame variations.
- This temporal coupling limits the video branch's potential for embodied control, preventing it from forming extended latent plans over future visual states.
- Computational demands of world modeling lead to high inference latency, hindering real-time, closed-loop robot control and deployment.

## Method

- **AHA-WAM** employs a ==dual-Diffusion Transformer== (DiT) architecture with a low-frequency Video DiT world planner for long-horizon scene evolution and a high-frequency Action DiT executor for short action chunks.
- Observation-Guided Video-Context Routing (OVCR) dynamically adapts the planner's layerwise latent context to the latest real-time observations, preventing context staleness during asynchronous execution.
- Horizon-adaptive offset training and rolling planner memory enhance robustness to planner-executor phase shifts and maintain historical scene information for the asynchronous planner.

## Results

- Achieved an average **92.80%** success rate on 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, surpassing the Fast-WAM baseline by **0.97** percentage points and matching performance of generalist VLA models without pretraining.
- Demonstrated significant inference efficiency, reducing closed-loop latency from **190.00 ms** (Fast-WAM) to **41.37 ms** (**4.59x** speedup) for AHA-WAM, and further to **17.56 ms** (**10.82x** speedup) for AHA-WAM-Flash.
- Attained an average **78.33%** success rate across four real-world manipulation tasks, outperforming WAM baselines and exhibiting strong robustness to generalization shifts comparable to large-scale pre-trained VLA models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the temporal scales of world modeling and action execution allows the video branch to form more meaningful, long-horizon plans while the action branch maintains high-frequency, reactive control.
> - Dynamically routing and adapting asynchronous planner context with real-time observations is crucial for maintaining performance when the planner and executor operate at different frequencies.
> - Optimizations like CUDA acceleration and ODE distillation can dramatically reduce inference latency, making complex world-action models viable for real-time robot control without sacrificing performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot manipulation policies are increasingly complex, requiring an understanding of both the current scene and its potential evolution under robotic actions. Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have utilized large vision-language model backbones and scaled imitation learning to improve robot control. However, these VLA models often rely on sparse action labels, which may implicitly capture physical dynamics rather than explicitly modeling them.

World-Action Models (WAMs) represent an alternative paradigm that addresses this by integrating action prediction with dense video-based world modeling. WAMs aim to learn how actions and visual scene dynamics co-evolve, thereby incorporating physical priors into control policies. This approach is intended to produce more generalizable and transferable policy representations.

Existing WAMs, despite their promise, have typically coupled world prediction and action execution at the same temporal resolution. This means the video branch, responsible for world modeling, often expends computational resources on modeling near-term, highly correlated frame variations that may not be optimally informative for control. This temporal coupling can constrain the video branch's potential for embodied control, as it forces it to operate at a short horizon similar to action execution.

This paper positions AHA-WAM as an evolution within the WAM paradigm. It seeks to address the limitation of temporal coupling by introducing an asynchronous and horizon-adaptive approach. This approach aims to allow the video world model to form extended latent plans over future visual states, while the action model remains closely integrated with the real-time control loop for immediate corrections based on the latest observations. The work builds upon previous efforts in dual-system or asynchronous robot policies (e.g., RoboDual, Reactive Diffusion Policy, AsyncVLA) but specifically applies the slow-fast split within the joint world-action modeling framework, where the video and action components remain coupled through attention but operate at different temporal frequencies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind AHA-WAM is the observation that existing world-action models (WAMs) often underutilize the capabilities of their video branches for embodied control. Current WAM designs typically bind world prediction and action execution to the same temporal rhythm. This forces the world modeling component to focus on short-horizon, adjacent frame variations, which are frequently redundant and provide limited unique information for effective control. The authors hypothesize that this strict temporal coupling represents a structural mismatch in temporal abstraction. They argue that the video world model could more effectively support embodied control by formulating a temporally extended latent plan of future visual states, while the action model should maintain a tight coupling with the real-time control loop, allowing it to incorporate the latest observation for timely, closed-loop corrections.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of the proposed AHA-WAM system are:

1. **Reorganize World-Action Modeling for Temporal Asymmetry:** Develop a novel architecture that explicitly decouples the temporal resolutions of world prediction and action execution. This involves creating a low-frequency "world planner" (video DiT) responsible for long-horizon scene evolution and a high-frequency "action expert" (action DiT) for executing short action chunks in a closed-loop manner.
2. **Amortize World Model Computation:** Allow the computationally intensive world planner to maintain reusable layerwise latent context over long-horizon scene evolution, thereby amortizing its computation across multiple action steps. This aims to reduce the computational burden on the critical path for real-time control.
3. **Ensure Context Alignment for Asynchronous Execution:** Address the challenge of context staleness or misalignment that arises when the planner context is reused across several action steps. This requires mechanisms to adapt the planner's context to the latest real-time observations without re-running the entire video DiT.
4. **Achieve Robustness to Planner-Executor Phase Shifts:** Design the system to be robust to arbitrary temporal phase offsets between the slow planner and the fast executor, which are inherent to asynchronous streaming deployment.
5. **Maintain or Improve Manipulation Performance:** Demonstrate that this asynchronous design can achieve state-of-the-art-level manipulation performance in both simulation and real-world tasks, without requiring extensive robot-data pretraining.
6. **Significantly Enhance Inference Efficiency:** Substantially improve the closed-loop control frequency and reduce inference latency compared to existing WAM baselines, making the system more viable for real-time robot deployment.

In essence, the motivation is to unlock the full potential of video-based world modeling for robotics by aligning its temporal scale with its informational value for long-horizon planning, while ensuring that rapid, reactive control remains responsive to immediate changes in the environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AHA-WAM is an Asynchronous Horizon-Adaptive World-Action Model designed to address the temporal mismatch in existing WAMs. Its methodology is built upon a dual Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture and introduces several mechanisms to support asynchronous execution.

#### 3.1. Dual-DiT Planner–Executor Architecture

AHA-WAM comprises two main components: a **Video DiT world planner** and an **Action DiT executor**.
- **Problem Setting:** The model learns a language-conditioned visuomotor policy that takes a visual observation history ($O_v^{\le t}$), a proprioceptive state ($s_t$), and a language instruction ($l$) to predict an action chunk ($A_t = \{a_t, \dots, a_{t+h_a-1}\}$) of horizon $h_a$.
- **Temporal Decoupling:** The key innovation is to decouple temporal roles. The video branch (planner) models future visual latents ($Z_v^{t:t+h_v}$) over a longer visual planning horizon ($h_v = 64$), while the action branch (executor) predicts shorter executable action chunks ($h_a = 16$).
- **Model Components:** Visual observations are processed by a pretrained VAE encoder. Language embeddings from a text encoder condition both DiT branches. The action DiT is a compact architecture designed for high-frequency execution. The video DiT is initialized from a pretrained large-scale video model (Wan2.2-5B).
- **Layerwise Planner–Executor Coupling:** The video planner generates a layerwise latent world-plan representation, $C_p^\tau = \{(K_{p,\ell}^\tau, V_{p,\ell}^\tau)\}_{\ell=1}^L$, where $\tau$ is the latest planner refresh and $\ell$ is the transformer layer. This context is intended for reuse across multiple action steps. The action DiT then consumes this adapted context via layerwise joint attention to denoise the current action chunk. During training, the video branch predicts future video latents under a causal mask, providing a world-modeling signal, but this explicit future-video prediction path is removed during inference.

#### 3.2. Joint World-Action Training Objective

AHA-WAM is trained using a joint flow-matching objective for both action chunks and future video latents. For a target variable $y$ (either action chunk $A_t$ or future video latents $Z_v^{t:t+h_v}$), Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ and a flow time $\rho$ are sampled to construct an interpolated sample $y_\rho$. The model is trained to predict the velocity field, minimizing the $L_{FM}$ loss. The total objective is $L = L_a + \lambda L_v$, where $L_a$ is the action loss and $L_v$ is the video loss, balanced by $\lambda$.

#### 3.3. Observation-Guided Video-Context Routing (OVCR)

This module addresses the challenge of context staleness due to asynchronous execution.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of feeding raw visual tokens directly to the high-frequency action DiT, OVCR compresses the latest visual observations into a small set of "routing queries."
- **Process:** Given $Q$ learnable base queries, OVCR constructs observation-guided routing queries ($Z_q^t$) by attention pooling over the current visual tokens ($X_v^t$) using a lightweight visual projection module. These queries then interact with the latest available planner context $C_p^{\tau(t)}$ (keys $K_{p,\ell}^{\tau(t)}$ and values $V_{p,\ell}^{\tau(t)}$). For each layer $\ell$, OVCR reads planner features using $Z_{q,\ell}^t$ and predicts residual key–value updates ($\Delta K_{p,\ell}^t, \Delta V_{p,\ell}^t$) via a lightweight layerwise router $g_\psi^\ell$. A gated residual update then combines these to produce the chunk-specific adapted context $\tilde{C}_p^t = \{(\tilde{K}_{p,\ell}^t, \tilde{V}_{p,\ell}^t)\}_{\ell=1}^L$, which is consumed by the action DiT.

#### 3.4. Horizon-Adaptive Offset Training

This training strategy aims to make the action executor robust to variable temporal phase shifts between the planner and executor during asynchronous deployment.
- **Mechanism:** During training, for each segment, the action-chunk grid is randomly shifted by an offset $\delta \sim U\{0, 1, \dots, h_a-1\}$ within the video planning horizon.
- **Effect:** This exposes the action DiT to diverse planner–executor phase relationships, enabling it to consume long-horizon planner context effectively regardless of the specific alignment at inference time.

#### 3.5. Rolling Planner Memory

To ensure the low-frequency video planner retains historical scene information across refreshes, a fixed-size FIFO rolling K/V memory is maintained internally within the video planner. This memory stores historical video states from recent planner refreshes. The video DiT attends to this memory when generating new planner video context, extending its temporal receptive field. This memory is internal to the planner and not directly accessed by the action DiT.

#### 3.6. Streaming Inference and Real-Time Optimization

- **Asynchronous Execution:** The video planner and action executor operate as two non-blocking streams. The video DiT's prefill runs asynchronously, while the action DiT needs to run at the closed-loop control frequency.
- **CUDA Acceleration:** The action-chunk inference path (including image encoding, planner-context access, OVCR routing, and action denoising) is optimized. This involves compiling repeated computations into static deployment paths using TensorRT and CUDA-graph capture, and removing redundant loop-invariant computations.
- **ODE Distillation (AHA-WAM-Flash):** For further speedup, the action sampler is distilled from 10 denoising steps to 2 steps. The video DiT is frozen during this process, and the student model is trained to directly predict the teacher's final action output from sampled intermediate states.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AHA-WAM encompassed both simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, focusing on manipulation performance and closed-loop inference efficiency.

#### 4.1. RoboTwin 2.0 Simulation Performance

- **Average Success Rate:** AHA-WAM achieved an average success rate of 92.80% across 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks. This included 93.40% success in the clean setting and 92.20% in the randomized setting.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - It surpassed Fast-WAM, a competitive WAM baseline, by 0.97 percentage points, demonstrating performance improvement alongside increased efficiency.
 - It also exceeded LingBot-VA, a strong VLA baseline that utilizes large-scale robot-data pretraining, by 0.60 points, despite AHA-WAM not using such pretraining.
- **AHA-WAM-Flash Performance:** The distilled variant, AHA-WAM-Flash, achieved an average success rate of 90.20%, indicating a modest performance drop for significant latency reduction.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies on RoboTwin

Ablation studies investigated the contribution of individual components to AHA-WAM's performance:
- **Naive-Async:** A baseline variant that decoupled video and action branches but lacked rolling K/V memory and OVCR. Its performance dropped to 88.60% (from Fast-WAM's 91.83%), suggesting that mere asynchronous execution without specific alignment mechanisms is insufficient.
- **+ KV Memory:** Adding rolling K/V memory to Naive-Async recovered performance to 91.01%, indicating the importance of maintaining persistent planner states for stable video context.
- **+ OVCR:** Further adding Observation-Guided Video-Context Routing (OVCR) improved success to 91.47%, highlighting the role of observation-conditioned context adaptation in addressing planner–executor mismatch.
- **Full AHA-WAM:** The complete model, incorporating both rolling K/V memory and OVCR, achieved 92.80% success, confirming the complementary nature of these mechanisms.

#### 4.3. Real-World Robot Experiments

- **Task Success Rate:** AHA-WAM achieved an average success rate of 78.33% across four real-world tasks (Fold Towel, Organize Desktop, Prepare Soy Milk, Store Plate).
- **Comparison to Baselines:** It outperformed WAM baselines Motus (21.67%) and Fast-WAM (68.33%). It matched the performance of the generalist VLA baseline $\pi_{0.5}$ (76.67%).
- **Generalization Shifts:** Under various generalization shifts (e.g., lighting variation, texture generalization, object configuration shift, novel environments), AHA-WAM achieved 53.3% success and the highest progress score (35.00) among WAM-based baselines. It exhibited comparable robustness to $\pi_{0.5}$ (55.0% success), which benefits from large-scale generalist pretraining. This suggests that the asynchronous world-action modeling approach improves deployment stability and robustness.

#### 4.4. Latency and Control Frequency

- **AHA-WAM vs. Fast-WAM:** AHA-WAM reduced closed-loop inference latency from 190.00 ms (Fast-WAM) to 41.37 ms. This corresponds to a 4.59× speedup and an increase in control frequency from 5.26 Hz to 24.17 Hz.
- **AHA-WAM-Flash:** With ODE distillation, AHA-WAM-Flash further reduced latency to 17.56 ms, achieving a control frequency of 56.95 Hz, representing a 10.82× speedup over Fast-WAM while maintaining the asynchronous planner–executor interface. These optimizations were achieved through CUDA acceleration and ODE distillation, without altering the core model architecture or sampling procedure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AHA-WAM represents a notable advancement in the field of robot manipulation by addressing a fundamental limitation in existing world-action models: the rigid temporal coupling between world prediction and action execution.

**Enhanced Efficiency and Performance:** The core significance of AHA-WAM lies in its demonstration that decoupling the temporal scales of visual world planning and reactive action execution can lead to simultaneous improvements in both control frequency and manipulation performance. By allowing a low-frequency video DiT to serve as a long-horizon world planner and a high-frequency action DiT as a closed-loop executor, the system achieves a substantial reduction in inference latency and a significant increase in control frequency (up to 56.95 Hz with AHA-WAM-Flash), making it more suitable for real-time robot deployment. Concurrently, AHA-WAM achieves state-of-the-art-level manipulation success rates on complex benchmarks like RoboTwin 2.0 and in real-world tasks, often surpassing or matching baselines, even those leveraging extensive robot-data pretraining.

**Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The introduction of Observation-Guided Video-Context Routing (OVCR) and horizon-adaptive offset training contributes to the system's robustness. OVCR ensures that the asynchronously provided planner context remains aligned with the robot's real-time execution state, preventing context staleness. Horizon-adaptive offset training explicitly prepares the action expert for the variable phase relationships encountered in asynchronous deployment, enhancing its resilience. This is evident in AHA-WAM's performance on real-world generalization tasks, where it demonstrated limited performance degradation under distribution shifts, indicating strong robustness.

**New Design Space for World-Action Models:** AHA-WAM opens up a novel design space for future WAMs. By isolating the computationally intensive world modeling from the latency-sensitive control loop, it enables the video branch to afford more complex, predictive, deliberative, or physically grounded computations without directly impacting the robot's action latency. This separation suggests a promising direction for developing more capable and intelligent world models that can perform richer forms of planning and understanding, while still supporting the fast, reactive control necessary for robust physical interaction.

**Practical Implications for Robot Deployment:** The achieved inference efficiency and improved robustness have direct practical implications for deploying intelligent robots in real-world, unstructured environments. Higher control frequencies allow for smoother, more responsive, and more stable robot movements, which are crucial for contact-rich tasks, deformable object manipulation, and precise interactions. The ability to generalize without extensive robot-specific pretraining makes the approach more accessible and scalable.

In summary, AHA-WAM presents a conceptually coherent and empirically validated approach to advance robot learning by reimagining the temporal architecture of world-action models. Its contributions pave the way for more efficient, robust, and capable robotic systems that can leverage complex visual dynamics for intelligent control.
