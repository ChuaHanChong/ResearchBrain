# LRR-Bench: Left, Right or Rotate? Vision-Language models Still Struggle With Spatial Understanding Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> LRR-Bench introduces a synthetic benchmark for assessing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on 2D and 3D spatial understanding tasks, including rotation and movement. The evaluation reveals that state-of-the-art VLMs significantly underperform humans, especially in complex 3D scenarios and multi-image sequence analysis, with the top model achieving a score of 272.5 out of 1050.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks for VLM spatial understanding primarily focus on basic 2D positional relationships and static images, failing to cover complex 3D transformations, motion, and sequential changes.
- Current VLMs lack robust spatial reasoning capabilities crucial for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving and robotics.
- There is a need for a comprehensive, cost-effective, and contamination-free dataset to rigorously evaluate advanced spatial understanding in VLMs.

## Method

- Developed **LRR-Bench**, a ==synthetic benchmark== categorizing spatial understanding into ==absolute positioning== and complex 3D reasoning (==rotation== and ==movement== from both camera and object perspectives).
- Generated a fully synthetic dataset using ==diffusion models== and ==Minecraft==, enabling low-cost sample generation and preventing data contamination.
- Evaluated over 20 state-of-the-art VLMs using an accuracy-based scoring system and compared direct prompting with ==Chain-of-Thought== reasoning strategies.

## Results

- Human evaluators achieved a near-perfect score of **1050**, while the best VLM scored only **272.5**, highlighting a substantial disparity in spatial intelligence.
- VLMs consistently scored near zero on complex 3D tasks like Camera Movement and Camera Rotation, indicating a lack of intrinsic understanding of 3D transformations and multi-view integration.
- Analysis revealed VLM failures stem from insufficient granularity in object recognition, deficiencies in logical reasoning for sequential/compositional tasks, and incomplete 3D spatial representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLMs demonstrate a significant performance gap compared to humans in spatial understanding, particularly for tasks involving 3D transformations and dynamic scene interpretation.
> - Reasoning-based prompting (Chain-of-Thought) does not consistently improve VLM spatial understanding and can sometimes degrade performance on challenging 3D tasks.
> - Model scaling, preference optimization, and fine-tuning on existing 3D datasets do not reliably lead to more robust or comprehensive spatial reasoning capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Spatial understanding, defined as the ability of a system to perceive and interpret the arrangement, positions, orientations, and movements of objects within an environment, is a foundational capability for real-world applications such as robotic control and autonomous driving. Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are increasingly being deployed in these safety-critical domains, necessitating robust spatial awareness.

Existing benchmarks for evaluating VLMs' spatial understanding primarily focus on inferring spatial relationships in natural images. These often involve queries about the relative positions of two objects (e.g., "left," "in front of") and frequently rely on auxiliary techniques like depth estimation and segmentation for annotation. Examples include VSR, What’sUP, and Spatial-MM, which generate spatial relation queries from natural images. Some benchmarks, such as EmbSpatial, 3DSRBench, COMFORT, and LEFT, formulate queries from 3D scenes, emphasizing embodied intelligence. Additionally, synthetic datasets, like those based on grid images or psychrometric charts, have been employed to investigate model behaviors and compare them with human perception.

However, the current landscape of spatial understanding benchmarks exhibits limitations. They primarily concentrate on basic positional relationships, such as "under" or "in front of," and generally overlook more complex spatial capabilities. These include motion perception, relative movement, sequential movement analysis, and understanding spatial transformations stemming from camera or object movements in 3D space. While several studies have explored ways to enhance the spatial understanding of foundation models, often involving coordinate alignment, structured textual descriptions, or synthetic 3D datasets (e.g., SpatialCoT, SALE, SpatialBot, SpaceQwen25, SpaceOM, Llava-3D), a comprehensive and diagnostic evaluation framework that covers a broader spectrum of 2D and 3D spatial reasoning challenges from both object and observer perspectives remains an area for further development. The presented work, LRR-Bench, aims to address these identified gaps by providing a benchmark that evaluates both absolute positioning and complex 3D spatial understanding, including rotation and movement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive benchmark, named LRR-Bench, and an associated evaluation pipeline to rigorously assess the spatial understanding capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). This endeavor is motivated by several factors:

- **Necessity for Real-World Applications:** Precise spatial perception is a critical requirement for safety-critical applications such as autonomous driving and humanoid robot manipulation. These domains demand that VLMs not only identify objects but also accurately interpret their positions, orientations, and movements in a dynamic 3D environment to ensure reliable and safe operations.
- **Underexplored VLM Capabilities:** Despite the increasing prominence of VLMs, their abilities to recognize complex spatial relationships and perceive spatial movement remain largely unexplored and underevaluated by existing benchmarks. Current evaluation frameworks often focus on basic 2D positional relationships, leaving a gap in understanding how VLMs handle more intricate scenarios involving motion, sequential changes, and 3D transformations.
- **Deficiencies in Existing Benchmarks:** Current spatial understanding benchmarks are often limited in scope. They frequently query basic positional relationships between objects in static natural images and typically do not extend to dynamic contexts, such as analyzing directions of motion or relative movement caused by changes in objects or camera viewpoints. The authors argue that such capabilities are crucial for real-world scenarios but are insufficiently covered.
- **Need for a Contamination-Free and Cost-Effective Dataset:** Evaluating VLMs often involves large datasets, which can be expensive to construct and susceptible to data contamination if models have been pre-trained on similar real-world images. The authors aim to overcome this by constructing an entirely synthetic dataset. This approach allows for the low-cost generation of diverse test samples and effectively prevents the issue of data contamination, ensuring that the evaluation truly reflects a model's intrinsic spatial reasoning abilities rather than its memorization of training data.
- **Diagnostic Insights into VLM Limitations:** By introducing a more challenging and diverse set of spatial tasks, the research seeks to provide diagnostic insights into the specific weaknesses of current state-of-the-art VLMs. This includes understanding whether advanced reasoning methods (e.g., Chain-of-Thought), preference optimization, or parameter scaling laws consistently improve spatial understanding, or if they introduce unintended side effects like hallucinations.
- **Advancing Research and Development:** The ultimate motivation is to highlight significant areas for improvement in VLM spatial understanding and to catalyze further research and development in this critical domain. The publicly available dataset and code are intended to serve as a resource for the research community.

In summary, the key objectives are to comprehensively evaluate VLM spatial understanding, particularly in 3D and dynamic contexts, using a novel, synthetic, and scalable benchmark, thereby identifying current limitations and guiding future advancements in VLM design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces LRR-Bench, a benchmark and evaluation pipeline designed to assess Vision-Language Models' (VLMs) spatial understanding. The methodology is characterized by a novel categorization of spatial tasks and the use of a fully synthetic dataset.

#### 3.1. Spatial Understanding Categorization

The benchmark categorizes spatial understanding into two primary types:
- **Absolute Spatial Understanding:** This involves querying the absolute position of an object within an image (e.g., "left," "right," "center"). This category is further subdivided into tasks focusing on static single images and sequences of images.
- **3D Spatial Understanding:** This encompasses more complex scenarios involving movement and rotation in a 3D environment. It is decomposed into rotation and movement, which are then applied to both camera and object perspectives. This category primarily relies on image sequences.

#### 3.2. Dataset Generation

The dataset is entirely synthetic, which serves to enable low-cost sample generation and mitigate dataset contamination risks. The generation process varies depending on the task:

**A. Absolute Position Reasoning Tasks**
These tasks assess a VLM's ability to locate an object at a specific position independent of the scene background.
- **Position (Pos.):** Evaluates whether an object is present at one of five specified locations (top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right, center) within a single image. Samples are generated using the Flux.1-S diffusion model with object and location prompts. Low-quality or non-adherent images are filtered using GroundingDINO, which predicts bounding boxes and confidence scores.
- **Position Combination (Pos. C.):** A more challenging task where multiple sub-images, each containing an object at a specific location, are combined into a single composite image. VLMs must identify the sub-image locations, their proportions, and the object's position within each sub-image. Three sub-images are randomly placed into three of nine equal parts of a 1024x1024 resolution image.
- **Position Sequence (Pos. S.):** Utilizes image sequences (three randomly selected images) where individual images have no inherent relationship. VLMs must process each image in the sequence independently to correctly answer queries.

**B. 3D Spatial Reasoning Tasks**
These tasks require VLMs to understand the entire 3D space and relationships between objects and that space. Most samples for these tasks are generated by capturing screenshots within Minecraft to create reliable image sequences at lower cost.
- **Depth (Dep.):** Focuses on the relative depth of objects. Images are generated by Flux.1-S and filtered by GroundingDINO. Bounding boxes are used with SAM for object segmentation, and Depth-Anything-V2 predicts the depth of segmented areas. VLMs are queried to identify which object is in front.
- **Camera Rotation (Ca. R.):** Assesses perception of camera rotation. The game camera's position is fixed, and it is rotated by a fixed angle multiple times, with screenshots taken after each rotation. VLMs are queried whether the viewpoint rotation direction remains consistent in the sequence. Negative samples involve swapping image positions.
- **Camera Movement (Ca. M.):** Evaluates perception of camera movement. The camera's angle is fixed, and it is moved to random positions (front, back, left, right), ensuring continuous visibility. VLMs are queried about the camera's movement direction in sequences.
- **Object Heading Direction (Obj. H. D.):** Detects the heading direction of a target object (e.g., a sheep described as a "red block with a white head"). A "Clear" version removes background objects.
- **Object Movement Direction (Obj. M. D.):** Examines if an object's movement direction aligns with its orientation. An API sets object orientation and moves it, with screenshots taken. Negative samples involve shuffled images or perpendicular orientation to movement. A "Clear" version is also available.
- **Object Movement (Obj. M.):** Involves both camera and object movement. Two screenshots are taken each time the object moves, with the camera also moving. VLMs are queried whether the object has moved, with images selected to keep the object's absolute position visually similar, forcing reliance on spatial relationships. A "Clear" version is provided.

**C. Dataset Characteristics**
Each task comprises 200 samples (100 positive, 100 negative). Input image resolution is 512x512, except for Position Combination (1024x1024). A "Clear" version of certain object-centric tasks (Obj. H. D., Obj. M. D., Obj. M.) removes distracting background objects. A control task "IsMC" confirms models can identify Minecraft objects.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

For each task *i*, accuracy *p_i* ∈ is converted to a task score *s_i* = 2(*p_i* - 50) * 1[*p_i* ≥ 50]. Scores below 50% result in 0 points for that task. The overall score *S* is the sum of all task scores. The maximum possible total score is 1050.

#### 3.4. Models and Prompting Strategies

- **Models:** Over 20 state-of-the-art VLMs were evaluated, including commercial models like GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, and GPT-4o-mini, as well as open-source models from series such as Qwen-VL2/2.5, InternVL2, Ovis, Janus, and Llava-OV. AWQ-quantized versions were used for models exceeding 40B parameters. Fine-tuned models like Llava-3D, SpaceOM, and SpaceQwen25 were also included.
- **Prompting:** Two strategies were employed: a direct inquiry and a reasoning-based prompt (Chain-of-Thought style, requiring reasoning before answering). Model responses were classified as positive if the word "No" was not present.
- **Human Evaluation:** 10 volunteers participated, evaluating 40 randomly selected samples per task to establish a human baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on LRR-Bench revealed several findings regarding their spatial understanding capabilities.

#### 4.1. Significant Performance Gap Between VLMs and Humans

Human evaluators achieved near-perfect performance across all tasks, with an overall score of 1050 (or approximately 90% accuracy), demonstrating effortless understanding. In contrast, VLMs showed a substantial performance gap. The highest-scoring VLM achieved a total score of 272.5, significantly lower than human performance. VLMs reached human-level performance only on the two simplest tasks: "Position" (identifying absolute object location) and "Depth" (determining which object is in front). For the remaining tasks, VLM performance was distinctly lower, with many models, particularly smaller ones, achieving near-zero scores on multiple complex tasks.

#### 4.2. VLMs Struggle with 3D Spatial Understanding

The experiments demonstrated that current VLMs exhibit significant deficiencies in tasks requiring 3D spatial understanding, especially those involving the reconstruction of a 3D scene or tracking object movements.
- **Camera Movement (Ca. M.) and Camera Rotation (Ca. R.):** Nearly all models scored close to zero on these tasks, which require inferring camera transformations from image sequences. The best performance was 16 for Camera Movement (GPT-4o) and 57 for Camera Rotation (InternVL2.5-72B), compared to human scores over 90. This indicates a lack of intrinsic understanding of 3D transformations and an inability to integrate multi-view information into a coherent spatial model.
- **Object Movement (Obj. M.):** Performance remained poor in tasks requiring tracking object motion relative to its background. GPT-4o, the best model, scored 55.
- **Object Movement Direction (Obj. M. D.):** This task, which involves determining if an object's heading aligns with its movement, exposed a tendency for models to hallucinate. Models frequently predicted non-existent directions (e.g., "bottom-right") despite the dataset offering only four discrete directions (up, down, left, right), suggesting a failure to accurately interpret structured motion cues. Furthermore, in some instances, larger models performed worse than smaller ones, and performance could surprisingly exceed that on the simpler "Object Heading Direction" task without necessarily reflecting correct understanding.

#### 4.3. Reasoning Does Not Consistently Improve Performance

The strategy of prompting models to provide reasoning (Chain-of-Thought) before answering did not consistently enhance spatial understanding:
- **Simple Tasks (Position):** Reasoning had minimal impact on performance.
- **Complex Absolute Positioning Tasks (Position Combination):** Reasoning significantly improved performance (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B improved by 43 points, InternVL2.5-mpo-72B by 31 points), suggesting benefits for tasks requiring higher-order logical reasoning.
- **Certain Challenging 3D Tasks (Camera Rotation, Object Movement):** Reasoning led to a *degradation* in performance. For example, InternVL2.5-72B scored 57 without reasoning on Camera Rotation but achieved zero with reasoning. This suggests that for some spatial tasks, explicit reasoning prompts may induce new hallucinations or introduce complexity that negatively affects accuracy.

#### 4.4. No Universal Superiority, Limited Impact of Scaling and Fine-tuning

- **Model Performance Variability:** No single VLM or reasoning strategy consistently outperformed others across all spatial tasks. Models that excelled in certain areas often struggled significantly in others, indicating a lack of robust, generalized spatial reasoning.
- **Ineffectiveness of Scaling and Optimization:** Parameter scaling (increasing model size) did not consistently enhance spatial understanding. While larger models generally scored higher, the overall gap with human performance remained substantial. Additionally, models fine-tuned with preference optimization (e.g., InternVL's MPO version) sometimes performed worse than their base versions on specific spatial tasks.
- **Limited Benefit from 3D Dataset Fine-tuning:** Models specifically fine-tuned on 3D datasets, such as Llava-3D, SpaceOM, and SpaceQwen, did not show significant improvement in performance on LRR-Bench. In some cases, their performance even degraded, suggesting that current approaches to training on specialized 3D data do not necessarily translate to a more comprehensive understanding of 3D spatial concepts.

#### 4.5. Analysis of Model Failures

The widespread failures and near-zero scores were attributed to three main causes:
1. **Insufficient Granularity in Object Recognition:** Smaller models struggled with basic object identification in space. Improvements observed in "Clear" versions of movement tasks (where distractions were removed) further supported that detailed object recognition is a bottleneck.
2. **Deficiencies in Logical Reasoning:** The significant performance drop from basic "Position" to "Position Sequence" and "Position Combination" tasks indicated a failure in higher-order logical reasoning required to process sequential or compositional spatial relationships.
3. **Incomprehensive Spatial Representations:** Poor performance on tasks involving intrinsic orientation (Object Heading Direction, Object Movement Direction) and observer viewpoint changes (Camera Rotation) suggested that current VLMs lack robust internal representations of 3D geometry and the relationship between objects, observers, and space.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LRR-Bench benchmark and its associated findings contribute significantly to the field of Vision-Language Model research and hold several potential impacts:

- **Novel Diagnostic Tool:** LRR-Bench provides a novel, comprehensive, and scalable diagnostic tool for evaluating VLM spatial understanding. By segmenting spatial reasoning into absolute positioning, 3D rotation, and 3D movement from both object and camera perspectives, it offers a granular framework to pinpoint specific strengths and weaknesses of VLMs that were previously underexplored by existing benchmarks.
- **Reveals Core Limitations of State-of-the-Art VLMs:** The research systematically demonstrates that even the most advanced VLMs currently struggle profoundly with complex spatial understanding tasks, particularly those involving 3D transformations, motion perception, and multi-image sequence analysis. This revelation highlights that despite advances in general visual-linguistic tasks, a fundamental gap persists in robust spatial reasoning.
- **Informs Future Research and Development:** The identified failure modes – insufficient object recognition granularity, deficiencies in logical reasoning for sequential/compositional tasks, and a lack of comprehensive 3D spatial representations – provide clear directions for future research. Developers can focus on building VLMs with more robust internal 3D geometric models, enhanced reasoning architectures for spatial hierarchies, and better integration of dynamic visual cues.
- **Challenges Current Training Paradigms:** The findings suggest that traditional approaches, such as simply scaling model parameters, applying preference optimization (like MPO), or fine-tuning on limited 3D datasets, do not consistently or sufficiently enhance spatial understanding. In some cases, these strategies even degrade performance or induce hallucinations, indicating a need for new, spatially-aware training methodologies.
- **Catalyst for Safety-Critical Applications:** For domains like autonomous driving and robotics, where precise spatial perception is paramount for safety and reliability, this benchmark underscores the necessity for more capable VLMs. The identified limitations imply that current models may not be sufficiently trustworthy for complex real-world spatial tasks, thus prompting the development of more robust solutions.
- **Prevents Data Contamination:** The fully synthetic nature of LRR-Bench effectively prevents data contamination, ensuring that evaluation results reflect a model's genuine spatial reasoning ability rather than its capacity for memorization from pre-existing training data. This enables more reliable and fair comparisons between different VLM architectures.
- **Facilitates Community Engagement:** By making the dataset and code publicly available, the authors invite broader research community participation. This open access can accelerate the development of VLMs with improved spatial intelligence, fostering collaborative efforts to address the identified challenges.
