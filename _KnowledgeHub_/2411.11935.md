# Calibrated and Efficient Sampling-Free Confidence Estimation for LiDAR Scene Semantic Segmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Leibniz University Hannover developed a sampling-free method for estimating well-calibrated confidence in LiDAR semantic segmentation, explicitly accounting for aleatoric uncertainty. The approach substantially reduced inference time by 15-18 times compared to sampling-based baselines and achieved superior calibration performance, particularly when integrated with epistemic uncertainty methods.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks frequently produce overconfident predictions, leading to unreliable decision-making in safety-critical applications such as autonomous driving.
- Existing methods for quantifying aleatoric uncertainty, which stems from inherent data noise, typically rely on computationally expensive Monte Carlo sampling, hindering real-time deployment.
- A comprehensive understanding of uncertainty in noisy sensor data like LiDAR requires explicit modeling of aleatoric uncertainty, which is often underrepresented in current approaches.

## Method

- The method predicts ==Gaussian distributions== for class logits, modeling both mean and variance to capture ==aleatoric uncertainty== in LiDAR point clouds.
- A novel ==closed-form lower bound== for multi-class confidence is proposed, which computes confidence without computationally expensive ==Monte Carlo sampling== during inference.
- The **sampling-free aleatoric uncertainty estimation** is combined with existing ==epistemic uncertainty== techniques (==Deep Ensembles==, ==MC Dropout==) to achieve a comprehensive uncertainty measure.

## Results

- The proposed sampling-free method significantly reduced inference time by **15-18 times** compared to sampling-based approaches for aleatoric uncertainty quantification.
- It demonstrated superior confidence calibration, with Adaptive Calibration Error (ACE) scores as low as **2.06%** on RangeViT, outperforming uncalibrated and temperature-scaled baselines.
- The combination of the sampling-free aleatoric method with epistemic uncertainty techniques like Deep Ensembles consistently achieved the highest calibration performance (e.g., **1.70%** ACE).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling class logits as Gaussian distributions effectively captures aleatoric uncertainty, yielding more accurately calibrated confidence compared to uncalibrated or temperature-scaled predictions.
> - A novel closed-form lower bound for multi-class confidence eliminates the need for Monte Carlo sampling, enabling efficient, real-time uncertainty quantification with a beneficial underconfident bias.
> - Optimal calibration performance is achieved by jointly considering both aleatoric (data-inherent) and epistemic (model-knowledge) uncertainties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reliable scene understanding, particularly in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, necessitates not only accurate predictions from deep neural networks (DNNs) but also well-calibrated confidence estimates. These estimates enable systems to quantify uncertainty, identify potentially erroneous predictions, and respond conservatively. The field of confidence calibration aims to align model-predicted probabilities with the true likelihood of correctness.

Historically, the maximum class probability (MCP) from a softmax output has served as a confidence measure, but it often produces overconfident estimates that do not reflect underlying uncertainties. Existing approaches to confidence calibration can be broadly categorized into post-hoc methods and uncertainty quantification techniques. Post-hoc methods, such as temperature scaling, adjust classifier outputs without retraining the model, offering simplicity but limited effectiveness in scenarios with significant class overlap. Uncertainty quantification methods, on the other hand, aim to model different sources of uncertainty inherently.

Uncertainty in deep learning is typically distinguished as epistemic or aleatoric. Epistemic uncertainty arises from limited model knowledge and can be reduced with more data or model complexity (e.g., via Bayesian Neural Networks, Deep Ensembles, or Monte Carlo Dropout). Aleatoric uncertainty stems from inherent, irreducible noise in the data itself, such as sensor inaccuracies or environmental variability. In the context of LiDAR point clouds, aleatoric uncertainty is particularly pronounced due to factors like varying point density, measurement noise, and surface reflectivity.

While many existing uncertainty quantification methods primarily focus on epistemic uncertainty, the accurate representation of total uncertainty, especially in noisy real-world data like LiDAR, requires explicit consideration of aleatoric uncertainty. Previous work, such as the logit-sampling approach, attempted to quantify aleatoric uncertainty by modeling class logits as Gaussian distributions. However, this method relies on computationally intensive Monte Carlo sampling during inference, rendering it unsuitable for real-time applications where speed is critical.

This research contributes to the landscape by proposing an efficient, sampling-free method for estimating well-calibrated confidence that explicitly models aleatoric uncertainty. It addresses the computational bottleneck of sampling-based approaches while integrating with epistemic uncertainty methods to achieve comprehensive uncertainty estimation. The work focuses on 3D semantic segmentation of LiDAR point clouds, a key component of autonomous driving systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for estimating well-calibrated confidence values in semantic segmentation of LiDAR point clouds that explicitly accounts for aleatoric uncertainty while maintaining computational efficiency for real-time applications. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Miscalibration in Safety-Critical Systems:** Modern deep neural networks frequently produce overconfident predictions, even when incorrect. In safety-critical domains like autonomous driving, such miscalibration can lead to unsafe decisions, as the system may not recognize situations where its predictions are unreliable. The research aims to improve the trustworthiness of DNNs by ensuring that confidence estimates accurately reflect the true likelihood of correctness.

2. **Explicitly Modeling Aleatoric Uncertainty:** Many existing uncertainty quantification methods predominantly focus on epistemic uncertainty, which is attributable to the model's limited knowledge. However, aleatoric uncertainty, which stems from inherent noise and variability in the data itself (e.g., LiDAR sensor inaccuracies, varying point cloud density, environmental conditions), is pervasive in real-world sensor data. The authors highlight that this irreducible noise is often overlooked but critical for robust scene understanding, motivating the development of an approach that directly integrates aleatoric uncertainty into confidence estimation.

3. **Enhancing Efficiency for Real-Time Applications:** Previous attempts to quantify aleatoric uncertainty by modeling logits as Gaussian distributions (e.g., logit-sampling) require Monte Carlo sampling during inference. This process introduces significant computational overhead, making these methods impractical for real-time deployment in applications such as autonomous driving, where low-latency decision-making is essential. A core motivation is to achieve similar or superior calibration performance without incurring this computational cost.

To achieve these objectives, the authors articulate three specific claims that motivate their proposed methodology:

- **Sampling-Free Calibration and Underconfidence:** To develop a sampling-free approach that generates confidence values closely approximating true confidence levels, effectively calibrated against actual classification accuracy. Furthermore, this method should produce underconfident rather than overconfident values, which is considered an advantageous property in safety-critical contexts.
- **Superior Calibration through Aleatoric Modeling:** To demonstrate that their confidence estimation approach, by accounting for the underlying data distribution (i.e., aleatoric uncertainty), outperforms comparable methods like temperature scaling. The authors further aim to show that combining this aleatoric uncertainty model with epistemic uncertainty estimation yields the highest calibration performance.
- **Reduced Inference Time:** To establish that the proposed sampling-free method significantly reduces inference time compared to sampling-based approaches for aleatoric uncertainty, while preserving confidence calibration and classification accuracy.

These motivations collectively aim to advance the state of the art in uncertainty-aware semantic segmentation, making deep learning models more reliable and deployable in demanding real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a sampling-free approach for estimating well-calibrated confidence values by explicitly modeling aleatoric uncertainty in semantic segmentation tasks.

**Core Concept: Gaussian Logit Distributions**
The method's foundation is the prediction of Gaussian distributions over the logit outputs for each class, rather than single scalar logit scores. For each input, the model estimates both the mean ($\mu_k$) and variance ($\sigma^2_k$) for each of the $C$ classes.

**Training Phase**
During training, the loss function is computed based on the average of softmax results derived from samples drawn from these Gaussian logit distributions. This implies that while the inference confidence estimation is sampling-free, the training process may involve sampling to optimize the parameters ($\mu_k, \sigma^2_k$) of these distributions.

**Confidence Computation (Inference Phase)**
For inference, the predicted class is identified as the one with the highest predicted mean logit ($\mu_k$). Without loss of generality, let this be class 1. The confidence in this prediction is then defined as the probability that the sampled logit score for class 1 ($X_1$) is greater than or equal to the sampled logit scores for all other competing classes ($X_i$, where $i \geq 2$). Mathematically, this is expressed as $P(X_1 \geq \max_{i \geq 2} X_i)$, where each $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\cdot | \mu_i, \sigma^2_i)$.

- **Exact and Approximate (Sampling-Based) Computation:**
 - Direct computation of $P(X_1 \geq \max_{i \geq 2} X_i)$ lacks a closed-form solution for more than two classes.
 - A Monte Carlo approximation involves drawing $N$ samples ($x_k$) from the distribution of $X_1$ and then, for each sample, computing the product of the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of all other classes evaluated at $x_k$: $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \prod_{i=2}^C \Phi_i(x_k)$. This approach, while more efficient than drawing samples for all classes, still requires $N$ samples and is computationally intensive.

- **Sampling-Free Confidence Quantification (Proposed Method):**
 - For the special case of two classes, a closed-form solution exists: $P(X_1 \geq X_2) = \Phi(\frac{\mu_1 - \mu_2}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_1 + \sigma^2_2}})$, where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal CDF.
 - For $C > 2$ classes, the authors introduce a novel closed-form lower bound for the confidence: $P(X_1 \geq \max_{i \geq 2} X_i) \geq \prod_{i=2}^C \Phi_{1,i}$, where $\Phi_{1,i} = \Phi(\frac{\mu_1 - \mu_i}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_1 + \sigma^2_i}})$.
 - This lower bound is derived from the property that the covariance of strictly monotonically increasing functions of a random variable is non-negative. Since Gaussian CDFs are strictly monotonically increasing, the expectation of their product is greater than or equal to the product of their expectations.
 - This formulation allows for confidence computation by evaluating $C-1$ pairwise cumulative distribution functions, entirely eliminating the need for Monte Carlo sampling. This makes the approach significantly more efficient. The method is noted to yield a slightly underconfident behavior, which is considered beneficial in safety-critical contexts.

**Integration with Epistemic Uncertainty**
To achieve comprehensive uncertainty estimation, the proposed aleatoric confidence method is integrated with existing epistemic uncertainty modeling techniques, specifically Deep Ensembles (DE) and Monte Carlo Dropout (MC Dropout). This joint modeling is hypothesized to lead to improved calibration performance.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Datasets:** Evaluated on SemanticKITTI and nuScenes, both standard benchmarks for LiDAR semantic segmentation. 3D scans are converted to 2D spherical range-view images ([64 × 2048 × 5] for SemanticKITTI, [32 × 2048 × 5] for nuScenes), with channels representing (x, y, z), intensity, and range.
- **Network Architectures:** SalsaNext (a CNN-based U-Net architecture) and RangeViT (a transformer-based model), representing different architectural paradigms for LiDAR segmentation.
- **Loss Function:** A composite loss combining multi-class focal loss and Lovász-Softmax loss, aimed at improving both segmentation performance and confidence calibration.
- **Comparative Methods:**
 - Uncalibrated: Maximum Class Probability (MCP).
 - Post-hoc: Temperature Scaling (TS).
 - Aleatoric: Logit-sampling (sampling-based baseline).
 - Total Uncertainty: Evidential Deep Learning (EDL).
 - Epistemic: Deep Ensembles (DE), Monte Carlo Dropout (MC Dropout).
 - Combinations: MCP+DE, MCP+MC Dropout, TS+DE, TS+MC Dropout, Logit-sampling+DE, Logit-sampling+MC Dropout, Our sampling-free+DE, Our sampling-free+MC Dropout.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Classification Performance:** Mean Intersection over Union (mIoU).
 - **Confidence Calibration:** Adaptive Calibration Error (ACE) and Reliability Diagrams. ACE quantifies the absolute deviation from perfect calibration, while reliability diagrams visually indicate over/underconfidence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments validated the three key claims of the proposed sampling-free confidence estimation method across LiDAR semantic segmentation benchmarks.

**Claim 1: Sampling-Free Approximation and Underconfidence**
- **Comparison with Exact Confidence:** A scatter plot (Figure 1a) comparing the proposed sampling-free lower bound estimation with exact confidence values (approximated via Monte Carlo integration) on SemanticKITTI demonstrated close alignment. The estimated values predominantly clustered near or slightly below the ideal y=x line, indicating that the lower bound consistently provides a conservative estimate, tending towards underconfidence rather than overconfidence.
- **Reliability Diagrams:** Reliability diagrams (Figure 1b) further confirmed this conservative behavior. The sampling-free lower bound (green line) aligned more closely with the ideal perfect calibration line and consistently showed slightly underconfident estimates compared to the logit-sampling baseline and the exact Monte Carlo values.
- **Qualitative Uncertainty Maps:** Qualitative comparisons (Figure 3) between the logit-sampling baseline and the proposed sampling-free method revealed similar uncertainty patterns, which correlated with model misclassifications. Crucially, the proposed method assigned higher uncertainty to erroneous regions (e.g., misclassified bike, trunk, pole instances), reinforcing its conservative (underconfident) nature in critical areas.

**Claim 2: Superior Calibration Performance and Joint Uncertainty Modeling**
- **Outperforming Temperature Scaling:** Reliability diagrams (Figure 2) showed that the proposed sampling-free method achieved better calibration than both the uncalibrated (MCP) and temperature-scaled (TS) models. Across both SalsaNext and RangeViT architectures on SemanticKITTI, the proposed method's curve remained closer to the perfect calibration line and exhibited mild underconfidence in the 0.4 to 0.7 confidence range.
- **Quantitative Calibration (ACE):** On SalsaNext, the proposed method achieved an ACE of 2.15%, outperforming TS (3.06%) and MCP (6.30%). Similarly, for RangeViT, it obtained an ACE of 2.06%, compared to 2.97% for TS and 5.77% for MCP (Table 1).
- **Benefit of Joint Uncertainty Modeling:** When the proposed aleatoric confidence estimation was combined with epistemic uncertainty modeling (Deep Ensembles or MC Dropout), the calibration performance further improved significantly. The lowest ACE scores were consistently achieved by methods that jointly considered both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties. For instance, on RangeViT with SemanticKITTI, the combination of "Our sampling-free + DE" yielded the lowest ACE of 1.70%, compared to 1.83% for "logit-sampling + DE". Similar trends were observed for SalsaNext (1.33% ACE for "Our sampling-free + DE") and nuScenes (1.73% ACE for "Our sampling-free + DE"). These results indicate that a comprehensive understanding of uncertainty requires accounting for both its irreducible data-inherent component and the model's knowledge limitations.
- **Qualitative Reliability:** Qualitative analyses (Figures 5, 6, 7 in the supplementary material) illustrated that the proposed method effectively captured semantic ambiguities and misclassifications with high uncertainty, unlike temperature scaling, which often assigned low uncertainty to erroneous regions. This included areas with confusion between sidewalk and street, parking areas, and mixed fence/vegetation regions.

**Claim 3: Reduced Inference Time**
- **Computational Efficiency:** The proposed sampling-free method demonstrated a substantial reduction in inference time compared to the sampling-based logit-sampling approach (Table 2). Specifically, it was 15 times faster for RangeViT (0.25 ms vs. 3.61 ms) and 18 times faster for SalsaNext (0.28 ms vs. 4.80 ms).
- **FLOPs Analysis:** This efficiency gain came with only a minimal increase in computational overhead. The sampling-free approach added approximately 0.07G FLOPs, whereas the logit-sampling method incurred an additional 6.82G FLOPs due to extensive sampling.

In summary, the research found that the sampling-free confidence estimation method successfully approximates true confidence values with a conservative bias, significantly improves calibration over baseline methods (especially when combined with epistemic uncertainty), and drastically reduces inference time, making it suitable for real-time applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an efficient and robust approach to confidence estimation, with several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Reliability in Safety-Critical Applications:** The most direct impact of this work is on safety-critical domains like autonomous driving. By providing well-calibrated confidence estimates, the proposed method allows autonomous systems to differentiate between reliable and unreliable predictions. This capability enables conservative decision-making in ambiguous or high-uncertainty situations, such as slowing down, requesting human intervention, or executing fallback maneuvers, thereby improving overall system safety and trustworthiness. The tendency of the method to produce underconfident rather than overconfident predictions is a particularly advantageous property in such scenarios.

- **Efficient Uncertainty Quantification:** The introduction of a sampling-free lower bound for confidence computation addresses a critical bottleneck in previous aleatoric uncertainty quantification methods. The significant reduction in inference time (more than an order of magnitude faster than sampling-based baselines) makes comprehensive uncertainty estimation computationally feasible for real-time LiDAR semantic segmentation, which is crucial for autonomous navigation systems that require immediate environmental understanding.

- **Comprehensive Uncertainty Modeling:** The work emphasizes the importance of jointly modeling both aleatoric (data-inherent) and epistemic (model-knowledge) uncertainties. By demonstrating superior calibration performance when combining the proposed aleatoric method with epistemic techniques like Deep Ensembles or Monte Carlo Dropout, the research reinforces a holistic view of uncertainty, leading to more accurate and reliable confidence scores. This comprehensive approach is particularly relevant for noisy sensor data like LiDAR, where inherent data variability is substantial.

- **Improved Trust in AI Systems:** Miscalibration and overconfidence often erode trust in AI predictions. By providing more transparent and accurate measures of uncertainty, this research contributes to building more trustworthy AI systems, especially when they are deployed in environments where human safety is at stake. Users, operators, and regulatory bodies can gain greater insight into the reliability of a model's output.

- **Advancement in Semantic Segmentation:** While the core method is about confidence calibration, its application to LiDAR semantic segmentation (a fundamental task in 3D scene understanding) directly enhances the utility of these models. The ability to generate detailed, uncertainty-aware semantic segmentation maps allows for a more nuanced interpretation of the environment, identifying not just what is present but also where the model is less certain about its classifications.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The methodology lays groundwork for further advancements in uncertainty quantification. The noted limitations, such as increased training time and challenges with distinguishing similar classes, also open avenues for future work, including exploring more flexible distributional models (e.g., normalizing flows) and extending the approach to out-of-distribution detection and domain shift scenarios.

In summary, this research offers a practical and effective solution to a significant challenge in deploying deep learning models in real-world safety-critical systems. By making uncertainty estimation both comprehensive and computationally efficient, it contributes to the development of more robust, reliable, and trustworthy AI-powered autonomous agents.
