# DC-WAM: Dynamic-Centric Visual Supervision and Reasoning for World-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> DC-WAM enhances World-Action Models (WAMs) by shifting their visual prediction focus from photorealistic appearance to interaction-induced dynamics, leveraging offline-derived dynamic maps and an attention routing mechanism. This approach notably improves robustness against visual distribution shifts by up to 9.4 points on LIBERO-Plus and demonstrates consistent gains in real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing RGB-based WAMs are computationally intensive, often generating photorealistic future videos, and dedicate significant learning capacity to superficial visual details irrelevant to action.
- Policies derived from appearance-dominated reconstruction are brittle and perform poorly under visual distribution shifts, such as changes in lighting, background, or object texture.
- Improving WAMs typically involves introducing new modality-specific prediction branches or requiring additional online inputs, increasing architectural complexity or deployment requirements.

## Method

- **DC-WAM** incorporates ==offline tracker-derived dynamic maps== to provide ==dynamic-centric visual supervision==, guiding the visual branch to prioritize interaction-induced motion.
- A ==Dynamics-Aware Attention Bias (DynaRoute)== module predicts token-wise dynamic relevance and injects it as an additive key-side bias into the ==visual-to-visual attention mechanism==, directing the model's focus.
- The method employs a multi-component training objective combining ==action flow matching==, ==temporal-difference visual supervision==, ==tracker-guided flow matching==, and DynaRoute relevance prediction, supporting efficient action-only inference via a ==routed video cache==.

## Results

- DC-WAM achieved a **98.1%** success rate on LIBERO (**1.4-point** improvement over FastWAM-AC) and **60.9%** on LIBERO-Plus, outperforming FastWAM-AC by **7.1 points** under visual perturbations.
- The approach demonstrated enhanced robustness in real-world bimanual tasks, consistently improving success rates under lighting and background shifts compared to FastWAM-AC, with smaller performance drops from clean conditions.
- Ablation studies confirmed that combining dense and sparse dynamic supervision with DynaRoute is critical for performance gains, and qualitative analysis showed DC-WAM maintained focused attention on dynamic-relevant objects even under severe visual corruption.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Control utility in WAMs does not necessarily correlate with photorealistic future visual prediction; instead, focusing on interaction-induced dynamics yields more robust and effective policies.
> - Guiding a model's internal attention mechanisms towards dynamic-relevant regions through an explicit attention bias significantly improves its ability to generalize across diverse visual distribution shifts.
> - A combination of dense temporal-difference supervision and sparse, localized tracker-guided motion cues offers complementary and crucial information for effectively learning control-relevant visual representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control has seen advancements with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which directly map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. Building upon this, World-Action Models (WAMs) integrate future visual prediction alongside action generation. WAMs leverage anticipated scene evolution as an additional learning signal, providing dense temporal supervision beyond action imitation.

However, existing WAMs often rely on generating photorealistic RGB future predictions. This approach presents several challenges:
1. **Computational Cost:** Generating complete, high-fidelity future videos is computationally intensive.
2. **Capacity Allocation:** Uniform RGB prediction can lead models to allocate substantial capacity to visual factors like texture, illumination, background, and sensor noise. These factors, while contributing to reconstruction fidelity, may be weakly related to effective action selection, especially when they change across environments without altering manipulation dynamics. This can result in policies that are brittle under visual distribution shifts.

Recent developments in WAMs have begun to address these issues. Some "efficient WAMs" suggest that the primary benefit of the visual prediction branch might stem from the control-relevant visual representations learned during training, rather than the explicit, iteratively generated future videos at inference time. Other methods introduce structured future representations, such as semantic masks, point trajectories, optical flow, or geometric states, to reduce the ambiguity of RGB prediction and make it more action-relevant. While effective, these approaches typically involve modifying or expanding the predicted future-state modality.

This paper positions DC-WAM as an exploration into whether an *existing* RGB-based WAM can be redirected towards dynamic-centric representations. The objective is to achieve stability across appearance shifts without introducing additional modality-specific prediction branches or requiring new online inputs during deployment. DC-WAM thus fits into the landscape by attempting to enhance the robustness and control utility of conventional RGB-based WAMs by focusing their internal learning and reasoning mechanisms on interaction-induced visual dynamics, thereby addressing the limitations of appearance-dominated reconstruction within the existing WAM framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and performance of World-Action Models (WAMs) for robotic manipulation, particularly under visual distribution shifts, by reorienting their visual prediction capabilities towards interaction-induced dynamics rather than photorealistic appearance reconstruction.

This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Inefficiency and Irrelevance of Photorealistic Prediction:** Current RGB-based WAMs often aim for photorealistic future video prediction. While providing dense supervision, this approach is computationally demanding. More critically, it encourages the model to dedicate significant learning capacity to reproducing superficial visual details such as texture, illumination, and background variations. These appearance factors are often peripheral to the core manipulation task and can change unpredictably in real-world scenarios without affecting the underlying physics of interaction (e.g., how a gripper interacts with an object).

2. **Brittleness to Visual Distribution Shifts:** The reliance on appearance-dominated reconstruction makes WAMs potentially fragile when deployed in environments with visual properties that differ from their training data (e.g., changes in lighting, background, or object appearance). A model heavily optimized for appearance might misinterpret novel visual cues or ignore crucial dynamic cues when confronted with out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, leading to performance degradation.

3. **Prior Observations on Control-Relevant Representations:** Previous research on efficient WAM variants has indicated that the main advantage of the visual branch for control might lie not in the explicit, complete future videos generated during execution, but in the implicit, control-relevant visual representations that are induced within the model during its training phase. This suggests that if the training process can be guided to prioritize these representations, robust control might be achieved without the overhead of generating full visual futures at inference time.

4. **Desire for Minimal Architectural Changes:** Many existing solutions to the problems of RGB-based WAMs involve introducing new modalities (e.g., semantic masks, optical flow, point trajectories) or separate experts, which modify or expand the predicted future-state representation. The authors' motivation is to investigate whether the benefits of dynamic-centric learning can be achieved by *redirecting* the existing RGB-based visual branch, rather than introducing additional modality-specific predictions or requiring new online inputs at deployment. This aims to leverage the established strengths of RGB visual processing while mitigating its weaknesses.

In essence, the research seeks to answer whether the visual modality in a WAM can be guided to learn what is truly essential for control—the "how" of scene changes induced by interaction—instead of merely reconstructing the "what" of future appearances, thereby fostering greater robustness and generalization in robotic manipulation policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DC-WAM operates by modifying the training and forward computation of an existing RGB visual branch within a World-Action Model (WAM) framework. It builds upon the Wan2.2 backbone, which employs a visual-action Mixture-of-Experts Transformer (MoT) architecture. The core of the approach involves introducing dynamic-centric visual supervision and a dynamics-aware attention bias, while retaining an action-conditioned visual branch for regularization.

#### 3.1. Architecture Overview

DC-WAM utilizes a Wan2.2 MoT backbone, which consists of two main branches: an RGB visual branch for latent video prediction and an action branch for action-chunk generation. The visual branch is action-conditioned, meaning future visual tokens can attend to action tokens. Conversely, action tokens are constrained to attend only to current observed visual tokens, preventing future information leakage during action prediction. DC-WAM does not introduce additional modality-specific experts or token vectors; it modifies how the *existing* RGB visual branch is trained and processes information.

#### 3.2. Tracker-Derived Dynamic Map Construction

A crucial component of DC-WAM is the creation of offline tracker-derived dynamic maps. These maps serve as supervision targets during training and are explicitly *not* used during policy execution.
1. **Point Trajectory Estimation:** For each training episode, candidate points are sampled either uniformly across the image or within foreground regions identified by a Segment Anything Model (SAM). An off-the-shelf point tracker (CoTracker3) then estimates the trajectories of these points throughout the episode.
2. **Motion Magnitude Calculation:** The frame-wise motion magnitude for each tracked point is computed using the backward temporal difference (Euclidean distance between current and previous point positions).
3. **Dynamic Point Selection:** Points whose motion magnitude exceeds a predefined threshold (`δ_mot`) are identified as "dynamic points," filtering out static elements and minor tracking fluctuations.
4. **Rasterization and Aggregation:** These dynamic points are then rasterized onto the VAE visual-token grid (a lower-resolution grid corresponding to the visual latent space). A spatial response kernel (Gaussian) maps the influence of each dynamic point to nearby visual tokens. These motion-weighted kernel responses are aggregated per token.
5. **Normalization:** The aggregated token-level dynamic responses are normalized across the entire episode and within each camera view, producing a dynamic map `m*` with values between 0 and 1. This normalization emphasizes both spatial saliency (regions with strong motion) and temporal saliency (time steps with pronounced interaction-induced changes).

This `m*` map is inherently less sensitive to persistent appearance shifts because it is derived from inter-frame point displacements rather than absolute RGB reconstruction errors. It guides the visual branch to prioritize gripper motion, object displacement, and contact events over static or slowly changing background and illumination. The map is further downsampled to the DiT token resolution for supervision of DynaRoute.

#### 3.3. Dynamics-Aware Attention Bias (DynaRoute)

DynaRoute is a lightweight module designed to predict token-wise dynamic relevance for future visual tokens and convert this relevance into an attention bias. This mechanism guides the visual attention of the model towards control-relevant, interaction-induced dynamics.
1. **Relevance Prediction:** At each diffusion timestep during training, DynaRoute takes the stop-gradient versions of visual tokens (current and noisy future), action tokens, language/proprioceptive conditioning tokens, and the current diffusion timestep as input. It predicts a relevance score `z_τ` for each visual token.
2. **Sigmoid Activation:** The predicted relevance scores are passed through a sigmoid function to produce `g_τ` values between 0 and 1.
3. **Log-Space Bias Conversion:** Each token's relevance `g_i` is converted into a log-space bias `b_i`, where a scaling factor `α` controls the routing strength. Low-relevance tokens receive stronger negative biases.
4. **Centering and Injection:** The biases `b_i` are centered across all visual tokens (`_b_i`) to stabilize the scale. This centered bias `_b_i` is then injected as an additive key-side bias into the visual-to-visual attention mechanism within the MoT block across all layers at the current diffusion timestep. This effectively increases the attention scores for tokens predicted to be dynamically relevant.

#### 3.4. Action-Only Inference with Routed Video Cache

For efficient deployment, DC-WAM adopts a Fast-WAM-style action-only inference strategy:
1. **Single-Step DynaRoute Evaluation:** At the initial diffusion step (`τ_init = 1`), DynaRoute is evaluated once. It uses the clean observed-frame latent, noisy future visual slots, noisy action inputs, and language/proprioceptive conditioning to predict the attention biases.
2. **Visual Cache Construction:** These biases are used during the "video-cache prefill" to construct a routed visual key-value cache (`C_V`) for the action branch.
3. **Video Branch Disablement:** After `C_V` is constructed, the video branch is no longer executed. The action branch reuses this cached `C_V` for all subsequent denoising steps, maintaining computational efficiency while benefiting from the dynamically routed visual information. This preserves train-inference consistency for DynaRoute, as it aligns with the high-noise regime used during training.

#### 3.5. Training Objective

The overall training objective combines four distinct loss terms:
1. **Action Flow Matching (`L_A_FM`):** A standard flow matching loss for action generation, where the model predicts the flow target (`u_A`) given a noisy action input.
2. **Temporal-Difference Supervision (`L_V_TD`, Dense):** This loss is applied to the visual branch at the VAE latent resolution. It enforces temporal consistency by matching the temporal difference of flow velocities between adjacent frames (i.e., `(predicted_u_t - predicted_u_{t-1})` vs. `(target_u_t - target_u_{t-1})`). This term primarily suppresses temporally invariant appearance components.
3. **Tracker-Guided Flow Matching (`L_V_TrackFM`, Sparse):** This reweights the standard visual flow matching error using the `m*` dynamic map. By weighting losses, it redirects the visual branch's focus towards sparse regions of strong tracked motion (end effector, manipulated objects, contact areas), which are critical for manipulation.
4. **DynaRoute Relevance Prediction (`L_Route`):** This loss supervises the DynaRoute module's predicted relevance scores (`g`) against the downsampled tracker-derived map (`e_m*`). It combines a binary cross-entropy (BCE) term for token-wise relevance and a soft Dice loss to mitigate class imbalance caused by the sparse nature of dynamic regions.

Together, `L_V_TD` and `L_V_TrackFM` provide complementary dense and sparse dynamic supervision, while `L_Route` guides the model's internal attention mechanisms toward these dynamic cues.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated DC-WAM's performance on standard in-distribution (ID) tasks, out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks with visual perturbations, and real-world manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Performance on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus

- **In-Distribution (LIBERO):** DC-WAM achieved an average success rate of 98.1% across the four LIBERO suites. This represented a 1.4-point improvement over FastWAM-AC (96.7%) and a 0.5-point improvement over FastWAM (97.6%).
- **Out-of-Distribution (LIBERO-Plus):** On the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, designed to evaluate robustness under seven types of visual perturbations (object layout, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction, lighting, background texture, sensor noise), DC-WAM demonstrated substantial gains. It achieved an average success rate of 60.9%, outperforming FastWAM-AC by 7.1 points (53.8%) and FastWAM by 9.4 points (51.5%).
- **ID-OOD Degradation:** DC-WAM exhibited the smallest drop in performance between ID and OOD conditions, with a 37.2-point difference, compared to 42.9 points for FastWAM-AC and 46.1 points for FastWAM.
- **Specific OOD Gains:** DC-WAM showed improvements across six of the seven perturbation dimensions in LIBERO-Plus, with the most notable gains observed under language, background, and lighting shifts.

#### 4.2. Real-world Evaluation

DC-WAM was evaluated on three long-horizon bimanual manipulation tasks on an Agilex Piper platform under clean, lighting-perturbed, and background-perturbed conditions. Policies were trained solely on clean demonstrations.
- **Consistent Improvements:** DC-WAM consistently improved success rates across all three tasks (Stack-Bowl, Pile-Plates, Collect-Potato) compared to FastWAM-AC.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** The gains were particularly pronounced under lighting shifts and background perturbations. For instance, on the "Pile-Plates" task under lighting perturbation, DC-WAM achieved 71% success (an 18-point drop from clean) compared to FastWAM-AC's 41% (a 39-point drop). Similarly, under background perturbation for the same task, DC-WAM reached 69% (a 20-point drop) versus 46% (a 34-point drop) for FastWAM-AC.
- **Smaller Clean-to-OOD Drops:** DC-WAM consistently demonstrated smaller performance drops relative to its clean condition under OOD visual perturbations, affirming its improved real-world OOD robustness.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Dense and Sparse Dynamic Supervision:**
 - Adding temporal-difference supervision (`+ Dense w/ Route`) improved LIBERO success to 97.1% and LIBERO-Plus to 56.6% over FastWAM-AC (96.7%, 53.8%).
 - Adding tracker-guided flow matching (`+ Sparse w/ Route`) resulted in 97.4% on LIBERO and 58.7% on LIBERO-Plus.
 - The combination of both (DC-WAM) achieved the highest performance (98.1% on LIBERO, 60.9% on LIBERO-Plus), indicating that dense temporal changes and localized interaction dynamics are complementary.
- **Dynamics-Aware Routing and Gradient Path:**
 - `No routing` (dynamic supervision without DynaRoute) achieved 97.7% on LIBERO and 59.1% on LIBERO-Plus, which was still lower than full DC-WAM.
 - `Shuffled relevance` (applying DynaRoute with randomized relevance maps) degraded performance compared to `No routing`, suggesting that spatially coherent dynamic cues are important.
 - `Action-query routing` (biasing attention for action tokens instead of visual tokens) severely degraded performance (95.8% on LIBERO, 49.2% on LIBERO-Plus), indicating that dynamic cues are more effective when guiding the visual branch rather than directly influencing action tokens.
 - Stopping gradients from visual objectives to the action branch (`DynaRoute w/ stop-grad`) caused drops of 1.1 points on LIBERO and 3.9 points on LIBERO-Plus compared to full DC-WAM. This indicates that the video supervision actively contributes to action learning via the action-conditioned visual computation pathway.

#### 4.4. Analysis of PSNR vs. Control Performance

The analysis demonstrated a clear mismatch between future-frame PSNR (a measure of visual fidelity) and policy success. Variants with lower PSNR sometimes outperformed those with higher PSNR, and DC-WAM achieved the highest policy success despite not having the highest PSNR across all comparisons. This finding suggests that standard appearance-level reconstruction metrics do not fully capture the control utility of future visual prediction in WAMs. Prioritizing interaction-induced dynamics, even at the expense of photorealistic accuracy, appears to be more beneficial for policy learning.

#### 4.5. Reshaping Visual Attention

Qualitative analysis of attention responses under increasing visual perturbations showed that FastWAM-AC's attention became diffuse and shifted towards static background distractors. In contrast, DC-WAM consistently maintained more concentrated attention on dynamic-relevant objects and interaction regions, even under severe corruption. This indicates that dynamic-centric supervision and relevance routing lead to robust, change-centric visual representations that preserve action-relevant cues amidst visual shifts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DC-WAM research presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation and World-Action Models:

1. **Enhanced Robustness to Visual Distribution Shifts:** A primary challenge in deploying robot policies in real-world, unconstrained environments is their vulnerability to visual variations not encountered during training. DC-WAM demonstrates a substantial improvement in out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization under diverse visual perturbations, including changes in lighting, background, and language instructions. This enhanced robustness is crucial for transitioning robotic systems from controlled laboratory settings to practical applications.

2. **Redefinition of Visual Prediction Utility in WAMs:** The work challenges the conventional assumption that photorealistic future video prediction is the optimal or sole method for providing useful visual supervision in WAMs. By showing that explicit appearance reconstruction (as measured by PSNR) does not necessarily correlate with improved control performance, DC-WAM emphasizes that focusing on *control-relevant dynamics* is more effective. This insight could guide future WAM development away from purely photorealistic generation towards more abstract, yet functionally relevant, predictive signals.

3. **Methodological Efficiency and Compatibility:** DC-WAM achieves its benefits without introducing new predicted modalities or requiring additional online inputs during deployment. It successfully redirects the learning of *existing* RGB video branches using only offline-derived dynamic maps and an attention routing mechanism. This approach maintains compatibility with efficient, action-only inference styles (like Fast-WAM), making it practical for real-time robot control where computational resources are often constrained.

4. **Complementary Supervision Strategy:** The proposed combination of dense temporal-difference supervision and sparse tracker-guided flow matching provides a comprehensive way to emphasize both global visual changes and localized manipulation dynamics. This dual approach offers a more nuanced and effective way to guide the learning process of visual representations for control.

5. **Attention Guidance for Control:** The DynaRoute mechanism introduces a novel way to inject dynamic relevance as an attention bias, actively guiding the model's focus towards interaction-induced motion. This token-level routing mechanism helps the model prioritize what matters most for action selection, improving the interpretability and effectiveness of its internal reasoning.

6. **Potential for Broader Applicability:** The core principles of dynamic-centric supervision and attention routing, being independent of specific robot platforms or tasks, could be broadly applicable. Future work may explore extending DC-WAM to larger and more diverse datasets, different robot platforms, and other WAM architectures beyond the MoT backbone used in this study, potentially contributing to the development of more generalist robot policies.

In summary, DC-WAM provides a refined approach to visual learning in WAMs, moving beyond mere appearance reconstruction to prioritize the underlying dynamics of interaction. This shift offers a pathway to more robust, efficient, and capable robotic systems that can operate reliably in varied real-world environments.
