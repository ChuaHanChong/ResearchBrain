# Learning Multi-Modal Whole-Body Control for Real-World Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Oregon State University developed the Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC), a learned whole-body controller for real-world humanoid robots, which utilizes a unified interface of masked target trajectories to interpret and execute diverse behaviors. This controller demonstrated robust execution across locomotion, mimicry, and loco-manipulation tasks, achieving successful sim-to-real transfer with a 5% real-world failure rate on a Digit V3 robot.

## Problem

- Traditional humanoid controllers are often specialized for individual skills, leading to limited versatility, difficulty in extension, and poor reusability across different applications.
- A unified input representation for diverse control modalities (e.g., velocity commands, end-effector targets, motion capture) is lacking, complicating application development and multi-modal input handling.
- Integrating full whole-body capabilities, including seamless transitions between locomotion modes and manipulation, remains a challenge, often requiring complex switching mechanisms between specialized controllers.

## Method

- Developed the **Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC)**, a ==reinforcement learning== policy trained via ==PPO== in simulation, which processes motion directives as future pose trajectories with optional binary masks.
- The MHC receives the robot's current proprioceptive state and a ==masked motion directive==, then outputs PD setpoints for all robot motors, allowing it to "fill in the blanks" for unspecified joints.
- Implemented a ==three-stage curriculum== (Locomotion, Stability, Whole-Body Directives) and extensive ==domain randomization== during training to foster robustness and enable effective sim-to-real transfer across various behaviors.

## Results

- MHC demonstrated competitive locomotion performance compared to the state-of-the-art SaW controller, achieving lower root drift in walking tasks (e.g., **0.413m** for 1m/s x-velocity vs. SaW's **0.803m**).
- For combined locomotion and upper-body mimicry, MHC significantly outperformed baselines, achieving a **0.450%** failure rate (vs. **49.213%** for OffsetBlind) and a lower mean end-effector positional error of **0.098m**.
- Successfully transferred complex, multi-modal behaviors to a real-world Digit V3 robot, executing joystick locomotion, boxing jab mimicry, and loco-manipulation with a **5%** real-world failure rate and a **0.117m** mean end-effector positional error.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representing target behaviors as future pose trajectories with optional masking provides a unified and expressive interface, enabling a single learned controller to handle diverse inputs from locomotion to full-body imitation.
> - A progressive, three-stage training curriculum significantly improves the controller's ability to learn complex tasks, resulting in enhanced walking, mimicry, and stability compared to training without a structured curriculum.
> - The framework's performance on various tasks shows a trend of improvement with larger and more diverse training datasets, indicating its scalability and potential for continuous repertoire expansion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of versatile and robust whole-body controllers for bipedal humanoid robots represents a persistent challenge in robotics. Historically, approaches have involved the manual engineering of distinct controllers for specific skills, such as standing, walking, manipulation, or mimicry. While effective in isolated tasks, these specialized controllers often exhibit limited versatility, are difficult to extend, and rarely facilitate seamless reuse across different applications.

Current research has explored several avenues to address these limitations. In motion tracking for simulated humanoids, methods commonly utilize reinforcement learning (RL) with tracking rewards over fully specified target motions. Examples include works like PHC, ASE, and VMP. These often focus on predefined types of input trajectory sparsity, and adapting to novel sparsity specifications may necessitate retraining. Furthermore, discrepancies between simulated and physical environments, such as differences in environmental physics or unrealistic joint definitions in simulated characters, can hinder direct transfer to real hardware. Some approaches, such as Versatile Motion Priors (VMP), aim to learn latent motion embeddings from unstructured human motion to generalize to diverse behaviors on real humanoids, indicating a focus on bridging the simulation-to-reality gap.

In the domain of robust locomotion control, significant progress has been made, particularly for quadrupedal robots and bipedal platforms like Cassie and Digit. These advancements demonstrate enhanced stability and agility across various terrains and under varying loads. However, managing transitions between different modes of locomotion, such as standing and walking, or integrating locomotion with other whole-body capabilities, has remained an area of active research. Controllers like SaW have addressed integrated standing and walking, but they do not extend to full whole-body control encompassing manipulation or mimicry.

Mimicry for humanoids has also seen advancements, with efforts focusing on replicating human motion using optimized trajectories and multi-stage training setups for sim-to-real transfer. While some mimicry-based controllers perform whole-body actions effectively, their performance can be constrained by the types of motions present in their training datasets and may lack robust joystick-style locomotion capabilities crucial for loco-manipulation tasks. Upper-body motion tracking has been explored as a partial-body control modality, often combined with joystick-based lower-body control. However, these systems may not handle full-body mimicry, standing, or automatically generate optimal arm swings for locomotion.

A common limitation across these disparate areas is the prevalent focus on a single input modality or the necessity of switching between multiple specialized controllers or using complex interpreters to generate whole-body motions. This fragmentation adds complexity, restricts interoperability, and impedes application development. Recent concurrent work, such as HOVER, has begun to explore unified policy architectures capable of handling multiple control modes without explicit switching.

This research introduces the Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC), which aims to provide a unified representational interface for multi-modal humanoid control. By representing target behaviors as future pose trajectories with optional masking, the MHC seeks to accommodate diverse inputs—ranging from velocity-based walking to arm-only imitation, whole-body imitation, and combined loco-manipulation tasks—within a single framework. This approach aligns with the objective of developing a general-purpose controller that can generalize across different input modalities and seamlessly integrate various control modes, thereby addressing the fragmentation and complexity inherent in existing multi-controller systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a unified, learned whole-body controller for real-world humanoid robots that can interpret and execute diverse behaviors through a simple yet expressive interface: the specification of masked target trajectories over selected subsets of the robot’s state variables.

The motivation for this research stems from several critical challenges and limitations observed in the current state of humanoid robotics control:

- **Lack of Versatility in Traditional Controllers:** Conventional approaches often rely on meticulously engineered, specialized controllers for individual skills (e.g., standing, walking, manipulation, mimicry). While effective in isolation, these controllers exhibit limited versatility, are difficult to extend as new capabilities are required, and rarely support seamless reuse across different tasks or scenarios. This necessitates significant manual effort for each new behavior.
- **Absence of a Unified Input Representation:** Different tasks and input modalities (e.g., high-level velocity commands, end-effector targets, video demonstrations, motion capture data, partial-body trajectories) typically require distinct processing pipelines. This fragmentation complicates application development, restricts the interoperability of different control modes, and introduces barriers to transferring policies from simulation to real hardware. A unified representation is seen as crucial to overcome this.
- **Need for Multi-Modal Input Handling:** A truly versatile controller should be capable of comprehending and executing behaviors specified through various input modalities, including combinations of these, such as concurrent velocity commands for locomotion and target poses for manipulation or upper-body mimicry. Existing systems often struggle with integrating such diverse inputs simultaneously without resorting to complex switching mechanisms or interpreters.
- **Robustness and Adaptability Requirements:** Humanoid robots operating in real-world environments must be robust to dynamic command updates, noisy or inexact input signals, inaccuracies in simulation parameters (sim-to-real gap), and external disturbances. Controllers need to maintain balance and stability while executing commands under these challenging conditions.
- **Extensibility of Repertoire:** The ability to straightforwardly extend the robot's repertoire of behaviors as new motion examples or task specifications become available, with minimal or no retraining, is a desirable property for practical deployment and ongoing development.
- **Autonomous "Filling in the Blanks":** When only partial control directives are provided (e.g., only upper-body movements or only torso velocity), the controller should autonomously generate physically plausible and stable whole-body motions, "filling in the blanks" for unspecified joints while maintaining overall balance and naturalness.

By addressing these motivations, the Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC) aims to provide a foundational control system that simplifies the commanding of complex whole-body behaviors, enhances robot autonomy, and facilitates more flexible human-robot interaction and task execution in real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC) is a learned whole-body controller trained via reinforcement learning (RL) in a simulated environment using the PPO algorithm and the MuJoCo physics engine with a Digit V3 humanoid model. The core approach revolves around a unified representational interface: masked target trajectories over selected subsets of the robot’s state variables.

**Problem Formulation:**
A motion is defined as a sequence of poses `q_1:H` for a humanoid. Each pose `q_i` includes joint angles, joint angular velocities, root planar linear velocities, turn rate, and Euler base orientation (x, y, height). A motion directive `d` is formulated as a masked motion sequence `d = (ˆq_1:H, I_1:H)`, where `ˆq` is the masked motion and `I` is a sequence of binary masks indicating which dimensions of the pose are specified (unmasked) and which are not (masked). Masked dimensions in `ˆq` are set to zero. The MHC receives the current humanoid proprioceptive state `s_t` and a target motion directive `d`, outputting PD setpoints for all robot motors, `a_t = π(s_t, d)`. The objective is to align future humanoid motion with the directive while maintaining stability and robustness.

**Motion Data Generation:**
A diverse set of reference motions is compiled from:
- Human motion capture (MoCap) datasets (AMASS, Reallusion).
- Video demonstrations.
- Optimized trajectories designed using a spring-mass model of Digit.
These motions are then retargeted to the Digit’s kinematic model using an Inverse Kinematics (IK) procedure based on Drake and the SNOPT solver. This process formulates a nonlinear program (NLP) to find a generalized position vector, incorporating critical kinematic feasibility requirements (e.g., stance foot locking, closed kinematic chain) as constraints, and stylistic targets (e.g., torso pose, hand positions) as costs. Frames that do not yield feasible solutions are discarded, and linear interpolation generates the final time-parameterized trajectories. The authors acknowledge discrepancies between human MoCap data and Digit's joint structure (e.g., 3D spherical vs. 1D rotational joints) as a source of potential challenges.

**Network Architecture:**
The MHC employs a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent neural network.
- **Input:** The current proprioceptive state `s_t` (joint positions/velocities, torso orientation in quaternion form) concatenated with a directive embedding.
- **Directive Encoder:** A single-layer feed-forward network processes the directive `d_i = (ˆq_i, I_i)` to produce a 160-dimensional embedding.
- **LSTM Block:** This embedding and `s_t` are fed into an LSTM block with two 64-unit recurrent layers to process temporal information.
- **Output Layer:** A final linear decoding layer outputs an offset for each of Digit V3’s 20 actuated joints.
- **Action Calculation:** The action `a_t` (PD setpoints) is computed by adding this offset to the actuated joint values specified in `ˆq_i`. For masked joints in `ˆq_i` (which are zero), the offset directly provides the PD setpoint.
The MHC operates at 50Hz, generating PD setpoints for a low-level PD controller running at 2kHz.

**Training Episode Generation:**
Each training episode begins with the robot in a standing position.
- A random command window length `w` is generated.
- A random directive `d` is sampled based on the current curriculum stage.
- The MHC executes this directive for `w` steps.
- This process of sampling new `w` and `d` continues until the robot falls or reaches the maximum episode length `e` (which varies by curriculum stage).
- Switching between multiple random directives within an episode facilitates learning smooth transitions between motion types.
- To enhance robustness, torso perturbations are applied during episode execution, with their distribution also dependent on the curriculum stage.

**Curriculum Stages:**
A three-stage curriculum is implemented for progressive skill acquisition:
1. **Stage 1 - Locomotion:** Focuses on basic balance and locomotion. Episodes primarily use randomized locomotion directives, masking all variables except root motion (planar linear velocities `v`, turn rate `w`, and base orientation `b`). Instantaneous torso perturbations (80-800N) are applied with 1% probability per policy step. Episodes are 300 steps (6 seconds) with command window `w ∈`.
2. **Stage 2 - Stability:** Builds on Stage 1 to enhance stability during locomotion. Continuous torso perturbations (20-150N) are applied over windows of 20-50 policy steps. Episode length increases to 800 policy steps (16 seconds), and `w ∈` to promote recovery.
3. **Stage 3 - Whole-Body Directives:** Trains the MHC to follow both fully and partially specified directives.
 - **Fully-specified:** No masking, requiring whole-body imitation.
 - **Partially-specified:**
 1. Locomotion commands only (e.g., joystick input).
 2. Upper-body motion only (e.g., standing with VR/video imitation).
 3. Upper-body target motions with locomotion commands (e.g., rich VR controller).
 The controller learns to generate coherent whole-body motions, filling in unspecified components. Episode length remains 800 steps, `w ∈`.

**Domain Randomization:**
Dynamics randomization is applied throughout all curriculum stages to facilitate sim-to-real transfer. This includes randomizing joint damping, link masses, center of mass positions, encoder noise, and ground friction, using ranges established in prior work on Digit locomotion.

**Reward Design:**
The reward function encourages stability, robustness, and tracking of unmasked joints. It builds upon previous work (van Marum et al. 2024) by incorporating:
- `r_task`: Rewards command following for locomotion directives.
- `r_style`: Encourages natural body postures for standing and walking.
- `r_reg`: Penalizes excessive joint motion and high torques for smooth control.
- **`r_track` (New Component):** `exp(-1.5 · ||(θ − ˆθ_i) · I_i||)` – specifically rewards tracking of unmasked joints in the directive.
The overall reward structure depends on the directive:
- If lower-body joints are masked (e.g., pure locomotion, loco-manipulation with upper-body tracking), `r = r_task + r_style + r_reg + r_track`.
- If lower-body joints are *not* masked (e.g., fully-specified whole-body imitation), `r = r_task + r_reg + r_track`. The `r_style` component is omitted in this case to prevent conflicts with explicit lower-body target directives.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC) was conducted through simulated benchmarking and real-world demonstrations on the Digit V3 robot, assessing its performance across various input modalities and under different training conditions.

**Motion Directive Dataset:**
A dataset of 75 diverse kinematic motion trajectories, generated via IK retargeting from MoCap (AMASS, Reallusion), video demonstrations, and optimized Digit-specific motions, was used. These motions were divided into three sets (setA, setB, setC) with increasing levels of perceived difficulty, primarily for evaluating generalization capabilities.

**Experimental Setup and Metrics:**
Evaluations covered partial/whole-body mimicry, walking, and standing with perturbations. Key metrics included:
- **Mean End-Effector Positional Error (E_MEPE):** L2 norm of positional error for end effectors (hands, elbows, knees, feet) relative to the torso, compared to the directive.
- **Root Drift (Root_∆):** Mean L2 norm of root position drift from its commanded position.
- **Failure Rate (Fail %):** Percentage of episodes resulting in the robot falling.

**Comparison to Baselines:**
1. **Locomotion Performance:** The MHC was compared against the state-of-the-art Stand-and-Walk (SaW) controller (van Marum et al. 2024) on tasks involving walking commands and standing under torso perturbations.
 - **Walking:** MHC demonstrated lower Root_∆ (0.413m for 1m/s x-velocity, 0.343m for 0.5m/s y-velocity) compared to SaW (0.803m, 0.388m respectively), indicating more accurate command following.
 - **Standing under Perturbations:** For a 50N torso force, MHC (Root_∆ 0.256m) had higher drift than SaW (0.109m). However, for a 100N force, MHC (0.311m) exhibited less drift than SaW (0.452m). The authors characterized MHC's locomotion and standing capabilities as competitive with SaW.
2. **Locomotion + Upper-Body Mimicry:** The MHC (trained on setA) was compared against two baselines for combined locomotion and upper-body mimicry:
 - **OffsetBlind:** Tracks only locomotion; upper-body IK offsets are sent directly to PD controllers without influencing locomotion planning.
 - **OffsetAware:** Similar to OffsetBlind but receives upper-body offsets as input, allowing it to adapt locomotion.
 - MHC significantly outperformed both baselines across all metrics:
 - **Failure Rate:** MHC achieved a 0.450% failure rate, substantially lower than OffsetBlind (49.213%) and OffsetAware (4.800%).
 - **E_MEPE:** MHC had the lowest error (0.098m) compared to OffsetBlind (0.156m) and OffsetAware (0.227m).
 - **Root_∆:** MHC showed the least root drift (0.141m) compared to OffsetBlind (0.168m) and OffsetAware (0.203m).
 - The improvement of OffsetAware over OffsetBlind indicated the utility of explicitly incorporating anticipated upper-body motions into the controller’s input.

**Evaluation of Generalization Performance:**
Three MHC models (MHC_A, MHC_AB, MHC_ABC) were trained on progressively larger subsets of the motion data (setA; setA & setB; all three sets).
- **Generalizability:** All models performed consistently well on their respective training datasets. Critically, MHC_A and MHC_AB demonstrated the ability to generalize to motions in datasets they were not directly trained on (e.g., MHC_A on setB/setC, MHC_AB on setC), albeit with higher error metrics compared to performance on their trained sets.
- **Performance with Larger Datasets:** A trend of increasing performance (lower E_MEPE and Root_∆) was observed as the training dataset size grew. For instance, MHC_AB often outperformed MHC_A on setA, and MHC_ABC achieved the best results on setB and setC, suggesting that performance can improve with additional training data.

**Curriculum Results:**
A comparison between the MHC (trained with the three-stage curriculum) and MHC_NC (trained without any curriculum on the full set of directives) revealed the effectiveness of the curriculum design.
- **Walking and Mimicry:** MHC clearly outperformed MHC_NC in walking (significantly lower Root_∆) and mimicry tasks (lower E_MEPE, Root_∆, and Fail %). For example, MHC's walking Root_∆ was 0.413m (1m/s x) compared to MHC_NC's 1.346m.
- **Standing Stability:** MHC_NC exhibited slightly lower Root_∆ for standing under 50N and 100N perturbations (0.189m and 0.234m, respectively) compared to MHC (0.256m and 0.311m). This suggests a trade-off where MHC_NC’s enhanced standing stability came at the cost of aligning less effectively with command directives in other tasks.

**Sim-to-Real Results:**
Qualitative and quantitative results were obtained on the real-world Digit V3 humanoid robot.
- **Qualitative Demonstrations:** The MHC successfully executed diverse real-world behaviors, including joystick-controlled locomotion, whole-body mimicry for a boxing jab, and complex loco-manipulation involving moving to a location, picking up a box, moving while holding it, and placing it.
- **Quantitative Evaluation:** For 20 motions in setA (3 repetitions each), the real-world failure rate was 5%, and the E_MEPE was 0.117m. While these metrics were higher than those observed in simulation (e.g., 0.450% fail rate, 0.098m E_MEPE), they indicate that the approach successfully achieved non-trivial sim-to-real transfer for complex, multi-modal control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research on the Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC) presents several significant contributions to the field of humanoid robotics, with potential for substantial impact:

- **Unified Multi-Modal Control Interface:** The introduction of "masked target trajectories" as a unified interface is a key development. This abstraction allows a single learned controller to process and execute diverse input modalities—including optimized trajectories, motion capture clips, re-targeted video, and real-time joystick signals—without requiring specialized controllers or complex interpreters for each. This simplifies the design and implementation of high-level control systems for humanoids, enhancing development efficiency and reducing system complexity.
- **Enhanced Versatility and Adaptability:** The MHC demonstrates the ability to robustly handle a wide range of whole-body behaviors, from precise locomotion to full and partial body mimicry, while maintaining balance and rejecting disturbances. Its capability to "fill in the blanks" for unspecified joints in partially masked directives allows for flexible command generation and adaptation to various task requirements where full state specification might be impractical or unavailable.
- **Robustness and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Through a carefully designed training curriculum that incorporates domain randomization and force perturbations, the MHC exhibits robustness to dynamic inputs and external disturbances. The successful, albeit with some performance degradation, transfer of complex, multi-modal behaviors from simulation to the real-world Digit V3 robot validates the practicality of the learned policy and the training methodology. This achievement contributes to bridging the simulation-to-reality gap, a critical challenge in robotics.
- **Scalability with Data:** The observed improvement in performance with larger and more diverse training datasets suggests that the MHC framework is scalable. As more high-quality motion data becomes available, the controller has the potential to continuously expand its repertoire and improve its performance, indicating a robust foundation for future development.
- **Foundation for Complex Human-Robot Interaction and Tasks:** By providing a reliable, multi-modal low-level control interface, the MHC can serve as a foundational component for more sophisticated higher-level robotic applications. This includes advanced teleoperation, intuitive human-robot collaboration, and autonomous execution of complex loco-manipulation tasks that require dynamic integration of movement and interaction with the environment.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The paper identifies clear avenues for future work, such as addressing remaining sim-to-real gaps (potentially by incorporating real-world data), expanding the diversity of training motions and masking patterns, and developing methods to handle inconsistent or impossible input directives. These suggestions provide a roadmap for continued advancements in learned whole-body control for humanoids.

In summary, the MHC represents a step towards creating more autonomous, versatile, and easily programmable humanoid robots by offering a unified and robust control solution that can interpret and execute a broad spectrum of behaviors from varied inputs.
