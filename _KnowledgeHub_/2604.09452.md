# SafeAdapt: Provably Safe Policy Updates in Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> SafeAdapt introduces a framework for deep reinforcement learning that enables policies to adapt to new tasks while provably preserving safety on previously learned tasks. The method consistently maintains a perfect critical-state safety rate of 1.00 +- 0.00 on source tasks, even as unconstrained or regularization-based baselines experience degradation as low as 0.88 +- 0.00, while achieving competitive performance on new tasks.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning (RL) agents need to continually adapt in non-stationary environments, but policy updates can lead to catastrophic forgetting of safety constraints from prior tasks.
- Existing safe RL methods often provide only probabilistic safety guarantees or require expensive post-hoc verification, lacking formal a priori assurances in parameter space.
- Standard continual learning techniques, like regularization, mitigate catastrophic forgetting but do not offer formal guarantees specifically for safety preservation, allowing strong reward signals to override safety.

## Method

- Defines a "Rashomon set" in policy parameter space, representing a certified region where all policies are guaranteed to be safe on a source task based on a distribution-independent safety constraint.
- Computes this certified safe region by maximizing its volume using a sound, differentiable safety surrogate in conjunction with Interval Bound Propagation (IBP) to establish lower bounds on safety.
- Integrates policy adaptation with projected gradient descent, constraining parameter updates to remain within the certified safe region to ensure continuous, provable safety throughout the learning process.

## Results

- SafeAdapt consistently achieved a perfect critical-state safety rate of 1.00 +- 0.00 on source tasks across all tested environments, demonstrating complete safety retention.
- In contrast, baselines (UnsafeAdapt, EWC) exhibited significant safety degradation, with critical-state safety rates as low as 0.88 +- 0.00 and trajectory safety rates as low as 0.10 +- 0.30 on source tasks.
- The method maintained competitive or optimal performance on downstream tasks, matching or outperforming baselines in smaller environments and showing only a modest performance drop on the largest environments while ensuring safety.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formal safety guarantees for RL policy updates can be achieved a priori by certifying regions in parameter space, shifting from reactive runtime intervention to proactive policy assurance.
> - A differentiable safety surrogate combined with Interval Bound Propagation (IBP) provides a practical way to compute provably safe parameter bounds (Rashomon sets) for deep neural network policies.
> - Catastrophic forgetting of safety can be rigorously prevented by explicitly projecting policy parameters onto a pre-certified safe region during adaptation, rather than relying on soft regularization penalties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge at the intersection of deep reinforcement learning (RL), continual learning, and AI safety. Deep RL has demonstrated capabilities in various complex tasks, from game playing to robotics and autonomous driving. However, its deployment in safety-critical domains necessitates formal guarantees regarding policy safety. Simultaneously, real-world environments are often non-stationary, requiring agents to continually adapt to evolving objectives, drifting dynamics, or new tasks.

The existing landscape presents several limitations when considering safe policy adaptation in continual learning settings. Many current safe RL methods focus on ensuring safety during training for a single task but do not explicitly address safety preservation when a learned policy is subsequently updated for new tasks. Continual learning techniques, particularly regularisation-based approaches like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), aim to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by discouraging parameter drift but typically lack formal guarantees for safety preservation. Other approaches, such as shielding or action-space filtering, enforce safety at runtime by overriding unsafe actions. While effective for preventing immediate hazards, they do not certify that the adapted policy itself remains safe in parameter space and may mask rather than prevent safety forgetting. Furthermore, safety-aware transfer and policy update methods often provide only probabilistic safety guarantees and necessitate expensive a posteriori verification.

This work contributes to the area by introducing an a priori, parameter-space certification approach for preserving source-task safety during downstream policy adaptation. It leverages neural network verification techniques to define a region of certified safe parameters, thereby offering a formal, provable guarantee that policies updated within this region maintain their safety properties on previously learned tasks. This positions the work as an advancement in enabling the deployment of RL agents in dynamic, safety-critical environments where both adaptation and robust safety guarantees are crucial.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for updating deep reinforcement learning policies in continual learning settings while formally and provably preserving safety properties on previously encountered tasks. The motivation for this work arises from several key challenges and shortcomings of existing approaches:

- **Non-Stationary Environments and Evolving Objectives:** Deployed RL agents frequently encounter environments where dynamics change, performance goals evolve, or new tasks emerge. This necessitates policy updates, which introduce a tension between improving performance on new tasks and retaining safety on old ones.
- **Catastrophic Forgetting of Safety:** Standard continual learning methods, particularly regularisation-based ones, attempt to prevent catastrophic forgetting of prior knowledge. However, they typically offer only soft penalties against parameter drift, which a strong reward signal from a new task can override. This can lead to a significant degradation or complete forgetting of safety constraints on the original task, without providing any formal guarantees that such degradation will not occur.
- **Lack of Formal a priori Safety Guarantees:** Many safe RL methods provide probabilistic guarantees or require post-hoc verification, meaning safety is checked *after* an update, often through expensive evaluations. Other methods, like shielding, intervene at runtime to correct unsafe actions but do not certify the underlying policy's safety in parameter space. The authors identify a gap in providing *a priori* (before deployment or update) and *provable* guarantees for policy safety directly in the parameter space.
- **Need for Robust Deployment in Safety-Critical Domains:** For applications such as autonomous vehicles, medical systems, or industrial control, informal or probabilistic safety assurances are often insufficient. Formal, deterministic guarantees (in finite settings) are a prerequisite for real-world deployment.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes SafeAdapt, an approach designed to:
- Formulate the concept of a "Rashomon set" in policy parameter space, defining a region where all policies are certified to meet specific safety constraints on the source task.
- Develop a method for computing these certified Rashomon sets using a sound, differentiable safety surrogate and Interval Bound Propagation (IBP), ensuring that any policy within this set satisfies the source-task safety specification under greedy deployment.
- Integrate this certification into the policy update process by combining standard RL algorithms (e.g., PPO) with projected gradient descent, which constrains parameter updates to remain within the certified safe region. This aims to provide formal, provable safety guarantees throughout the adaptation process.

By achieving these objectives, SafeAdapt seeks to enable RL agents to adapt to new tasks effectively while rigorously preventing the catastrophic forgetting of safety requirements from prior tasks, thereby moving closer to the safe deployment of RL in complex, dynamic, and safety-critical environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SafeAdapt method introduces an a priori approach to provably safe policy updates in continual reinforcement learning. It operates in three main phases: (1) Safe Behaviour Demonstration, (2) Certified Safe Region Computation, and (3) Safe Downstream Adaptation. The method’s theoretical underpinnings rely on several assumptions to provide formal guarantees.

#### 3.1. Algorithm Overview

SafeAdapt takes an initial source-task policy, an unsafety labelling function, a global safety specification with its surrogate, and a safety surrogate threshold as input. It outputs a policy adapted to a new task that is provably safe on the source task. The core idea is to first define and certify a safe region in policy parameter space and then constrain all subsequent policy updates to remain within this region.

#### 3.2. Phase 1: Safe Behaviour Demonstration

The first phase addresses the challenge of defining a safety constraint in RL, where the state distribution depends on the policy. To overcome this, the method constructs a distribution-independent safety constraint based on an unsafety labelling function, $U_{source}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$. This function identifies state-action pairs $(s, a)$ where taking action $a$ in state $s$ can lead to an unsafe state in the next step ($U(s,a)=1$).
From $U_{source}$, the method identifies:
- **Safety-critical states ($\mathcal{S}_{sc}$):** States where at least one action is unsafe.
- **Safe action set ($\mathcal{A}_{safe}(s)$):** Actions in a given state $s$ that guarantee the next state will be safe.
These definitions are used to form the **safe demonstration dataset ($D_{safe_{source}}$)**, which consists of all safety-critical states paired with their respective safe action sets: $D_{safe_{source}} = \{ (s, \mathcal{A}_{safe}(s)) : s \in \mathcal{S}_{sc} \}$. By enforcing safety for every state in this dataset, the method aims to guarantee safety along any trajectory that an updated policy might take in the source task.

#### 3.3. Phase 2: Certified Safe Region

This phase focuses on computing a **Rashomon set ($\Theta_{safe_{source}}$)**, which is a certified region in parameter space where all policies are guaranteed to be safe on $D_{safe_{source}}$. The Rashomon set is defined as a center-symmetric orthotope $\{ \theta' : \theta_{source} - \alpha \leq \theta' \leq \theta_{source} + \alpha \}$ centered at the source policy's parameters $\theta_{source}$, with half-widths $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^p_{\geq 0}$. The objective is to maximize the volume of this orthotope subject to a safety constraint.

The method distinguishes between a non-differentiable "hard safety specification" ($\phi_{safe}(s)$), which is 1 if the greedy action in state $s$ is safe and 0 otherwise, and a "safety surrogate" ($\tilde{\phi}_{safe}^{\tau}(s)$). The surrogate is defined as the sum of softmax probabilities assigned to safe actions in state $s$, with a temperature parameter $\tau$.

**Key properties of the safety surrogate:**
- **Differentiability:** It is smooth for $\tau > 0$, enabling gradient-based optimization.
- **Consistency:** As $\tau \rightarrow 0^+$, the surrogate approaches the hard safety specification.
- **Soundness (one-sided guarantee):** If $\tilde{\phi}_{safe}^{\tau}(s)$ exceeds a specific threshold (related to the number of safe actions), it implies that the hard safety specification is met ($\phi_{safe}(s)=1$). This means a high surrogate value guarantees safety.
- **Non-completeness:** A low surrogate value does not necessarily imply unsafety, introducing an "uncertainty region."

The method uses a **critical state safety surrogate ($\tilde{\Phi}_{safe}^{\tau,sc}(\pi)$)**, defined as the minimum per-state safety surrogate across all safety-critical states. A crucial implication is that if this global surrogate constraint is satisfied above a specific threshold $\delta^* = M/(1+M)$ (where $M = \max_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{sc}} |\mathcal{A}_{safe}(s)|$), then the global hard safety specification is also satisfied, meaning the policy is safe in all safety-critical states.

The maximal orthotope for the Rashomon set is computed by solving an optimization problem (Equation 7), which maximizes the log-volume of the orthotope subject to the condition that the minimum value of the safety surrogate within the orthotope exceeds the threshold $\delta^*$. This optimization is solved using a primal-dual algorithm in conjunction with **Interval Bound Propagation (IBP)** to compute lower bounds on the safety surrogate over parameter regions.

#### 3.4. Phase 3: Safe Downstream Adaptation

Once the certified safe region $\Theta_{safe_{source}}$ is computed, the downstream policy adaptation proceeds as a constrained optimization problem: $\max_{\theta \in \Theta_{safe_{source}}} J_{down}(\theta)$. Any gradient-based policy optimization method (e.g., PPO) can be used. After each gradient step, the policy parameters are explicitly projected back onto the certified orthotope $\Theta_{safe_{source}}$ using element-wise clipping (Equation 11). This ensures that throughout the adaptation process, the policy parameters always remain within the region guaranteed to be safe for the source task.

#### 3.5. Assumptions and Theoretical Guarantees

The method's safety guarantees rely on four key assumptions:
1. **Finite discrete state-action space:** This enables exhaustive construction of $D_{safe_{source}}$.
2. **Existence of safe actions:** Every safety-critical state must have at least one safe action.
3. **Safe source policy:** The initial source policy must satisfy the safety surrogate constraint.
4. **Greedy action selection:** The agent selects actions greedily at deployment.

Under these assumptions, **Theorem 1 (Provably safe policy updates)** states that if the verification procedure provides a sound lower bound for the safety surrogate such that its minimum value within $\Theta_{safe_{source}}$ exceeds $\delta^*$, then any policy $\pi_{\theta'}$ with parameters $\theta' \in \Theta_{safe_{source}}$ will satisfy the hard critical state safety specification ($\Phi_{safe}^{sc}(\pi_{\theta'}) = 1$). Consequently, if Phase 3 uses projected gradient descent to keep parameters within $\Theta_{safe_{source}}$, then all policy iterates $\theta_t$ will satisfy $\Phi_{safe}^{sc}(\pi_{\theta_t}) = 1$.

**Corollaries** further detail that under these conditions:
- **Per-state certified safety:** For any $\theta' \in \Theta_{safe_{source}}$ and $s \in \mathcal{S}_{sc}$, the greedy action selected by $\pi_{\theta'}$ in state $s$ will be a safe action.
- **Safety preservation throughout adaptation:** All policy parameters $\theta_t$ generated during adaptation remain in $\Theta_{safe_{source}}$, ensuring continuous safety.
- **Distribution-independent source-task safety:** Greedy execution of any adapted policy $\pi_{\theta_t}$ on the source task will never select an unsafe action, irrespective of the initial state distribution.

These guarantees highlight that SafeAdapt provides an explicit, formal, and continuous safety certificate in parameter space, preventing the policy from entering unsafe regions during adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of SafeAdapt was conducted in discrete-state, discrete-action grid-world environments, specifically Frozen Lake and Poisoned Apple, to assess its ability to retain source-task safety while adapting to downstream tasks.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:**
 - **Frozen Lake:** A grid-world navigation task where the agent must reach a goal while avoiding holes. Task 1 and Task 2 differ in hole placement. It is a Task-Incremental Learning (TIL) setting as the task ID is part of the state.
 - **Poisoned Apple:** A custom grid world where the agent collects safe apples (+1 reward) and avoids poisoned apples (-1 reward, unsafe event). Task 1 and Task 2 differ in apple placement. This is a Domain-Incremental Learning (DIL) setting as no explicit task ID is provided.
- **Policies and Baselines:**
 - **Source:** Policy trained only on Task 1, no adaptation.
 - **UnsafeAdapt:** Unconstrained PPO fine-tuning on Task 2.
 - **EWC:** PPO fine-tuning on Task 2 with Elastic Weight Consolidation regularization.
 - **SafeAdapt (ours):** PPO fine-tuning on Task 2 with per-update projection onto the certified Rashomon set.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Critical-state safety rate ($\Phi_{safe}^{sc}(\pi)$):** Fraction of safety-critical states where the policy selects only safe actions.
 - **Trajectory safety rate ($\Phi_{safe}^{traj}(\pi)$):** Fraction of episodes with a fully safe trajectory.
 - **Total reward:** Episodic return.
 - **Success rate:** Task completion rate.
- **Certification:** For all experiments, the source policy was confirmed to be safe on the source-task safety dataset before adaptation. SafeAdapt is the only method that provides a formal a priori guarantee that $\Phi_{safe}^{sc}(\pi)=1$ on the source task after adaptation.

#### 4.2. Results

**Safety Retention in the Source Task (Table 3):**
- **Critical-state safety rate ($\Phi_{safe}^{sc}(\pi)$):** SafeAdapt consistently maintained a perfect critical-state safety rate of 1.00 $\pm$ 0.00 across both Frozen Lake and Poisoned Apple environments. In contrast, UnsafeAdapt and EWC exhibited degradation, with rates as low as 0.88 $\pm$ 0.00 (UnsafeAdapt in Frozen Lake) and 0.93 $\pm$ 0.11 (both UnsafeAdapt and EWC in Poisoned Apple), indicating they forgot how to be safe in some critical states.
- **Trajectory safety rate ($\Phi_{safe}^{traj}(\pi)$):** SafeAdapt also achieved a perfect trajectory safety rate of 1.00 $\pm$ 0.00 in all cases, a consequence of its guarantee in critical states. UnsafeAdapt and EWC showed significantly lower trajectory safety rates in Frozen Lake (0.10 $\pm$ 0.30 and 0.60 $\pm$ 0.49, respectively), implying frequent unsafe trajectories. In Poisoned Apple, all methods maintained 1.00 $\pm$ 0.00 trajectory safety despite critical-state safety degradation in baselines, which might be attributed to environmental factors or the specific definition of unsafe termination.
- **Total Reward (Source Task):** While all adaptation methods showed some degradation in source-task total reward compared to the original Source policy, SafeAdapt maintained competitive performance. For instance, in Frozen Lake, SafeAdapt achieved 0.90 $\pm$ 0.30 compared to EWC's 0.30 $\pm$ 0.46 and UnsafeAdapt's 0.00 $\pm$ 0.00. This suggests that SafeAdapt's safety preservation does not necessarily lead to severe performance compromises on the source task.

**Adaptation to Downstream Task (Table 4):**
- **Total Reward / Success Rate (Downstream Task):**
 - In **Frozen Lake**, all three adaptation methods (UnsafeAdapt, EWC, SafeAdapt) achieved optimal downstream performance (1.00 $\pm$ 0.00 total reward and success rate). This indicates that SafeAdapt's safety constraints did not hinder its ability to adapt and perform well on the new task when the task difficulty allowed.
 - In **Poisoned Apple**, SafeAdapt matched UnsafeAdapt's average total reward (0.96 $\pm$ 0.00) and outperformed EWC (0.86 $\pm$ 0.30). All adaptation methods achieved a 1.00 success rate in this environment.
- These results collectively demonstrate that SafeAdapt can achieve effective downstream adaptation while uniquely preserving source-task safety through its formal guarantees.

**Scalability Analysis (Figure 4, Tables 10 & 11):**
- Experiments on larger Frozen Lake layouts (diagonal_4x4, _6x6, _8x8) revealed a stability-plasticity trade-off.
- SafeAdapt consistently maintained perfect source-task safety ($\Phi_{sc} = 1.00 \pm 0.00$, $\Phi_{traj} = 1.00 \pm 0.00$) across all layout sizes.
- UnsafeAdapt and EWC showed increasing source-task safety degradation (lower $\Phi_{sc}$ and near-zero $\Phi_{traj}$) as the environment size increased, particularly on larger layouts, while achieving optimal downstream performance.
- SafeAdapt remained competitive on the downstream task, achieving optimal performance on 4x4 and 6x6 layouts, with a modest drop only on the 8x8 layout (0.88 $\pm$ 0.33 total reward/success rate compared to 1.00 $\pm$ 0.00 for baselines).
- This suggests that while the current implementation may exhibit a slight performance drop on the largest environments, its ability to maintain perfect safety across scales differentiates it from baselines that sacrifice safety for plasticity.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that SafeAdapt successfully addresses the challenge of provably safe policy updates in continual RL. It prevents catastrophic forgetting of safety constraints by enforcing parameter updates within a certified safe region, a capability not matched by regularization-based or unconstrained adaptation methods. This is achieved while largely retaining competitive performance on new tasks, especially in smaller to medium-sized environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SafeAdapt framework represents a notable advancement in the field of safe reinforcement learning, particularly concerning continual learning in dynamic and safety-critical domains. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Formal and a priori Safety Guarantees:** The most significant contribution is the provision of formal, provable, and *a priori* safety guarantees for source tasks during downstream policy adaptation. Unlike methods that check safety *a posteriori* or rely on runtime interventions (shielding), SafeAdapt certifies safety directly in the policy's parameter space. This shifts the safety assurance from reactive to proactive, providing a higher level of confidence in deployed RL agents. This is crucial for applications where errors could lead to severe consequences, such as autonomous systems, medical robotics, or industrial control.
- **Prevention of Catastrophic Forgetting of Safety:** The research demonstrates that traditional continual learning methods (like EWC) and unconstrained adaptation can lead to catastrophic forgetting of safety properties. SafeAdapt explicitly prevents this by constraining policy updates to a certified safe region, ensuring that the agent never loses its learned safe behaviors on previous tasks. This addresses a critical vulnerability of current continual RL systems in safety-sensitive applications.
- **Enabling Continual Adaptation in Safety-Critical Contexts:** By guaranteeing safety, SafeAdapt facilitates the deployment of RL agents that can continuously learn and adapt to changing environments or new tasks without requiring full retraining or re-verification from scratch. This could accelerate the adoption of RL in real-world systems that demand both flexibility and strict safety compliance.
- **Leveraging Neural Network Verification:** The method effectively integrates neural network verification techniques (specifically, Interval Bound Propagation within the Local Invariant Domain framework) into the RL policy update loop. This demonstrates a practical application of formal verification to dynamic RL scenarios, a challenging area where such integration has been limited.
- **Theoretical Foundation for Safe Policy Space:** The concept of a "Rashomon set" as a certified region of safe policy parameters provides a novel theoretical tool for understanding and managing safety in complex deep learning models. This framework could be extended to other aspects of AI certification beyond safety.

**Potential Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations that also point to important avenues for future research:
- **Discrete State-Action Spaces:** The current formulation is limited to finite, discrete state-action spaces because it relies on exhaustive construction of the safety-critical state dataset to obtain deterministic guarantees. Extending this framework to continuous or very large state-action spaces is a significant challenge.
- **Conservatism of Certification:** The use of Interval Bound Propagation (IBP) for computing certified regions can be conservative, especially for deeper networks or more complex environments. Exploring tighter verification methods (e.g., CROWN, $\alpha/\beta$-CROWN) could lead to larger, less restrictive safe regions, potentially improving downstream plasticity.
- **Scalability of Certified Regions:** While the scalability analysis showed promising results, understanding how certified regions behave in terms of size and shape for much larger environments and across longer sequences of tasks requires further investigation.
- **Probabilistic Safety:** For highly complex or inherently stochastic environments where deterministic guarantees might be too conservative or intractable, integrating probabilistic verification techniques could be a complementary direction.
- **Beyond Single-Step Safety:** The current unsafety labelling function focuses on single-step safety. Deriving the function from synthesized shields could extend the framework to more expressive, multi-step safety specifications.

In conclusion, SafeAdapt offers a robust and theoretically grounded method for addressing safety in continual deep reinforcement learning, providing a critical step towards the trustworthy deployment of AI in safety-critical applications.
