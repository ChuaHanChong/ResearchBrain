# CBF-RL: Safety Filtering Reinforcement Learning in Training with Control Barrier Functions

> [!summary] Summary
> Caltech MCE researchers developed CBF-RL, a framework that integrates a Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based safety filter with barrier-inspired reward shaping directly into reinforcement learning training. This enables policies to internalize safety constraints, allowing real-world deployment on complex robots like the Unitree G1 for tasks such as stair climbing and obstacle avoidance without requiring continuous runtime safety filters.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) often prioritizes performance, leading to unsafe actions and potential catastrophic failures in real-world, safety-critical applications like robotics.
- Existing safety approaches either rely on computationally costly runtime safety filters, which can limit policy learning, or use reward shaping, which lacks direct safety enforcement during training.
- High-dimensional and complex systems such as humanoid robots require robust safety guarantees, where unsafe control inputs can cause significant physical damage or instability.

## Method

- The **CBF-RL** framework introduces a ==dual approach== during RL training, combining a lightweight, closed-form ==Control Barrier Function (CBF)== safety filter with a ==barrier-inspired reward== term.
- A theoretical connection between ==continuous-time CBFs and discrete-time systems== allows for an analytically derived, ==closed-form solution== to the safety filtering problem, avoiding computationally expensive online optimization.
- The safety filter minimally modifies proposed actions to ensure safety, while the reward function provides both an ==intervention penalty== and an incentive for the policy to generate intrinsically safe actions, facilitating ==safety internalization==.

## Results

- Ablation studies in a 2D navigation task showed the "Dual" CBF-RL method achieved **99.0%** success rates without a runtime filter during deployment, successfully internalizing safety, unlike the "Filter-only" approach which degraded significantly (**38.7%** success rate).
- The framework enabled zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating safe obstacle avoidance by autonomously modulating velocities to maintain positive barrier function values.
- On complex stair climbing tasks, policies trained with CBF-RL reliably ascended **0.3m** high stairs without failure, whereas nominal policies frequently stumbled due to safety violations, showcasing internalized safety for high-dimensional locomotion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The integration of both direct action filtering and reward shaping provides the RL policy with comprehensive feedback, enabling it to internalize safety constraints rather than merely having its outputs corrected.
> - The closed-form solution for the CBF safety filter significantly enhances computational efficiency, making it practical for large-scale, parallel RL training environments.
> - Internalized safety leads to policies that can operate safely in real-world environments without the continuous computational overhead of a runtime safety filter, improving robustness and real-time performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated efficacy in developing complex behaviors for robotic systems, including humanoid locomotion. However, a known challenge with standard RL approaches is their tendency to prioritize task performance over safety, which can lead to undesirable or catastrophic outcomes in real-world deployments. Ensuring safety is particularly critical for high-dimensional and complex systems like humanoid robots, where an unsafe action can result in damage to the robot or its environment.

Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) offer a formal method to encode and enforce dynamic safety constraints, traditionally applied online as safety filters. These filters minimally modify a proposed control input to ensure the system remains within a predefined safe set. In the context of RL, two primary approaches exist for integrating CBFs:
1. **Runtime Safety Filtering:** Actions proposed by an RL policy are filtered before execution, guaranteeing safety during deployment. However, this approach necessitates the filter remaining active at runtime, can be computationally expensive (requiring an optimization program at each step), and may prevent the RL policy from internalizing the safety constraints, potentially leading to overly conservative behaviors or hindering the discovery of novel, efficient actions.
2. **Reward Shaping:** Safety-related terms are incorporated into the reward function to penalize unsafe states or actions, guiding the policy towards safer behaviors without explicit filtering. While this encourages safety, it does not directly guarantee safe actions during training and can be sensitive to the chosen penalty weights, potentially being insufficient for safety-critical applications.

This paper positions itself as a synthesis of these two approaches, proposing a method that leverages both safety filtering and reward shaping *during training*. The aim is to overcome the limitations of each standalone method, specifically to enable the RL policy to *internalize* safety constraints, allowing for safe deployment without the continuous need for an online safety filter. The work also contributes to addressing the computational burden of traditional CBF-based filtering by providing a closed-form solution for a specific class of CBF-QPs, making it more amenable to massively parallel RL training environments. Furthermore, it explores the robustness of such policies under uncertainty, leveraging domain randomization rather than explicit robust control techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, termed CBF-RL, that enables reinforcement learning policies to generate safe behaviors by incorporating Control Barrier Functions during the training phase. The specific motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Internalizing Safety Constraints:** A core motivation is to move beyond runtime safety guarantees, where a filter constantly intervenes, towards policies that *learn* and *internalize* safety constraints. This means the trained policy should inherently propose safe actions at deployment, eliminating the need for an active, computationally intensive safety filter during operation. This is particularly relevant for high-dimensional systems like humanoid robots with tight real-time and computational constraints.
2. **Minimally Modifying RL Policies for Safety:** The framework aims to integrate safety mechanisms with minimal disruption to the nominal RL policy learning process. This involves encoding safety constraints through a CBF term that guides policy learning rather than replacing the core RL objective.
3. **Enabling Safe Exploration and Faster Convergence:** Traditional safety filtering can aggressively prune the exploration space, potentially slowing down training or preventing the discovery of optimal behaviors. Reward shaping alone might be too weak to prevent catastrophic failures during exploration. The proposed dual approach seeks to prevent catastrophic unsafe actions with an active filter while simultaneously biasing the policy towards avoiding safety interventions via rewards, thereby fostering safer exploration and potentially accelerating convergence by providing clearer learning signals.
4. **Providing Theoretical Justification for Continuous-to-Discrete CBF Application:** RL environments are discrete-time, while much of CBF theory is formulated in continuous time. A key objective is to formally analyze and prove the relationship between continuous-time CBF conditions and discrete-time policy rollouts, justifying the use of continuous-time CBF tools in discrete RL simulations. This theoretical grounding facilitates a lightweight, closed-form solution for the safety filter.
5. **Demonstrating Practical Efficacy on Complex Systems:** The research aims to validate the CBF-RL framework not only in simplified simulated environments (e.g., 2D navigation) but critically, on high-dimensional, complex robotic platforms like humanoid robots (Unitree G1). This includes demonstrating safe obstacle avoidance and stair climbing in real-world settings with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, showcasing the framework's robustness and generality.
6. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Methods:** The work is motivated by overcoming the shortcomings of:
 - **Runtime safety filters:** Computational cost, lack of internalization, and potential for conservative behavior.
 - **Reward shaping alone:** Insufficient guarantee of safety during training, sensitivity to penalty weights, and slower learning.
 - **Model-based approaches:** Requirement for accurate dynamics models, which are often unavailable or complex for high-dimensional robots.
 - **Robust CBFs:** Added complexity and computational burden despite improving reliability under uncertainty.

By achieving these objectives, CBF-RL seeks to provide a pragmatic and effective method for developing safe and robust RL policies for real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CBF-RL framework proposes a dual approach to integrating Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) into Reinforcement Learning (RL) training, combining active safety filtering with CBF-inspired reward shaping. The methodology involves theoretical analysis for continuous-to-discrete safety, practical implementation via a closed-form QP solver for filtering, and a modified reward structure.

**Theoretical Foundation: Continuous-to-Discrete Safety**
The paper first establishes a theoretical link between continuous-time CBFs and discrete-time RL environments.
1. **Lemma 1:** For a C¹ CBF `h` on a continuous-time single integrator system (`˙q = v`), if a locally Lipschitz controller `k_s` satisfies the continuous-time CBF condition (`∇h(q)ᵀk_s(q) ≥ -αh(q)`), then its Euler discretization (`q_k+1 = q_k + Δt v_k`) also bounds the evolution of `h`. Specifically, `h(f_Δt(q, k_s(q))) ≥ (1 - Δt α)h(q) - |R(q, Δt k_s(q))|`, where `R` is a remainder term that vanishes faster than `Δt` as `Δt → 0`.
2. **Theorem 1:** Extends Lemma 1 to show that if a compact, forward-invariant set exists for the discrete-time system, the CBF value `h(q_k)` is lower-bounded by `(1 - Δt α)ᵏh(q_0) - μ(Δt)/(Δt α)`. Crucially, `lim_{Δt→0} μ(Δt)/Δt = 0`, implying that for sufficiently small `Δt` (common in physics engine simulations), continuous-time CBF tools can be directly applied to discrete-time RL environments with negligible error. This justification allows the use of simpler continuous-time CBF conditions for discrete-time safety filtering.

**Practical Implementation of CBF-RL**
The framework integrates two main components during RL training:

1. **Safety-Filtering during Training (Active Filtering):**
 - At each training step `k`, the nominal RL policy `π_θ` proposes an action `v_policy_k`.
 - Instead of directly executing `v_policy_k`, a safety filter is applied. Based on the continuous-to-discrete theoretical link, the filtering problem is formulated as a single linear-constraint Quadratic Program (QP) derived from the continuous-time CBF condition:
 `v_safe_k = argmin_v_k ||v_k - v_policy_k||²`
 `s.t. ∇h(q_k)ᵀv_k ≥ -αh(q_k)`
 - A key aspect is that this QP has a closed-form analytical solution (Equation 20), which avoids the computational cost of numerical QP solvers at every step. This makes the filtering process lightweight and suitable for massively parallel simulation environments.
 - The robot then executes `v_safe_k` in the environment.

2. **Penalizing Unsafe Behavior (Reward Shaping):**
 - A dedicated safety reward term, `r_cbf(q_k, v_k)`, is added to the nominal task reward (`r_nominal`).
 - `r_cbf` has two components (Equation 22, 23):
 - `min(a_kᵀv_policy_k - b_k, 0)`: A linear penalty term that is active when the policy's proposed action `v_policy_k` violates the CBF condition (i.e., `a_kᵀv_policy_k < b_k`). This term directly penalizes unsafe *proposed* actions, even if the filter prevents their execution.
 - `exp(-||v_policy - v_safe||²/σ²) - 1`: An exponential penalty term that incentivizes the policy to propose actions `v_policy` that are as close as possible to the `v_safe` actions determined by the filter. This encourages the policy to reduce the intervention of the safety filter. The term is lower-bounded by -1, ensuring numerical stability.
 - The total reward used for policy updates is `r = r_nominal + r_cbf`.

**RL Algorithm and Environment:**
- The framework is designed to be model-free and agnostic to the specific RL algorithm, though Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used in the experiments.
- Training is conducted in massively parallel environments (IsaacLab) with domain randomization to enhance robustness.
- During deployment, the policy directly outputs actions (`v_policy_cbf`) without an active safety filter, as safety is expected to be internalized.

**Experimental Validation:**

1. **Single Integrator Navigation Task:**
 - **Dynamics:** 2D point robot with `q_k+1 = q_k + v_k Δt`.
 - **Safety Function `h(q)`:** Defined as the minimum distance to obstacles (circles) and world boundaries (walls), `h(q) = min(min_j(||q - p_j|| - (r_agent + r_j)), x - r_agent..., L - y - r_agent)`.
 - **Ablation Studies:** Four variants (Dual, Reward-only, Filter-only, Nominal) were trained and evaluated on task completion rates and robustness, both with and without runtime filtering during deployment.
 - **Robustness Tests:** Policies were tested with added noise to dynamics (`q_k+1 = q_k + (v_k + d)Δt`) to assess generalization under uncertainty, using domain randomization during training for the dual approach.

2. **Humanoid Locomotion Tasks (Unitree G1 Robot):**
 - **Platform:** Unitree G1 humanoid robot, trained in IsaacLab with 4096 parallel environments (`Δt = 0.005s`), then deployed with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance:**
 - **Reduced-Order Model:** Planar base velocity `v_base_planar = [v_x, v_y]`.
 - **Safety Function `h(p)`:** Distance from the robot to the closest obstacle (approximated as a cylinder), `h(p) = ||p_r_o|| - R_r - R_o`.
 - **CBF Reward:** `r_obstacle_cbf` uses the planar velocity and its projection with respect to the obstacle.
 - **Stair Climbing:**
 - **Reduced-Order Model:** Swing foot position `q_sw = [p_x, p_y]^T` with dynamics `q_sw_k+1 = q_sw_k + Δt J_sw(q_sw_k)v_sw_k`.
 - **Safety Function `h(q)`:** Distance of the swing foot to a hyperplane tangent to the next stair riser, `h(q) = p_stair_x - p_x`.
 - **CBF Reward:** `r_next_stair_cbf` is defined based on the swing foot's velocity and the stair hyperplane.
 - **Hardware Deployment:** Experiments on a Unitree G1 robot involved navigating an obstacle course and climbing various indoor and outdoor stairs, assessing real-world performance without a runtime filter. Asymmetric actor-critic was used for blind stair climbing, with critic observation including height scans.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings across both simulated and hardware experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness of the CBF-RL framework.

**Single Integrator Navigation Task:**

1. **Faster Convergence and Safety During Training:** Ablation studies showed that the "Dual" (reward + filter) and "Filter-only" approaches achieved rapid convergence and maintained safety throughout the training process (Fig. 3). In contrast, "Reward-only" and "Nominal" policies exhibited slower convergence and a higher incidence of safety violations during training.
2. **Internalization of Safety for Deployment:** The "Dual" policy, when deployed *without* a runtime safety filter, demonstrated significantly higher success rates (92.7% without domain randomization, 91.7% with domain randomization) compared to the "Filter-only" policy deployed without a runtime filter (38.7% without DR, 36.8% with DR) (Table I, Fig. 4). This indicates that the dual approach successfully enabled the policy to internalize safety constraints, allowing it to propose safe actions autonomously. The "Filter-only" approach, while safe during training, did not internalize safety effectively for filter-free deployment.
3. **Robustness to Dynamics Uncertainty:** When trained with domain randomization (DR) and tested under dynamics disturbance (20% scaled standard normal noise), the "Dual" policy showed minimal degradation in success rate (1% reduction when deployed without a runtime filter). This suggests that the dual training, combined with DR, enhances the robustness of the learned policy to system uncertainties without requiring explicit robust CBF formulations (Table I).

**Humanoid Locomotion (Unitree G1 Robot):**

1. **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies trained purely in IsaacLab using CBF-RL could be directly deployed on the physical Unitree G1 robot without further fine-tuning, exhibiting effective and safe behaviors.
2. **Obstacle Avoidance:** The robot successfully navigated around obstacles approximated as cylinders, even when given velocity commands that would lead to collisions. Visualization of the CBF `h` and velocity plots (Fig. 5) indicated that the robot autonomously modulated its velocities to maintain safe distances, demonstrating the internalization of obstacle avoidance behavior without a runtime filter.
3. **Stair Climbing:**
 - The robot successfully climbed stairs of varying riser heights, tread depths, and roughness in both indoor and outdoor environments (Fig. 5, Fig. 7).
 - For high stairs (0.3m riser height), the dual-trained policy was able to ascend and descend successfully, whereas a nominal policy failed, clipping its feet against the riser and stumbling (Fig. 6). This highlights CBF-RL's ability to learn intricate, safety-critical locomotion patterns.
 - The robot utilized proprioception to infer terrain features and adjust its foot placement and center of mass (e.g., torso pitch angle) accordingly, demonstrating adaptive safe locomotion.
4. **Learned Self-Correction:** In both obstacle avoidance and stair climbing, the robot demonstrated an ability to adapt its actions in real-time based on its learned safety parameters, such as adjusting its swing foot trajectory to avoid toe-stubbing or modifying base velocity to circumvent obstacles.

Overall, the results consistently support the hypothesis that combining training-time CBF filtering with reward shaping allows RL policies to internalize safety, leading to robust and safe behaviors at deployment without the computational overhead of continuous runtime filtering.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CBF-RL framework presents a significant advancement in the field of safe reinforcement learning, with several potential impacts on robotics and autonomous systems:

1. **Bridging the Gap Between Formal Methods and Data-Driven Control:** By formally integrating Control Barrier Functions directly into the RL training loop, CBF-RL offers a principled approach to combine the rigorous safety guarantees of formal methods with the adaptability and learning capabilities of RL. This addresses a critical challenge in deploying RL in safety-critical applications.
2. **Enabling Safe Autonomous Deployment without Runtime Filters:** The ability of trained policies to internalize safety constraints and operate without an explicit runtime safety filter is a major contribution. This eliminates the computational burden and real-time constraints often associated with continuous online QP solving, making CBF-RL policies more suitable for deployment on resource-constrained platforms like humanoid robots. This could accelerate the real-world adoption of RL in domains requiring high assurance.
3. **Improved Training Efficiency and Exploration:** The dual approach, by preventing catastrophic failures during training via active filtering and simultaneously providing informative safety-related rewards, can lead to more stable training, faster convergence, and safer exploration. This can reduce the time and computational resources required to develop robust RL policies.
4. **Enhanced Robustness to Uncertainty:** The empirical demonstration of robustness under dynamics uncertainty, achieved through a combination of the dual training approach and domain randomization, suggests a promising avenue for developing reliable RL policies without relying on complex, explicit robust control techniques. This is particularly valuable for real-world scenarios where precise system models are often unavailable or imperfect.
5. **Applicability to High-Dimensional and Complex Systems:** The successful application of CBF-RL to humanoid locomotion tasks, including obstacle avoidance and challenging stair climbing with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, highlights its potential for complex, high-dimensional robotic systems. This opens possibilities for teaching humanoids and other advanced robots to perform intricate tasks safely in unstructured environments.
6. **Pragmatic Integration with Standard RL Algorithms:** The framework's model-free nature and lightweight integration (closed-form QP solution, reward shaping) with standard policy-gradient RL algorithms like PPO make it accessible and adaptable to existing RL pipelines, facilitating broader adoption by researchers and practitioners.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as automated barrier discovery and perception-based barriers, could further enhance the framework's capabilities and expand its application to whole-body loco-manipulation, addressing even broader humanoid robot challenges. The CBF-RL framework contributes to making RL a more viable and trustworthy tool for developing autonomous systems that can operate safely in the real world.
