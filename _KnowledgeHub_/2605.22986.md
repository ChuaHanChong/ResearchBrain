# Robots That Know What to Ask: Recovering Misaligned Rewards through Targeted Explanations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT developed Ambiguity-Sensitive Querying (ASQ), a framework enabling robots to identify and recover misaligned reward functions caused by underspecified human demonstrations. This method statistically detects unoptimized features, uses an LLM to generate targeted natural language queries, and then integrates subsequent demonstrations with specific attention weights, leading to a normalized reward improvement of 0.021 in a user study with a physical robot.

## Problem

- Human demonstrations for robot learning often underspecify certain task-relevant features due to cognitive load, physical difficulty, or limited training environments.
- Existing Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods typically assume complete supervision from demonstrations, struggling to distinguish genuine human indifference from inadvertent omissions.
- This ambiguity can lead to robots learning misaligned reward functions, resulting in suboptimal or unsafe behaviors during deployment.

## Method

- **ASQ** diagnoses underspecified features by analyzing the ==empirical variance== of feature values across initial demonstrations, inferring which features were not consistently optimized.
- A ==Large Language Model (LLM)== prunes irrelevant features from the candidate set, and the robot generates targeted natural language explanations to query the human about specific uncertain features.
- Reward functions are learned using a ==weighted Maximum Entropy IRL== approach that distinguishes between initial and targeted demonstrations by assigning different ==rationality (attention) vectors== to each set.

## Results

- Simulated experiments showed ASQ consistently outperformed random baselines, achieving higher reward recovery and faster learning when one or two features were initially underspecified.
- A user study with a physical robot demonstrated that ASQ's explanation-guided approach yielded significantly greater normalized reward improvement (**M = 0.021**) compared to unguided or rollout-based methods.
- The LLM-based filtering successfully maintained performance in environments with irrelevant features without increasing cognitive workload for human participants, validating its robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Features actively optimized by a human demonstrator exhibit lower variability across demonstrations, providing a statistical signal to diagnose underspecification.
> - Utilizing an LLM as a prior effectively filters for task-relevant features, allowing the system to focus queries on meaningful dimensions and avoid irrelevant distractions.
> - Modeling human attention shifts by using separate rationality vectors for initial and targeted demonstrations is crucial for accurately recovering the complete underlying reward function.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of learning from demonstrations (LfD), particularly inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), where robots infer a human's reward function or policy from observed behaviors. A fundamental assumption in many LfD approaches is that human demonstrations provide comprehensive supervision over all task-relevant features. However, the paper identifies a significant challenge: demonstrations are frequently imperfect or incomplete. Humans may inadvertently under-emphasize certain features due to cognitive load, physical difficulty, or limitations in the training environment, leading to a phenomenon termed "underspecification."

Existing literature in LfD has explored various methods to address imperfections in demonstrations, such as collecting additional feedback modalities (e.g., pairwise preferences, natural language instructions, physical corrections), modeling demonstrator suboptimality, or quantifying objective misspecification. However, many of these approaches improve learning from a fixed dataset without actively diagnosing which specific features are underspecified or proactively soliciting targeted feedback. Some prior work has focused on characterizing different types of queries a robot can pose to a human, or on communicating what the robot has already learned. This paper distinguishes itself by introducing a framework that not only *detects* specific underspecified features but also *actively queries* the human demonstrator with *targeted, natural language explanations* to resolve this ambiguity, thus moving beyond passive data collection or general corrective feedback. The work thereby contributes to enhancing the robustness and efficiency of reward learning in human-robot interaction settings, particularly where initial demonstrations are prone to ambiguity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, named Ambiguity-Sensitive Querying for Reward Learning (ASQ), that enables robots to recover accurate reward functions even when initial human demonstrations are imperfect and underspecify certain task-relevant features. The authors are motivated by a critical challenge in learning from demonstrations: the inherent ambiguity in interpreting variations in demonstrated behavior. When a feature (e.g., distance to a laptop) varies across demonstrations, it is unclear to the robot whether the human demonstrator genuinely considers that feature unimportant (low reward weight) or simply failed to consistently emphasize it due to various constraints (underspecification). This ambiguity can lead to a robot learning a "misaligned" reward function, where it fails to account for important human preferences, potentially resulting in suboptimal, inefficient, or unsafe behaviors during deployment.

The paper identifies several reasons for this underspecification:
1. **Cognitive Load:** Demonstrating complex tasks is cognitively demanding, making it difficult for humans to attend to all relevant features simultaneously and with equal care.
2. **Physical Difficulty:** Certain features may be physically challenging for the human to consistently optimize during a demonstration.
3. **Limited Training Regime Coverage:** The environment or task setup might not sufficiently expose situations where certain features need to be actively controlled (e.g., an obstacle always positioned far from the robot's path), making it difficult for demonstrators to convey their preferences for these features.

Current IRL approaches often assume that demonstrations provide complete supervision, treating any inconsistency as evidence of low reward weight. This assumption can be flawed. ASQ aims to overcome this by introducing a method to diagnose which features are underspecified and then engaging the human for targeted clarification. The core motivation is to move from passive learning from potentially ambiguous demonstrations to an active, informed querying strategy that elicits precise feedback, ultimately leading to more accurate and robust reward recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ASQ framework is designed to detect underspecified features from initial demonstrations, query the human for targeted feedback, and then integrate this feedback to learn a more accurate reward function. The methodology is grounded in the observation that the consistency (or variability) of feature values across demonstrations provides a statistical signal about whether those features were actively optimized.

**Problem Formulation:**
The environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), and the reward function is parameterized linearly as $R(s) = \theta^T \phi(s)$, where $\phi(s)$ is a $k$-dimensional feature vector and $\theta$ represents reward weights. The probability of a human generating a trajectory $\tau$ is based on a generalized Boltzmann noisily-rational process: $P(\tau | \theta^*, \beta) \propto \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^k \beta_i \theta^*_i \phi_i(\tau)\right)$. Crucially, this model includes a per-feature rationality vector $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}$, where $\beta_i$ modulates the human's attention or "care" for feature $\phi_i$. Underspecification is formalized as $\beta_i \approx 0$ for one or more features, meaning demonstrations provide weak signal for $\theta^*_i$. The goal is to recover the true weights $\theta^*$ by identifying $\phi_{under}$ and eliciting targeted demonstrations $D_{extra}$.

**A. Detecting Underspecified Features:**
1. **Statistical Footprint of Optimization:** The key insight is that features actively optimized by a demonstrator exhibit low variability across demonstrations, while underspecified features tend to vary widely.
2. **Empirical Variance Calculation:** For a set of initial demonstrations $D_{init}$, the empirical variance $\sigma_i^2$ is computed for each feature $\phi_i$ across the trajectories.
3. **Inferring Optimization Status via Bayesian Model Selection:** The process aims to identify which features were not consistently optimized. A binary indicator $o_i \in \{0,1\}$ signifies whether feature $\phi_i$ was consistently optimized ($o_i=1$) or not ($o_i=0$). This is framed as a Bayesian model selection problem, where each hypothesis $\phi_{hyp} \subseteq \phi$ represents a candidate set of underspecified features. The posterior probability $P(\phi_{hyp} | \{\sigma_i^2\})$ is computed based on the likelihood $P(\{\sigma_i^2\} | \phi_{hyp})$ and a prior $P(\phi_{hyp})$. The likelihood factorizes as $P(\{\sigma_i^2\} | \phi_{hyp}) = \prod_{i \in \phi} P(\sigma_i^2 | o_i, \phi_i)$. The set of likely underspecified features $\phi_{under}$ is selected via maximum a posteriori inference.
4. **Filtering to Task-Relevant Features (LLM-Based Prior):** To prevent querying about irrelevant features that naturally have high variance, the method first restricts the candidate set to task-relevant features. A Large Language Model (LLM) (e.g., GPT-5.2) is used to provide a prior over feature subsets, effectively estimating $P(\phi_{hyp})$ by identifying which features a typical human would consider necessary for a given task description. This reduces the hypothesis space and focuses queries on meaningful dimensions.
5. **Constructing Reference Distributions:** To interpret raw variances, reference distributions $P(\sigma_i^2 | o_i=1, \phi_i)$ (optimized) and $P(\sigma_i^2 | o_i=0, \phi_i)$ (non-optimized) are constructed. This involves simulating trajectories under various policies, where some features are intentionally optimized (positive weight) and others ignored (zero weight). Empirical samples of variances from these simulations are then used to fit approximate Chi-squared distributions.

**B. Eliciting Targeted Feedback:**
Once $\phi_{under}$ is identified, the robot generates a natural language explanation informing the demonstrator about the specific features it is uncertain about (e.g., "I am uncertain how to handle distance to laptop"). This explanation is designed to explicitly direct the demonstrator's attention. The user is then asked to provide additional demonstrations ($D_{extra}$) while explicitly considering how the robot should handle these identified features.

**C. Reward Learning:**
Standard IRL methods typically pool all demonstrations and assume a single rationality model. ASQ deviates by recognizing that $D_{init}$ and $D_{extra}$ are generated under different attention regimes.
1. **Separate Rationality Vectors:** The initial demonstrations $D_{init}$ are modeled with $\beta_{init}$, reflecting the human's natural, potentially uneven, attention. The targeted demonstrations $D_{extra}$ are modeled with $\beta_{extra}$, reflecting the explicit guidance provided by the robot.
2. **Assigning Rationality Weights:** For $D_{extra}$, rationality $\beta_{extra,i}$ is set to $\beta_{high}$ for features in $\phi_{under}$ (as the human was asked to attend to them) and $\beta_{low}$ for other features. Conversely, for $D_{init}$, $\beta_{init,i}$ is set to $\beta_{low}$ for features in $\phi_{under}$ (reflecting their initial underspecification) and $\beta_{high}$ for others.
3. **Weighted Maximum Entropy IRL:** The reward weights $\theta^*$ are learned by minimizing a combined negative log-likelihood loss, which is the sum of losses for $D_{init}$ and $D_{extra}$, each calculated with its respective rationality vector ($\beta_{init}$ and $\beta_{extra}$). This approach ensures that the initial demonstrations inform about naturally attended features, while the targeted demonstrations fill in the gaps for initially underspecified features, enabling recovery of the human's complete preferences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ASQ through simulated experiments and a user study with a physical robot.

**A. Simulated Experiments:**
The simulated environment featured a 7-DoF Jaco arm manipulating a coffee cup on a cluttered tabletop. Four primary task-relevant features (laptop distance, table height, human distance, coffee cup orientation) were defined. Experiments used Boltzmann-rational agents as simulated human demonstrators.

1. **Single and Dual Feature Underspecification (4-feature setting):**
 - ASQ consistently outperformed random baselines (Random-Demo, Random-Feat) in recovering the true reward function when one or two task-relevant features were initially underspecified (Figures 3a and 3b).
 - ASQ achieved higher final performance and learned more rapidly with fewer additional targeted demonstrations, approaching the performance of an Oracle that possessed ground-truth knowledge of underspecified features.
 - The "table" feature condition sometimes showed high initial normalized reward, suggesting that performance on this feature could be incidentally high even when not explicitly optimized.

2. **Scaling to Extraneous Features (8-feature setting):**
 - The feature set was augmented with four irrelevant distractor objects (apple, bowl, fork, marker).
 - LLM-based filtering effectively pruned these distractors before variance-based detection, allowing ASQ to focus queries only on task-relevant features.
 - In both single and dual underspecified feature scenarios, ASQ maintained its performance advantage over baselines, demonstrating robustness in environments with irrelevant features (Figures 4a and 4b). The LLM's prior helped avoid querying about features the human genuinely didn't care about.

**B. User Study:**
A user study was conducted with 12 participants using a real Franka Emika FR3 robot arm in a tabletop manipulation task, comparing ASQ's explanation-guided approach against Unguided and Rollout conditions.

1. **H1: Reward Improvement (Supported):**
 - The Explanation condition (where the robot named the underspecified feature) yielded significantly greater normalized reward improvement (M = 0.021) compared to Unguided (M = -0.018) and Rollout (M = -0.016) conditions (F(2, 22) = 14.94, p < .001).
 - This improvement was contingent on the rationality weighting scheme, which assigns demonstration-specific weights based on the assumption of targeted attention. When an ablation study (ASQ-Unweighted) removed this weighting, the improvement diminished.
 - Behavioral analysis of participant demonstrations (Figures 9 and 10) revealed that under the Explanation condition, participants tended to vary their behavior more on un-flagged features, consistent with focusing their attention on the explicitly named underspecified feature. Self-reports further corroborated this shift in attention (Figure 6).

2. **H2: Cognitive Workload (Not Supported):**
 - No significant effect of condition was found on NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) composite scores (F(2, 22) = 0.76, p = .480). Workload was comparable across all conditions.
 - The authors attribute this to the physical demands of kinesthetic guidance remaining constant across conditions, irrespective of attentional guidance.

3. **H3: Subjective Preference (Not Supported):**
 - Participant preferences were split, with no single condition being significantly preferred (5 for Explanation, 5 for Rollout, 2 for Unguided).
 - Qualitative feedback indicated diverse user preferences: some preferred Rollout for providing a concrete behavior to correct, while others preferred Explanation for explicit guidance and reduced cognitive load.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes significant contributions to the field of human-robot interaction and inverse reinforcement learning. The ASQ framework addresses a core limitation in learning from demonstrations: the challenge of disambiguating genuine human indifference towards a task feature from inadvertent underspecification in demonstrations. By leveraging the statistical properties of feature variability, the method offers a novel approach to actively diagnose reward ambiguity, moving beyond passive observation.

The framework's ability to generate targeted, natural language explanations and solicit corrective demonstrations has several potential impacts:
- **Enhanced Reward Recovery:** ASQ demonstrably improves the accuracy and efficiency of reward function learning, especially in scenarios with imperfect initial demonstrations. This can lead to robots that better understand and execute human preferences, resulting in more aligned, safer, and user-acceptable behaviors in practical applications.
- **Reduced Human Effort:** By actively pinpointing and querying about specific uncertain features, ASQ can reduce the overall cognitive load and the number of demonstrations required from a human teacher. Instead of trial-and-error teaching or providing numerous generic demonstrations, users can offer focused, informative feedback.
- **Robustness in Complex Environments:** The integration of LLM-based filtering allows ASQ to operate effectively in environments with numerous features, distinguishing between task-relevant aspects and irrelevant distractors. This is crucial for deploying robots in complex, unstructured real-world settings.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** By giving the robot the capability to "know what to ask," the framework fosters more intelligent and adaptive human-robot teaching interactions. This moves towards a more collaborative learning paradigm where the robot proactively seeks clarification rather than silently making assumptions.

The user study provides crucial validation of the method's transferability from simulation to physical robots and real human demonstrators, although it also highlights the diversity in human learning styles and preferences for feedback modalities. Future work could build upon these findings by exploring dynamic attention modeling, incorporating multimodal user feedback (e.g., natural language alongside demonstrations), and evaluating ASQ in more complex and dynamic environments, potentially leading to a more comprehensive and adaptive robotic learning system.
