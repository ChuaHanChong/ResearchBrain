# Multimodal Diffusion Transformer: Learning Versatile Behavior from Multimodal Goals

> [!summary] Summary
> The Multimodal Diffusion Transformer (MDT) enables robots to learn versatile behaviors from multimodal goal specifications using only sparse language annotations. It achieves state-of-the-art performance on benchmarks like CALVIN and LIBERO, particularly with limited language data, and demonstrates successful transfer to real-world kitchen manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Robot learning from multimodal goals (language, images) is difficult when datasets are sparsely labeled.
- Most existing robot learning approaches require fully annotated data, which is expensive and time-consuming to create.
- The challenge of training robots efficiently from 'play data' with limited language annotations limits broader applicability.

## Method

- Introduces the **Multimodal Diffusion Transformer** (**MDT**), an architecture combining a ==transformer encoder== with a ==diffusion-based policy decoder==.
- The encoder processes visual observations and multimodal goals (language or image) through specialized encoders and a ==transformer block==.
- Incorporates two ==self-supervised learning== techniques: ==Masked Generative Foresight== (==MGF==) for future state prediction and ==Contrastive Latent Alignment== (==CLA==) for goal modality alignment.

## Results

- Achieved approximately **85%** success on the CALVIN benchmark for executing **5** consecutive instructions, outperforming previous methods, especially with limited language annotations.
- Demonstrated a **65%** success rate on the LIBERO benchmark with only **2** labeled demonstrations out of **20**, competitive with methods trained on fully labeled datasets.
- Successfully transferred to real-world scenarios, enabling a robotic arm to complete various kitchen manipulation tasks based on natural language instructions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Masked Generative Foresight (MGF) improves learning by training the model to reconstruct masked future image patches, enabling robust latent feature learning.
> - Contrastive Latent Alignment (CLA) ensures consistency across latent representations conditioned on different goal modalities (language and image) for the same desired behavior.
> - The combination of a multimodal transformer encoder, a diffusion-based policy, and these self-supervised objectives enables effective robot learning from sparsely annotated multimodal play data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Robot learning from multimodal goals remains a significant challenge, particularly when dealing with sparsely labeled datasets. Most existing approaches require fully annotated data, which is expensive and time-consuming to create. The ability to learn from play data with limited language annotations would enable more efficient robot training paradigms.

The Multimodal Diffusion Transformer (MDT) addresses this challenge by introducing a novel framework that learns versatile robot behaviors from multimodal goal specifications while requiring only sparse language labels. The approach combines the strengths of transformer architectures and diffusion models with innovative self-supervised learning techniques to effectively learn from partially labeled datasets.

MDT builds upon recent advances in diffusion models for robot control, which have shown promise in generating diverse and precise action sequences. However, unlike previous methods, MDT is specifically designed to handle multiple goal modalities (language and images) and to learn effectively from sparse data through self-supervised auxiliary objectives.

### 2. Multimodal Diffusion Transformer Architecture

The MDT architecture consists of two main components: a multimodal transformer encoder and a diffusion-based policy decoder.

The encoder processes inputs from multiple sources:
- Image observations from static and wrist cameras
- Multimodal goals (either language instructions or goal images)

These inputs are processed through specialized encoders:
- Visual inputs are encoded using Voltron and Perceiver/ResNet-18 models
- Language goals are encoded using CLIP-text
- Image goals are encoded using CLIP-image

The encoded representations are then combined in a transformer encoder to create latent representation tokens that capture the relevant information for task execution.

The policy decoder is implemented as a score-based diffusion model that iteratively denoises a sequence of future actions. The diffusion process can be described by the following stochastic differential equation:

$$dx = -\frac{1}{2}x\beta(t)dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}dw$$

where $\beta(t)$ is the noise schedule, and $w$ is the standard Wiener process. The model is trained to predict the score function $\nabla_x \log p(x_t|c)$, which is used to denoise the action sequence.

The architecture includes specialized denoising blocks that leverage multi-head self-attention and cross-attention mechanisms to effectively incorporate the encoded state and goal information during the denoising process.

### 3. Self-Supervised Learning Techniques

A key innovation in MDT is the introduction of two self-supervised learning techniques that improve learning from sparse data:

#### 3.1. Masked Generative Foresight (MGF)

MGF is designed to learn latent features that encode sufficient information to predict future states. The process works as follows:

1. Random patches in future image frames are masked
2. The model is trained to reconstruct these masked patches based on the current state and goal
3. A Vision Transformer (ViT) decoder is used to predict the masked patches

The MGF loss is defined as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MGF}} = \mathbb{E}_{i,j,v} \left[ \| \hat{o}_{i+v,j} - o_{i+v,j} \|_2^2 \right]$$

where $\hat{o}_{i+v,j}$ is the predicted patch and $o_{i+v,j}$ is the ground truth patch.

#### 3.2. Contrastive Latent Alignment (CLA)

CLA aims to align the latent representations conditioned on different goal modalities:

1. For a given state, the model generates latent representations conditioned on both language and image goals
2. These representations are pushed closer together if they correspond to the same desired behavior
3. This ensures consistent behavior regardless of the goal modality

The CLA loss uses a contrastive formulation:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CLA}} = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z^{img}, z^{lang})/\tau)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\text{sim}(z^{img}, z^{lang}_k)/\tau)}$$

where $\text{sim}$ is a similarity function and $\tau$ is a temperature parameter.

### 4. Experimental Setup

The MDT approach was evaluated on three different setups:

1. **CALVIN Benchmark**: A tabletop manipulation environment with 24 hours of play data across 4 environments. The evaluation task requires completing 1000 sets of 5 text instructions in sequence.

2. **LIBERO Benchmark**: Contains 5 different benchmark suites with 130 tasks across 5 different scenes. The evaluation includes goal-conditioned, object-oriented, spatial reasoning, and long-horizon tasks.

3. **Real Kitchen Play**: A real-world setup with a robotic arm performing 20 different tasks in a toy kitchen environment, involving articulated body manipulation and long-horizon task completion.

The experiments were designed to evaluate several key aspects:
- Performance with varying amounts of language annotations
- Effectiveness of the self-supervised learning techniques
- Comparison with state-of-the-art baselines
- Transferability to real-world scenarios

### 5. Results and Performance

MDT achieved state-of-the-art performance across all benchmarks, particularly when dealing with sparsely labeled data.

On the CALVIN benchmark, MDT demonstrated superior performance compared to existing methods:
- For the task of executing 5 consecutive instructions, MDT achieved an average success rate of approximately 85%, significantly outperforming previous methods
- The performance advantage was most pronounced when training with limited language annotations

For the LIBERO benchmark, the results show:

- With only 5 demonstrations (1 labeled), MDT achieved a 29% success rate
- With 20 demonstrations (2 labeled), MDT reached a 65% success rate
- This performance is competitive with methods trained on fully labeled datasets, despite using significantly fewer annotations

In real-world experiments, MDT successfully completed various kitchen manipulation tasks, demonstrating its ability to transfer to physical robots. The system was able to understand and execute tasks such as "Move the pot from right to left" and "Open the microwave" based on language instructions.

### 6. Ablation Studies

The researchers conducted several ablation studies to understand the contribution of different components:

The ablation studies revealed:

1. **Impact of Self-Supervised Losses**: Removing either MGF or CLA led to performance degradation, with the full model consistently outperforming the ablated versions across all tasks.

2. **Architectural Design Choices**: Experiments with different noise encoding methods and transformer configurations (shown in Figure 2 below) demonstrated the importance of the multimodal transformer encoder in the overall architecture.

3. **Effectiveness across Model Types**: The self-supervised learning techniques were also effective when applied to other transformer policies, suggesting their broader applicability beyond the MDT architecture.

The results consistently showed that both MGF and CLA contribute to improved performance, with the combination of both yielding the best results.

### 7. Real-World Implementation

The MDT approach was successfully deployed on a real robotic system in a kitchen environment:

The real-world implementation included:
- A robotic arm operating in a toy kitchen environment
- Multiple camera views (bird's-eye and side-view)
- A second robot for kinesthetic teaching

The system demonstrated the ability to:
- Understand and execute natural language instructions
- Handle long-horizon tasks requiring multiple steps
- Manipulate articulated objects in the environment

The successful real-world implementation validates the practical applicability of the approach beyond simulation environments.

### 8. Conclusion

The Multimodal Diffusion Transformer represents a significant advancement in robot learning from multimodal goals with sparse annotations. By combining transformer architectures, diffusion-based policies, and innovative self-supervised learning techniques, MDT enables robots to learn versatile behaviors from play data without requiring extensive language labeling.

Key contributions of this work include:

1. A novel multimodal transformer architecture that effectively processes and integrates information from different modalities
2. Two self-supervised learning techniques (MGF and CLA) that improve learning from sparse data
3. State-of-the-art performance on established benchmarks, particularly in scenarios with limited language annotations
4. Successful transfer to real-world robotic manipulation tasks

The MDT approach addresses a critical challenge in robot learning: the need for extensive labeled datasets. By enabling effective learning from sparsely labeled data, MDT makes it more practical to train robots for diverse tasks in real-world environments.

Future directions for this work might include extending the approach to even more modalities, exploring zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks, and scaling the system to more complex environments and manipulation challenges.
