# EGM: Efficiently Learning General Motion Tracking Policy for High Dynamic Humanoid Whole-Body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> EGM introduces an efficient learning framework for high dynamic humanoid whole-body control, achieving robust and precise motion tracking while demonstrating that data quality and diversity are more critical than sheer quantity. The framework shows superior generalization and accuracy for a Unitree G1 robot in simulation compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Existing general motion tracking policies struggle to accurately and stably execute highly dynamic and complex humanoid movements.
- Inefficient data utilization in training, as current methods often rely on vast, redundant, and imbalanced motion capture datasets.
- Limitations in model architectures that fail to balance general control features (like balance) with specialized motion features or to adequately specialize control for different body parts.

## Method

- A Bin-based Cross-motion ==Curriculum Adaptive Sampling (BCCAS)== strategy to efficiently utilize data and prioritize learning from difficult or undersampled motions.
- A Composite Decoupled Mixture-of-Experts (CDMoE) architecture, featuring general-specialized decoupled experts and separate expert groups for upper and lower body control.
- A three-stage teacher-student curriculum training procedure that progressively builds skills from basic motion tracking to robust control in complex, randomized environments.

## Results

- EGM significantly outperformed the GMT baseline, achieving a Mean Per Keypoint Position Error (EmpkpeE_{mpkpe}Empkpe) of **57.35** mm on a diverse test set, compared to GMT's **67.09** mm.
- Policies trained on a small, high-quality dataset (**4.08** hours) demonstrated superior tracking capabilities (EmpkpeE_{mpkpe}Empkpe **31.07**) compared to those trained on a much larger (**27.47** hours) but less curated dataset (EmpkpeE_{mpkpe}Empkpe **33.58**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that both BCCAS and CDMoE components independently contributed substantial performance gains, with CDMoE improving EmpkpeE_{mpkpe}Empkpe from **64.74** (MoE) to **57.35**, and BCCAS reducing it from **71.04** to **57.35**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Data quality and diversity are paramount for training general motion tracking policies, yielding better results with a smaller, curated dataset than with a much larger, less curated one.
> - Decoupling general and specialized control features, combined with body-part-specific expert routing, enhances the policy's capacity to handle diverse and dynamic motions stably and precisely.
> - An adaptive sampling strategy integrated into a multi-stage curriculum significantly improves learning efficiency and policy robustness by focusing training on challenging motion patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is rapidly advancing, with a fundamental goal of enabling these robots to perform complex tasks in diverse environments. A critical component for this versatility is a robust and general whole-body controller capable of executing a wide array of human-like motions. EGM specifically addresses challenges within two interconnected sub-fields: **humanoid whole-body control** and **humanoid motion tracking**.

Traditionally, humanoid control has relied on model-based approaches, which are complex, task-specific, and require extensive system modeling. The emergence of learning-based approaches, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning, has revolutionized this domain. Early learning methods focused on teaching specific skills (e.g., walking, jumping, fall-recovery) through meticulously designed reward functions. While successful for individual tasks, this "one policy per skill" paradigm is inefficient and hinders the seamless transition between diverse movements required for versatile humanoid operation.

To overcome this, recent research has shifted towards learning "general" or "universal" motion tracking policies capable of imitating a vast repertoire of human motion data. Works like DeepMimic pioneered learning task-specific controllers from human motion references, reducing the need for explicit reward engineering. However, many subsequent efforts, such as KungfuBot and HuB, while achieving precise tracking of highly dynamic motions, often remained limited to individual motions.

The true challenge lies in creating a *single policy* that can adapt to *multiple motions seamlessly*. Efforts like OmniH2O, ExBody2, HumanPlus, TWIST, and CLONE have demonstrated general whole-body control, but often suffer from suboptimal accuracy, stability, especially for highly dynamic or complex motions, or are primarily tailored for static tasks. More recent advancements, including GMT, UniTracker, and KungfuBot2, introduced adaptive sampling strategies and improved model architectures (like Mixture-of-Experts, MoE, and Orthogonal Mixture-of-Experts, OMoE) to handle dynamic motions and imbalanced data.

EGM positions itself directly to address two fundamental limitations that persist in these advanced general motion tracking methods:
1. **Inefficiency in data utilization and training processes:** Existing methods often rely on dozens of hours of motion capture (Mocap) data (e.g., AMASS) to ensure generalization. This is not only costly but also inefficient due to data imbalance, redundancy, and low-quality samples, preventing policies from effectively learning complex features.
2. **Inadequate performance in tracking highly dynamic motions:** Current architectures, while attempting specialization (e.g., MoE, OMoE), often overlook control specialization, neglect general features (like balance), or struggle when faced with complex, explosive movements, leading to unstable or inaccurate tracking.

EGM distinguishes itself by proposing a framework that specifically aims for **efficient learning** and **robust performance** on **highly dynamic motions** by focusing on data quality, diverse sampling, and a specialized architectural design that balances general and dedicated control features. It builds upon the teacher-student framework and curriculum learning paradigms prevalent in the field but introduces novel components to enhance efficiency and robustness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a framework, named EGM (Efficient General Mimic), that enables the **efficient learning of a general motion tracking policy for high dynamic humanoid whole-body control**. This policy should be capable of accurately and stably executing a wide range of human motions, including those requiring high agility and rapid changes.

The primary motivations for developing EGM stem from two critical challenges identified in the current state of humanoid motion tracking:

1. **Addressing Data Redundancy and Inefficiency in Large-Scale Mocap Datasets:** Conventional approaches typically necessitate vast amounts of motion data (dozens of hours) to achieve generalization. However, large datasets like AMASS contain significant redundancies, low-quality samples, and an inherent imbalance across different motion types and durations. This inefficiency means that a large volume of data does not necessarily translate to better learning, as the policy struggles to prioritize complex or rare motion features. The goal is to maximize the utility of available data, even smaller, high-quality subsets, thereby reducing training costs and improving learning efficiency.
2. **Overcoming Inadequate Performance in Tracking Highly Dynamic Motions:** Existing methods often fall short when attempting to track highly dynamic and complex maneuvers with both precision and stability. This inadequacy is attributed to several factors:
 - **Inefficient sampling:** Existing sampling strategies fail to adequately emphasize the learning of complex, high-difficulty motion patterns.
 - **Architectural limitations:** Current model architectures (e.g., MoE, OMoE) primarily focus on extracting motion-specific specialized features but often neglect the crucial general features common to all motions, such as balance maintenance. Furthermore, they may not sufficiently specialize control for different body parts (e.g., upper vs. lower body), leading to dispersed attention and reduced expressive capability. The objective is to design an architecture that can simultaneously capture both dedicated and general control features while allowing for body-part-specific specialization.

By tackling these challenges, EGM aims to create a more capable and practical whole-body controller for humanoid robots, enabling them to robustly perform versatile and dynamic tasks in real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EGM proposes a novel framework built upon four core designs, integrated within a three-stage teacher-student curriculum training flow. The system utilizes the 29-DoF Unitree G1 robot in the IsaacGym simulator, controlling 23 DoFs (excluding fixed wrist DoFs). The problem is formulated as a goal-conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RL) task.

#### 3.1. Bin-based Cross-motion Curriculum Adaptive Sampling (BCCAS)

To address the issues of imbalanced data distribution and varying motion sequence durations in large Mocap datasets, EGM introduces BCCAS. This strategy aims for efficient data utilization through three designs:

- **Bin-based Cross-motion Global Sampling:** Instead of sampling entire motion clips, all motion sequences are segmented into 1-second "motion bins," each with a global ID. During training, global sampling occurs across these bins. A sampled bin serves as a starting frame, followed by a random offset (0-5 seconds) and extraction of a subsequent motion clip (up to 20 seconds) for tracking. This flexible approach handles both varying action types within sequences and diverse sequence lengths.
- **Sampling Probability based on Composite Tracking Error:** A composite tracking error, derived from a weighted combination of multiple error metrics (e.g., position, velocity), is calculated for each bin. An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) updates these errors, which are then globally normalized and converted into sampling probabilities for each bin. This ensures that more attention is given to motions where the policy performs poorly.
- **Sampling Curriculum:** A uniform sampling ratio and a power function are introduced to smooth the probability distribution. Dynamically adjusting this ratio and the smoothing temperature allows the policy to prioritize exploration in early training stages and gradually focus on high-error regions as training progresses, balancing exploration and exploitation.

#### 3.2. Composite Decoupled Mixture-of-Experts (CDMoE) Architecture

To enhance the policy's expressive capability for diverse and dynamic motions, CDMoE improves upon existing Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures with two key innovations:

- **General-Specialized Decoupled Experts:** This design incorporates both specialized and general feature extraction. It uses the Gram-Schmidt (GS) process to constrain a set of *M* orthogonal experts ($E^o_i$) to extract specialized motion features. A top-k mechanism selects the *k* orthogonal experts with the highest weights from a gating network. Crucially, a *shared expert* ($E^s$) is introduced, which directly outputs general motion features (e.g., balance maintenance) without undergoing the GS process or gating. The final combined motion features are a sum of the weighted orthogonal expert outputs and the shared expert output, passed through a unified output network to produce actions. This ensures that the policy captures both dedicated motion nuances and fundamental commonalities.
- **Upper and Lower Body Grouped Experts:** Recognizing the distinct dynamic characteristics and control difficulties of the upper (8 DoFs) and lower (15 DoFs) body, CDMoE employs two independent groups of experts. Each group has its own orthogonal experts, shared expert, and routing networks, outputting actions only for its corresponding joints. Both groups receive the same whole-body observations. More experts are allocated to the lower body due to its higher control difficulty, optimizing resource allocation and promoting control specialization.

#### 3.3. Data Curation: The "Quality and Diversity are Paramount" Insight

The authors emphasize a crucial insight: for training a general motion tracking policy, **data quality and diversity are paramount, not merely the quantity of data**. This was validated through a meticulous data curation process:

- Human motion data from AMASS and LAFAN1 were retargeted to the Unitree G1 robot.
- A small, high-quality dataset ($D_{small}$, 674 diverse motions, 4.08 hours) was created through manual screening, removing unachievable or failed retargeting motions.
- A larger dataset ($D_{large}$, 10,393 motions, 27.47 hours) was obtained through initial rule-based filtering, followed by an additional filtering step using a policy pre-trained on $D_{small}$ to remove motions with excessive tracking errors.
- Experiments showed that policies trained on $D_{small}$ consistently outperformed those trained on $D_{large}$, confirming that low redundancy, high diversity, and superior quality in a smaller dataset lead to better training outcomes than a massive, but less curated, dataset.

#### 3.4. Three-Stage Curriculum Training Procedure

EGM employs a teacher-student framework, enhanced with a three-stage curriculum:

- **Stage 1: Basic RL (Teacher Policy)**: A teacher policy is initially trained using the PPO algorithm in a simple simulation environment.
- **Stage 2: Complex RL (Teacher Policy)**: The checkpoint from Stage 1 is loaded. This stage introduces domain randomization and complex terrains (rough, uneven) to enhance robustness. The reward function is refined with more stringent tracking terms and stricter penalty terms. This progressive approach allows the policy to first master basic tracking before tackling environmental disturbances and demanding performance criteria.
- **Stage 3: Imitation Learning (Student Policy)**: The trained teacher policy, which uses privileged observations (not available in the real world), is distilled into a deployable student policy using the DAgger algorithm. The student policy lacks privileged observations but uses a history encoder to compensate for information loss by encoding proprioceptive history.

This structured curriculum ensures that the policy progressively builds capabilities, from basic motion tracking to high-precision, robust, and generalizable control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of EGM reveals significant improvements over baseline methods and demonstrates the effectiveness of its core components. The policies were trained on the $D_{small}$ dataset (4.08 hours) and evaluated on a diverse test set ($D_{eval}$, 49.25 hours) as well as the training set, using metrics such as Mean Per Keypoint Position Error ($E_{mpkpe}$), Upper/Lower body Mean Per Joint Position Error ($E_{umpjpe}$, $E_{lmpjpe}$), and average joint acceleration/velocity errors ($E_{acc\_dist}$, $E_{vel\_dist}$). Lower values indicate better performance.

#### 4.1. Performance Against Baselines

- **EGM vs. GMT:** EGM significantly outperforms GMT (a strong baseline) across all evaluation metrics on both test and train datasets. For instance, on the test dataset, EGM achieved an $E_{mpkpe}$ of 57.35 mm compared to GMT's 67.09 mm, and $E_{vel\_dist}$ of 12.95 mm/frame compared to 14.21 mm/frame. This demonstrates EGM's superior generalization capability and tracking accuracy.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Bin-based Cross-motion Curriculum Adaptive Sampling (BCCAS):**
 - EGM with BCCAS showed improvements across all metrics compared to "EGM w.o. BCCAS" (e.g., $E_{mpkpe}$ 57.35 vs. 71.04).
 - BCCAS was particularly effective for longer-duration motions (Figure 4), addressing the issue of insufficient learning for such sequences.
 - Analysis of sampling ratios (Figure 5) confirmed that BCCAS successfully assigned higher sampling probabilities to more difficult and rarer motions (e.g., climbing, running) and lower probabilities to easier ones (e.g., walking, standing), validating its efficiency in attention allocation.
- **Composite Decoupled Mixture-of-Experts (CDMoE):**
 - CDMoE outperformed both MoE and OMoE architectures across all metrics (e.g., $E_{mpkpe}$ 57.35 vs. 64.74 for MoE and 60.34 for OMoE), with notable improvements in velocity and acceleration tracking errors.
 - Qualitative evaluation (Figure 6) demonstrated CDMoE's superior stability and accuracy in tracking highly dynamic motions like "running-to-sudden-stop," where MoE was conservative and OMoE unstable. This highlights CDMoE's ability to balance speed, precision, and stability.
- **Data Curation (Data Quality and Diversity):**
 - The hypothesis that "data quality and diversity are paramount" was strongly supported. Policies trained solely on $D_{small}$ (4.08 hours, manually curated) achieved better tracking capabilities ($E_{mpkpe}$ 31.07) than those trained on $D_{large}$ (27.47 hours, rule-filtered) ($E_{mpkpe}$ 33.58) or even pre-trained on $D_{large}$ and fine-tuned on $D_{small}$ ($E_{mpkpe}$ 31.75). This crucial finding implies that a smaller, high-quality, diverse dataset can yield better results at reduced computational and data processing costs.
- **Three-Stage Training Procedure:**
 - The three-stage curriculum significantly outperformed a simpler two-stage approach that directly introduced complex rewards and environments (EGM's $E_{mpkpe}$ 57.35 vs. 110.42 for the two-stage method). This confirms the importance of progressively enhancing policy robustness and skill acquisition.
 - Analysis of performance at different stages (Table 3) showed substantial improvement from Stage 1 to Stage 2 (basic to complex RL), validating the curriculum's effectiveness. The student policy in Stage 3 maintained most of the teacher's performance, indicating effective distillation despite the loss of privileged observations.

Overall, the results consistently demonstrate that EGM's integrated designs contribute synergistically to achieving superior data efficiency, robust generalization, and high-precision tracking for highly dynamic humanoid whole-body control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The EGM framework represents a significant advancement in humanoid whole-body control and motion tracking, offering substantial significance and potential impact across several fronts:

1. **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Reduced Training Costs:** The most striking finding is the principle that "data quality and diversity are paramount" over sheer data quantity. EGM achieves superior performance by training on only 4.08 hours of high-quality, diverse data, yet generalizes robustly across 49.25 hours of test motions. This paradigm shift can drastically reduce the computational resources, data collection efforts, and overall cost associated with training sophisticated humanoid controllers. It makes advanced humanoid capabilities more accessible and sustainable.
2. **Robust and Precise Tracking of Highly Dynamic Motions:** EGM's ability to accurately and stably track highly dynamic and complex motions (e.g., running and sudden stops, hurdling, spinning jumps) is a critical breakthrough. This addresses a major limitation of previous general motion trackers, which often struggled with such challenging movements. This capability is fundamental for humanoids to operate effectively in unstructured, real-world environments that demand agility, rapid response, and robust balance.
3. **Novel Architectural Contributions (CDMoE):** The Composite Decoupled Mixture-of-Experts (CDMoE) architecture provides a sophisticated solution for managing the complexity of whole-body control. By decoupling general and specialized motion features and grouping experts for upper and lower body parts, EGM effectively enhances the policy's capacity to handle motions from varying distributions. This architectural innovation offers a robust blueprint for designing future general-purpose controllers in complex robotic systems.
4. **Effective Curriculum Learning and Sampling Strategies:** The Bin-based Cross-motion Curriculum Adaptive Sampling (BCCAS) strategy efficiently balances training across motions of varying difficulty and duration. Coupled with the three-stage curriculum training flow, it provides a structured and efficient learning pathway, enabling policies to progressively acquire complex skills and robustness. These methodologies offer valuable insights for designing more effective and stable reinforcement learning environments for robotics.
5. **Accelerated Real-World Deployment:** By enabling efficient learning and robust performance on dynamic tasks, EGM moves closer to enabling the widespread deployment of versatile humanoid robots in practical applications. Robots with such capabilities could perform diverse roles in logistics, assistance, manufacturing, and hazardous environments, requiring fluid and adaptable physical interaction. The teacher-student distillation approach further ensures that the learned policies are deployable on real hardware by stripping away privileged observations.

In conclusion, EGM provides a powerful and practical framework that tackles core challenges in humanoid control. Its innovations in data handling, model architecture, and training methodologies pave the way for a new generation of more capable, efficient, and versatile humanoid robots, pushing the boundaries of what these machines can achieve in complex, dynamic, and human-centric environments.
