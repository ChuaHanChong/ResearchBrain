# Expected Policy Gradients

> [!summary] Summary
> Expected Policy Gradients (EPG) unifies stochastic and deterministic policy gradient methods by analytically integrating over action distributions for gradient estimation, leading to provable variance reduction and a principled Hessian-based exploration strategy. This approach demonstrates superior and more consistent performance over Deep DPG and standard SPG in continuous control benchmarks, particularly by avoiding performance declines seen in other methods.

## Problem

- Policy gradient theory is fragmented, with separate frameworks for stochastic (SPG) and deterministic (DPG) policies, lacking a unified theoretical foundation.
- Stochastic policy gradient (SPG) methods suffer from high variance in gradient estimates, leading to poor sample efficiency and computational cost.
- Deterministic policy gradient (DPG) methods face limited theoretical justification for practical implementations and rely on unprincipled, heuristic exploration strategies like Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise.

## Method

- Introduces **Expected Policy Gradients (EPG)**, which redefines the policy gradient by ==analytically integrating over the policy's action distribution==, eliminating sampling noise for inner gradient computations.
- Develops a general policy gradient theorem that unifies SPG and DPG, treating them as different quadrature methods for the same underlying integral.
- Derives analytical policy gradient components for Gaussian policies with quadratic (or Taylor-approximated) critics, enabling a principled, adaptive exploration strategy based on the ==Hessian of the Q-function== ($\Sigma \propto e^{cH}$).

## Results

- EPG with Hessian-based exploration outperformed Deep DPG (with OU noise) and standard SPG across **four** challenging MuJoCo continuous control tasks.
- EPG demonstrated more consistent learning trajectories and **substantially lower** empirical standard deviation of returns compared to DPG, indicating greater robustness.
- The adaptive Hessian-driven exploration prevented "unlearning" (performance decline late in training) observed in DPG and SPG, by intelligently adjusting exploration noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A general policy gradient theorem unifies stochastic and deterministic policy gradients, suggesting they represent different approximation strategies for the same underlying objective.
> - Expected Policy Gradients inherently reduce gradient estimate variance by analytically computing the expectation over actions, improving sample efficiency compared to stochastic methods.
> - Deterministic Policy Gradients (DPG) can be viewed as an on-policy variant of EPG when using a Gaussian exploration policy, providing theoretical justification for its practical implementations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) in environments with large or continuous action spaces commonly employs policy gradient methods. These methods optimize a policy (actor) by following a gradient estimated by a value function (critic). This field has generally bifurcated into two main approaches: Stochastic Policy Gradients (SPG) and Deterministic Policy Gradients (DPG).

SPG methods, as formalized by Sutton et al. (2000), update a stochastic policy based on sampled actions. While theoretically robust, SPG often suffers from high variance in gradient estimates, leading to poor sample efficiency. This necessitates the collection of many trajectories, which can be computationally expensive.

DPG methods, introduced by Silver et al. (2014), utilize deterministic policies. This approach reduces gradient variance but introduces its own set of challenges. The theoretical underpinnings of DPG are considered more limited, as they assume a critic that approximates the action-gradient of the Q-function (∇_a Q), whereas in practice, critics typically approximate the Q-function itself (Q). Furthermore, DPG is an off-policy method, which means learning does not inherently account for the cost of exploration. Designing effective exploration strategies for DPG is an open problem, with common solutions involving simple noise models like independent Gaussian noise or the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process (Lillicrap et al., 2015) applied to the deterministic action.

The paper addresses this fragmented theoretical landscape and the practical shortcomings of existing methods. It draws inspiration from "expected Sarsa" (Sutton and Barto, 1998), which reduces variance in value function updates by marginalizing over the next action. By proposing Expected Policy Gradients (EPG), the authors aim to unify SPG and DPG within a single framework and to leverage the variance-reduction principle of expected Sarsa for policy gradient estimation. This work positions itself as a foundational theoretical contribution that also yields practical benefits, particularly in continuous control tasks where efficient exploration and low-variance gradient estimates are critical. The paper also connects to other variance minimization techniques and independently developed concepts like Mean Actor Critic (Asadi et al., 2017).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Expected Policy Gradients (EPG), a novel framework intended to unify stochastic and deterministic policy gradient methods in reinforcement learning. This unification is motivated by several theoretical and practical challenges present in the existing landscape of policy gradient algorithms.

The motivation for developing EPG stems from the following specific issues:

- **Theoretical Fragmentation:** Current policy gradient theory is largely separated for stochastic and deterministic policies, each relying on distinct policy gradient theorems. This fragmentation suggests a lack of a comprehensive underlying principle that could encompass both. The authors aimed to establish a general policy gradient theorem that could subsume both SPG and DPG as special cases, thereby providing a more unified theoretical foundation.
- **High Variance in SPG:** Stochastic policy gradient methods are known for requiring numerous trajectories due to the high variance of their gradient estimates. This leads to poor sample efficiency, making SPG computationally expensive, especially in environments where interaction with the environment (sampling trajectories) is costly. A key objective was to develop a method that could reduce this variance without abandoning stochastic policies.
- **Limitations of DPG:** Deterministic policy gradients, while addressing the variance issue to some extent by using deterministic policies, introduce other problems. Their theoretical support is considered limited, particularly regarding the common practice of using a critic that approximates the Q-function rather than its action-gradient. More importantly, DPG's off-policy nature necessitates the design of an auxiliary exploration policy, which remains a significant open problem. Existing exploration strategies, such as adding Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise, are often heuristic and lack theoretical guarantees or adaptability. The authors sought to provide a principled, on-policy exploration strategy within a unified framework that could also shed light on the theoretical underpinnings of DPG's practical implementations.
- **Analytical Tractability for Gradient Estimation:** Inspired by expected Sarsa, which computes an expectation over future actions to reduce variance in value updates, the authors aimed to apply a similar principle to policy gradient estimation. By integrating across the policy's action distribution when calculating the gradient, instead of relying on a single sampled action, they hypothesized that variance could be reduced. A specific objective was to explore conditions under which this integration could be performed analytically, particularly for common policy distributions like Gaussian policies, to ensure computational feasibility.
- **Improved Practical Performance:** Ultimately, the motivation included demonstrating that the proposed EPG framework, particularly with its derived exploration strategy, could achieve superior and more consistent empirical performance compared to existing state-of-the-art policy gradient methods in challenging continuous control tasks.

In summary, the key objectives were to unify policy gradient theory, reduce gradient variance without losing policy stochasticity, provide a principled and adaptive exploration mechanism, and translate these theoretical advancements into improved practical reinforcement learning algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of Expected Policy Gradients (EPG) involves estimating the policy gradient by integrating across the action distribution, rather than relying on a single action sampled from a trajectory. This approach mirrors the variance reduction technique used in expected Sarsa, but applies it to the actor's gradient estimation.

**Theoretical Framework:**

1. **Redefinition of the Policy Gradient:** The standard policy gradient integral for stochastic policies is given by ∇J = ∫_s dρ(s) ∫_a dπ(a|s) ∇ log π(a|s) (Q(a,s) + b(s)). The authors denote the inner integral as I_Q_π(s), highlighting that for a given state, this term is an expectation over actions that can be computed. The EPG gradient estimate is then formed by summing these integrated terms over time: ˆ∇J = ∑_T_t=0 γ^t ˆI_Q_π(s_t).
2. **General Policy Gradient Theorem (Theorem 1):** The paper establishes a new general policy gradient theorem: ∇J = ∫_s dρ(s) [∇V(s) - ∫_a dπ(a,s) ∇Q(a,s)]. This theorem is presented as a unification, showing that both the Stochastic Policy Gradient Theorem (Corollary 1) and the Deterministic Policy Gradient Theorem (Corollary 2) can be derived as special cases by applying additional assumptions about policy differentiability or determinism. This implies that the choice between SPG and DPG can be viewed as a choice of quadrature method for approximating the same underlying integral.
3. **Variance Reduction (Lemma 3):** EPG is formally proven to reduce the variance of gradient estimates compared to SPG. Lemma 3 demonstrates that if the random variable ∇ log π(a|s) ˆQ(s,a) (where `a` is sampled from the policy) has non-zero variance, then the total variance of the EPG estimator is strictly less than that of the SPG estimator. This holds because EPG analytically computes the expectation for the inner integral `I_Q_π(s)`, removing the sampling noise inherent in Monte Carlo estimation of this term, which is central to SPG.
4. **Extension to Entropy Regularization:** The paper shows that entropy regularization in SPG can be reinterpreted within the EPG framework as modifying the critic's Q-values by an additional term, making it compatible with EPG.

**Analytical Quadrature for Gaussian Policies (GPG):**

A key aspect of EPG's practicality is the ability to perform the integration `I_Q_π(s)` analytically, especially for common policy distributions.

1. **Gaussian Policy Gradient (Lemma 1):** For Gaussian policies (π(·|s) ~ N(μ_s, Σ_s)) and critics that are quadratic in actions (Q(a,s) = a^T A(s)a + a^T B(s) + const), the integral `I_Q_π(s)` can be computed analytically. The policy gradient components for the mean (μ_s) and covariance (Σ_s) are derived as:
 - I_Q_π(s),μ_s = (∇μ_s)(2A(s)μ + B(s))
 - I_Q_π(s),Σ_1/2_s = (∇Σ_1/2_s)2A(s)Σ_1/2_s
 This formulation makes EPG computationally feasible for these common scenarios. The computational cost for the Hessian (A(s)) is small (d x d matrix, where d is action dimension), which is often significantly less than the Hessian of neural network parameters in other methods.
2. **Approximate Gaussian Policy Gradients for Arbitrary Critics (Corollary 3):** When the critic Q(a,s) is not strictly quadratic in actions, it can be approximated using a second-order Taylor expansion around the policy mean (μ_s). This approximation leads to similar analytical gradient components:
 - I_Q_π(s),μ_s ≈ (∇μ_s)∇_a Q(a=μ_s, s)
 - I_Q_π(s),Σ_1/2_s ≈ (∇Σ_1/2_s)H(μ_s, s)Σ_1/2_s, where H(μ_s, s) is the Hessian of Q with respect to `a` evaluated at `μ_s`.
 This allows EPG to be applied to critics represented by arbitrary function approximators (e.g., neural networks) by computing or approximating the Hessian.
3. **Equivalences to DPG:** The paper demonstrates that the update for the policy mean obtained in Corollary 3 is equivalent to the DPG update. This implies that DPG can be viewed as a specific form of EPG when using a Gaussian exploration policy. This reinterpretation suggests that DPG, typically considered off-policy, can be seen as on-policy when exploring with Gaussian noise, especially when its compatible critic is linear in actions.

**Principled Exploration Strategy:**

Based on the covariance update derived in Corollary 3, the authors propose a novel exploration strategy.

1. **Exploration Limit (Lemma 2):** By iteratively applying the covariance update rule (Σ_1/2_s ← Σ_1/2_s + αH(s)Σ_1/2_s) with a diminishing learning rate α = 1/n, the covariance matrix Σ_s converges to a form proportional to e^(cH(s)) as n → ∞ (where c is a scaling constant and H(s) is the Hessian of the critic with respect to actions).
2. **Hessian-based Exploration:** This derivation leads to a principled exploration strategy where a Gaussian policy explores with a covariance proportional to the exponential of the scaled Hessian of the critic (e^(cH)). This mechanism intuitively means that exploration is higher in directions where the Q-function is sharply curved (large positive eigenvalues of H), helping the agent escape local optima. Conversely, if Q has a maximum, exploration in that direction is reduced. This offers a theoretically grounded alternative to heuristic exploration methods like Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise.

**Empirical Evaluation:**

The proposed EPG with Hessian-based exploration was empirically evaluated on four challenging continuous control tasks using the MuJoCo physics simulator: HalfCheetah-v1, InvertedPendulum-v1, Reacher2d-v1, and Walker2d-v1.

- **Comparison Groups:** EPG was compared against Deep DPG (Lillicrap et al., 2015) using Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) exploration and SPG using OU exploration with a constant diagonal covariance.
- **Hyperparameters:** Hyperparameters for DPG and shared EPG parameters were adopted from existing benchmarks. EPG's exploration hyperparameters (σ_0^2 and c for σ_0^2 e^(cH)) were selected via a grid search on HalfCheetah-v1 and then applied to other domains.
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed using learning curves (mean and 90% confidence intervals over multiple runs) and the empirical standard deviation of returns across runs at the end of learning. Evaluation runs used the policy mean without exploration noise.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several theoretical advancements and empirical validations for Expected Policy Gradients (EPG).

**Theoretical Findings:**

1. **General Policy Gradient Theorem:** The paper established a new general policy gradient theorem, which encompasses both the stochastic policy gradient (SPG) theorem and the deterministic policy gradient (DPG) theorem as specific instantiations under certain assumptions. This provides a unified theoretical framework for understanding policy gradient methods, interpreting the choice between SPG and DPG as a choice of quadrature method for evaluating an integral.
2. **Variance Reduction in EPG:** A formal proof (Lemma 3) demonstrated that EPG intrinsically reduces the variance of gradient estimates compared to SPG, provided that the underlying sampled variable (∇ log π(a|s) ˆQ(s,a)) has non-zero variance. This reduction occurs because EPG analytically computes the expectation over actions for the inner integral of the gradient, eliminating sampling noise at that stage. This property holds without restricting policies to be deterministic.
3. **Analytical Quadrature for Gaussian Policies:** For Gaussian policies and critics that are quadratic in actions (or approximated as such via Taylor expansion), the policy gradient can be computed analytically (Lemma 1, Corollary 3). This makes EPG computationally efficient for these common scenarios, particularly by approximating the Hessian of the critic with respect to actions.
4. **Equivalences to DPG:** The research showed that on-policy EPG, particularly in its Gaussian Policy Gradient (GPG) form with a linear or quadratic critic, is equivalent to DPG with a Gaussian exploration policy. This equivalence implies that DPG, typically considered an off-policy method, can be reinterpreted as an on-policy method when exploring with Gaussian noise. It also provides theoretical justification for DPG's common practice of approximating Q instead of ∇_a Q.
5. **Principled Exploration Strategy:** The iterative update rule for the covariance matrix, derived from the EPG framework, was shown to converge to a form proportional to the exponential of the scaled Hessian of the critic with respect to actions (Σ_s ∝ e^(cH(s)), Lemma 2). This provides a theoretically grounded, adaptive exploration strategy for continuous action spaces, contrasting with heuristic approaches like Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise. The strategy suggests exploring more in directions where the Q-function indicates a sharp minimum (large positive Hessian eigenvalues) to escape local optima, and less where a maximum exists.

**Empirical Findings:**

1. **Superior Performance over DPG and SPG:** Empirical results on four MuJoCo continuous control domains (HalfCheetah-v1, InvertedPendulum-v1, Reacher2d-v1, Walker2d-v1) demonstrated that EPG with Hessian-based exploration generally outperformed DPG with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) exploration. SPG performed poorly across most domains, only achieving reasonable progress on the simplest task (InvertedPendulum-v1) and slow progress on HalfCheetah-v1.
2. **Consistent Learning and Reduced Unlearning:** EPG typically exhibited more consistent learning trajectories compared to DPG with OU. In HalfCheetah-v1 and Reacher2d-v1, the empirical standard deviation of returns across runs for EPG was substantially lower than for DPG, indicating greater robustness. Furthermore, in the InvertedPendulum-v1 domain, both DPG and SPG showed "unlearning" (a decline in performance late in training), which was attributed to coarse OU exploration or gradient noise. EPG avoided this issue by adaptively reducing exploration noise when approaching good optima, preventing the optimizer from exiting favorable regions.
3. **Effective Adaptive Exploration:** The Hessian-driven exploration strategy in EPG was shown to be effective, leading to better and more stable performance than the commonly used OU heuristic in DPG. This validates the practical utility of the theoretically derived exploration method.

In summary, the research presents EPG as a method that unifies policy gradient theory, offers a provable variance reduction, and introduces a principled exploration strategy. These theoretical benefits translate into superior and more consistent empirical performance in continuous control tasks compared to established policy gradient algorithms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed Expected Policy Gradients (EPG) framework and its associated theoretical and empirical findings carry several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of reinforcement learning:

1. **Theoretical Unification of Policy Gradients:** The establishment of a general policy gradient theorem is a fundamental theoretical contribution. By showing that both stochastic and deterministic policy gradient theorems are special cases, EPG provides a more cohesive understanding of policy gradient methods. This unified perspective can simplify future theoretical derivations and foster a more integrated approach to designing new policy gradient algorithms, moving beyond the historical division.
2. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency and Stability:** The proven variance reduction of EPG compared to SPG is a significant practical advantage. High variance in gradient estimates is a major bottleneck for the sample efficiency of many policy gradient methods. By analytically integrating over action distributions, EPG can lead to more stable and faster learning, reducing the number of costly environment interactions required to achieve good performance. This is particularly crucial in real-world applications where data collection is expensive or time-consuming.
3. **Principled Exploration for Continuous Control:** The derivation of the Hessian-based exploration strategy (covariance proportional to e^(cH)) offers a theoretically sound and adaptive alternative to heuristic exploration methods like Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise. This strategy dynamically adjusts exploration based on the local curvature of the Q-function, promoting more efficient search and preventing premature convergence or "unlearning." This could lead to more robust and generalizable exploration techniques, particularly in complex, high-dimensional continuous control problems where naive noise addition is often insufficient.
4. **Reinterpretation of DPG:** The demonstration that DPG can be viewed as a form of on-policy EPG with Gaussian exploration clarifies its theoretical underpinnings and resolves some of the ambiguities regarding its use of a Q-function critic. This reinterpretation provides new insights into DPG's success and could guide future extensions or modifications of DPG algorithms.
5. **Practical Performance Improvement:** The empirical results confirm that EPG with Hessian-based exploration outperforms both DPG with OU noise and standard SPG in several challenging MuJoCo domains. This practical superiority, combined with greater learning consistency and reduced unlearning, suggests that EPG can be a more reliable and effective algorithm for continuous control tasks. The approach's relatively low computational overhead (for the Hessian) further enhances its practical applicability.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** EPG opens several avenues for future research. This includes exploring its application with different policy distributions beyond Gaussian, developing more efficient methods for approximating the Hessian for arbitrary complex critics (e.g., deep neural networks), extending the framework to off-policy learning, or integrating it with other advanced RL techniques like actor-critic architectures or model-based RL. The concept of integrating over action distributions for gradient estimation could also be extended to other components of RL algorithms, similar to how expected Sarsa influenced value function updates.

In conclusion, EPG represents a significant step towards unifying policy gradient methods, addressing critical issues of variance and exploration, and demonstrating practical advantages. Its impact is likely to be felt in both theoretical understanding and the development of more robust and efficient reinforcement learning algorithms for continuous control.
