# MiniF2F: a cross-system benchmark for formal Olympiad-level mathematics

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents miniF2F, a cross-system benchmark for formal Olympiad-level mathematics, to enable direct comparison of neural theorem provers across different formal systems. The work demonstrates that neural models achieve substantially higher performance when integrated with formal systems offering high-level tactics, with a GPT-f/PACT model reaching a 29.2% Pass@8 rate on Lean.

## Problem

- Existing neural theorem proving benchmarks were system-specific, leading to siloed progress and an inability to directly compare provers across different formal systems.
- Benchmarks often contained library-specific artifacts rather than universal mathematical statements, making it difficult to assess true mathematical reasoning abilities.
- A lack of a unified, cross-system platform hindered the quantitative measurement and acceleration of advancements in formal mathematical reasoning, particularly towards ambitious goals like the IMO Grand Challenge.

## Method

- Introduced **miniF2F**, a ==unified, cross-system benchmark== consisting of 488 manually formalized Olympiad-level mathematical statements compatible with Metamath, Lean, Isabelle, and HOL Light.
- Problems are drawn from competitive math sources (AIME, AMC, IMO) and the MATH dataset, focusing primarily on algebra, number theory, and inequalities to ensure genuine mathematical reasoning.
- Provided baseline results using ==neural theorem provers== (**GPT-f/PACT**) and a non-neural approach (Lean's **Tidy**) across different formal systems to demonstrate benchmark utility and explore system interplay.

## Results

- GPT-f/PACT on Lean achieved a Pass@8 rate of **29.2%** on miniF2F-test, leveraging Lean's high-level tactics.
- GPT-f on Metamath achieved a Pass@8 rate of **1.6%** on miniF2F-test, demonstrating the challenge of low-level proof generation without higher-level automation.
- Formalized MATH problems solved by GPT-f/PACT on Lean showed significantly higher pass rates (e.g., **51.4%** in algebra) compared to large language models solving informal MATH problems (e.g., **6.0%** in algebra).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Neural theorem provers achieve substantially higher performance when integrated with formal systems that provide powerful high-level tactics, as evidenced by the Lean baseline's 29.2% Pass@8 rate compared to Metamath's 1.6%.
> - Formal systems act as robust verifiers and provide access to automated tactics, allowing neural models to focus on guidance rather than generating every low-level proof step, which significantly improves formal reasoning performance over informal methods.
> - Olympiad-level mathematics problems offer a suitable testbed for advanced mathematical reasoning in formal systems due to their non-trivial nature and requirement for creative problem-solving.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of automated theorem proving (ATP) and, more recently, neural theorem proving (NTP), aims to develop AI systems capable of constructing or verifying mathematical proofs. This field has seen significant interest due to its potential to revolutionize mathematics, software verification, and logical reasoning in AI.

Prior to miniF2F, benchmarks in neural theorem proving were largely system-specific. Examples include HOList (Bansal et al., 2019a) for HOL Light, CoqGym (Yang & Deng, 2019) for Coq, and LeanStep (Han et al., 2021) for Lean. While these benchmarks were crucial for advancing research within their respective formal systems, they suffered from several limitations:
1. **Siloed Progress:** Results obtained on one system's benchmark were not directly comparable to results on another. Each formal system has its own unique syntax, proof language, and mathematical library, making cross-system evaluation difficult.
2. **Library-Specific Artifacts:** Formal mathematics libraries, akin to software repositories, contain many lemmas that are specific to the implementation details of that library, rather than universal mathematical statements. This makes it hard to gauge true mathematical reasoning ability.
3. **Lack of Unified Measurement:** There was no common ground to quantitatively compare the mathematical reasoning capabilities of different neural theorem provers that might operate on different underlying formal systems.

miniF2F directly addresses these limitations by introducing a **unified, cross-system benchmark**. This work is explicitly positioned as a stepping stone towards the **IMO Grand Challenge (Selsam et al., 2019)**, a long-term goal to build an AI system capable of winning a gold medal in the International Mathematical Olympiad by accepting formal problem statements and producing formal, machine-checkable proofs. By focusing on Olympiad-level problems, miniF2F aims to test advanced mathematical reasoning, not just routine computations or library navigation.

The paper also draws a distinction from informal mathematical reasoning benchmarks like MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) and NaturalProofs (Welleck et al., 2021). While these informal benchmarks test understanding and generation of mathematical text in natural language, miniF2F focuses on *formal* mathematics, where statements and proofs are machine-checkable. This emphasizes the role of the formal system as a verifier, a critical aspect that separates formal theorem proving from informal mathematical problem-solving.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of miniF2F is to **provide a unified, cross-system benchmark for neural theorem proving** that can measure progress in formal mathematical reasoning. The authors are motivated by the historical impact of shared benchmarks in driving advancements in fields like computer vision and natural language processing. They believe a similar resource is crucial for the rapidly developing area of neural theorem proving.

Specific motivations and objectives include:
- **Addressing the "silo problem":** To overcome the fragmentation caused by system-specific benchmarks and enable direct comparison of neural theorem provers across different formal systems (Metamath, Lean, Isabelle, HOL Light, Coq).
- **Facilitating assessment of mathematical reasoning:** To create a dataset of problems that require genuine mathematical reasoning, moving beyond tasks that might be solved by merely navigating a proof assistant's library. Olympiad-level problems are chosen for this purpose due to their non-trivial nature and progressive difficulty.
- **Supporting the IMO Grand Challenge:** To serve as an end-to-end verifiable and cross-platform stepping stone towards building AI capable of solving IMO problems in a formal-to-formal (F2F) format.
- **Promoting community-driven effort:** To encourage researchers to contribute proofs and evaluation results, fostering collaborative development in the field.
- **Investigating the synergy between neural models and formal systems:** By reporting baselines on different systems (Metamath vs. Lean), the authors aim to shed light on how the design of formal systems (e.g., availability of high-level tactics) impacts the performance of neural theorem provers.

### 3. Methodology and Approach: miniF2F Benchmark Construction

miniF2F is a dataset of manually formalized statements of Olympiad-type problems, designed to be compatible with multiple formal proof assistants.

**A. Data Sources and Scope:**
The benchmark currently targets Metamath, Lean, Isabelle (partially), and HOL Light (partially). It consists of 488 problem statements drawn from a variety of sources, representing different difficulty levels and mathematical sub-subjects:
- **Olympiad-level Competitions:** Problems from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME), American Mathematics Competitions (AMC), and the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO). These problems are known for requiring creative problem-solving.
- **MATH Dataset:** A subset of problems from the informal MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), which are segmented into 5 difficulty levels (Level 1 to Level 5). Formalizing these allows for a direct comparison between informal and formal reasoning capabilities.
- **Custom Problems:** Additional problems from high-school and undergraduate mathematics courses, designed to broaden coverage.

The focus is primarily on algebra, number theory, and inequalities, as geometry and combinatorial problems are generally more challenging to formalize in most existing systems. The statements are selected to span a wide range of difficulty for both human and machine provers.

**B. Formalization Process and Challenges:**
All problem statements in miniF2F are **manually formalized**. The authors estimate that formalizing a statement takes about 15 minutes on average for a trained practitioner, with review taking about half that time. Formal proofs for these statements are optionally attached to the benchmark, but not part of the benchmark itself, allowing systems to generate their own proofs.

The authors explicitly acknowledge several challenges encountered during the formalization process:
- **Multi-choice Questions:** These are formalized by reformulating the statement using only the correct answer, or by formalizing all choices for a competitive setup.
- **Word Problems:** These require significant effort to formalize, often by explicitly modeling the mathematical concepts described in natural language. Problems where formalization itself constitutes most of the difficulty are discarded.
- **Problems Requiring Explicit Witness/Set:** Problems like "find all X such that..." are not directly formalizable. The workaround involves formalizing the statement with a provided witness/answer, transforming the task into proving correctness (and sometimes uniqueness) of the given answer. This can sometimes simplify the problem compared to its original intent.
- **Limited Coverage of Problem Types:** Due to the nascent state of formalization efforts in areas like geometry and combinatorics across many systems, miniF2F's distribution of problem types might skew research towards algebra and number theory.

**C. Benchmark Structure and Licensing:**
miniF2F v1 comprises 244 test statements and 244 validation statements, created through a stratified random split to ensure equal coverage of problem types and difficulty levels. The benchmark is versioned, with new statements continuously added and versions periodically frozen to ensure stability. The licensing aligns with the target theorem provers' main libraries (MIT License for Metamath, Apache License for Lean and Isabelle). The authors invite researchers to contribute proofs found by their approaches back to the benchmark, fostering a community-driven development model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results: Baseline Performances

The paper reports baseline results using two neural theorem provers and one non-neural baseline.

**A. Metamath Baseline (GPT-f):**
- **Model:** GPT-f (Polu & Sutskever, 2020), a 700M parameter decoder-only transformer model, trained on Metamath's set.mm library.
- **Performance:** Achieved a Pass@1 rate of 1.3% and a Pass@8 rate of 1.6% on miniF2F-test.
- **Context:** Metamath is characterized by its simplicity and low-level proof steps, meaning proofs are significantly longer and more tedious compared to other systems, as there's no assistance from high-level tactics. The authors note that the low pass rates are "as expected" due to the length of typical Metamath proofs for even simple math exercises.

**B. Lean Baselines (Tidy and GPT-f/PACT):**
- **Lean's Nature:** Lean benefits from a rich set of powerful tactics, which significantly shortens typical proof lengths compared to Metamath. It has gained attention in the mathematical community for formalizing advanced theories (e.g., Perfectoid Spaces, Liquid Tensor experiment).
- **Tidy Baseline:** This is a non-neural, best-first search algorithm implemented in Lean, incorporating a curated list of tactics, including high-level ones like `nlinarith`, `linarith`, `ring nf`, and `norm num`.
 - **Performance:** Achieved a Pass@1 rate of 18.0% on miniF2F-test. It is a deterministic baseline, so Pass@N is not applicable in the same way.
- **GPT-f/PACT Baseline:** This uses GPT-f applied to Lean following the PACT methodology (Han et al., 2021), a 700M parameter model trained on the mathlib library.
 - **Performance:** Achieved a Pass@1 rate of 24.6% and a Pass@8 rate of 29.2% on miniF2F-test.

**C. Discussion: Impact of High-Level Tactics:**
A critical insight from the baseline results is the drastic performance difference between GPT-f applied to Metamath (1.6% Pass@8) and GPT-f/PACT applied to Lean (29.2% Pass@8), despite comparable model sizes and computational resources during training and testing. The authors hypothesize that this disparity is primarily explained by the Lean model's **access to high-level tactics**. Lean's tactics encapsulate complex reasoning steps, allowing the neural model to learn how to effectively *guide* these automated procedures rather than generating every low-level proof step. An illustrative example is provided where Lean's `nlinarith` solver, when provided with essential premises by the model, can complete a proof that would require hundreds of low-level steps in Metamath or be unsolvable by the tidy baseline alone. This suggests a powerful synergy between neural generative models and expressive formal systems that expose powerful automated reasoning tools.

**D. Discussion: Comparison of Informal and Formal Setups:**
The inclusion of formalized statements from the informal MATH dataset allows for an approximate comparison of neural theorem proving in formal vs. informal settings.
- **Informal (GPT-3 on MATH):** GPT-3 (a larger model than GPT-f) achieved 6.0% accuracy in the algebra category and 3.9% in number theory.
- **Formal (GPT-f/PACT on miniF2F's formalized MATH):** GPT-f applied to Lean achieved significantly higher pass rates: 51.4% in algebra and 41.7% in number theory.
- **Tidy Baseline (on miniF2F's formalized MATH):** Even the non-neural tidy baseline outperformed GPT-3 in the informal setting (31.4% in algebra, 30.0% in number theory).
This comparison highlights the substantial benefit of having a formal system, which acts as a reliable verifier and provides access to powerful automated tactics. The ability to verify generated proofs automatically enables more advanced search strategies and reduces the burden on the generative model to produce perfect, end-to-end low-level steps.

**E. Limitations Acknowledged by Authors:**
The authors are transparent about current limitations of miniF2F:
- **Problem Type Skew:** The current benchmark's systemic focus on algebra, number theory, and inequalities means that problem types less expressible in certain formal systems (e.g., geometry, combinatorics) are underrepresented. This could inadvertently skew research direction. They aim to extend coverage as formalization efforts in these areas mature within the respective theorem provers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

miniF2F represents a significant contribution to the field of neural theorem proving for several reasons:

1. **Unified Benchmarking:** It fills a critical gap by providing the first unified, cross-system benchmark for formal Olympiad-level mathematics. This allows for direct, apples-to-apples comparison of different neural theorem provers, regardless of the underlying formal system they operate on. This fosters healthy competition and collaboration, crucial for accelerating research.
2. **Driving Advanced Reasoning:** By focusing on Olympiad-level problems, miniF2F pushes the boundaries of what current AI systems can achieve in mathematical reasoning, moving beyond routine tasks to problems requiring creativity and deeper understanding. It serves as a concrete milestone towards the ambitious IMO Grand Challenge.
3. **Insights into System Design:** The baseline results provide valuable insights into the interplay between neural models and formal proof assistants. The superior performance observed in Lean, attributed to its high-level tactics, suggests that future advancements might heavily rely on the synergistic design of formal systems that expose powerful automation to neural guidance, rather than requiring AI to generate every atomic proof step. This finding has implications for both the design of theorem provers and the development of AI strategies.
4. **Community Resource:** Designed as a "community-driven effort," miniF2F encourages broader participation, open contribution of proofs, and shared evaluation metrics, fostering a collaborative research environment.
5. **Quantifying Formal System Benefits:** The comparison with informal mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH dataset) empirically demonstrates the significant advantage gained from leveraging formal systems, both through automated verification and access to powerful tactics. This strengthens the case for investing in formal methods for AI research.

In conclusion, miniF2F is not just a dataset; it is a foundational infrastructure that aims to organize and accelerate progress in neural theorem proving. By providing a common, challenging, and verifiable ground for evaluation, it is poised to spur significant innovations in AI's ability to understand and generate formal mathematical proofs.
