# Enhancing Interpretability in Deep Reinforcement Learning through Semantic Clustering

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Arizona developed a method integrating dimensionality reduction and online clustering into Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to enhance model interpretability by revealing the intrinsic semantic organization of learned feature spaces. This approach generates stable, well-separated state clusters that correspond to human-understandable agent "skills" and enables dynamic policy analysis, with human evaluators giving mean Likert scores above 4 for interpretability.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) models operate as "black boxes," making their decision-making processes opaque, hindering trust, and posing risks in critical applications.
- Previous attempts at DRL semantic analysis were limited to fixed-scene environments, struggling to discern true semantic understanding from pixel-level similarities.
- Existing methods for DRL interpretability, often relying on t-SNE, suffered from instability, sensitivity to initialization, and required extensive manual effort for analysis and annotation.

## Method

- An end-to-end ==Semantic Clustering Module== (SCM) is integrated into the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) pipeline, which includes a Feature Dimensionality Reduction (FDR) network and an online clustering mechanism.
- The FDR network maps high-dimensional DRL state features to a stable, low-dimensional space (e.g., 2D) using a KL divergence loss that preserves the neighborhood structure of the original feature space.
- Online clustering is implemented via a modified Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE), where a learnable codebook's embeddings act as cluster centroids that are updated to group semantically similar states efficiently.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved superior clustering effectiveness and stability, producing clearly separated and robust semantic clusters in the FDR space, outperforming t-SNE and other dimensionality reduction techniques in quantitative metrics.
- Human evaluators confirmed the interpretability of the clusters, giving mean Likert scores above 4 for statements indicating consistency of "skills" and aid in understanding agent decision-making.
- New analytical methods were introduced, allowing for interactive visualization and dynamic policy dissection using VQ codes, which clarify hierarchical behaviors and help identify decision-making triggers and potential risks without significantly impacting DRL performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Deep Reinforcement Learning models intrinsically develop semantic clustering capabilities, organizing policy-relevant information dynamically through agent-environment interactions, even without explicit supervision.
> - The Semantic Clustering Module (SCM) effectively sharpens and stabilizes these naturally occurring semantic clusters, providing a more distinct and robust representation for analysis.
> - Integrating interpretable VQ codes into the state features allows for a latent-conditioned policy, offering a foundation for hierarchical learning and providing human-understandable "skills."

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated efficacy across complex decision-making domains such as robotics, autonomous systems, game playing, and healthcare. Despite its successes, the inherent "black-box" nature of DRL models presents a significant challenge to interpretability, potentially introducing unforeseen risks and hindering trust in critical applications. This research addresses the demand for greater transparency in DRL by exploring and enhancing its semantic clustering properties.

Semantic clustering, defined as the grouping of inputs based on their semantic similarity in a feature space, has been extensively studied in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV). However, its application in DRL remains less explored due to the complexities introduced by temporal dynamics and the absence of direct supervised signals. Prior attempts to induce semantic clustering in DRL often relied on external constraints, such as bisimulation metrics or contrastive learning. In contrast, this work investigates the intrinsic capability of DRL to exhibit semantic clustering.

Earlier studies by Mnih et al. (2015) and Zahavy, Ben-Zrihem, and Mannor (2016) utilized t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) to visualize DRL feature spaces in Atari games. However, these approaches faced several limitations:
1. **Environment Specificity**: They were restricted to Atari games with fixed scenes, making it challenging to ascertain whether observed clustering resulted from pixel-level similarities or genuine semantic understanding.
2. **Manual Effort**: The work by Zahavy, Ben-Zrihem, and Mannor (2016) necessitated manual definition of features for specific games, requiring substantial human intervention.
3. **Visualization Instability**: Both studies relied on t-SNE, a visualization technique known for its instability, sensitivity to initialization, and lack of automated clustering mechanisms, which hindered comprehensive semantic analysis and integration into downstream tasks.

This research distinguishes itself by moving beyond fixed-scene environments, employing procedurally generated environments like Procgen to ensure semantic diversity. It aims to develop a stable, automated method for semantic clustering that integrates directly into the DRL training pipeline, thereby overcoming the limitations associated with t-SNE-based analyses and extensive manual annotation. The proposed methodology offers an alternative perspective to other DRL interpretability research, which often focuses on techniques such as prototype-based explanations (PW-Net), saliency maps (DIGR), concept policy models, decision trees, attention mechanisms, or symbolic reasoning frameworks. Instead, this study delves into how DRL models intrinsically organize information and structure learned representations, thereby offering new insights into the internal workings of DRL agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the interpretability of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) models by systematically exploring and leveraging their semantic clustering properties. This exploration aims to deepen understanding of the internal semantic organization within these complex, often opaque, decision-making systems.

The motivation for this work stems from the inherent "black-box" nature of DRL models. While DRL has achieved high performance across various challenging tasks, the lack of transparency in its decision-making processes can lead to unforeseen risks, particularly in safety-critical applications like autonomous systems and healthcare. Understanding *why* a DRL agent makes a particular decision, or *how* it perceives and categorizes its environment, is crucial for building trust, verifying behavior, and debugging potential issues.

Specifically, the authors define semantic clustering in DRL as the process of grouping states that elicit similar agent behaviors under comparable environmental contexts. Studying this phenomenon is motivated by the potential to reveal the model's internal knowledge structure and the semantic relationships it learns between different states. This, in turn, can significantly improve the interpretability and transparency of DRL models.

The research is also driven by the identified limitations of prior efforts in DRL semantic analysis:
1. **Ambiguity in Semantic vs. Pixel-level Clustering**: Previous studies primarily used fixed-scene Atari games. This limited environment made it difficult to definitively determine if observed clusters represented true semantic understanding or merely similarities in pixel patterns. The motivation here is to employ more semantically diverse, procedurally generated environments (Procgen) to ensure that any observed clustering is indicative of learned semantic coherence.
2. **High Manual Effort**: Existing methods often required substantial human effort, either in manually defining features or in laboriously annotating t-SNE visualizations. A key motivation is to develop an automated and integrated clustering mechanism that minimizes manual annotation, making the analysis scalable and practical.
3. **Instability of Visualization Tools**: The reliance on t-SNE for visualizing and analyzing DRL feature spaces introduced instability due to its non-convex objective function and sensitivity to initialization. This motivated the development of a more robust and stable dimensionality reduction and clustering approach that can consistently and effectively reveal semantic properties.

In pursuit of these objectives, the paper outlines three key contributions:
1. A comprehensive exploration of DRL's semantic clustering properties using Procgen environments to advance understanding of DRL's decision-making processes.
2. The introduction of an end-to-end architecture that integrates feature dimensionality reduction with online clustering, designed to offer a stable and effective means of studying semantic properties.
3. The presentation of new analytical methods derived from these properties, intended to provide insights into hierarchical policy organization and identify potential risks within DRL models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates a novel Semantic Clustering Module (SCM) directly into a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) training pipeline. The architecture is built upon a classic DRL framework, using a feature extractor, a policy network, and a value network. The SCM consists of two main components: a Feature Dimensionality Reduction (FDR) network and an online clustering mechanism implemented via a modified Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE).

**Overall Architecture:**
The standard DRL pipeline generates state features. These features are then fed into the SCM. Within the SCM, the FDR network reduces the dimensionality of these features. The reduced-dimensionality features, termed FDR features, are then processed by a vector quantizer to generate discrete VQ codes and their corresponding VQ embeddings. The VQ code is subsequently expanded to match the dimensionality of the original state feature and added element-wise to the state feature. This "fused" feature is then used as input to the policy and value networks for DRL training.

**Semantic Clustering Module (SCM):**

1. **Dimensionality Reduction:**
 - **Motivation**: High-dimensional state features (e.g., 512-dimensional in DQN for Atari) pose challenges for clustering due to the curse of dimensionality and complicate human interpretability. Previous t-SNE-based methods suffered from instability.
 - **FDR Network**: A neural network (e.g., two fully connected layers) remaps high-dimensional DRL state features to a lower-dimensional space, typically 2D, suitable for visualization and robust clustering.
 - **Online Training**: The FDR network is trained online using data batches from the DRL policy training, ensuring that the learned mapping is continuously updated and stable after convergence.
 - **Loss Function (L_FDR)**: Designed to preserve the consistency of distance relationships between the high-dimensional and low-dimensional feature spaces. It calculates pairwise similarities using the Student's t-distribution for both high-dimensional state features ($p_{ij}$) and low-dimensional FDR features ($q_{ij}$). The FDR loss is defined as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between these similarity distributions: $L_{FDR} = -\sum_i \sum_j p_{ij} \log(q_{ij})$. Minimizing this loss encourages the low-dimensional space to retain the neighborhood structure of the high-dimensional features. Crucially, the same degrees of freedom parameter ($\alpha$) is used for both distributions to ensure faithful preservation of relative distances.

2. **Online Clustering:**
 - **Motivation**: Traditional t-SNE analyses require laborious per-state inspection and manual grouping due to indistinct cluster boundaries. An automated, online approach is necessary for scalability and downstream integration.
 - **Modified VQ-VAE**: The clustering is implemented using a modified VQ-VAE. The key modification focuses on the codebook updates. The method assigns each FDR feature to its nearest embedding ($e_k$) in a learnable codebook.
 - **Modified VQ-VAE Loss (L'_VQ-VAE)**: This loss term specifically targets moving the VQ embeddings closer to the FDR features assigned to them. It is defined as $L'_{VQ-VAE} = ||sg[g(f(s))] - e_k||^2_2$, where $sg$ is a stop-gradient operator. This term effectively acts as an online k-means update, where the VQ embeddings serve as cluster centroids, aligning with the batch-wise distance preservation objective. Other terms from the original VQ-VAE loss are omitted as they are not essential for this clustering objective.

**Total Loss Function:**
The overall training objective combines the standard DRL loss ($L_{DRL}$) with the SCM losses:
$L_{total} = L_{DRL} + \lambda_{ctrl} (w_{FDR} L_{FDR} + w_{VQ-VAE} L'_{VQ-VAE})$
- $L_{DRL}$: The loss function of the chosen DRL algorithm (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in this work).
- $w_{FDR}$ and $w_{VQ-VAE}$: Hyperparameters weighting the contributions of the FDR and modified VQ-VAE losses, respectively.
- $\lambda_{ctrl}$: An adaptive control factor that scales the contribution of the SCM losses. This factor starts low and increases with DRL performance (average episode score) to allow the DRL model to develop a meaningful feature space before the clustering module significantly influences it. This strategy ensures that semantic clustering is enhanced when the policy is stable and the feature space is sufficiently organized.

**Training Process and Enhancements:**
The training proceeds iteratively, collecting trajectories and updating DRL and SCM parameters jointly. The integration of VQ codes (discrete cluster assignments) into the state features (by element-wise addition after expansion) enables a latent-conditioned policy, $\pi(a|s, k)$, which supports extensions to hierarchical learning tasks. The online nature of training offers memory efficiency by not requiring storage of large state sets. The joint training, particularly with the stop-gradient in $L'_{VQ-VAE}$, leads to denser clusters in the FDR space and tighter, more separable clusters of semantically similar states.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated primarily on Procgen environments (CoinRun, Ninja, Jumper, FruitBot), which offer diverse, procedurally generated levels, crucial for validating generalized semantic understanding. State collection for visualization involves using a trained model with a probability of random action selection to ensure diverse state coverage.

**Analytical Methods:**
Beyond quantitative evaluation, the approach introduces qualitative and semi-quantitative analytical methods:
1. **Visualization Tools**: An interactive visualization tool allows hovering over FDR features to display corresponding state images and supports zooming for detailed cluster inspection.
2. **Dynamic Policy Analysis**: VQ codes enable semantic segmentation of entire episodes, revealing hierarchical policy structures and providing human-understandable explanations of agent behavior transitions. This helps in dissecting complex policies and identifying decision-making triggers or potential issues.
3. **Human Evaluation**: A structured human study is conducted where evaluators review video clips grouped by cluster and their natural language descriptions, assessing consistency, matching to descriptions, and overall interpretability using Likert scales.
4. **Ablation Studies**: Experiments involving disabling parts of the SCM (e.g., stop-gradient ablation) are performed to understand the intrinsic nature of semantic clustering in DRL and the module's contribution to sharpening these clusters.
5. **Quantitative Comparison of DR Methods**: Statistical metrics (Silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin Index, Calinski–Harabasz score) are used to compare the clustering quality of the FDR space against other dimensionality reduction techniques (UMAP, Isomap, PCA, MDS, t-SNE).
6. **Temporal Coherence Metrics**: Episode Cluster Entropy (ECE) and Temporal Cluster Agreement (TCA@k) are used to assess the stability and focus of semantic grouping within episodes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the intrinsic semantic clustering properties of DRL and the effectiveness of the proposed methodology in enhancing interpretability.

**Clustering Effectiveness and Stability:**
The proposed method demonstrates superior clustering effectiveness and stability compared to traditional t-SNE visualizations.
- **Clearer Cluster Separation**: t-SNE visualizations of a baseline PPO model produced widely dispersed features without forming distinct clusters, limiting analytical utility. In contrast, the t-SNE visualization of the proposed method revealed numerous small, distinct clusters, which correspond to semantically identified groups from the FDR space. The FDR space itself displayed clear, well-separated, and complete clusters identified by VQ codes (Figure 2a, 2b, 2d).
- **Robustness to Data and Initialization**: The FDR space exhibited stability when the number of processed states was reduced by 50% or when different random seeds were used, maintaining its spatial distribution. t-SNE, however, showed significant changes in feature distribution and representation under these varying conditions (Figure 2c, 2e, 2f). This indicates that the FDR network produces a stable feature mapping after training, addressing a key limitation of t-SNE.
- **Quantitative Validation**: Statistical comparisons of clustering quality across various dimensionality reduction methods (UMAP, Isomap, PCA, MDS, t-SNE) demonstrated that the proposed method's FDR space achieved higher Silhouette scores, lower Davies–Bouldin Indices, and significantly higher Calinski–Harabasz scores, confirming superior compactness within clusters and separation between them (Appendix E, Figure E.1).

**Semantic Clustering in DRL:**
The study provides evidence that semantic clustering is an intrinsic property of DRL, not solely a result of external constraints or explicit supervision.
- **Policy-Relevant Grouping**: The model learned to group semantically similar and temporally adjacent states into the same clusters (Figure 3, Table 1). For instance, in the Ninja game, sequences of states depicting "agent performing a high jump" consistently fell into a single cluster.
- **Beyond Pixel Similarity**: This clustering extends beyond mere pixel distances, operating at a level of semantic understanding of the environment, such as identifying a higher ledge to jump to or approaching a specific object. The clusters reflect dynamic environmental interactions rather than static image content.
- **Intrinsic Nature**: An ablation study (stop-gradient experiment, Appendix C.2) showed that while the semantic clustering module enhances cluster separability, states still exhibited similar semantic interpretations even when the module's influence on the feature space was removed. This suggests that DRL intrinsically forms these semantic groupings, and the proposed module primarily sharpens these naturally occurring clusters.
- **Temporal Coherence**: The proposed method consistently achieved lower Episode Cluster Entropy (ECE) and higher Temporal Cluster Agreement (TCA@3/6) compared to the stop-gradient ablation (Appendix F, Table 5). This indicates more focused semantic grouping within episodes and smoother transitions between semantically consistent states.

**Human Interpretability and Evaluation:**
Human evaluators validated the interpretability benefits of the proposed approach.
- **High Agreement Scores**: 15 human evaluators, reviewing segmented video clips and cluster descriptions, provided mean Likert scores largely above 4 (on a 5-point scale) for statements like "The clips of each cluster consistently display the same skill being performed" and "The identified skills aid in understanding the environment and the AI’s decision-making process" (Table 2).
- **Summarizable Behaviors**: Video sequences within clusters could be summarized using natural language, akin to "skills" in human learning, thereby making the agent's behavior understandable.

**Model and Policy Analysis Capabilities:**
The research introduces new analytical methods leveraging semantic clustering.
- **Interactive Visualization**: A tool enables static analysis by displaying corresponding state images upon hovering over FDR feature points, facilitating detailed inspection of feature distributions within clusters (Figure 5).
- **Dynamic Policy Dissection**: The VQ codes allow for semantic segmentation of episodes (Figure 4), effectively breaking down complex policies into sequences of interpretable "skills." This helps in understanding hierarchical policy structures.
- **Identification of Decision-Making Triggers and Risks**: Policy analysis examples demonstrated how the model clarifies behavioral shifts based on environmental cues (e.g., detecting a right-side wall in Ninja, Figure 6). It also highlighted potential risks, such as an agent in Jumper relying solely on radar data for decision-making, even when visual information (e.g., presence of a carrot) might be contradictory (Appendix G.2, Figure G.3).

**Impact on DRL Performance:**
The integration of the semantic clustering module had minimal impact on the agent's overall performance. Performance curves for the proposed method closely aligned with baseline PPO performance across various Procgen environments, indicating that the module primarily reorganizes existing information without significantly altering the learned policy's effectiveness (Appendix C.1, Figure C.1).

**Influence of VQ Embeddings (K):**
The number of VQ embeddings (K) did not affect model performance. However, for interpretability, K required careful selection. Too few clusters (e.g., K=4) resulted in clusters containing multiple distinct semantic explanations, reducing clarity. Too many clusters (e.g., K=12) led to over-fragmentation, making coherent semantic explanations difficult. K=8 was found to balance interpretability and granularity, ensuring a one-to-one mapping between clusters and explanations (Appendix D, Table 3, Figure D.2).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) by significantly advancing its interpretability and providing a deeper understanding of the internal mechanisms through which DRL agents organize environmental information.

**Advancing DRL Interpretability**: The primary significance lies in offering a robust, automated method to peer into the "black box" of DRL models. By revealing the semantic organization of learned feature spaces, this work provides human-understandable insights into *what* an agent perceives and *why* it makes certain decisions. This is critical for increasing trust and adoption of DRL in real-world applications, especially in domains requiring high levels of scrutiny and accountability.

**Novel Methodological Contribution**: The proposed architecture, integrating dimensionality reduction and online clustering with DRL training, represents a novel approach. It overcomes key limitations of prior interpretability methods, particularly the instability and manual annotation burden associated with t-SNE. The end-to-end, online nature of the module makes it practical for integration into existing DRL frameworks. The theoretical analysis of the loss functions further underpins the method's ability to preserve semantic neighborhood structure and emulate online k-means.

**Revealing Intrinsic DRL Properties**: A notable finding is the demonstration that DRL models intrinsically develop semantic clustering capabilities, emerging dynamically from agent-environment interactions. This suggests that the internal organization of policy-relevant knowledge is an inherent characteristic of successful DRL learning, providing a fundamental insight into how these complex systems abstract and categorize their experiences. This intrinsic property differentiates DRL semantic clustering from static clustering observed in NLP or CV, highlighting the dynamic nature of DRL learning.

**Enhanced Policy Analysis and Risk Identification**: The new analytical methods, including interactive visualization and dynamic policy segmentation via VQ codes, offer powerful tools for researchers and practitioners. These methods enable detailed dissection of complex policies, uncovering hierarchical structures of learned behaviors. Crucially, they facilitate the identification of specific decision-making triggers and potential failure modes or undesirable behaviors, such as over-reliance on a single sensory input (e.g., radar over visual cues in Jumper). This capability is vital for debugging, refining, and ensuring the safety of DRL systems.

**Potential for Downstream Applications**: The interpretable VQ codes, representing distinct semantic "skills," hold significant potential for facilitating various downstream tasks. These include:
- **Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning**: The semantic segmentation of policies can naturally inform the design of hierarchical agents, where high-level policies choose among learned "skills" (clusters).
- **Skill Discovery**: The method offers a pathway to automatically discover and define meaningful skills or sub-behaviors within complex tasks.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Systems**: Providing interpretable segments of agent behavior can enable more effective human oversight and intervention, improving collaboration between human operators and autonomous systems.
- **Automated Explanation Generation**: Future work aiming to automate natural language summarization of clusters (e.g., using large language models) could further enhance the utility of this approach for generating human-readable explanations.

**Broader Societal Impact**: By making DRL models more understandable, this research has positive implications for the deployment of DRL in safety-critical domains such as autonomous vehicles, medical diagnosis, and robotics. Increased interpretability can lead to more reliable, trustworthy, and auditable AI systems, contributing to public safety and acceptance. While the paper itself focuses on foundational machine learning research and game environments, its principles are broadly applicable. The authors note no immediate specific societal or ethical risks for the core contribution but encourage responsible application in sensitive domains.

**Avenues for Future Work**: The acknowledged limitations, such as dependence on clear semantic distributions, the need for adaptive cluster number selection, and the current reliance on manual policy interpretation, provide clear directions for future research. Addressing these limitations could lead to even more robust, adaptable, and fully automated interpretability solutions for DRL.
