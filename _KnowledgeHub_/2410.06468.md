# Does Spatial Cognition Emerge in Frontier Models?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Apple introduced SPACE, a new benchmark to systematically evaluate spatial cognition in frontier large language and multimodal models. Their findings demonstrate that these models consistently perform significantly below human levels on classic spatial tests, particularly when faced with complex or immersive spatial scenarios.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) and Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) show impressive performance in various cognitive tasks, leading to broad characterizations of their general intelligence.
- Spatial cognition, a foundational form of intelligence universally present in biological systems and linked to embodiment, has been comparatively under-evaluated in disembodied frontier AI models.
- A need exists for a rigorous, cognitively-informed benchmark to diagnose the presence and limitations of spatial reasoning in advanced AI models.

## Method

- Developed **SPACE** (==Spatial Perception And Cognition Evaluation==), a novel benchmark built on decades of cognitive science research and classic animal cognition experiments.
- **SPACE** includes two broad categories: Large-Scale Spatial Cognition (e.g., direction/distance estimation, map sketching, navigation) and Small-Scale Spatial Cognition (e.g., ==mental rotation==, ==perspective taking==, maze completion).
- Tasks are presented in diverse modalities, including egocentric images, discrete map images, and discrete map text arrays, to evaluate both multimodal and text-only models.

## Results

- Frontier models performed near chance level on most large-scale spatial cognition tasks, particularly with egocentric (first-person) observations, significantly below human performance (e.g., **80-100%** for humans vs. near chance for models).
- Even with simplified allocentric (map-like) views, models struggled with navigation tasks like route retracing and shortcut discovery, achieving less than **30%** Success weighted by Path Length (SPL).
- While some LLMs showed relative strength in textual Selective Attention (over **95%** accuracy) and visuospatial working memory tasks, multimodal models performed poorly (near chance) on visual mental rotation and perspective taking, confirming a lack of genuine spatial reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frontier models possess a fundamentally different intelligence profile, excelling in abstract domains while demonstrating severe deficiencies in foundational spatial cognition, a pattern unlike biological intelligence.
> - The SPACE benchmark acts as a crucial diagnostic tool, revealing specific weaknesses in AI models' spatial perception, reasoning, and memory that can guide future AI development.
> - The results challenge assumptions that general intelligence emerges solely from vast datasets, suggesting that embodiment or novel architectural/training paradigms may be critical for robust spatial intelligence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in large language models (LLMs) and large multimodal models (LMMs) have led to impressive performance across a wide array of cognitive tasks, including mathematics, coding, general knowledge, and commonsense reasoning. These successes have sometimes inspired broad characterizations of AI models as possessing human-level or even super-intelligence. This paper critically engages with such claims by turning its attention to spatial cognition, a foundational form of intelligence that is universally present and extensively studied in a broad spectrum of animals, including humans.

Historically, the development of spatial cognition in biological intelligence has been strongly linked to **embodiment** – the ability to perceive and interact physically with one's surroundings. Animals and humans build mental representations of objects and environments through physical traversal and manipulation. However, frontier AI models are predominantly trained in a disembodied manner on vast corpora of text, images, and videos, leading to the central question posed by this research: "Does spatial cognition emerge in disembodied frontier models?"

This work is situated within a broader research landscape that aims to rigorously evaluate and diagnose the capabilities and limitations of advanced AI models. While existing literature has explored:
- **Emergent Spatial Representations:** Some neural networks trained from scratch for path integration or navigation tasks have shown the emergence of grid-like representations, similar to those found in biological brains. This work, however, focuses specifically on whether *frontier models* – pre-trained at massive scales – exhibit general spatial cognition.
- **Spatial Reasoning in LLMs:** Other benchmarks like PlanBench, CogEval, Yamada et al., and EWOK have evaluated LLMs on text-based planning, navigation, or spatial plausibility reasoning. "DOES SPATIAL COGNITION EMERGE IN FRONTIER MODELS?" distinguishes itself by encompassing a broader array of cognitive abilities within spatial reasoning and, crucially, by implementing multimodal presentations of *classic animal cognition experiments*, providing a more direct comparison to biological intelligence.
- **Benchmarks for Large Multimodal Models:** There is a growing ecosystem of benchmarks for LMMs, addressing various aspects from general image understanding (VQA, image captioning) to spatiotemporal reasoning in video. Recent diagnostic benchmarks (e.g., HallusionBench, MMVP, MMStar, SpatialVLM, SpatialRGPT, MOCHI, Perception Test) have highlighted shortcomings in LMMs related to fine-grained perception, visual illusions, or the lack of genuine spatial perception and reasoning. This paper builds upon and contributes to this diagnostic trend by providing a dedicated and comprehensive benchmark (SPACE) specifically designed to probe a wide range of spatial cognitive abilities, which have been comparatively under-evaluated in frontier models.

By systematically evaluating spatial cognition through the lens of cognitive science, this research offers a unique perspective that bridges traditional cognitive science and modern AI. It challenges the assumption that mastery in some advanced cognitive domains automatically implies broad intelligence, echoing Brooks's first law of AI, and sets the stage for a deeper, more nuanced understanding of artificial intelligence's current capabilities and future needs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **systematically evaluate the emergence of spatial cognition in contemporary frontier models, specifically large language models (LLMs) and large multimodal models (LMMs).**

This primary objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Critical Assessment of AI Capabilities:** Recent advancements in LLMs and LMMs have led to impressive performance in various complex tasks, prompting widespread discussions and sometimes overinflated claims about their general intelligence or impending superintelligence. The authors, referencing Brooks's first law of AI, motivate their work by arguing for a critical and empirical assessment of these models' capabilities, particularly in foundational cognitive domains.
- **Focus on Foundational Intelligence:** Spatial cognition is not just another skill; it is a fundamental and pervasive form of intelligence across the animal kingdom, underpinning the ability to perceive, interact with, and navigate physical environments. It is known to be a prerequisite for more advanced cognitive abilities in biological systems. By evaluating spatial cognition, the research aims to probe whether frontier models possess this basic, yet crucial, building block of intelligence.
- **Bridging AI and Cognitive Science:** The benchmark, SPACE, is explicitly built on "decades of research in cognitive science" and incorporates classic tests of animal cognition. This multidisciplinary approach is motivated by the desire to leverage established scientific understanding of intelligence for AI evaluation, moving beyond task-specific metrics to more generalizable cognitive abilities.
- **Addressing the Embodiment Question:** Biological evidence strongly links the development of spatial cognition to embodiment and direct interaction with the physical world. Frontier models, however, are largely disembodied, trained on passive data. A key motivation is to explore whether spatial cognition can still emerge in such disembodied systems, or if embodiment is indeed a necessary condition for its development in artificial intelligence.
- **Diagnostic Tool for AI Development:** By identifying specific areas where frontier models fall short, the SPACE benchmark serves as a diagnostic tool. This is motivated by the need to pinpoint current limitations to guide future AI research, potentially leading to the development of more robust, general-purpose, and "intelligent" AI systems.
- **Rethinking the Nature of Intelligence:** The potential discrepancy between high performance in symbolic or abstract tasks and poor performance in spatial cognition in AI models is highly intriguing from a scientific standpoint. This motivates the authors to inspire a "new cognitive science" that can explain the unique constellation of cognitive traits observed in artificial intelligence, free from direct analogies to biological systems.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide empirical evidence to answer whether frontier AI models truly possess broad cognitive competence, using spatial cognition as a critical litmus test derived from cognitive science.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research's methodology is the introduction of **SPACE (Spatial Perception And Cognition Evaluation)**, a novel benchmark designed to systematically evaluate spatial cognition in frontier AI models. The benchmark is meticulously constructed based on decades of research in cognitive science and is tailored for both text-only (LLMs) and multimodal (LMMs) models.

The SPACE benchmark is structured around two broad classes of spatial cognition tasks:

#### 3.1. Large-Scale Spatial Cognition

These tasks assess a model's ability to understand its surroundings, build mental representations of environments, and use these representations for navigation and reasoning.
- **Familiarization Phase:** Models are first exposed to an environment via a "video walkthrough." For multimodal models, this is a sequence of egocentric (first-person) or discrete map (allocentric, bird's-eye view) images. For text-only models, the walkthrough is presented as a sequence of discrete map text arrays (character arrays representing the local environment).
- **Evaluation Tasks (5 types):**
 1. **Direction Estimation:** Given a landmark (A) and facing direction, estimate the direction (in degrees) to another landmark (B). Formulated as multiple-choice QA.
 2. **Distance Estimation:** Given a landmark (A) and facing direction, estimate straight-line Euclidean distances to other landmarks. Formulated as multiple-choice QA, with incorrect options distorting distance ratios.
 3. **Map Sketching:** After familiarization, models must choose the correct bird's-eye map of the environment from multiple-choice options that accurately preserves spatial relationships.
 4. **Route Retracing:** An interactive task where the model must retrace a previously observed (shortest) route from start to goal. Performance is measured by Success weighted by Path Length (SPL).
 5. **Shortcut Discovery:** An interactive task where the model observes a suboptimal route and must then discover and navigate a shorter, novel route to the goal. Also measured by SPL.
- **Implementation Details:**
 - **3D Environment Generation:** Ten distinct 3D environment layouts are created based on cognitive science protocols, with 3 variations each, totaling 30 environments. These environments are populated with visual landmarks (ImageNet images on walls) and simulated using the Habitat simulator.
 - **Observation Spaces:**
 - **Egocentric (Ego) Image:** First-person camera views, requiring understanding of perspective geometry.
 - **Discrete Map (DM) Image:** Quantized, allocentric bird's-eye view of the immediate surroundings (2.5m x 2.5m), simplifying perspective issues.
 - **Discrete Map (DM) Text:** Text array representation of the DM image, used for text-only LLMs.

#### 3.2. Small-Scale Spatial Cognition

These tasks evaluate the model's ability to perceive, imagine, and mentally transform objects or shapes in 2D and 3D, drawing heavily from classic visuospatial ability tests in humans.
- **Tasks (10 types):**
 1. **Mental Rotation Test (MRT):** Mentally manipulating 2D (text) or 3D (visual) stimuli to identify rotated versions.
 2. **Perspective Taking Test (PTT):** Imagining a new viewpoint and determining relative orientations of objects.
 3. **Water Level Test (WLT):** Predicting the horizontal water surface in a tilted container (visual-only).
 4. **Minnesota Paper Form Board (MPFB):** Mentally assembling puzzle pieces into a coherent shape.
 5. **Judgement of Line Orientation (JLO):** Determining the angle between two lines by matching them to reference lines.
 6. **Selective Attention Task (SAtt):** Identifying target stimuli amidst distractors in a grid.
 7. **Maze Completion Task (MCT):** An interactive game requiring planning and navigation through a maze (full visibility).
 8. **Corsi Block-Tapping Task (CBTT):** Remembering and reproducing a sequence of tapped blocks on a board (visuospatial working memory).
 9. **Spatial Addition Task (SAdd):** Adding two 2D grids based on specific rules, testing spatial information manipulation in memory.
 10. **Cambridge Spatial Working Memory (CSWM):** An interactive game requiring models to remember spatial locations of hidden treasures as numerical labels randomize.
- **Presentations:** Most small-scale tasks have both visual and purely textual (character array-based) presentations to evaluate LMMs and LLMs, respectively. The textual versions often simplify the spatial complexity (e.g., 2D vs. 3D rotation).

#### 3.3. Evaluation Protocol

- **Models:** A diverse set of frontier LLMs (GPT-4v, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama3 family, Mistral models, Yi 1.5) and LMMs (LlaVA-NeXT-Interleave, Pixtral 12B, Phi-3.5-vision) are benchmarked.
- **Prompting:** Zero-shot prompting is used, with detailed task descriptions and expected response formats provided.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against a random chance baseline.
- **Human Performance:** A human baseline is established for multiple-choice QA tasks by evaluating 29 participants, providing a crucial reference point for biological intelligence.
- **Ecological Compatibility Test (Appendix A.1):** A critical diagnostic step to ensure that models *understand* the input modalities (e.g., image content, grid structure, landmark identities) even if they fail at spatial reasoning tasks. This rules out input comprehension as the primary reason for poor performance.

This comprehensive methodological approach ensures a rigorous and multifaceted evaluation of spatial cognition in frontier AI models, directly comparing their capabilities against human performance and grounding the assessment in established cognitive science principles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core finding of the paper is definitive: **contemporary frontier models, despite their impressive capabilities in other domains, broadly fall short of the spatial intelligence observed in animals and humans.** Their performance on many classic tests of animal cognition is often near chance level.

Here’s a breakdown of the main findings:

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- Frontier models consistently performed significantly below human levels on most spatial cognition tasks across both large-scale and small-scale categories.
- A key takeaway is that these models exhibit a fundamentally different form of intelligence, excelling in some aspects of "higher cognition" while failing profoundly in basic spatial cognition, a pattern not observed in biological intelligence.

#### 4.2. Large-Scale Spatial Cognition Results

- **Near Chance Level for Multimodal Models (Ego Image):** When presented with egocentric (first-person immersive) observations, which most closely mimic how animals experience environments, all evaluated multimodal models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4v) performed near chance level on all tasks: direction estimation, distance estimation, map sketching, route retracing, and shortcut discovery. This indicates a severe lack of environmental understanding and navigation capabilities in a realistic perception setting.
- **Poor Performance Even with Allocentric Views (DM Image/Text):** Even when provided with simplified, allocentric (map-like) views (DM image and DM text), models struggled significantly.
 - On direction and distance estimation, and map sketching, models generally scored below 50% accuracy (human performance ranged from 66.7% to 100%).
 - On interactive tasks like route retracing and shortcut discovery, models achieved less than 30% SPL (Success weighted by Path Length), indicating poor navigation and planning, even with a clear bird's-eye map.
 - Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4o showed the best, albeit still low, average performance on DM text and DM image modalities, respectively, but remained far from human competency.
- **Human Performance:** Humans demonstrated high accuracy (80-100%) on image-based multiple-choice tasks, dropping slightly (65-80%) for text-based presentations due to the inherent difficulty of processing large text arrays.

#### 4.3. Small-Scale Spatial Cognition Results

- **Modality-Specific Performance Differences:** Performance varied significantly between multimodal and purely textual presentations for the same tasks.
 - For tasks like Mental Rotation (MRT), Minnesota Paper Form Board (MPFB), and Judgement of Line Orientation (JLO), models performed much better with textual inputs. The authors attribute this partly to the simplified nature of the text-only implementations (e.g., 2D vs. 3D rotations, constrained shapes).
 - The best-performing models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o) achieved ~65% average accuracy with textual presentations, but only ~40% with multimodal presentations (where chance is <25%).
- **Areas of Relative Strength (Textual):**
 - On **Selective Attention (SAtt)**, top LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Mistral 123B, GPT-4v) achieved over 95% accuracy with textual inputs, matching or exceeding human performance. This suggests proficiency in selective spatial attention when information is encoded symbolically.
 - On **Corsi Block Tapping (CBTT)** and **Spatial Addition (SAdd)**, some LLMs also showed strong performance (e.g., 80-97%), indicating decent visuospatial working memory capabilities with abstract representations.
- **Persistent Weaknesses:**
 - All models performed poorly on **Maze Completion (MCT)** in both modalities, despite full visibility of the maze, highlighting significant deficiencies in spatial planning and executive functioning.
 - With multimodal presentation, models were near chance on **Perspective Taking (PTT)** and the **Minnesota Paper Form Board (MPFB)**.
 - On **Mental Rotation (MRT)**, multimodal models were near chance (3D shapes), and text-only models (2D arrays) were only marginally better, indicating a general struggle with mental manipulation of objects.
 - The **Water Level Test (WLT)**, a visual-only task, also saw models performing poorly (around 30-59%), struggling with basic spatial perception of physical properties.

#### 4.4. Ecological Compatibility

- The supplementary tests confirmed that models could largely comprehend the raw inputs (images, text arrays) for non-spatial tasks (e.g., counting objects, identifying landmarks, basic localization), ruling out input understanding as the primary reason for failure in spatial cognition tasks. The failures are indeed due to a lack of spatial reasoning abilities.

In summary, the results paint a clear picture: current frontier models possess limited to no genuine spatial cognition, especially when dealing with complex or immersive spatial scenarios. Their strengths appear concentrated in tasks that can be solved through pattern matching on abstract symbolic representations (textual arrays) rather than deep spatial understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "DOES SPATIAL COGNITION EMERGE IN FRONTIER MODELS?" holds significant implications for both the scientific understanding of artificial intelligence and the future trajectory of AI development.

#### 5.1. Re-evaluation of AI Intelligence and the "Superintelligence" Narrative

The most profound impact of this work is its direct challenge to the often-optimistic characterizations of frontier models as possessing broad cognitive competence, akin to or exceeding human intelligence. By rigorously demonstrating severe deficiencies in a foundational intelligence like spatial cognition—a capability present across a vast range of biological organisms, from mice to humans—the paper urges a more sober and scientific assessment. It reinforces Brooks's Law of AI, cautioning against overinflating estimates of general competence based on mastery in specific, often abstract, domains. This nuanced perspective is crucial for responsible discourse and development in AI.

#### 5.2. Inspiration for a "New Cognitive Science"

The observation that AI models can excel in highly complex, abstract tasks (e.g., mathematics, coding) while simultaneously failing at seemingly simple, foundational spatial tasks is unprecedented in biological intelligence. In humans and animals, spatial cognition is often a prerequisite for higher cognitive functions, and its breakdown is diagnostic of disorders. This unique "constellation of traits" in frontier models, as highlighted by the authors, presents a fascinating new phenomenon that demands a fresh scientific inquiry. The paper suggests that this could inspire a "new cognitive science" dedicated to understanding these non-biological forms of intelligence, without necessarily relying on analogies from human or animal cognition.

#### 5.3. Diagnostic Value and Roadmap for Future AI Development

The SPACE benchmark itself is a significant contribution. It provides a comprehensive, systematic, and cognitively-informed diagnostic tool for identifying specific weaknesses in AI models' spatial perception, reasoning, and memory. This diagnostic capability is invaluable for guiding future research. The results indicate that simply scaling models or feeding them more diverse data might not be sufficient to imbue them with robust spatial cognition. This could catalyze:
- **Architectural Innovations:** New model architectures specifically designed to capture spatial relationships and geometric understanding.
- **Training Paradigms:** Exploration of novel training methodologies, potentially incorporating elements of embodied learning, active perception, or more sophisticated forms of spatial pre-training beyond passive observation of text and images.
- **Integration with Embodied AI:** The paper implicitly reignites the debate around the necessity of embodiment. If disembodied training proves insufficient for spatial cognition, it could lend strong support to research in embodied AI, robotics, and physically grounded learning as essential pathways for developing truly general and robust AI.

#### 5.4. Addressing "Puzzling Breakdowns" in Higher-Level Tasks

The authors raise an intriguing hypothesis: could the identified deficiencies in spatial cognition be causally linked to some of the "puzzling breakdowns" observed in frontier models when performing other, seemingly higher-level tasks? Many complex real-world problems inherently involve spatial reasoning, even if not explicitly framed as such. A lack of robust spatial grounding might manifest as brittle reasoning, poor planning, or an inability to generalize in scenarios with spatial components. Addressing these foundational spatial gaps could, therefore, lead to more robust, reliable, and generally capable AI systems.

In conclusion, this research is a critical and timely intervention in the AI landscape. It provides empirical evidence of a fundamental gap in frontier models' capabilities, challenges prevailing assumptions about general intelligence in AI, and offers a clear, actionable benchmark to guide the next generation of AI research towards developing more spatially intelligent and, potentially, more broadly competent artificial agents.
