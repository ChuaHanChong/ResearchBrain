# Evolving Curricula with Regret-Based Environment Design

> [!summary] Summary
> ACCEL (Adversarially Compounding Complexity by Editing Levels) is an unsupervised environment design method that makes small random edits to previously high-regret training levels and keeps only the edits that still score high regret. Its curricula start simple and grow steadily more complex. ACCEL keeps minimax-regret robustness guarantees and beats PLR, PAIRED and domain randomization on mazes and BipedalWalker, using a fraction of POET's compute.

## Problem

- Regret-based teachers like PAIRED must learn to design levels through sparse-reward, long-horizon RL, which is hard to train and often fails to converge in practice.
- Robust PLR only curates randomly sampled levels, so it cannot build on structures it has already found and suffers the curse of dimensionality in large design spaces.
- Evolutionary methods like POET rely on domain-specific heuristics such as hand-set reward ranges, need CPU clusters, and produce populations of specialists rather than one generalist agent.

## Method

- ==Level editing==: small random mutations (adding or removing maze blocks, moving goals, perturbing terrain parameters) applied to replayed ==high-regret levels==, assuming regret changes smoothly between nearby levels.
- ==Regret-based curator==: each edited level is scored by ==positive value loss==, a GAE-based regret proxy, and enters the ==replay buffer== only if its regret is high; the student trains only on replayed levels.
- ==Start-simple initialization==: generation begins from minimal base levels such as empty rooms or flat terrain, so complexity emerges purely from regret-driven editing without novelty bonuses or domain rules.

## Results

- MiniGrid mazes: aggregate zero-shot IQM nears a perfect solve rate versus under **80%** for PLR, and ACCEL reaches **53%** success versus **25%** on large 51x51 PerfectMaze.
- 8D BipedalWalker: ACCEL reaches close to **75%** of optimal normalized score, almost three times the best baseline PLR, while using under **0.5%** of POET's environment steps.
- 5D POET comparison: ACCEL solves Hardcore **0.65** versus **0.21** for PLR and extreme levels **12%** versus **1%**; ablations show editing helps and starting simple matters most in BipedalWalker.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Regret can serve as a domain-agnostic fitness function, letting evolutionary level search replace hand-crafted filters like reward ranges while keeping game-theoretic robustness guarantees.
> - Mutating levels already at the frontier finds new frontier levels far more efficiently than random sampling, because difficulty tends to change smoothly under small edits.
> - Emergent complexity depends on a simple starting point, and this start-simple bias may be unavailable in domains where the simplest levels are themselves complex.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields within artificial intelligence: Reinforcement Learning (RL), Automatic Curriculum Learning (ACL), Unsupervised Environment Design (UED), and Evolutionary Computation (EC). The overarching goal is to address a critical challenge in modern RL: training agents that are not only capable but also *robust* and *generalize* systematically to unseen or out-of-distribution environments.

Traditionally, RL agents are trained in fixed environments, which often leads to overfitting and poor generalization. **Domain Randomization (DR)** emerged as a simple solution, randomizing environment parameters during training to force agents to learn more robust policies. While effective, DR can be inefficient as it might include many unhelpful or too-easy/too-hard environments.

To overcome this, **Automatic Curriculum Learning (ACL)** and **Unsupervised Environment Design (UED)** aim to adapt the training distribution to the student agent's current capabilities.
- **ACL** broadly encompasses methods that dynamically adjust tasks or goals.
- **UED** specifically focuses on designing or generating entire environment instances (called "levels") themselves. Key approaches in UED include:
 - **PAIRED (Dennis et al., 2020):** This work formalized UED as a zero-sum game between a student agent and a teacher agent. The teacher's objective is to maximize the "regret" of the student (the difference between optimal performance and the student's current performance on a given level). This framework offers theoretical guarantees: at equilibrium, the student converges to a minimax regret policy, capable of solving all solvable environments. However, training such an adversarial teacher in practice is often challenging and computationally intensive, especially in complex, high-dimensional design spaces.
 - **Prioritized Level Replay (PLR) (Jiang et al., 2021a,b):** As a practical improvement over PAIRED, PLR uses a simpler approach. Instead of training an explicit teacher to *design* levels, it relies on random sampling of levels and then *curates* a buffer of those levels that induce high regret (approximated by metrics like positive value loss). The student agent primarily trains on these high-regret levels, which effectively provides a curriculum. PLR has shown strong empirical results but is limited by its reliance on random search for new levels, which becomes inefficient in very large or sparse design spaces, and it cannot leverage structures of previously found challenging levels.

Alongside UED, **Evolutionary Computation (EC)**, particularly methods like **POET (Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer) (Wang et al., 2019, 2020)**, has explored "open-ended learning." POET co-evolves a population of agent-environment pairs, continuously generating new, increasingly complex and diverse environments and corresponding specialized agents to solve them. This approach naturally discovers novel challenges and can lead to impressive levels of emergent complexity. However, POET's main drawbacks are its reliance on domain-specific heuristics (e.g., manually setting reward thresholds to define "solvable" tasks) and its immense computational cost, requiring large CPU clusters to maintain and train a population of agents. It also produces multiple specialized agents rather than a single generalist.

ACCEL attempts to bridge the gap between these two lines of research. It seeks to harness the *open-ended complexity generation* and *leveraging of discovered structures* from evolutionary methods like POET, while maintaining the *principled, domain-agnostic, and theoretically grounded regret-based objectives* of UED methods like PAIRED and PLR, all while drastically reducing the computational footprint.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) algorithm, ACCEL, that can effectively generate challenging and diverse curricula for training robust, general-purpose reinforcement learning agents. The core motivation stems from the observed limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Addressing Practical Challenges of Regret-Based UED:** While regret-based UED methods like PAIRED offer strong theoretical guarantees for generalization, they face significant practical hurdles. Training an adversarial teacher to *design* high-regret levels is a difficult RL problem in itself, often leading to unstable training or failure to find effective levels in complex design spaces. PLR simplifies this by *curating* randomly sampled levels, but its effectiveness degrades as the environment design space grows, as randomly sampling sufficiently challenging levels becomes exceedingly rare.
2. **Overcoming Limitations of Evolutionary Approaches:** Evolutionary methods like POET excel at generating complex, open-ended environments. However, they typically rely on handcrafted, domain-specific heuristics (e.g., predefined reward ranges for "solvability") to guide the evolution. This limits their generality and transferability to new domains. Furthermore, these methods are computationally expensive, requiring vast resources (e.g., CPU clusters) to co-evolve populations of agents and environments, and they often yield a collection of specialized agents rather than a single generalist.
3. **Achieving "Best of Both Worlds":** The authors are motivated to combine the strengths of these disparate approaches. They aim for an algorithm that:
 - Maintains the principled, domain-agnostic theoretical benefits of regret-based UED (specifically, the minimax regret objective that naturally promotes levels at the frontier of the agent's capabilities).
 - Leverages the power of evolutionary processes to incrementally build complexity and directly extend previously discovered useful environment structures, avoiding the "curse of dimensionality" inherent in random sampling.
 - Significantly reduces computational demands compared to population-based evolutionary methods, ideally allowing training on a single GPU.
 - Produces a *single, generalist agent* capable of robust performance across a wide range of challenges, rather than a collection of specialists.

In essence, the key motivation is to enable the automatic generation of curricula that start simple and progressively increase in complexity, constantly pushing the student agent to its learning frontier, leading to more robust and generalizable policies in a computationally efficient and theoretically sound manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm, **Adversarially Compounding Complexity by Editing Levels (ACCEL)**, integrates an evolutionary level generation mechanism with a principled regret-based curriculum. ACCEL is designed to constantly produce levels at the frontier of the student agent’s capabilities, leading to curricula that start simple but become increasingly complex.

**Core Components and How ACCEL Works:**

1. **Underspecified POMDPs (UPOMDPs):** The problem is framed within the UPOMDP framework, where environments are parameterized by $\Theta$. A specific setting of parameters $\theta \in \Theta$ defines a "level" $M_\theta$. The goal is to train a student policy $\pi$ that generalizes across variations in $\theta$.

2. **Regret-Based Curation (Inherited from PLR):**
 - ACCEL uses a regret-based objective for curriculum learning, similar to PLR. The "regret" of a level for a student agent is approximated by the **positive value loss** (Equation 5). This metric quantifies how "hard" a level is for the current agent, reflecting its learning potential.
 - Only levels that yield a high regret score for the student agent are added to a **level replay buffer**. The student agent is then trained *exclusively* on levels sampled from this buffer. This curation process is crucial for the theoretical guarantee that, at equilibrium, the student converges to a minimax regret policy.

3. **Evolutionary Level Editing (Novel Contribution):**
 - Instead of relying solely on random level generation (like PLR) or a complex learned teacher (like PAIRED), ACCEL introduces an **editing mechanism**.
 - The process begins with an initial set of simple levels (e.g., empty rooms in maze environments), serving as an inductive bias for "starting simple."
 - During training, after the student trains on a level sampled from the replay buffer, this high-regret level is *edited* (mutated) to produce a new level (`$\theta'$`).
 - These edited levels (`$\theta'$`) are then evaluated for their regret. If they meet the high-regret threshold, they are *also* added to the level replay buffer.
 - **Editing Mechanism:** The paper states that the editing mechanism can be any mutation process. In their experiments, it primarily involves simple, random mutations (e.g., adding/removing obstacles, changing goal locations). This assumes that regret varies smoothly with environment parameters, allowing small edits to lead to new high-regret levels.
 - This iterative process of training on high-regret levels and then editing them allows the curriculum to continuously "compound complexity," as successful edits tend to generate even more challenging levels that are still within the agent's learnable frontier.

**ACCEL's Operational Flow (Algorithm 1):**

1. **Initialization:** Initialize the student policy and a level replay buffer ($\Lambda$). Populate the buffer with an initial set of simple levels.
2. **Main Training Loop:**
 - **Decision to Sample or Replay/Edit:** At each step, a stochastic decision is made: either generate a new level from scratch (using a base generator, which could be random) or select an existing level from the replay buffer for training and subsequent editing.
 - **New Level Generation (Exploration):** If generating a new level, it's sampled from a base level generator (e.g., domain randomization). The student evaluates this level, its regret score (PVL) is computed, and if high, the level is added to the buffer.
 - **Replay and Edit (Exploitation and Evolution):** If replaying/editing, a level $\theta$ is sampled from the replay buffer ($\Lambda$). The student trains on this level. Crucially, *after* training, $\theta$ is edited to produce a new level $\theta'$. The student then evaluates $\theta'$, its regret score is computed, and if high, $\theta'$ is added to the buffer. This is the core evolutionary step.
 - **Optional Editor Update:** An optional step allows updating the editing mechanism itself based on the regret score. (The paper states they predominantly use simple random mutations, so this might be less critical for their main results).

**Key Differentiators and Advantages:**

- **Computational Efficiency:** By evolving levels but only training a *single* student agent, ACCEL avoids the prohibitive computational cost of population-based co-evolution methods like POET, which require many agents and environments. It can run on a single GPU.
- **Domain Agnostic:** ACCEL uses regret (PVL) as a universal fitness function for its evolutionary process, eliminating the need for domain-specific heuristics or hand-coded reward ranges that POET relies on.
- **Emergent Complexity:** The increasing complexity of environments in ACCEL emerges naturally from the continuous pursuit of high-regret levels, without needing explicit novelty bonuses or complex "stepping-stone" procedures (as seen in POET).
- **Generalist Agent:** ACCEL focuses on training a single, robust generalist agent capable of solving a wide distribution of challenges, contrasting with POET's aim to find multiple specialized agents.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** ACCEL retains the theoretical benefits of PLR, ensuring that the student agent, at equilibrium, converges to a minimax regret policy, promoting systematic generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling empirical results across three diverse environments: a simple MiniHack lava grid, more complex MiniGrid mazes, and a continuous-control BipedalWalker task, comparing ACCEL against several baselines (PAIRED, Minimax adversarial teacher, Domain Randomization (DR), and Prioritized Level Replay (PLR), and ALP-GMM for BipedalWalker).

#### 4.1. MiniHack Lava Grid (Proof-of-Concept)

- **Emergent Complexity:** ACCEL quickly generates levels with a higher number of lava tiles and longer shortest paths compared to baselines, even starting from empty rooms. This demonstrates its ability to "compound complexity" driven purely by the regret objective.
- **Performance:** ACCEL achieves higher training returns and superior aggregate test performance, including on an out-of-distribution human-designed maze.

#### 4.2. Partially Observable MiniGrid Navigation (Mazes)

- **Emergent Complexity:** ACCEL significantly outpaces other methods in producing complex mazes during training, characterized by higher block counts and longer solution paths (Figure 3). It shows an impressive ability to create structured mazes (Figure 5).
- **Zero-Shot Transfer:** ACCEL achieves drastically stronger performance on a suite of held-out human-designed test environments. Its aggregate Inter-Quartile Mean (IQM) approaches a perfect solved rate, substantially outperforming PLR (the next best method) with an 80.2% probability of improvement (Figure 4, Table 5).
- **Scalability to Larger Mazes:** On a challenging 51x51 procedurally-generated maze (an order of magnitude larger than training levels), ACCEL significantly outperforms all baselines with a 53% success rate, compared to PLR's 25%, while others largely fail (Figure 7).
- **Diversity:** The analysis shows that editing can lead to a diverse batch of new challenges from a single parent level, which helps prevent overfitting by forcing the agent to explore different strategies (Figure 6). This also acts as a form of data augmentation.

#### 4.3. BipedalWalker (Continuous Control on Challenging Terrain)

- **Aggregate Performance:** ACCEL significantly outperforms all baselines on a suite of five challenging test environments (Basic, Hardcore, Stairs, PitGap, Stump, Roughness). It achieves close to 75% of optimal performance, nearly three times that of the best baseline, PLR (Figure 9, Table 6). Other baselines struggle, often learning only to avoid falling rather than progress.
- **Emergent Complexity & Computational Efficiency:** ACCEL generates and solves a large quantity of difficult levels of comparable complexity to those seen in POET (Figure 10). Critically, it achieves this with a *fraction of the compute*: ACCEL uses less than 0.5% of the total environment interaction samples used by POET (2.07B vs. over 400B in Wang et al., 2019), and does so on a *single GPU*.
- **POET Comparison (5D BipedalWalker):**
 - ACCEL's level replay buffer increasingly populates with "extremely challenging" levels (Figure 11), a category POET struggled to create without explicit "stepping-stone" mechanisms. This demonstrates that complexity can emerge purely from the regret objective without a novelty bonus.
 - ACCEL trains a *single generalist agent* that achieves robust performance across diverse challenges, including held-out "extremely challenging" levels (12% solved rate vs. 1-2% for baselines) (Table 2). This contrasts with POET's aim to find a population of specialists.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- An ablation study in MiniGrid and BipedalWalker (Figure 12) demonstrates that **level editing is crucial** for ACCEL's performance improvements over PLR.
- **Starting simple** (i.e., initializing with empty rooms and evolving) provides a significant inductive bias and is particularly important in the BipedalWalker environment, helping the agent learn foundational skills before complex challenges are introduced.
- Using a *learned editor* (an RL policy attempting to maximize PVL of generated levels) degraded performance compared to simple random editing, suggesting that a sophisticated teacher is not necessarily required, and the simple evolutionary mechanism is effective enough when combined with regret-based curation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ACCEL represents a significant advancement in the field of automatic curriculum learning and unsupervised environment design, offering a compelling solution to train robust and generalizable reinforcement learning agents. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Gap Between Principled UED and Evolutionary Creativity:** ACCEL successfully combines the theoretical robustness guarantees and domain-agnostic nature of regret-based UED with the powerful, open-ended complexity generation capabilities of evolutionary algorithms. This hybrid approach demonstrates that these seemingly disparate methodologies can be synergistically integrated to yield superior performance.

2. **Unlocking Computational Efficiency:** One of ACCEL's most impactful contributions is its remarkable computational efficiency. By evolving environment levels while training only a *single* student agent, it drastically reduces the hardware requirements compared to population-based co-evolution methods like POET. Achieving comparable or even greater levels of environmental complexity with orders of magnitude less computational resources (e.g., a single GPU versus CPU clusters) makes sophisticated curriculum learning far more accessible to a broader research community and to practical applications. This democratizes access to advanced UED techniques.

3. **Training Generalist Agents for Robustness:** ACCEL prioritizes the development of a *single, highly generalist agent* capable of systematically generalizing across a wide array of challenging and unseen environments. This contrasts with approaches that yield multiple specialized agents, which might struggle with novel tasks outside their niche. The ability to produce agents that perform robustly out-of-distribution is crucial for deploying RL in real-world scenarios where environmental variations are inevitable.

4. **Emergence of Complexity without Heuristics:** The paper convincingly shows that complex and diverse environment challenges can *emerge* purely from the pursuit of high-regret levels, without the need for hand-crafted novelty bonuses, explicit complexity metrics, or domain-specific heuristics. This validates the power of the minimax regret objective as a natural driver for a curriculum that pushes an agent to its learning frontier.

5. **Foundation for Future Open-Ended Learning:** ACCEL takes a crucial step towards truly open-ended learning by iteratively compounding complexity. While not a complete open-ended system on its own, it lays a strong foundation. Future work can build upon ACCEL's framework by exploring more sophisticated editing mechanisms (e.g., neural cellular automata, latent space perturbations from generative models) to generate even more varied and complex levels. It also opens avenues for investigating how to actively promote diversity within the level buffer to ensure comprehensive generalization, potentially even in conjunction with concepts like "extinction events" from evolutionary biology.

In summary, ACCEL offers a principled, efficient, and empirically powerful method for automatic curriculum generation. Its ability to create increasingly complex and diverse training environments without excessive computational cost or domain-specific tuning makes it a promising direction for developing more capable, robust, and general-purpose artificial intelligences.
