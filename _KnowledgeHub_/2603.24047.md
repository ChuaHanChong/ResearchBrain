# PCHC: Enabling Preference Conditioned Humanoid Control via Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The PCHC framework enables humanoid robots to dynamically adjust their control policies based on user preferences for conflicting objectives, such as balancing energy efficiency and robustness during fall recovery, or stride length and disturbance resistance in walking. It achieves this with a single, unified policy that provides a continuous spectrum of behaviors, outperforming fixed-weight baselines across different tasks and demonstrating successful sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) for humanoid control relies on fixed weights for competing objectives, yielding a single, static policy that cannot adapt to changing demands.
- Multi-objective problems in humanoid control (e.g., speed vs. energy, stability vs. agility) require dynamic policy adaptation, but existing Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) struggles with high-dimensional, continuous control and complex reward functions.
- Conventional MORL often requires training multiple distinct policies to approximate the Pareto front, which is computationally intensive and lacks real-time adaptability for a single agent.

## Method

- **PCHC** formulates humanoid control as a ==Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL)== problem, training a single, ==preference-conditioned policy== that dynamically adapts based on an input preference vector.
- A ==Multi-Critic Value Estimation== approach uses separate critics for overall task return and objective-specific rewards, with a ==preference-conditioned advantage== combining them to guide policy optimization.
- The ==Preference Condition Injection (PCI)== module, utilizing a ==Beta distribution-based expert routing== within a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) network, robustly integrates preference requirements into the policy architecture.

## Results

- In the fall recovery task, PCHC achieved a **96.56%** success rate prioritizing robustness (**666.24 J** energy) versus **62.24%** prioritizing energy efficiency (**463.45 J** energy), continuously dominating a fixed-weight baseline.
- For humanoid walking, the policy produced adaptive locomotion, from **0.79m** stride with **0.85m** trajectory deviation (stride focus) to **0.42m** stride with **0.23m** deviation (stability focus), continuously outperforming a baseline.
- The framework demonstrated seamless real-time adaptation to dynamically changing preference vectors during task execution and achieved successful sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot for both tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, unified policy, when conditioned on a preference vector, can generalize across different objective trade-offs, providing versatile control for multiple conflicting objectives without needing multiple trained policies.
> - The Beta distribution-based expert routing in the PCI module effectively aligns preference vectors with expert contributions, leading to superior coverage of the objective space and a more faithful approximation of the Pareto front.
> - Distinguishing between preference-scalarized objective-specific rewards and invariant auxiliary rewards is crucial for maintaining fundamental robot stability while allowing flexible trade-offs for target objectives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots represent a significant platform for various applications, including hazardous environments and labor-intensive tasks. Traditionally, Model Predictive Control (MPC) has been employed for locomotion across diverse terrains. More recently, Reinforcement Learning (RL), facilitated by high-performance parallel simulators, has enabled humanoid robots to achieve robustness in complex control scenarios, such as fall recovery, blind locomotion over uneven ground, and vision-based locomotion. These advancements primarily utilize policy gradient methods.

A common limitation in many existing RL frameworks for humanoid control is their reliance on predefined, fixed weights to scalarize competing reward objectives. This approach typically yields a single, static policy, which may be suboptimal for tasks that inherently involve multiple, conflicting objectives. Such multi-faceted problems, common in real-world humanoid applications (e.g., balancing speed with energy consumption, or stability with agility), require policies that can dynamically adapt to changing environmental demands or user priorities.

Multi-objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) is a paradigm specifically designed to address such problems by approximating the Pareto front, which represents a set of optimal trade-offs between conflicting objectives. While MORL has seen developments in Q-learning and policy gradient methods, extending these to high-dimensional, continuous humanoid control remains challenging. This is due to the complexity of the state-action space, the intricate multi-objective trade-offs inherent in complex robot dynamics, and the multitude of dense, manually crafted reward functions typically used in humanoid control. Prior MORL work often evaluates on simplified environments, making its direct application to humanoid locomotion non-trivial.

This paper addresses this gap by integrating MORL principles into complex humanoid control. It aims to develop a unified multi-objective humanoid control policy capable of navigating complex Pareto trade-offs in real-time, thereby moving beyond the limitations of fixed-weight, single-objective RL policies. By focusing on preference-conditioned control, the work seeks to provide a mechanism for dynamic adaptation to user-defined priorities, enhancing the versatility and practical applicability of humanoid robots in dynamic environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a fundamental challenge in current humanoid robot control: the need to balance inherently competing objectives. For instance, a robot might need to maximize its walking speed while simultaneously minimizing energy consumption, or prioritize stability against disturbances over achieving a long stride length. Existing Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods, despite their success in mastering complex skills like fall recovery and perceptive locomotion, typically rely on fixed weighting strategies for scalarizing these competing objectives. This approach invariably leads to the generation of a single, suboptimal policy that cannot dynamically adapt to changing operational demands or user preferences.

The key objectives of this work are therefore detailed as follows:

1. **Develop a framework for Preference-Conditioned Humanoid Control (PCHC) using Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL):** The central aim is to move beyond static, single-objective policies. The motivation is to enable a humanoid robot to exhibit a wide spectrum of diverse behaviors by adapting its objective priorities in real-time based on an input preference condition. This contrasts with conventional MORL methods that often require training a series of distinct policies to approximate the Pareto front, which can be computationally intensive and lack dynamic adaptability.

2. **Enable a single, unified policy to achieve versatile control for multiple preference-conditioned objectives:** Instead of learning multiple policies, the objective is to train one policy that can generalize across different preference settings. This is motivated by the need for efficient deployment and real-time adaptation, avoiding the overhead associated with switching between multiple pre-trained policies.

3. **Introduce an effective mechanism for integrating preference requirements into the policy architecture:** A specific objective is to design a robust method for modulating a neural network policy based on preference vectors. The motivation here is that directly concatenating preference vectors with observations may not be sufficient for the highly non-linear and complex mapping required for low-level motor behaviors in humanoid control.

4. **Distinguish between objective-specific and auxiliary rewards for preference-based scalarization:** Humanoid control tasks typically involve numerous reward functions. A key objective is to selectively apply preference-based scalarization only to rewards directly relevant to the target-specific conflicting objectives, while keeping auxiliary rewards (e.g., those ensuring fundamental stability) invariant. This is motivated by the need to maintain essential robot behaviors and stability while allowing flexibility in objective trade-offs.

5. **Validate the proposed framework on representative humanoid tasks with intrinsically conflicting objectives:** The objective is to demonstrate the practical applicability and effectiveness of PCHC on real-world relevant humanoid scenarios. Specifically, the authors target:
 - **Humanoid Walking:** To investigate the trade-offs between disturbance resistance and stride length.
 - **Humanoid Fall Recovery:** To explore the trade-offs between disturbance resistance and energy efficiency.

In summary, the core motivation is to enhance the adaptability and intelligence of humanoid robots by allowing them to dynamically adjust their behavior based on user-defined priorities for conflicting objectives, all within a single, efficiently trained control policy. This aims to bridge the gap between theoretical MORL advancements and the practical demands of complex, high-dimensional humanoid robot control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, PCHC (Preference-Conditioned Humanoid Control), formulates humanoid control as a Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) problem within a Multi-Objective Markov Decision Process (MOMDP). The core approach involves training a single, preference-conditioned policy that can dynamically adapt its behavior based on an input preference vector, rather than learning multiple policies.

**A. Preliminaries and Problem Formulation**

- **MOMDP:** The environment is modeled as a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ, ρ₀), where the reward function R: S×A → R^m returns a vector of `m` rewards (r₁..., r_m) for `m` objectives.
- **Pareto Front:** The goal is to approximate the Pareto front, which consists of a set of non-dominated policies where no policy can improve one objective without worsening another.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Hypervolume (quantifies the volume of objective space dominated by the Pareto front) and sparsity (indicates the diversity/density of solutions) are used to evaluate the approximation of the Pareto front.

**B. Preference-Conditioned Humanoid Control**

- **Single-Policy Formulation:** Unlike multi-policy MORL, PCHC trains a single policy `π(a|s, λ)` conditioned on a preference vector `λ ∈ R^m`. Each entry `λ_i ≥ 0` represents the weight of objective `i`, with `∑λ_i = 1`.
- **Preference Injection:** During training, `λ` is sampled from a Dirichlet distribution and concatenated with the robot's observation.
- **Multi-Critic Value Estimation:** To handle dynamic preference vectors, multiple critics are employed:
 - One critic estimates the overall task-related return (`V_task`).
 - Additional critics estimate returns for each objective-specific reward (`V_obj_i`).
 - Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is used to estimate advantages (`A_task`, `A_obj_i`) for each critic.
 - The overall preference-conditioned advantage `Â_λ` is computed as a weighted sum: `Â_λ = (∑_i λ_i Â_obj_i) + Â_task`. This allows the policy update to reflect user-specified trade-offs while maintaining essential task constraints.
- **Policy Optimization:** The preference-conditioned policy is optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), minimizing a clipped surrogate loss function `L(θ)` based on `Â_λ`.
- **Reward Scalarization:** Only rewards directly relevant to the target-specific conflicting objectives are subjected to preference-based scalarization using `λ`. Auxiliary rewards, intended for fundamental stability (e.g., maintaining balance), remain invariant to ensure robust foundational behaviors.

**C. Preference Condition Injection (PCI) Module**

- **Motivation:** Directly concatenating `λ` to observations may be insufficient due to the complex, non-linear mapping required for humanoid motor control.
- **Architecture:** A multi-expert network is utilized within the actor.
- **Beta Distribution-based Expert Routing:** For two conflicting objectives (where `λ = [λ₁, λ₂]` lies on a 1D probability simplex), a Beta distribution is used to parameterize expert routing:
 - Parameters `α` and `β` of the Beta distribution are defined as `α = α₀ + λ₁ * s` and `β = β₀ + λ₂ * s`, where `α₀, β₀ > 0` are base offsets and `s` is a scaling factor.
 - The Beta distribution's domain naturally aligns with the `` interval for interpolating between two objectives.
 - For each of `N_B` experts positioned at fixed `x_j ∈`, an unnormalized weight `˜w_j` is computed by evaluating the Beta density at `x_j`.
 - These `˜w_j` are then normalized to obtain the final routing weights.
- **Expert Aggregation:** Each expert network receives the current observation and history encoder output. Their outputs are aggregated via a weighted sum based on the computed routing weights.
- **Expert Configuration:** `N_B = 5` experts are used, with fixed positions `x_j` evenly distributed at `0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0` along the objective space. Each expert implicitly learns a distinct solution on the Pareto front, and their interpolation generates solutions at other points.

**D. Humanoid Control Tasks**

The framework is evaluated on two tasks using the Unitree G1 humanoid robot:

1. **State and Action:**
 - **State:** Proprioceptive information `s_t = [ω_t, g_t, q_t, ˙q_t, a_t-1, η]` (angular velocity, gravity vector, joint positions/velocities, previous action, action scaling factor).
 - **History Encoding:** Both actor and critic receive a combination of five history states and the current state.
 - **Action:** `a_t` (predicted action) is mapped to target joint positions `q_target_t = q_default + ηa_t`.
 - **Control:** A joint-level PD controller (`τ_t = K_p * (q_target_t - q_t) - K_d * ˙q_t`) computes motor torques.
 - **Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP):** A style reward `r_style` is incorporated by training a discriminator to distinguish policy-generated trajectories from reference motion data, promoting natural behaviors.

2. **Humanoid Walking Task:**
 - **Objectives:** Stride length vs. Disturbance resistance.
 - **`r_stride`:** Encourages longer steps, computed from foot position on contact.
 - **`r_disturb`:** Emphasizes stability against external pushes, computed from base displacement and velocity after a disturbance.
 - **Observation:** Includes `v_cmd` (velocity commands).

3. **Humanoid Fall Recovery Task:**
 - **Objectives:** Energy efficiency vs. Disturbance resistance.
 - **`r_energy`:** Minimizes torque and power consumption across all actuated joints.
 - **`r_disturb`:** Similar to the walking task, emphasizing stability against external pushes during recovery and standing.
 - **Robot:** Controls 23 actuated joints.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The PCHC framework was evaluated through extensive simulations using Isaac Gym and real-world experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Training was conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, with 4096 parallel environments for 12000 iterations.

**A. Simulation Results**

1. **Fall Recovery Task:**
 - **Preference-Conditioned Behavior:** PCHC demonstrated adaptive behavior based on the preference vector `λ = (λ₁, λ₂)`. When `λ = (1.0, 0.0)` (prioritizing energy efficiency), the robot consumed less energy but exhibited lower robustness and success rates under perturbations. Conversely, `λ = (0.0, 1.0)` (prioritizing robustness) led to higher energy consumption but maintained stable standing under large disturbances, achieving higher success rates. Intermediate preferences resulted in smooth trade-offs.
 - **Quantitative Metrics:**
 - **(1.0, 0.0):** Avg. Energy Overall (463.45 J), Success rate (62.24%).
 - **(0.0, 1.0):** Avg. Energy Overall (666.24 J), Success rate (96.56%).
 - Average COM displacement under small disturbance ranged from 1.82m (energy focus) to 0.75m (robustness focus).
 - **Comparison to Baseline (HoST):** The baseline HoST policy (augmented with AMP motion priors) represented a single fixed solution (e.g., Avg. Energy Overall 550.15 J, Success rate 69.22%, COM displacement 1.69m), which was strictly dominated by multiple solutions achieved by PCHC. PCHC's policies spanned a broader set of non-dominated points, providing a better approximation of the Pareto front. Figure 4(a) visually supported this, illustrating how energy usage modulated with different preferences.

2. **Walking Task:**
 - **Preference-Conditioned Behavior:** The policy produced different locomotion styles based on preferences. Prioritizing stride length (`λ = [0.0, 1.0]`) resulted in larger steps but increased trajectory deviation (Avg. Stride 0.79m, Traj. Deviation 0.85m). Emphasizing stability (`λ = [1.0, 0.0]`) led to minimal trajectory deviation at the cost of shorter strides (Avg. Stride 0.42m, Traj. Deviation 0.23m). Intermediate preferences allowed for gradual transitions and balanced compromises.
 - **Comparison to Baseline:** The baseline policy (Avg. Stride 0.59m, Traj. Deviation 0.46m) was confined to a single operating point, while PCHC covered a broader portion of the stride–stability spectrum, demonstrating its ability to dominate the baseline by offering a continuous set of solutions. Figure 4(b) provided a direct visualization of these trade-offs.

3. **Dynamic Preference Switching:** PCHC policies demonstrated the ability to seamlessly transition their behavior in real-time when the preference vector was instantaneously altered during task execution (Figure 5). This indicated that the framework can dynamically and safely adapt to evolving objective priorities without instability or failure.

4. **Ablation Study on Preference Condition Injection (PCI):**
 - **Effectiveness:** Comparing the full PCHC framework (with PCI) against a version using a standard trainable gating network (w/o PCI) showed that the PCI module significantly improved the quality of the approximated Pareto front.
 - **Metrics:** For fall recovery, PCI increased hypervolume from 306.02 to 313.78 and reduced sparsity from 3493.85 to 3210.61. For the walking task, hypervolume increased from 0.14 to 0.20 with little change in sparsity.
 - **Conclusion:** The Beta-distribution-based routing mechanism provided superior coverage of the objective space and a more faithful approximation of the Pareto front compared to a conventional gating architecture (Figure 6).

5. **Comparison with Fixed Preference Policies:** PCHC, trained with randomly sampled preference vectors, achieved comparable performance at specific preference points to policies explicitly trained with fixed preference vectors (Figure 8). This suggested that the preference conditioning approach retains the effectiveness of fixed-preference training while extending it to a continuous solution space within a single policy.

**B. Real-world Results**

The trained PCHC policies were successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating effective sim-to-real transfer:

1. **Fall Recovery Task:** Real-world experiments validated the trade-off between energy efficiency and robustness. With `λ = [1.0, 0.0]` (energy focus), the robot rose with lower torque but lost balance more readily. With `λ = [0.0, 1.0]` (robustness focus), it generated stronger, more stable motions to withstand perturbations, albeit with higher joint effort (Figure 7).

2. **Walking Task:** In locomotion, `λ = [0.0, 1.0]` resulted in longer strides with increased trajectory deviation under external forces, while `λ = [1.0, 0.0]` maintained better disturbance resistance at the cost of shorter steps (Figure 1).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PCHC framework represents an advancement in humanoid robot control by addressing a core limitation of conventional single-objective Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods: the inability to dynamically balance competing objectives. The primary significance of this work lies in its proposal of a single, preference-conditioned policy capable of exhibiting a wide spectrum of diverse behaviors by adapting its objective priorities in real-time based on an input preference condition. This departs from previous MORL approaches that often necessitate training multiple policies, thereby improving computational efficiency and enabling dynamic adaptation.

The technical contribution of introducing a Beta distribution-based alignment mechanism for modulating a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module is notable. This method provides a structured and mathematically bounded approach for integrating preference vectors, facilitating a smooth interpolation between expert-learned behaviors along the Pareto front. The ablation study demonstrated the efficacy of this Preference Condition Injection (PCI) module in achieving superior coverage of the objective space and a more faithful approximation of the Pareto front compared to standard gating networks.

The practical impact of PCHC is evident in its demonstrated ability to allow humanoid robots to adaptively shift objective priorities in real-time. This capability is critical for deploying robots in complex, dynamic real-world environments where operational requirements frequently change. For instance, in search-and-rescue operations, a robot might need to prioritize agility over energy efficiency, whereas in industrial logistics, the emphasis might shift to speed and energy efficiency. The framework's validation on two distinct humanoid tasks (walking and fall recovery) with inherently conflicting objectives, both in simulation and real-world experiments, underscores its potential for practical application. The demonstrated seamless transition of behaviors during dynamic preference switching further highlights its robustness and adaptability for practical deployment.

While the current framework is primarily applicable to tasks with two objectives and relies on proprioceptive information, the foundational approach lays groundwork for future developments. Potential future impacts include:
- **Enhanced Robot Versatility:** Humanoid robots could become more versatile and user-adaptive, adjusting their performance metrics (e.g., speed, stability, energy use) on the fly to suit immediate task demands.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** Users could more intuitively guide robot behavior by specifying preferences, leading to more natural and efficient human-robot interaction.
- **Foundation for Complex MORL:** The methods developed could be extended to more complex multi-objective scenarios involving more than two conflicting objectives, or integrated with external sensor modalities (e.g., vision) for tasks requiring perceptual awareness.

In conclusion, PCHC contributes to the field of humanoid robotics by enabling more intelligent and adaptable control policies that can dynamically navigate complex trade-offs, moving closer to the goal of autonomous and flexible humanoid operation in diverse real-world settings.
