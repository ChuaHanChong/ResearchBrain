# EVIS: A Physics-Grounded Event Camera Plugin for NVIDIA Isaac Sim

> [!summary] Summary
> Johns Hopkins University researchers developed EVIS, a physics-grounded event camera plugin for NVIDIA Isaac Sim, to generate high-rate, fully labeled event streams synchronized with physics simulations, offering frame-perfect ground truth data for event-based robotics development. The framework achieves real-time data generation through motion-vector frame interpolation and incorporates realistic sensor non-idealities.

## Problem

- The scarcity and high cost of acquiring precisely labeled, real-world event camera data tailored for diverse robot platforms and tasks hinders event-based robotics research.
- Existing video-to-event (V2E) conversion methods lack physics consistency and synchronized ground truth, which is critical for training robust robot control and perception algorithms.
- Current direct-rendering event simulators are not integrated with modern GPU-parallel physics engines like NVIDIA Isaac Sim, limiting their scalability and utility for robot learning research.

## Method

- An event camera plugin, **EVIS**, is implemented within NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab, utilizing a ==log-intensity contrast model== to faithfully simulate event generation from HDR color buffers.
- A ==motion-vector-based frame interpolation== pipeline efficiently synthesizes intermediate frames between lower-rate keyframes, enabling kHz-scale effective event rates for high throughput.
- The framework includes configurable models for six types of ==sensor noise== (e.g., threshold mismatch, leak events, hot pixels) and ==motion blur== to accurately replicate real event camera non-idealities.

## Results

- EVIS achieved real-time or faster event stream generation, with a `30 x 8` configuration generating an effective **240 Hz** event stream at **1.2x** real-time speed on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.
- On downstream tasks, E-RAFT optical flow maintained sub-pixel accuracy (EPE **~0.96-0.99 pixels**) and high correlation across varying interpolation rates, while E2VID reconstruction quality saw a SSIM drop of up to **0.148** with an **8x** warp.
- The plugin demonstrated adaptability across diverse robot embodiments (e.g., Franka manipulator, ANYmal quadruped) and its modeled non-idealities qualitatively replicated real sensor effects like motion blur and spatiotemporal noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-fidelity event simulation for robotics requires meeting three critical criteria simultaneously: accurate physics-consistent event generation, precise ground truth synchronization, and high rendering throughput.
> - Motion-vector frame interpolation is an effective strategy to significantly boost event stream generation speed while largely preserving the scene's true motion field, making it suitable for training perception algorithms.
> - Simulated event data, even with interpolation and noise modeling, can be directly consumed by existing pre-trained event-based networks, indicating a strong match in data characteristics with real-world sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Event cameras represent a distinct sensing paradigm compared to conventional frame-based cameras, offering microsecond temporal resolution, low latency, and high dynamic range. These characteristics make them particularly suitable for robotics applications involving rapid motion, contact-rich interactions, or scenes with high dynamic range. Despite these advantages, the development of event-based perception and control systems is constrained by the limited availability and high cost of acquiring labeled real-world event camera data tailored for specific robot platforms and tasks.

The existing research landscape for event camera data generation includes several approaches:

- **Real-world datasets:** Initiatives such as M3ED and OctoSense provide valuable multi-sensor event data from real environments. However, these datasets are inherently tied to particular robot platforms, sensor configurations, and task domains, limiting their generalizability and adaptability for diverse robotic research.
- **Video-to-event (V2E) simulators:** A common approach involves converting existing RGB video sequences into event streams. Examples include v2e, Vid2E, PIX2NVS, DVS-Voltmeter, and V2CE. While these methods can generate event data from diverse visual content, they operate on pre-recorded or pre-rendered frames and are decoupled from real-time physics simulations. This decoupling means they cannot provide synchronized, physics-consistent ground truth data, which is crucial for training and evaluating robot control and perception algorithms that require precise state information.
- **Direct-rendering event simulators:** A more advanced category of simulators renders events directly from 3D scenes or models. ESIM adaptively renders synthetic 3D scenes, a classic event-camera simulator uses Blender, EvD-NeRF renders events from dynamic neural radiance fields, and a DVS plugin extends the Gazebo robot simulator. While these simulators provide direct event generation from virtual environments, none are integrated with modern, GPU-parallel physics simulators that are commonly used for robot learning and simulation, such as NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab.

This work, EVIS, addresses a notable gap in this landscape by developing a physics-grounded event camera plugin directly integrated within NVIDIA Isaac Sim and its Isaac Lab framework. This integration enables the generation of high-rate, fully labeled event streams that are synchronized with physics simulations and provide frame-perfect ground truth data. By combining high-fidelity event generation with a modern GPU-parallel physics simulator, EVIS aims to overcome the limitations of existing data generation methods and facilitate the scalable development of event-based robotic systems.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the challenges in developing and validating event-based perception and control algorithms for robotics. The scarcity and high cost associated with collecting precisely labeled and aligned real-world event camera data for specific robot embodiments and tasks significantly impede progress in this field. Real-world data collection often requires extensive manual effort for annotation and alignment, and the resulting datasets are typically constrained by the particular experimental setup.

To address this bottleneck, the authors identify simulation as a logical and potentially scalable alternative. However, for a simulator to be effective in generating useful data for event-based robotics, it must satisfy three critical requirements simultaneously:

1. **Fidelity:** The simulator must accurately model how events are generated by real hardware. This includes implementing a correct contrast-triggering mechanism, operating at a sufficiently high effective frame rate to capture rapid changes, and incorporating realistic sensor noise processes that mimic non-ideal behaviors of physical event cameras. High fidelity ensures that the simulated data closely resembles real-world data, making algorithms trained on it more likely to generalize to physical systems.
2. **Physics Consistency:** The events generated by the simulator must originate from a genuine physics-simulated scene. This implies that the visual changes triggering events are consistent with the physical interactions and motions occurring within the simulated environment. Crucially, the simulator must provide exact, frame-perfect ground truth data (e.g., object poses, velocities, optical flow) that is precisely synchronized with the event stream. This contrasts with methods that convert pre-recorded videos, which cannot provide true physics consistency or perfectly aligned ground truth.
3. **Rendering Throughput:** The process of generating event data must be efficient and scalable. Ideally, event generation should operate at or above real-time speeds on a single GPU. This high throughput is essential to enable large-scale training of event-based perception and control pipelines, allowing them to iterate and learn at a similar pace to their conventional RGB camera counterparts. Without sufficient throughput, the benefits of simulation in terms of data volume would be negated by slow generation times.

The authors motivate the choice of NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab as the underlying platforms because they are widely adopted for GPU-parallel robot learning, offering a robust environment for physics simulation and high-performance rendering. By integrating an event camera model directly into this ecosystem, the work aims to meet all three requirements, thereby lowering the barrier to entry for event-based research and accelerating the development of event-based robotics.

### 3. Methodology and approach

EVIS is implemented as a physics-grounded event camera plugin for NVIDIA Isaac Sim, integrated through the Isaac Lab framework. Its methodology encompasses a faithful event model, an efficient frame interpolation pipeline, and a comprehensive suite of sensor non-idealities.

#### 3.1. Event Model

The core of EVIS's event generation is a log-intensity contrast model, mirroring the operation of real event cameras.
1. **Input:** Events are computed from the renderer's High Dynamic Range (HDR) color buffer, which provides intensity values linear in light.
2. **Luminance Calculation:** For each pixel, a luminance value `I` is derived from the RGB color values using Rec. 709 weights: `I = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B`.
3. **Log Response:** A log transformation is applied to the luminance to match the sensor's response: `L = log(I + ϵ)`, where `ϵ = 10^-5` prevents issues with log(0).
4. **Contrast Triggering:** The plugin maintains a per-pixel reference log-intensity `L_ref`. An event fires when the absolute difference between the current log-intensity `L` and `L_ref` exceeds a predefined contrast threshold `C` (default `C = 0.15`). The event polarity `p` is `+1` for an increase (ON event) and `-1` for a decrease (OFF event).
5. **Reference Update:** Upon an event firing, the `L_ref` for that pixel is immediately updated to the current `L`, mimicking the asynchronous "fire-and-latch" mechanism of real event cameras.
6. **Implementation:** The entire event update process is executed using GPU-batched tensor operations, allowing simultaneous processing across multiple environments and cameras. Generated events are stored as `(x, y, t, p)` tuples in HDF5 format, grouped per camera.

#### 3.2. Motion-Vector Frame Interpolation

To achieve the kHz-scale frame rates required for faithful event streams while mitigating the computational cost of RTX rendering, EVIS incorporates a motion-vector-based frame interpolation pipeline.
1. **Principle:** Keyframes are rendered at a lower rate `f_k`. The `K-1` intermediate frames within each keyframe gap are synthesized, resulting in an effective event model rate of `f_k * K`.
2. **Bidirectional Warping:**
 - At each keyframe, the renderer provides the color frame, depth map, and one-directional motion vectors (mapping pixels back to their previous frame positions).
 - To synthesize an intermediate frame at fraction `f = i/K`, a bidirectional warping approach is used:
 - **Forward Warping:** The content of the previous keyframe (A) is warped forward using its approximate forward velocity (derived from its previous-step motion vector).
 - **Backward Warping:** The content of the current keyframe (B) is warped backward using its exact `A → B` correspondence motion vector.
 - **Resampling and Compositing:** Both warped keyframes are resampled using softmax splatting. An importance weight based on normalized inverse depth (`1/(Z_X(p)+ϵ)`) ensures that nearer surfaces take precedence when pixels collide. The backward-warped image from keyframe B serves as the primary result, while the forward-warped image from keyframe A fills any disocclusion holes. Pixels not covered by either are left black.
 - **Efficiency:** Intermediate frames across multiple environments and cameras are processed in batched scatter calls.
3. **Border Handling:** An optional over-render margin allows rendering extra pixels on the borders, which are then cropped after warping, mitigating edge artifacts.
4. **Anti-aliasing:** When interpolation is active, anti-aliasing is disabled. This is because anti-aliasing can cause silhouette pixels to blend with the background, inheriting incorrect near-zero motion vectors and leading to ghosting artifacts after warping.

#### 3.3. Modeling Sensor Non-Idealities

To narrow the gap between simulated and real event cameras, EVIS includes optional models for common sensor non-idealities:
1. **Sensor Noise:** Six configurable noise components are available, approximated as Poisson processes with rates `r * Δt` per synthesis step:
 - **Threshold Mismatch:** Per-pixel ON/OFF thresholds are sampled from a Gaussian distribution (`N(C, σ^2_θ)`), modeling fixed-pattern mismatch.
 - **Refractory Period:** After a pixel fires, it cannot fire again for a duration `τ_r`.
 - **Leak Events:** Spurious ON events occur with a probability `P_leak = r_ℓΔt`.
 - **Shot Noise:** Random-polarity events whose rate `P_shot = r_sΔt(1 - Ī)` increases in darker regions (`Ī` is normalized intensity).
 - **Hot Pixels:** A fixed subset of pixels fires at a high rate `P_hot = r_hΔt`.
 - **Finite Bandwidth:** The log-intensity is low-pass filtered with an intensity-dependent time constant (`τ/Ī`), causing darker pixels to respond more slowly.
2. **Motion Blur:** This models the integration of light over a real camera's shutter time. When enabled, fine frames synthesized by the warp are averaged over the exposure duration. The event model then operates on these blurred frames, resulting in event streams where edges are smeared into wide bands, representing the integrated motion.
All randomness for noise generation uses a single seeded generator, ensuring reproducible datasets.

### 4. Main findings and results

The evaluation of EVIS focused on its real-time performance, its utility with existing event-based networks, and qualitative assessment of its event generation fidelity.

#### 4.1. Real-time Performance

Performance tests were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, with one environment and a monocular 640x480 event camera. Table 1 indicates that RTX rendering is the most significant cost factor, scaling inversely with the interpolation factor `K`. The warping and event generation stages maintain a relatively constant cost.
- The `30 x 8` configuration, generating an effective 240 Hz event stream, achieved a speed of 1.2 times faster than real-time.
- Other configurations like `40 x 8` also approached or exceeded real-time performance.
- The system's throughput can be further scaled by utilizing parallel environments, which amortizes rendering costs across batches.

#### 4.2. Downstream-Task Test

A key finding is that EVIS generates event streams directly usable by publicly released, pre-trained event-based networks without any fine-tuning or adaptation. This validates the fidelity of the simulated data. The evaluation used three representative tasks: E2VID for intensity video reconstruction, E-RAFT for dense optical flow estimation, and Match-Any-Events for feature matching. The test scene involved a camera sweeping through an Isaac Sim warehouse environment while YCB objects were thrown through the view, producing egomotion and independent object motion. Four configurations were tested at an effective 1000 Hz rate: a full 1000 Hz render (reference), and three warp variants (500x2, 250x4, 125x8).

- **Reconstruction (E2VID):**
 - E2VID was used to reconstruct intensity video from events, with outputs scored against simulator ground truth luminance (histogram-equalized) using MSE, SSIM, and LPIPS metrics.
 - As shown in Table 2, reconstruction error increased (higher MSE, LPIPS) and structural similarity decreased (lower SSIM) monotonically with more aggressive interpolation. A 2x warp resulted in a 0.047 SSIM drop, while an 8x warp caused a 0.148 SSIM drop relative to the full reference. This indicates a trade-off between generation speed and reconstruction quality.
- **Optical Flow (E-RAFT):**
 - E-RAFT estimated dense optical flow from consecutive 20ms event windows. The results were compared against the simulator's exact per-pixel motion vector ground truth using average endpoint error (EPE) and Pearson correlation.
 - Table 3 shows that E-RAFT recovered optical flow with sub-pixel accuracy (EPE around 0.96-0.99 pixels) across all configurations, and correlation remained high (around 0.86-0.87). This robustness to keyframe sparsity is attributed to the warp preserving the scene's true motion field due to its reliance on the renderer's motion vectors.
- **Feature Matching (Match-Any-Events):**
 - The pretrained Match-Any-Events matcher was used to find correspondences between event streams from two cameras. Matching precision (ratio of correct matches based on epipolar distance) and AUC of pose error were measured.
 - Table 4 indicates that matching performance, while still functional, degraded gently with increasing interpolation aggressiveness. For example, precision dropped from 81.2% (full render) to 74.2% (125x8 warp).

#### 4.3. Qualitative Results

- **Adaptability:** Figure 1 demonstrates the plugin's adaptability across diverse robot embodiments, including a Franka manipulator with a wrist-mounted camera, a head-mounted camera on an ANYmal quadruped, and a stereo rig. Integration required minimal changes to the camera configuration.
- **Spatiotemporal Geometry:** Visualizations of event point clouds (x, y, t) in Figure 5 for canonical motions (free fall, roll, approach, spin) showed that the warp-accelerated streams (e.g., 35x8) largely reproduce the global spatiotemporal geometry observed in native renders (280 Hz, 1000 Hz). This confirms that interpolation largely preserves true motion.
- **Residual Effects:** Two subtle artifacts were observed:
 - Temporal quantization: Under fast displacement, consecutive time slices can separate, visible even in native 280 Hz renders.
 - Banding: More prominent under rotation or occlusion in warped streams. This occurs when newly revealed surfaces lack source pixels for warping, causing events to pulse at the keyframe rate, or when the linear motion assumption of the warp slightly deviates from true rotation. These effects were mild and diminished with higher base render rates.
- **Sensor Non-Idealities:** Figure 5 illustrates the visual impact of modeled noise: per-pixel threshold mismatch introduced vertical striping roughness, and leak events appeared as a sparse spatiotemporal noise haze. Figure 2 demonstrates motion blur, where a 20ms exposure resulted in smeared edges and wide event bands, encompassing the motion within the exposure window.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The EVIS plugin represents a notable advancement in the generation of synthetic data for event-based vision systems in robotics, with several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Addresses Data Scarcity and Cost:** By providing a scalable and efficient method for generating high-rate, fully labeled event streams within a physics simulator, EVIS directly tackles the critical bottleneck of scarce and expensive real-world event camera data. This removes a significant barrier to entry and progress for researchers in event-based perception and control.

2. **Enables Scalable Training of Event-based Pipelines:** The ability to generate event data at or above real-time speeds on a single GPU, coupled with the GPU-parallel capabilities of NVIDIA Isaac Sim/Isaac Lab, means that event-based perception and control algorithms can be trained at scales comparable to those using conventional RGB data. This acceleration is crucial for the development and validation of complex learning-based robotic systems.

3. **High Fidelity and Physics-Consistent Ground Truth:** EVIS ensures that generated events are physics-consistent, derived from a genuine physics simulation, and accompanied by exact, frame-perfect ground truth data. This level of fidelity and precise ground truth is essential for developing robust and accurate perception and control algorithms, as it eliminates ambiguities and inaccuracies often present in real-world or video-derived datasets.

4. **Flexibility and Configurability:** The plugin's direct integration into Isaac Sim/Isaac Lab allows researchers to easily convert any existing scene or robot setup into an event-camera-enabled environment. Its configurability, including options for motion-vector frame interpolation, various sensor noise models, and motion blur, offers a broad range of simulation conditions to match specific research needs or approximate diverse real-world event camera characteristics.

5. **Lowering Barriers for Research:** By simplifying the process of obtaining high-quality, labeled event data, EVIS aims to democratize event-based vision research. It enables researchers to focus more on algorithm development and less on data collection logistics, potentially leading to faster innovation in areas where event cameras excel, such as high-speed robotics, vision in high dynamic range environments, and low-latency control.

6. **Contribution to the Simulation Ecosystem:** EVIS fills a crucial gap by providing a high-fidelity event camera simulation solution integrated within a modern, GPU-parallel physics simulator like NVIDIA Isaac Sim. This enhances the capabilities of these widely adopted platforms, making them more versatile for multimodal sensor simulation and robot learning research.

In summary, EVIS has the potential to significantly accelerate the research and development of event-based perception and control systems, facilitating their adoption in advanced robotic applications that benefit from the unique capabilities of event cameras.
