# Flow as Flow: Modeling Robot Velocity Fields as Probability Velocity Fields for Flow-Based Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Keio University researchers developed “Flow as Flow,” a framework that models robot velocity fields as probability velocity fields using an autoregressive flow matching formulation for efficient robot flow generation. This method enabled a 33x faster inference speed and achieved an average success rate of 58% in 13 real-world mobile manipulation tasks, outperforming prior baselines by 10 percentage points.

## Problem

- Training robotic foundation models is bottlenecked by the need for extensive, costly, and robot-specific teleoperation data.
- Prior flow-based manipulation methods often represent robot flows as sparse keypoint displacements, which less accurately capture the continuous nature of motion.
- Traditional generative models for motion generation typically suffer from slow inference speeds, hindering real-time robotic applications.

## Method

- The framework models robot velocity fields as dense probability velocity fields using ==Conditional Flow Matching (CFM)==, leveraging a stabilizing feedback form for robustness.
- An autoregressive generation approach is employed, where the model conditions on past point coordinates to accelerate the generation process.
- A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture is used for the flow generation module, trained on diverse cross-embodiment video data (human and robot) preprocessed with segmentation masks.

## Results

- Flow as Flow achieved ADE scores of **21.23** on Fractal and **27.11** on Bridge V2 benchmarks, surpassing the best baselines by **15.91** and **20.18** points respectively, and demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization.
- The method provided approximately **33x** faster inference speed, generating flows in 44 ms per sample compared to 1,430 ms for the best baseline.
- In real-world mobile manipulation tasks, the framework attained an average success rate of **58%**, outperforming the strongest baseline (Track2Act) by 10 percentage points and improving existing language-conditioned methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating robot velocity fields as dense probability velocity fields within a flow matching framework improves fidelity and robustness for motion representation.
> - Autoregressive generation significantly reduces inference time for generative models, making them more practical for real-time robot control.
> - Embodiment-agnostic robot flow representations extracted from diverse video data (human and robot) enable scalable policy learning and better generalization across different robot platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the active and evolving field of robotic learning, particularly focusing on the development of generalizable robot manipulation capabilities through robotic foundation models. A significant challenge in this domain is the bottleneck in data collection, as training these models typically requires extensive, labor-intensive teleoperation using real robots. This research addresses this limitation by focusing on flow-based object manipulation, which utilizes "robot flows" (robot velocity fields) as embodiment-agnostic motion representations. These representations can be extracted from diverse visual data, including human demonstrations, thereby reducing reliance on costly robot-specific data and enabling scalable policy learning from readily available video data.

Previous approaches in flow-based manipulation have often modeled robot flows as displacements of sparse keypoints, rather than as dense velocity fields. This earlier formulation is noted as a less accurate representation of the continuous-time nature of motions and can lead to approximations that are problematic in real-world robotic implementations. This paper aims to advance the field by proposing a method that directly models these continuous velocity fields, leveraging recent advancements in generative modeling, specifically flow matching, which has demonstrated capabilities in tasks like image generation and object segmentation. By formulating robot motions as probability velocity fields within a flow matching framework, this work seeks to improve the fidelity and efficiency of robot flow generation, contributing to more robust and generalizable robotic manipulation policies capable of learning from cross-embodiment data. It also builds upon the concept of autoregressive generation methods within diffusion and flow matching frameworks to mitigate inference speed limitations typically associated with such models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the "Flow as Flow" framework are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing the Data Scarcity in Robot Learning:** A fundamental motivation is to overcome the data bottleneck in training robotic foundation models. Current methods often necessitate substantial robot teleoperation for each specific embodiment, making large-scale data collection prohibitive. The authors aim to enable scalable policy learning by utilizing low-cost, widely available video data, including human demonstrations, to reduce the dependence on costly robot-specific data.
- **Developing Embodiment-Agnostic Motion Representations:** To effectively leverage diverse data from various sources (robots and humans), the work seeks to establish motion-centric representations that are "embodiment-agnostic." Robot velocity fields are identified as suitable for this purpose, as they can be extracted from visual observations without requiring additional processing to reconcile embodiment-specific differences.
- **Improving the Fidelity of Robot Motion Modeling:** Prior flow-based object manipulation studies are criticized for formulating robot flows as displacements of sparse keypoints rather than dense velocity fields. This sparse representation is considered an approximation that may not fully capture the continuous-time nature of robot motions and can lead to issues in real-world implementation. A key objective is, therefore, to model robot motions as *dense probability velocity fields* that are physically consistent and better reflect continuous temporal evolution.
- **Achieving Efficient and High-Quality Robot Flow Generation:** The paper aims to propose a framework that not only generates high-quality robot flows but also does so efficiently. Traditional generative models, such as diffusion models, often suffer from slow inference speeds due to the large number of sampling steps required. By leveraging an autoregressive flow matching formulation, the authors intend to accelerate the generation process without compromising the expressive power of the model.
- **Validating the Utility in Downstream Robot Manipulation Tasks:** Beyond generating robot flows, a significant objective is to demonstrate that these generated flows can effectively condition manipulation policies for real-world tasks. The motivation is to show that the improved quality and efficiency of the robot flows translate into enhanced robot performance in practical object manipulation scenarios, including mobile manipulation tasks that extend beyond tabletop settings.
- **Enhancing Generalization to Unseen Embodiments and Environments:** The use of cross-embodiment data and embodiment-agnostic representations is motivated by the desire to improve the generalization capabilities of robotic policies. The goal is to develop a framework that performs robustly not only in in-domain settings but also in zero-shot scenarios involving substantially different robot embodiments and environments from those encountered during training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Flow as Flow" framework addresses goal-conditioned object manipulation, decomposing it into two subtasks: robot flow generation and object manipulation. The core innovation lies in the robot flow generation subtask, which models robot velocity fields as probability velocity fields using a flow matching formulation. The methodology is structured as follows:

#### 3.1. Problem Statement and Context

The task involves generating a robot flow (2D end-effector flow under a fixed camera setup) conditioned on an initial image (I) and a goal image (G). This flow then conditions an action generation module to output end-effector poses (a_t) at each timestep, enabling the robot to manipulate objects to achieve the specified goal state. The assumption is that the end-effector is visible in both I and G.

#### 3.2. Flow as Flow Framework

The framework builds upon the concept that robot motions are time-evolving physical processes. It formulates robot flows as probability flows in a generation space by leveraging Conditional Flow Matching (CFM).

- **Flow Matching Preliminary:** Flow matching is a generative modeling technique that learns a time-dependent probability velocity field `v(x_t, t)`. This field transports samples from a simple prior distribution `p_prior` (e.g., Gaussian) to a target data distribution `p_data`. The generation process involves integrating an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) `dx_t/dt = v(x_t, t)` from `t=0` to `t=1`. Training is performed via regression on the velocity field using the objective `L_FM = E[||v_ψ(x_t, t) - v(x_t, t)||^2]`.

- **Modeling Robot Velocity Fields:** The method initializes N points uniformly on the initial image I. Their future positions, `Ξ_h ∈ R^(N×2)`, are ground-truth point coordinates at timestep h. The corresponding velocities are `˙Ξ_h ∈ R^(N×2)`. The target velocity field `v(Ξ_h, X, h)` is constructed in a stabilizing feedback form:
 `v(Ξ_h, X, h) = ˙Ξ_h - k(X - Ξ_h)`
 Here, `X` is a noisy sample drawn from `N(Ξ_h, σ_0^2 e^(-2kh) I)`, and the term `k(X - Ξ_h)` acts as a stabilization term, with `k` being a coefficient. This formulation encourages robustness to out-of-distribution samples and models dense velocity fields.

- **Conditional Flow Matching Loss for Robot Flows:** A velocity model `v_θ`, parameterized by `θ`, is trained using the CFM loss:
 `L_CFM = E[||v_θ(X, h | I, G, Ξ_0:h-1) - v(Ξ_h, X, h)||^2]`
 The model `v_θ` predicts the velocity field conditioned on the initial image I, the goal image G, and the history of point coordinates up to the previous timestep, `Ξ_0:h-1`.

- **Autoregressive Generation:** The point coordinates at step `h`, denoted `X_h`, are obtained by integrating the predicted velocity field autoregressively:
 `X_h = X_0 + ∫_0^h v_θ(X_τ, τ | I, G, X_<τ) dτ`
 This autoregressive approach, where the model conditions on past coordinates, contributes to faster generation, similar to methods in autoregressive diffusion and flow matching. The full generated flow `X_0:H-1` is then a concatenation of `X_h` over the sequence length `H`.

#### 3.3. Model Architecture

The architecture comprises two modules:

- **Flow Generation Module:** This module implements `v_θ` using a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture, which aligns with previous work in the field. Each DiT block is modulated via adaLN-Zero using a shared conditioning vector. This vector is created by embedding I and G (using ResNet-18) and the timestep `h` (using sinusoidal positional encoding), and summing these embeddings. The input sequence `X_0:h` is constructed autoregressively, with flattened point coordinates treated as tokens. The DiT output at the latest step represents the predicted velocity.

- **Action Generation Module:** This module predicts the end-effector pose `a_t` based on the current observation `O_t`, the robot state `s_t`, and the generated robot flow `X_0:H-1`. It is based on Diffusion Policy (DP), but uses a DiT-based architecture rather than the original U-Net. This module also uses flow matching for action generation, training a velocity model `u_φ` with a CFM loss, where `a_t` is generated by integrating an ODE defined by `u_φ`.

#### 3.4. Training Data and Preprocessing

The Flow Generation module is trained on a diverse set of cross-embodiment videos, including human video clips from Something-Something-v2 and EPIC-KITCHENS, and robot video clips from Fractal and Bridge V2.
- Videos are preprocessed by resizing to 256x256.
- Segmentation masks for human hands or robot end-effectors are generated (using SAM3 for humans, fine-tuned Robot-SAM for robots) and incorporated as an alpha channel in the input image. This step helps focus the model on relevant regions.
- Egocentric videos are filtered to exclude clips with substantial camera motion (ground-truth flow magnitude exceeding 100 pixels) to prevent global motion from obscuring task-relevant flows.
- Ground-truth point trajectories (`Ξ_0:H-1`) are obtained by tracking a uniform grid of points (e.g., 10x10) on I using CoTracker3.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents both quantitative and qualitative results, demonstrating the performance of Flow as Flow in robot flow generation and downstream real-world manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Performance (Flow Generation)

- **Benchmark Superiority:** On standard benchmarks for robot flow generation, Flow as Flow consistently outperformed representative baseline methods.
 - In-domain settings (Fractal, Bridge V2): Flow as Flow achieved ADE scores of 21.23 and 27.11, respectively, surpassing the best baseline scores of 37.14 (Im2Flow2Act) and 47.29 (Track2Act) by substantial margins (15.91 and 20.18 points).
 - Zero-shot settings (DROID-100, Fanuc Manipulation): The method demonstrated strong generalization, obtaining ADE scores of 35.89 and 22.46. These were superior to the best baseline scores of 40.73 (Track2Act) and 27.37 (Track2Act), with improvements of 4.84 and 4.91 points, respectively. These differences were reported as statistically significant (p < 0.01).
- **Integration into Baselines:** When Flow as Flow was incorporated into existing language-conditioned methods like FLIP and Im2Flow2Act, it improved their ADE scores across all benchmarks, indicating the framework's effectiveness across various model architectures. For instance, Im2Flow2Act's ADE scores were reduced from 37.14 to 33.21 on Fractal, and from 51.48 to 42.96 on Bridge V2.
- **Inference Speed:** A notable finding was the significant acceleration in inference speed. Flow as Flow achieved approximately 33× faster generation compared to the best baseline (Track2Act), with an inference time of 44 ms per sample versus 1,430 ms for Track2Act.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Performance (Flow Generation)

- Qualitative comparisons with Track2Act (a baseline) showed that Flow as Flow generated more appropriate robot flows. Examples included correctly targeting objects and generating motions towards the specified goal locations, even in scenarios where the baseline deviated or targeted incorrect objects. The method also maintained robust performance in zero-shot scenarios, generating plausible end-effector motions where the baseline failed to produce meaningful flows.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Effectiveness of Flow as Flow Formulation:** Replacing Flow as Flow with standard flow matching (which lacks the autoregressive formulation and stabilizing feedback for dense velocity fields) resulted in degraded performance. ADE scores increased across all benchmarks (e.g., by 7.99 points on Fractal), and inference speed became approximately 24× slower (1,062 ms vs. 44 ms). This suggests that the proposed formulation contributes to both higher quality and faster generation.
- **Contribution of Stabilizing Feedback and Dense Velocity Fields (Appendix):** Further ablation studies confirmed the importance of specific components of the target velocity field design (Equation 4).
 - Removing the stabilizing feedback term (`k=0`) degraded ADE scores significantly (e.g., 13.95 points higher on Fractal).
 - Using a sparse formulation (`σ_0=0`, where coordinates were not perturbed, preventing dense velocity field learning) also resulted in higher ADE scores (e.g., 14.34 points higher on Fractal). These results indicate the effectiveness of both stabilizing feedback and dense velocity-field modeling for high-quality flow generation.

#### 4.4. Real-World Manipulation Performance

- **Superior Average Success Rate:** In real-world experiments involving 13 diverse mobile manipulation tasks, Flow as Flow achieved an average success rate of 58%. This outperformed the strongest baseline, Track2Act (a goal-conditioned flow-based method), by 10 percentage points (48% success rate).
- **Benefits of Flow-Based Methods:** The study observed that flow-based methods generally achieved higher average success rates than non-flow-based Diffusion Policy variants (DP-Lang with 38% and DP-Goal with 22%). This highlights the advantage of robot flows as conditioning signals over language instructions or raw goal images in these tasks.
- **Improved Baseline Performance:** Similar to the flow generation task, incorporating Flow as Flow into FLIP and Im2Flow2Act increased their average success rates from 28% to 43% and from 45% to 55%, respectively, in real-world tasks.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Performance (Real-World)

- Qualitative examples demonstrated successful rollouts in tasks such as bussing a table, closing a laptop, and pushing a chair. The robot, conditioned on the flows generated by Flow as Flow, performed appropriate manipulations even in cluttered configurations, with hinged objects, and requiring whole-body movement of the mobile manipulator.

#### 4.6. Error Analysis (Appendix)

- An error analysis of 100 failure cases from the Bridge V2 dataset identified "intermediate trajectory deviation" as the primary bottleneck (39% of failures). This indicates that while the method often targets the correct object and goal, the generated path between them can sometimes diverge from the optimal or ground-truth trajectory. Other failure modes included inappropriate behavior in source demonstrations, target object comprehension errors, target location mismatches, and tracking errors in ground-truth flows.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Flow as Flow" framework represents an advancement in robot motion representation and generation, with several potential impacts on the field of robotic learning and its applications.

- **Enhanced Fidelity and Efficiency in Robot Motion Modeling:** By directly modeling robot velocity fields as dense probability velocity fields using an autoregressive flow matching formulation, the work offers a more physically consistent and continuous-time representation of motion compared to prior sparse keypoint displacement methods. This improved fidelity, combined with a 33× faster generation speed, could significantly accelerate the development and deployment of robotic manipulation skills. The efficiency gain is particularly important for real-time control applications where rapid action generation is critical.

- **Improved Generalization Capabilities:** The framework's ability to learn from cross-embodiment data (human and robot videos) and its demonstrated robustness in zero-shot scenarios suggest enhanced generalization. This is crucial for building more versatile robotic systems that can operate in diverse, unseen environments and adapt to different robot platforms without extensive re-training. This addresses a core challenge in scaling robot learning to broader tasks and settings.

- **Practical Applicability in Real-World Mobile Manipulation:** The real-world experiments, especially in complex mobile manipulation tasks, highlight the practical utility of the generated robot flows. Achieving higher success rates than strong baselines, even in cluttered and sensitive tasks, indicates that "Flow as Flow" can contribute to more reliable and capable autonomous robots in human-centric environments such as factories, warehouses, and homes. The capacity to handle whole-body movement in mobile manipulators extends the scope of robotic applications beyond fixed-base systems.

- **Addressing Data Scarcity and Cost:** By leveraging embodiment-agnostic motion representations and enabling effective learning from readily available human video data, the framework offers a pathway to mitigate the reliance on labor-intensive and costly robot-specific teleoperated demonstrations. This has the potential to democratize access to training data for robotic learning, fostering innovation and enabling smaller research groups or companies to develop advanced robot capabilities.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the use of 2D flows and the assumption of a static camera, suggest clear avenues for future research. Extending the flow representation to 3D or 6-DoF flows would enable handling depth and orientation-sensitive manipulation, while incorporating dynamic camera parameter estimation could broaden applicability to egocentric human videos and mobile robot platforms with moving cameras. The error analysis on intermediate trajectory deviation also points to opportunities for improving temporal consistency in generated motions, potentially through techniques like action chunking.

In summary, "Flow as Flow" presents a method for robot flow generation that improves upon prior approaches in terms of both the quality of motion representation and the efficiency of generation. Its demonstrated effectiveness in diverse virtual and real-world manipulation tasks positions it as a significant contribution towards developing more generalizable, efficient, and robust robotic systems.
