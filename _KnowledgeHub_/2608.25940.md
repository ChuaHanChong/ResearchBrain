# A Statistical Audit of Physical AI Benchmark Redundancy

> [!summary] Summary
> A statistical audit of physical-AI evaluation: the authors build a dense 51-model by 12-benchmark score matrix from model cards, benchmark papers, and 159 of their own official-protocol runs, then measure shared information via rank correlation, ridge leave-one-out prediction, and PCA. Benchmarks are heavily redundant, and greedy utility-based forward selection yields a four-benchmark suite retaining 78.5% of the full suite's discriminating power.

## Problem

- Vendors report each model on hand-picked benchmark sets that barely overlap, leaving the model-by-benchmark matrix mostly empty and competing systems not directly comparable.
- New benchmarks are introduced on the unchecked premise they measure something existing ones do not; nobody has quantified the unique signal a physical-AI benchmark adds.
- Existing statistical audits of language benchmarks need dense item-level response data, which physical AI lacks since results are published only as benchmark-level aggregates.

## Method

- Dense matrix construction: 12 benchmarks chosen from a 51-benchmark registry by density, recency, and diversity; 51 of 152 models retained with at least 8 of 12 scores, gaps filled by own runs.
- Redundancy measurement: pairwise ==Spearman rank correlations== plus average-linkage clustering, ==ridge regression== predicting each benchmark from the other eleven with leave-one-model-out cross-validated R-squared as a uniqueness score.
- ==Greedy forward selection== under utility U = Gini discrimination times marginal information (one minus R-squared on the selected set), then a ==Bradley-Terry== fit treating each selected benchmark as a judge.

## Results

- Mean pairwise Spearman correlation is **0.487**, all positive; substitute pairs reach **0.876** (EmbSpatial and CV-Bench) and **0.860** (Where2Place and RefSpatial-Bench).
- Collapsing the two substitute pairs into single columns moves **22 of 51** models by three or more places, with MiMo-Embodied dropping **9** and GPT-4o gaining **9** under equal weighting.
- Four benchmarks (RefSpatial-Bench, MindCube, VSI-Bench, BLINK) retain **78.5%** of total utility; the last four contribute **4.4%**, and all four survive any forced core opening pick.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Equal-weight averaging over a correlated suite silently weights an ability by how often it happens to be measured, so ranking partly reflects suite composition, not capability.
> - A first component explaining 55.2% of suite variance tracks general vision-language ability at 0.95; residualizing halves mean correlation, so most sharing is not physical-specific.
> - Marginal informativeness is conditional, never intrinsic: a highly discriminating benchmark can be worthless beside its substitute, and the audit needs only overlapping aggregate scores.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of physical AI, a domain involving AI systems interacting with the physical world, is characterized by its nascent evaluation methodologies. Unlike more mature areas such as natural language processing (NLP) or computer vision, physical AI lacks a standardized, widely adopted set of benchmarks for model comparison. This results in a fragmented evaluation landscape where developers frequently report new models on hand-picked benchmark suites that often exhibit minimal overlap. Consequently, direct comparisons between competing physical AI systems are challenging due to the absence of a shared evaluation axis.

A critical issue in this landscape is the potential for benchmark redundancy. If multiple benchmarks within a suite measure similar capabilities, an aggregated score (often an average across selected benchmarks) can inadvertently overweight those shared abilities, leading to misleading performance metrics and model rankings. The premise that each new benchmark introduces a unique signal is often unchecked, contributing to this accumulation of potentially redundant evaluations.

This research addresses this gap by conducting a statistical audit of physical AI benchmarks. While similar auditing efforts exist for large language models (LLMs)—such as Metabench (Kipnis et al., 2024), which uses item-level response data to identify redundancy, or the factor analysis by Burnell et al. (2023) on HELM tasks—these approaches typically require dense item-level data. Such granular data is generally unavailable in physical AI, where results are predominantly published as benchmark-level aggregates. This paper contributes by demonstrating that a meaningful audit can be performed effectively at the benchmark level, thus extending the methodology of quantitative evaluation to a domain where detailed item-level responses are not consistently accessible. It aims to quantify shared and unique information among physical AI benchmarks and propose methods for constructing more efficient and representative evaluation suites.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from two challenges prevalent in the evaluation of physical AI models: the lack of comparability among models and the potential for misleading performance metrics due to benchmark redundancy.

1. **Lack of Comparability:** The practice of model vendors reporting performance on disparate, hand-picked benchmark sets creates a sparse model-by-benchmark matrix. This fragmentation hinders the establishment of a common evaluation ground, making it difficult for researchers and practitioners to compare the capabilities of different physical AI systems directly. There is no equivalent of an MTEB-style leaderboard that offers a unified axis for model performance assessment.

2. **Misleading Aggregated Scores:** When model performance is summarized as an average across a selected set of benchmarks, and if these benchmarks measure overlapping or highly correlated abilities, the average score can silently double-count the shared signal. This leads to an inflated representation of a model's capabilities, where its reported standing might partly reflect how frequently the chosen suite measures a particular strength, rather than a comprehensive assessment of distinct abilities. The paper highlights examples of this repetition in published reports, where a single capability like "pointing" is evaluated across multiple distinct benchmarks within one model report.

To address these issues, the research sets out to answer two specific research questions:

- **RQ1 - Redundancy:** This objective seeks to quantify the extent of shared information among a set of physical AI benchmarks. It investigates how strongly this redundancy influences pooled average scores, which are common in vendor reporting. The goal is to identify how much unique signal each benchmark contributes beyond what existing benchmarks already provide.

- **RQ2 - Sufficiency:** This objective aims to determine the minimal size a benchmark suite can be while still effectively differentiating models and capturing the non-redundant abilities measured by a larger, more comprehensive suite. This involves developing criteria for selecting a compact set of benchmarks that maintain the discriminative power of the full suite without introducing unnecessary redundancy.

The overarching motivation is to move towards a more principled and transparent evaluation framework for physical AI. By quantifying redundancy and identifying a sufficient subset of benchmarks, the authors intend to enable more accurate model comparisons, reduce evaluation costs, and ensure that reported performance metrics reflect distinct capabilities rather than the composition of the chosen benchmark suite.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a data-driven, statistical auditing approach to analyze the redundancy and sufficiency of physical AI benchmarks.

#### 3.1. Data Collection and Matrix Construction

1. **Benchmark and Model Indexing:** The process began by indexing 51 physical AI benchmarks and 152 models from model cards, research papers, and official blogs.
2. **Benchmark Selection (12 Benchmarks):** Out of the initial 51, a subset of 12 physical AI benchmarks was selected based on three criteria:
 - **Density:** Each benchmark needed to be reported for at least five candidate models to ensure sufficient data for covariance analysis.
 - **Recency:** Benchmarks were chosen for their recurring presence in recent model reports, reflecting current field practices.
 - **Diversity:** The selected benchmarks collectively spanned key tasks measured by physical AI, preventing the findings of redundancy from being attributed to a narrow selection of similar tasks.
 The chosen benchmarks included VSI-BENCH, EMBSPATIAL-BENCH, REFSPATIAL-BENCH, WHERE2PLACE, ERQA, CV-BENCH, SAT, ROBOSPATIAL, REALWORLDQA, OMNISPATIAL, MINDCUBE, and BLINK. They varied in claimed ability, task format (multiple-choice or coordinate emission), size, and performance scale (0-100).
3. **Model Selection (51 Models):** From the 152 indexed models, 51 were selected. These models had scores reported for at least two-thirds (8 of 12) of the chosen benchmarks. The selected models, released between 2024 and 2026, represent diverse providers, architectures (open-weight and closed), and sizes.
4. **Score Acquisition and Matrix Completion:** Scores were primarily extracted from model cards and papers. To overcome sparsity in the model × benchmark matrix (where many cells were empty), the authors performed their own evaluation runs for missing scores. This contributed 159 additional data points to the matrix, using each benchmark’s official evaluation code and prompts with greedy decoding.
5. **Data Preprocessing:** Before multivariate analysis, all benchmark columns were z-scored to normalize for differing difficulty levels and score spreads. Spearman rank correlations were used as the default pairwise measure to ensure independence from absolute score values.
6. **Benchmark Categorization:** For descriptive purposes, the 12 benchmarks were classified into five groups based on the tasks they measure: Pointing, Single-image relational, Multi-view and video, Embodied, and General visual perception (BLINK). This categorization served as a taxonomy rather than a basis for analysis grouping.

#### 3.2. Redundancy Analysis (RQ1)

1. **Pairwise Correlation:** Spearman pairwise rank correlations were computed across the 12 benchmarks to measure how similarly they rank models, irrespective of difficulty. Hierarchical clustering using `1 - ρ` as a distance metric was applied to visually represent benchmark groupings.
2. **Benchmark Predictability (Uniqueness):** To assess how much unique information each benchmark provides, ridge regression was employed. Each benchmark was predicted from the other 11 using leave-one-out cross-validated R^2. A lower R^2 indicates higher uniqueness.
3. **Impact on Model Rankings:** The effect of redundancy on model rankings was evaluated by comparing two ranking methods:
 - An arithmetic average score across all 12 benchmarks (equal weighting).
 - An arithmetic average after collapsing highly correlated "substitute pairs" (identified in the pairwise analysis) into single averaged columns, reducing the effective number of benchmarks.
4. **General Capability Analysis (Appendix B):** To differentiate between redundancy due to specific physical AI skills versus general vision-language capabilities, an additional set of 9 general language and vision benchmarks (e.g., MMMU, MMSTAR, MMLU-PRO, LM-ARENA) was incorporated.
 - Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the 12 physical benchmarks to identify the first principal component (PC1).
 - This physical PC1 was then correlated with the 9 general benchmarks (and their own PC1) to quantify the alignment between physical AI performance and general capability.
 - Each of the 12 physical benchmarks was then residualized on an externally estimated general axis (the first PC of the 9 general anchors) to isolate physical-specific redundancy, and pairwise correlations were recomputed.

#### 3.3. Minimal Benchmark Suite Selection (RQ2)

1. **Greedy Forward Selection:** A greedy forward selection algorithm was used to construct a minimal benchmark suite. Benchmarks were added one at a time, based on their marginal gain conditional on already selected benchmarks.
2. **Utility Function:** A utility function `U(b | S) = g(b) * (1 - R^2(b ~ S))` was defined to evaluate candidate benchmarks `b` against an already selected set `S`.
 - **Discrimination `g(b)`:** Measured by the Gini coefficient of the benchmark's score distribution across models. A higher Gini coefficient indicates better separation of models.
 - **Marginal Information `1 - R^2(b ~ S)`:** Represents the share of a benchmark's variance that cannot be reproduced by the already selected set `S`, estimated via OLS regression.
3. **Selection Process:** Starting with an empty set `S`, the benchmark with the highest utility (or highest discrimination for the first pick) was added. This process was repeated, recomputing the utility of all remaining candidates against the enlarged set, until all 12 benchmarks were included. The cumulative utility at each step was tracked.
4. **Model Ranking on Compact Suite:** Models were ranked on the selected compact suite using a Bradley–Terry model, which treats each benchmark as a "judge" casting a vote for the higher-scoring model in every pairwise comparison. An L2-penalized maximum likelihood fit was used to derive Elo ratings.
5. **Robustness Check (Appendix C):** The stability of the selected core suite was tested by forcing different benchmarks into the initial selection slot and observing the resulting compositions and captured utility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis yielded several findings regarding the structure, redundancy, and sufficiency of physical AI benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Dense Evaluation Matrix

- The study successfully constructed a dense evaluation matrix of 51 physical AI models across 12 physical AI benchmarks. This matrix integrated scores from published model cards and benchmark papers with 159 additional evaluation runs conducted by the authors, making previously incomparable models amenable to direct statistical comparison.

#### 4.2. Redundancy (RQ1)

- **Positive Correlations:** All pairwise Spearman rank correlations among the 12 physical AI benchmarks were positive, with an average correlation coefficient (ρ) of 0.487. This indicated a substantial degree of shared signal across the suite.
- **Substitute Pairs:** Two pairs of benchmarks exhibited high correlations, suggesting they are close substitutes:
 - EMBSPATIAL ↔ CV-BENCH (ρ = 0.876)
 - WHERE2PLACE ↔ REFSPATIAL-BENCH (ρ = 0.860)
- **Benchmark Predictability (Uniqueness):** The leave-one-out cross-validated R^2 analysis demonstrated varying levels of uniqueness:
 - WHERE2PLACE (R^2 = 0.727), REFSPATIAL-BENCH (R^2 = 0.721), and ERQA (R^2 = 0.706) were the most predictable from their peers, indicating high redundancy.
 - REALWORLDQA (R^2 = 0.319), ROBOSPATIAL (R^2 = 0.351), and BLINK (R^2 = 0.378) were the least predictable, suggesting they offer more unique information.
 - MINDCUBE, ERQA, and REFSPATIAL-BENCH frequently appeared among the top predictors for other benchmarks, suggesting their central role in the shared capability structure.
- **Impact on Model Rankings:** Collapsing the two identified substitute pairs (REFSPATIAL-BENCH/WHERE2PLACE and EMBSPATIAL/CV-BENCH) into single averaged columns significantly altered model rankings. Twenty-two of the 51 models shifted their positions by three or more places. For instance, MIMO-EMBODIED-7B dropped 9 places, while GPT-4O gained 9 places, illustrating how suite composition can influence a model's perceived standing.
- **General Capability Overlap (Appendix B):** A principal component analysis on the 12 physical benchmarks revealed that the first principal component (PC1) explained 55.2% of the total variance. This physical PC1 strongly correlated with general vision-language capability, as estimated from 9 non-physical benchmarks, with a Spearman ρ = 0.952. Residualizing the physical benchmarks on this external general capability axis roughly halved the mean pairwise correlation within the physical suite (from 0.487 to 0.250). This suggests that approximately half of the shared signal among physical AI benchmarks is attributable to general vision-language capabilities rather than abilities specific to physical AI. Specific redundancy, such as that within the pointing or 2D-perception pairs, remained substantial after residualization.

#### 4.3. Minimal Benchmark Suite (RQ2)

- **Compact Suite Identification:** Using greedy forward selection based on the defined utility function, a four-benchmark subset was identified: REFSPATIAL-BENCH, MINDCUBE, VSI-BENCH, and BLINK. This compact suite retained 78.5% of the total utility (discriminating power and unique information) of the full 12-benchmark suite.
- **Complementary Skills:** The selected benchmarks encompassed complementary skills: precise localization (REFSPATIAL-BENCH), spatial mental modeling from limited views (MINDCUBE), spatial reasoning over video (VSI-BENCH), and multi-image perceptual primitives (BLINK).
- **Leaderboard Generation:** A Bradley–Terry model, where each of the four selected benchmarks acted as a judge, was used to produce a compact leaderboard of 51 models.
 - The top 10 models included both open-weight and closed-API systems.
 - Models specifically post-trained for embodied or spatial tasks (e.g., HY-EMBODIED-0.5, ROBOBRAIN-32B-2.0) featured prominently in the top ranks, indicating that domain-specific training can provide an advantage beyond general capabilities.
 - Observations within model families (e.g., QWEN-VL) showed performance scaling with size, but across different training recipes, larger parameter counts did not consistently guarantee higher ranks, suggesting architectural or training recipe differences play a role.
- **Robustness of Selection (Appendix C):** The core four benchmarks demonstrated robustness to variations in the initial greedy selection pick. While forcing a core member as the first selection resulted in a highly similar final set and captured utility, forcing a rejected benchmark or a direct substitute led to slightly lower captured utility and the displacement of one core member. MINDCUBE and VSI-BENCH were consistently included in the first four, and REFSPATIAL-BENCH was always selected.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a quantitative, data-driven audit of physical AI benchmarks, offering several contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Framework for Efficient Evaluation:** The study introduces a generalizable methodology for statistically auditing benchmark suites at the aggregate score level, which is particularly relevant for domains like physical AI where item-level data is often unavailable. This method can be applied to any field facing issues of benchmark redundancy and can guide the construction of more efficient evaluation protocols.
2. **Reduced Redundancy and Evaluation Costs:** By quantifying shared information and identifying highly correlated benchmarks, the work highlights avenues to streamline evaluation. Identifying and potentially removing redundant benchmarks can significantly reduce the computational and resource costs associated with model evaluation without sacrificing discriminative power.
3. **Improved Model Comparability and Ranking Accuracy:** The construction of a dense model-by-benchmark matrix facilitates direct comparisons between a wider range of physical AI models. By exposing how benchmark redundancy can distort model rankings, the research encourages the use of more balanced and less biased evaluation suites, leading to more accurate and reliable leaderboards. This addresses the problem of reported rankings being artifacts of suite composition rather than intrinsic model capability.
4. **Differentiated Capabilities:** The analysis demonstrates how to disentangle general vision-language capabilities from those specific to physical AI. This distinction is crucial for understanding what benchmarks truly measure and for focusing future benchmark development on genuinely novel or underspecified abilities within the physical AI domain.
5. **Guidance for Benchmark Development:** The findings provide empirical evidence for benchmark developers to consider both discriminative power (Gini coefficient) and marginal uniqueness when introducing new evaluations. This encourages the creation of benchmarks that genuinely extend the scope of assessed abilities rather than replicating existing ones.
6. **Insights into Model Strengths:** The compact leaderboard derived from the audit provides a more concise view of model performance on a diverse, non-redundant set of physical AI capabilities. The observation that domain-specific post-training can significantly improve model standing suggests the continued importance of specialized training in physical AI, rather than relying solely on general-purpose vision-language models.

While the study acknowledges limitations such as its reliance on aggregate scores, the observational nature of its findings, and the greedy nature of its selection algorithm, it lays a foundational step towards a more rigorous and standardized evaluation paradigm for physical AI. By promoting transparency and efficiency in benchmarking, this work has the potential to accelerate progress and foster more meaningful comparisons in the rapidly evolving field of physical AI.
