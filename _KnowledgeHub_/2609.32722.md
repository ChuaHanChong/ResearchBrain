# Scaling Properties of Same-Family On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> An empirical scaling study of on-policy distillation (OPD) across weak-to-strong, same-base and strong-to-weak Qwen2.5 pairs from 0.5B to 14B on math reasoning. In an early useful-transfer regime, held-out accuracy rises linearly in the square root of token-level reverse KL from the student's starting point. Joint power laws in student size, capped teacher size and teacher score predict peak accuracy within one point at held-out scales.

## Problem

- OPD is now standard in LLM post-training, yet nobody has characterized how its outcomes depend on teacher and student scale, so results are only discovered after training.
- Scaling laws for reward overoptimization exist for PPO and direct alignment against learned rewards, but no comparable KL-indexed law covers the token-level implicit reward that an OPD teacher provides.
- Recent weak-to-strong OPD methods that contrast teacher policies improve transfer from small RL experts, but none systematically measures how much capability transfers across scales or how fast.

## Method

- ==KL-indexed trajectory analysis==: plots gold score against d, the square root of the per-token average ==reverse KL== from the student's initialization, and defines a ==useful-transfer slope== plus a ==predictive-band transfer endpoint==.
- ==Joint multiplicative power laws== predict peak remaining error and useful-transfer slope from student size, effective teacher size capped at student size, and the teacher's remaining error, fitted across a 25-pair grid.
- Controlled design studies compare **Vanilla-OPD** with **Delta-OPD** (reward is the teacher's ==log-ratio== after versus before RL), vary how on-policy supervision is via ==SFT cold start== or pure off-policy distillation, and test ==bootstrapped weak-to-strong chains==.

## Results

- Early linear fits reach R-squared **0.932-0.988** across all **25** runs, and the joint law extrapolates to the held-out largest student and teacher within **0.7** (Vanilla) and **0.4** (Delta) accuracy points.
- A score-matched intermediate 3B teacher scoring **66.0** gives a 7B student peaking at **73.8**, below the **77.5** reached with the 1.5B teacher. Only the joint law predicts this ordering, at **74.1**.
- An SFT cold start from a 0.5B expert costs **6.6**, **15.7** and **19.4** points for 3B, 7B and 14B students. Every bootstrapped chain peaks below direct OPD, for example **76.8** versus **77.7** at 14B.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A teacher's benchmark score does not set its teaching value. At matched score smaller teachers transfer better, and teachers larger than the student stop helping or even hurt.
> - Weak-to-strong OPD amortizes one small RL expert across a whole model family, with students surpassing their teacher, though direct RL on the student itself still scores higher.
> - Keep weak-to-strong supervision on-policy. Off-policy imitation pins a strong student near the weak teacher's level, and later on-policy training cannot recover the lost accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Repeating task-specific reinforcement learning (RL) separately at every model size is a practical bottleneck. Bao et al. study whether one small RL expert can be reused instead. In their setup, a small RL-trained model guides a larger model as it works through math problems. The larger student works through its own answers while the teacher advises on them, rather than learning only from the teacher's completed examples. This can transfer beyond the expert's own score. Yet the apparent teacher ranking can reverse once teacher size and score are separated: a higher-scoring teacher is not necessarily a better guide.

Vanilla-OPD runs for a 7B student, guided by the 1.5B RL-endpoint teacher, a 3B intermediate checkpoint, and the 3B RL endpoint. Dashed horizontal lines mark each teacher's own gold score, and stars mark student peaks. The intermediate 3B checkpoint scores slightly above the 1.5B endpoint, but its student trajectory peaks lower. Single-run trajectories, not uncertainty intervals.

### 2. A student learns on its own answers

The technique is on-policy distillation (OPD). The student generates fresh responses, the fixed teacher provides token-level guidance on them, and the student updates from that feedback. Off-policy SFT, by contrast, imitates teacher-generated responses. The study uses Qwen2.5 Base models at 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, and 14B parameters. The math training and test data are GSM8K/MATH mixtures.

Gold score is held-out test accuracy. Training progress is the square root of a token-mean reverse-KL estimate from the student's initial SFT policy. It indexes policy change, not optimizer steps or compute.

Across the 25 Vanilla-OPD runs, the first 30 checkpoints have approximately linear fits, with $R^{2}$ from 0.932 to 0.988. Initial slopes range from 0.175 to 0.721. Every trajectory initially improves. Later phases can attenuate, saturate, or regress, and there is no shared tail shape. The paper explains the leading local behavior this way: score changes to first order in a small parameter update, while KL changes to second order.

Held-out validation accuracy (%) for 0.5B, 3B, and 14B students, with colored traces for different teachers. Dashed traces are linear fits to the first 30 observations. Curves initially rise, and their later portions vary, including flattening and decline. These are observed one-run trajectories without error bars.

### 3. Predicting the peak

The authors fit peak score and early slope as separate outcomes. The peak law accounts for student size, teacher size capped at the student's size, and measured teacher score. Peak remaining error, meaning the distance from perfect accuracy, is nearly proportional to the effective teacher's remaining error, scaled by student size.

For Vanilla-OPD, the joint peak law's leave-one-scale-out RMSE is 1.66 accuracy points, versus 3.43 for the scale-only law. These figures are prediction errors, not accuracy gains. The early-rate laws are less reliable predictors.

Observed peak accuracy across student and teacher sizes. The 7B and 14B student peaks rise with teacher size, but smaller-student trends have exceptions. The 0.5B student peaks at 40.8% with a 3B teacher and reaches only 39.0% with 7B and 37.7% with 14B. These are one-run descriptions.

### 4. A score is not a teaching signal

The decisive held-out Vanilla-OPD test uses a 7B student. Its 1.5B RL-endpoint teacher scores 63.6% and yields a student peak of 77.5%. An intermediate checkpoint of a 3B teacher scores 66.0% but yields a lower peak of 73.8%. The joint law predicts 74.1% for that cell and correctly ranks it below the 1.5B teacher. The scale-only and score-only laws instead favor the larger, higher-scoring teacher. This is one controlled checkpoint comparison. It does not establish that smaller teachers always work better.

A second test concerns chaining teachers. Bootstrapped OPD chains in this study all peak below direct transfer from the smallest RL expert at the same student size.

### 5. Predict the guide, not just its score

OPD's early transfer is regular enough to fit, and peak performance depends on both student scale and teacher competence. A teacher's value depends on more than its benchmark score. The evidence comes from one Qwen2.5 family, one math mixture, and one canonical run per pair, and generalization to other families, tasks, and recipes is untested.
