# RLVE: Scaling Up Reinforcement Learning for Language Models with Adaptive Verifiable Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> RLVE introduces adaptive verifiable environments that procedurally generate an unbounded supply of dynamically challenging problems for language models. This approach enables continuous learning, improving generalizable reasoning by 3.37% over a data-saturated baseline and achieving 2% higher average performance than a strong RLVR baseline in compute-constrained settings.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) methods for language models (LMs) face high costs and scarcity of diverse datasets with ground-truth answers for verifiable rewards.
- Traditional RL training suffers from learning signal vanishing, where static problem distributions lead to data saturation on easy tasks or stalled learning on overly difficult ones.
- Current RL training paradigms for LMs struggle with scalability and efficient continuous improvement due to finite data and inefficient learning signals.

## Method

- Introduction of ==verifiable environments== that procedurally generate an unbounded number of problems, each defined by an input template, problem generator, and an algorithmic verifier.
- Implementation of an ==adaptive difficulty mechanism== that dynamically adjusts the problem distribution based on the language model's evolving performance, maintaining a challenging learning frontier.
- Creation of ==RLVE-GYM==, a large suite of 400 manually engineered verifiable environments designed for pedagogical reasoning and leveraging the ==solving-verification asymmetry==.

## Results

- RLVE achieved a **3.37%** absolute average improvement on six reasoning benchmarks for a data-saturated 1.5B LM, outperforming continued traditional RL training by over **7x** in improvement per compute.
- For compute-constrained scenarios, RLVE training from an SFT model yielded approximately **2%** absolute average improvement over using the high-quality DeepMath-103K dataset for the same training steps.
- Scaling the number of diverse training environments from **1** to **256** consistently improved performance on 50 held-out reasoning environments across various language models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adapting problem difficulty to the model's capabilities is essential for continuous, efficient learning and preventing data saturation.
> - Scaling the diversity of training environments, rather than just data volume, is crucial for developing generalizable reasoning abilities in language models.
> - "Environment engineering," focusing on designing adaptive and verifiable problem spaces, offers a new foundational paradigm for scalable language model development.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LMs) has increasingly incorporated reinforcement learning (RL) to enhance their capabilities, particularly in complex reasoning tasks. Works such as those involving InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) and subsequent models by prominent AI laboratories (OpenAI, 2024; Google DeepMind, 2025; DeepSeek-AI, 2025) demonstrate the potential of RL for LMs. However, current RL training paradigms for LMs encounter several challenges.

A primary issue is the saturation of model improvement on finite training data, as noted by Kumar et al. (2024) and Hu et al. (2025b). This saturation is often attributed to two factors:
1. **Cost of Data Collection:** Verifiable rewards, common in RL for LMs, often necessitate ground-truth answers for a large number of problems, which can be resource-intensive to collect (Lambert et al., 2025; DeepSeek-AI, 2025).
2. **Inefficient Learning Signals:** RL training can become inefficient or stall if the problem distribution is static. Problems that are too easy provide little learning signal, while those that are too difficult result in consistently poor rewards, hindering gradient updates (Razin et al., 2024; 2025). This leads to a suboptimal "effective prompt ratio," where a large proportion of rollouts do not contribute to parameter updates.

Previous research has explored RL training of LMs using procedurally generated data (Hu et al., 2025c; Chen et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025), which addresses the data collection cost. However, these methods typically employ static problem difficulty distributions, potentially leading to the learning inefficiencies described above. While some works, like Li et al. (2025), observed benefits from adding more environments, their evaluations did not consistently guarantee generalization to unseen environments. Curriculum learning approaches (Baker et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Portelas et al., 2020) and dynamic filtering of prompts (Cui et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025) attempt to mitigate these issues by reordering or filtering existing data, but do not inherently address the generation of an unbounded supply of appropriately challenging problems. Liu et al. (2025c) used adaptive difficulty for a single environment, but generalizable reasoning may require a broader range of tasks.

This work, RLVE, positions itself by addressing these limitations through the introduction of Adaptive Verifiable Environments. It combines the scalability of procedurally generated data with dynamic difficulty adjustment and emphasizes the importance of scaling the *diversity* of environments rather than merely the volume of data. This approach aims to provide continuous, appropriate learning signals to LMs, facilitating the development of generalizable reasoning capabilities beyond what static datasets and non-adaptive environments can offer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to scale up Reinforcement Learning (RL) for language models (LMs) by addressing limitations in current training methodologies. This objective is motivated by several challenges observed in existing approaches:

1. **Mitigating Data Saturation and Collection Costs:** Traditional RL training for LMs often relies on large, manually curated datasets with ground-truth answers for reward computation. Acquiring such data is expensive and prone to saturation, where increasing data volume yields diminishing returns in model performance. The motivation is to overcome this by introducing verifiable environments that can procedurally generate an unbounded number of problems.
2. **Overcoming Stalled Learning from Static Difficulty Distributions:** LMs trained with RL often encounter periods of stalled learning when the difficulty of training problems does not match the model's current capabilities. If problems are consistently too easy, the learning signal diminishes; if too hard, rewards are consistently low, hindering effective updates. This necessitates a mechanism for dynamic adaptation of problem difficulty to maintain an informative learning signal.
3. **Enhancing Generalizable Reasoning Capabilities:** The goal extends beyond improving performance on specific tasks to fostering more generalizable reasoning abilities in LMs. The motivation is that exposure to a diverse set of progressively challenging problems, rather than simply more problems from a limited distribution, is crucial for developing robust reasoning that transfers to unseen tasks and environments.
4. **Improving Learning Efficiency:** Static problem distributions often lead to inefficient use of computational resources, as a significant portion of rollouts may yield identical or uninformative rewards. The motivation is to increase learning efficiency by ensuring a higher proportion of appropriately challenging problems, thereby maximizing the impact of each training step.
5. **Establishing Environment Engineering as a Core Discipline:** The researchers are motivated to demonstrate that the careful design and construction of diverse, adaptive, and verifiable environments—referred to as "environment engineering"—is a fundamental and scalable approach for advancing LM development, analogous to existing practices in feature, data, and prompt engineering.

In pursuit of these motivations, the key objectives are:
- To design and implement Reinforcement Learning with Adaptive Verifiable Environments (RLVE), an approach that dynamically adjusts problem difficulty based on the policy model's performance.
- To construct RLVE-GYM, a large-scale suite of 400 manually engineered verifiable environments spanning various reasoning domains.
- To empirically demonstrate that environment scaling (increasing the number of diverse training environments) consistently improves generalizable reasoning capabilities in LMs.
- To show that RLVE can effectively scale up RL training for LMs in scenarios where models have reached saturation on existing strong RL datasets, and in compute-constrained scenarios where data efficiency is critical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Reinforcement Learning with Adaptive Verifiable Environments (RLVE), a framework designed to scale up RL training for LMs by utilizing procedurally generated data with dynamic difficulty.

#### 3.1. Verifiable Environment (E = (I, P, R))

A verifiable environment is formally defined as a tuple consisting of:
- **Input Template (I):** A template for generating problem inputs.
- **Problem Generator (P):** A program that procedurally samples specific problem parameters (`p`) to instantiate the input template `I`, producing a concrete input `I_p`.
- **Verifier (R):** An algorithmically defined reward function `R_p(o)` that computes a scalar reward for a model's output (`o`) to a specific problem `E_p`. The verifier is implemented as a program.

Each environment is associated with an integer **difficulty level (d)**, which controls the expected reasoning complexity of generated problems. For instance, in an array sorting environment, a higher `d` might correspond to a longer array. The problem generator `P_d` samples parameters `p` conditioned on `d`. The verifier `R_p` provides rewards, which can be more flexible than simple ground-truth comparisons, such as checking if a Sudoku solution satisfies all constraints rather than matching a single pre-computed solution.

#### 3.2. RL with Adaptive Verifiable Environments (RLVE)

RLVE extends the concept of verifiable environments by making their difficulty adaptive to the policy model's capabilities.
- **Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment:** For each verifiable environment, RLVE maintains a difficulty range `[ℓ_π, h_π]` for the policy model `π`. Training begins with the simplest problems (`ℓ_π = h_π = 0`). A difficulty level `d` is uniformly sampled from this range to generate a problem.
- **Performance Monitoring:** The model's accuracy (`a/b`, where `a` is correct rollouts and `b` is total attempted rollouts) is tracked for problems generated at the current upper-bound difficulty `h_π`.
- **Difficulty Increment:** If the accuracy `a/b` exceeds a predefined performance threshold `τ_acc` (set to 0.9 by default) and a minimum sample threshold `τ_num` (8 times rollouts per problem) is met, `h_π` is incremented by one (`h_π ← h_π + 1`). This introduces harder problems. The statistics (`a`, `b`) are then reset.
- **Sliding Window:** To prevent an excessively broad difficulty range, a sliding window is implemented. If the range `h_π - ℓ_π + 1` exceeds a hyperparameter `d_Δ` (set to 4 by default), `ℓ_π` is updated to `h_π - d_Δ + 1`. This ensures the model is exposed to problems near its capability frontier.
- **Joint Training:** When training across multiple environments, RLVE uniformly samples an environment `E^(i)` from the collection and then applies the adaptive difficulty logic independently for that specific environment, maintaining separate `[ℓ^(i)_π, h^(i)_π]` and statistics (`a^(i)`, `b^(i)`).

#### 3.3. RLVE-GYM: A Suite of 400 Environments

To implement RLVE, the authors constructed RLVE-GYM, a large-scale suite of 400 manually engineered verifiable environments. Environment engineering followed two main principles:
- **Pedagogical Tools:** Environments are designed to foster reasoning capabilities, often by requiring the LM to manually perform tasks that could be automated by a program (e.g., manual sorting or calculation), thereby encouraging the model to learn the underlying reasoning process. Examples include programming competition problems and mathematical operations.
- **Verification via Environment Advantages:** The design leverages inherent advantages of the environment over the LM:
 - The environment can execute programs (e.g., to verify solutions to programming problems), while the LM cannot.
 - The environment only needs to *verify* outputs, not *solve* the problems. This exploits solving-verification asymmetry, particularly for NP-complete problems (e.g., Sudoku, Hamiltonian Path), where verification is computationally less demanding than finding a solution. This allows for supervision signals that are otherwise infeasible to obtain.
- **Configurable Difficulty Design:** For each environment, increments in difficulty level `d` are designed such that solving lower-difficulty problems is a subproblem of or reducible to solving higher-difficulty ones (e.g., longer arrays for sorting, larger expression trees for integration).

#### 3.4. RL Algorithm

The research adopted the **DAPO (Dynamic Advantage-weighted Policy Optimization)** algorithm (Yu et al., 2025), a variant of GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). This algorithm incorporates dynamic sampling, where rollouts are oversampled and those yielding identical rewards across all outputs are discarded. This mechanism aims to ensure that only "appropriately challenging" problems contribute to parameter updates, increasing the "effective prompt ratio" and improving learning efficiency.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Setup

For controlled evaluation, a test set `D_ood` of 2,500 problems was created from 50 randomly selected held-out environments from RLVE-GYM, ensuring out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation. Training experiments utilized subsets of the remaining 350 environments, specifically `C_1`, `C_4`, `C_16`, and `C_256` containing 1, 4, 16, and 256 environments, respectively. Various 1.5B and 7B LMs were used, including Qwen2.5-7B-Base, R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepScaleR-1.5B, ProRL-1.5B-v2, and OpenThinker3-1.5B. Performance was measured on six reasoning benchmarks covering mathematics, code generation, and logical reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings regarding the efficacy of RLVE and its components:

#### 4.1. Adaptivity for Unstalled and Efficient Learning

- **Comparison of Adaptive vs. Static Difficulty:** Experiments using the Qwen2.5-7B-Base model on "Sorting" and "Multiplication" environments demonstrated differences between adaptive and static difficulty settings.
 - When static environments had a low upper-bound difficulty, the model achieved mastery, leading to an effective prompt ratio that dropped to zero and subsequent learning saturation.
 - When static environments had a high upper-bound difficulty, the effective prompt ratio remained non-zero, but was substantially lower than that of adaptive difficulty. This indicated that a smaller fraction of problems were appropriately challenging, resulting in reduced ID (in-distribution) and OOD (out-of-distribution) performance.
 - Adaptive difficulty consistently maintained a high effective prompt ratio, preventing learning stalls and achieving superior performance across both ID and OOD evaluations.
- **Joint Training with Adaptive Difficulty:** When training jointly across 256 environments, adaptive difficulty outperformed static difficulty, even when the static range was set to cover the full range of difficulties explored by the adaptive environments. This suggests that the individualized tuning of difficulty for each environment, naturally facilitated by RLVE, is more effective than a single, wide static range.

#### 4.2. Environment Scaling as a Key Driver of Generalizable Reasoning Capabilities

- **Impact of Environment Collection Size:** Experiments with four LMs (Qwen2.5-7B-Base, R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepScaleR-1.5B, ProRL-1.5B-v2) across environment collections of increasing sizes (1, 4, 16, and 256 environments) showed a consistent trend. Expanding the collection of training environments led to improved performance on held-out, unseen environments.
- **Diversity Over Volume:** This finding suggests that increasing the diversity of reasoning tasks, rather than merely the volume of data from a limited set of tasks, is a crucial factor for developing generalizable reasoning capabilities in LMs.

#### 4.3. Scaling Up RL Training with RLVE

The research evaluated RLVE with joint training on all 400 environments in RLVE-GYM in two distinct scenarios:

- **Scaling beyond Data Saturation:**
 - Starting from ProRL-1.5B-v2, a model previously trained to saturation using over 20,000 H100 GPU hours on the large ProRL dataset, RLVE enabled further improvement.
 - Within approximately 1,100 H100 GPU hours, RLVE achieved an absolute average improvement of 3.37% across six reasoning benchmarks.
 - In contrast, continuing the original RLVR training on the ProRL dataset yielded a marginal 0.49% absolute average gain, despite using more than three times the compute (3,600 H100 GPU hours). This indicates RLVE's capacity to scale RL training beyond existing data-saturation points.

- **Compute-Efficient Scaling:**
 - Starting from OpenThinker3-1.5B, a prominent 1.5B SFT model without prior reasoning RL training, RLVE was compared against training on DeepMath-103K, a high-quality, math-specific RLVR dataset.
 - RLVE consistently outperformed training on DeepMath-103K for an identical number of training steps. RLVE achieved higher performance on non-mathematical benchmarks (LiveCodeBench, BBEH) and most mathematical benchmarks (OMEGA-500, OlympiadBench), with comparable peak performance on AIME 2024/2025.
 - The construction of RLVE-GYM was also noted as being substantially more cost-efficient compared to DeepMath-103K, which required approximately $138,000 USD and 127,000 GPU hours. This positions RLVE as a compute-efficient method for fostering generalizable reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, RLVE, and an associated environment suite, RLVE-GYM, that address several challenges in scaling reinforcement learning for language models. The significance and potential impact are multifold:

1. **Overcoming Data Saturation and Enhancing Learning Efficiency:** RLVE offers a method to move beyond the limitations of static datasets that often lead to data saturation and inefficient learning. By dynamically adapting problem difficulty, RLVE maintains an informative learning signal throughout training, potentially enabling models to continue learning and improving for longer periods. This could lead to more robust and capable LMs that are not bottlenecked by fixed data distributions.

2. **Driving Generalizable Reasoning Capabilities:** The findings highlight that the diversity of training environments is a key scaling dimension for developing generalizable reasoning in LMs. This implies a shift in focus from merely increasing data volume to strategically expanding the range of tasks and problem types. Such an approach could foster LMs that are more adaptable and performant on a wider array of unseen reasoning tasks, a critical step towards advanced AI systems.

3. **Introduction of RLVE-GYM:** The creation of RLVE-GYM, a large-scale suite of 400 manually engineered verifiable environments, provides a valuable resource for the research community. This suite serves as a prototype for future work in environment engineering, offering a diverse set of pedagogical tools for developing various reasoning skills. Its design principles, emphasizing problem-solving asymmetry and programmatic verification, demonstrate a scalable method for generating supervision signals for complex tasks.

4. **Cost-Effective and Performance-Competitive Training:** The results indicate that RLVE can deliver performance improvements that are competitive with or superior to those achieved with expensive, specialized datasets, but at a potentially lower cost for data creation. This has implications for democratizing access to high-quality RL training for LMs, enabling more researchers and institutions to build advanced reasoning capabilities without prohibitive data acquisition budgets.

5. **Shifting Paradigms in LM Development:** The work suggests that "environment engineering" could become as foundational to LM development as data, feature, and prompt engineering. By providing systematic principles for designing environments with configurable difficulty and verifiable rewards, this research opens avenues for more deliberate and scalable approaches to teaching LMs complex reasoning processes.

6. **Path Towards More Complex Tasks:** By exploiting the asymmetry between problem-solving and verification, RLVE-GYM demonstrates a potential pathway to training LMs on tasks that are difficult for humans to solve or generate ground-truth solutions for, but where verification is tractable. This could enable LMs to tackle highly complex, even intractable, problems in various scientific and technical domains.

The research also calls for future work on adaptive non-verifiable environments and model-based automatic environment engineering, suggesting a broader effort to scale RL training through increasingly sophisticated and autonomous environment design.
