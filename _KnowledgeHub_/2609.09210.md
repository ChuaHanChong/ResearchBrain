# Identifying Habit, Physics, and Nuisance in Robot World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This work frames demonstration multimodality as an identification problem, positing a structural causal model that routes operator habit only through actions, nuisance only through rendering, and physics through a shared state map. Action-shuffle and appearance interventions test the split, and the implied rule, freeze the shared physics readout and adapt a thin interface, improves low-shot transfer on StackCube, DROID, and RH20T.

## Problem

- Next-observation predictors fit to the observation-to-observation distribution absorb operator habit, shared physics, and visual nuisance in one model, so finetuning rewrites dynamics for a new user or camera.
- Preference-conditioned and hierarchical imitation policies address demonstrator heterogeneity but rarely publish freeze-dynamics diagnostics under appearance or viewpoint interventions on the learned law.
- Latent-action methods recover pseudo-actions from video, a different object than operator habit in action selection, leaving habit-induced versus physics-induced multimodality unattributed.

## Method

- A ==structural causal model== with actions from habit, state and command, a shared transition map, and rendered observations, imposing two exclusions, no habit-to-state edge and no nuisance-to-dynamics edge, probed by an ==intervention suite== splitting physics-facing from habit-facing readouts: wrong-action shuffle, cross-operator action swap, near-zero action drift, appearance jitter, and a held-out second camera.
- Joint-primary pixel readout of encoder, joint probe, and residual delta head, with a ==hinge== forcing shuffled actions to raise error, avoiding action-insensitive latent-consistency collapse.
- **Freeze-and-adapt protocol** comparing frozen dynamics plus thin interface against joint finetuning and scratch at matched shot budgets, plus ==reverse scoring== combining physics and habit likelihoods.

## Results

- Action interventions dominate: wrong actions raise next-state error about **10.4x** on the StackCube kernel, about **13x** on DROID joints, and about **1362x** when swapping another operator's action on RH20T.
- Nuisance barely moves structural error: appearance ratio **0.988** and cross-camera ratio **1.42** against **9.5x** for wrong actions, with multi-step wrong-action ratios **1.9**, **7.3**, **13.7** at horizons **1**, **5**, **10**.
- Freezing wins in low-shot settings: pixel next-joint error **0.22** versus **0.65** scratch at **32** shots, closed-loop stacking **0.22** versus **0.10** at **128** shots, and roughly **150x** cleaner physics under doubled-action demos.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diagnostic action sensitivity is necessary but not sufficient: over-hard wrong-action hinges preserve large diagnostic ratios while destroying low-shot transfer, so hinge strength needs moderation.
> - Freezing a shared law and adapting only a thin interface shields dynamics from corrupted or idiosyncratic demonstrations, whereas joint finetuning absorbs operator habit into the physics.
> - A failed freeze protocol can indict a coarse command interface or an off-domain frozen encoder rather than disprove shared physics, so read negative results as interface diagnostics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a recurring problem in robot learning and world-model research: demonstrations collected from human teleoperation often produce multimodal action or observation distributions, even when the underlying robot and environment dynamics are largely deterministic. Existing world models frequently learn an entangled conditional predictor such as \(P(o_{t+1}\mid o_t)\). Such a predictor may attribute variation to the environment when the variation actually arises from different operator preferences, changes in visual appearance, or genuine physical consequences of different actions.

The paper situates itself at the intersection of several research areas. First, it relates to **latent world models**, including models that learn compact state representations and predict future states for planning or control. Second, it connects to **visual foresight and inverse dynamics**, where models infer how actions affect observations or learn controllable representations from video. Third, it relates to **imitation learning and behavior cloning**, in which multiple human demonstrators can generate heterogeneous action distributions for the same task. Finally, it draws on **causal representation learning and domain-invariance methods**, using interventions to test whether a learned representation distinguishes causal dynamics from nuisance variation.

The paper’s central distinction is that three sources of variation should not be treated as equivalent:

1. **Operator habit**, meaning a person’s preference or style in choosing among feasible actions.
2. **Shared physics**, meaning the state-transition law governing how an action changes the robot and environment.
3. **Observation nuisance**, such as lighting, camera viewpoint, background, or image appearance.

This framing differs from latent-action approaches that infer hidden actions from video. The authors explicitly state that an inferred latent action is not necessarily the same as operator habit. They also do not aim to build large-scale video-generation systems or model operator psychology across unrelated tasks. Instead, they focus on identifying useful structural distinctions for robot dynamics learning and low-shot adaptation.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Diagnosing multimodality in demonstrations

The primary objective is to determine whether multimodal next-observation predictions are caused by different physical outcomes or by differences in how operators select actions. If two operators manipulate an object in different but mechanically valid ways, the resulting action distribution may be broad even though the transition function is shared. A model trained only on next observations can absorb operator-specific behavior into its dynamics estimate.

The authors argue that this entanglement creates practical problems. When adapting a model to a new operator, task interface, or visual environment, standard fine-tuning may modify the learned dynamics unnecessarily. This can lead to brittle adaptation, especially when only a small number of demonstrations are available or when the demonstrations contain action corruption.

#### 2.2. Separating causal mechanisms

The paper formalizes the proposed separation using a structural causal model:

\[
a_t = g(h_t,z_t,u_t),
\]

\[
z_{t+1}=f(z_t,a_t),
\]

\[
o_t=r(z_t,c_t).
\]

Here, \(h_t\) represents operator habit, \(z_t\) is the underlying physical state, \(u_t\) is a task or command interface, \(a_t\) is the executed action, \(c_t\) represents observational nuisance, and \(o_t\) is the observation.

The model imposes two exclusion restrictions. Habit affects the future state only through the action; there is no direct \(h\rightarrow z'\) pathway. Similarly, nuisance affects only rendering and does not directly change the dynamics; there is no \(c\rightarrow f\) pathway. These assumptions motivate intervention-based tests. Changing the action while holding the state fixed should substantially affect next-state prediction, whereas changing appearance or viewpoint should have little effect on a structural state prediction.

#### 2.3. Developing a transfer rule

The practical objective is to test whether a pretrained dynamics model can be retained during adaptation. The proposed rule is to **freeze the shared physics model** and update only a thin policy or interface component, represented by \(g\) or a residual map \(\delta\). This is compared with jointly fine-tuning the dynamics and interface, and with training both components from scratch.

The authors also seek to establish conditions under which freezing is inappropriate. These include an overly coarse command interface, dynamics models trained with excessively aggressive objectives, cross-task transfer of operator style, and cases in which the downstream controller does not actually use the injected dynamics model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Experimental domains and data

The study uses three principal settings:

- **ManiSkill StackCube**, with a simulated Physical Kernel for controlled physics experiments and closed-loop stacking tests.
- **DROID**, an in-the-wild robot manipulation dataset containing exterior RGB images, seven-degree-of-freedom actions, and joint states. One camera stream is used for training and a second exterior camera for cross-camera evaluation.
- **RH20T**, which provides proprioceptive transitions collected from multiple operators.

The experiments include simulation, proprioceptive inputs, and pixel observations. Results are generally averaged over three seeds for the DROID pixel, multi-step, downstream, and reverse-scoring experiments.

#### 3.2. Proprioceptive and kernel models

For StackCube, the authors use a physical representation \(z_p\), a dynamics function \(f\), and a thin command-to-action map \(g\). For DROID and RH20T proprioception, they use an MLP that maps current joint state and action to the next joint state.

Three adaptation regimes are evaluated at matched demonstration budgets:

1. Freeze the pretrained dynamics \(f^*\) and train only \(g\).
2. Fine-tune both \(f\) and \(g\).
3. Train both components from scratch.

This design directly tests whether preserving the dynamics model improves low-shot transfer.

#### 3.3. Pixel-based structural prediction

For DROID images, the model first encodes an image into a representation:

\[
z=\mathrm{Enc}(o).
\]

A probe predicts the current joint state:

\[
\hat{j}=\mathrm{probe}(z),
\]

and a residual dynamics term predicts the next joint state:

\[
\hat{j}'=\hat{j}+\delta(z,a).
\]

The training objective is joint-primary rather than purely latent. It aligns the encoded representation with the current joint state, predicts the next joint state, and applies a hinge loss requiring shuffled actions to produce larger prediction errors. An appearance-consistency term may also be included. Transitions with larger joint changes are retained so that idle frames do not dominate training.

The authors emphasize a failure mode: if training only enforces latent consistency of the form \(f(\mathrm{Enc}(o),a)\approx \mathrm{Enc}(o')\), the representation may become insensitive to actions. In that case, appearance changes would appear harmless, but incorrect actions would also fail to produce a meaningful error increase.

#### 3.4. Interventions and diagnostics

The intervention suite includes:

- **Action shuffling or replacement at a fixed state**, testing physics sensitivity.
- **Swapping actions between operators on the same task**, testing within-task habit effects.
- **Zero-action probes**, testing whether predictions remain concentrated during inactivity.
- **Appearance perturbations**, including color and blur changes.
- **Second-camera evaluation**, testing viewpoint-related nuisance.
- **Multi-step rollouts**, checking whether action errors accumulate while appearance changes do not.
- **Reverse scoring**, which evaluates candidate past states and actions given an observed successor.

Physics likelihood is based on the error between \(f(z,a)\) and the observed successor. Habit likelihood is based on the discrepancy between the action and the operator-specific policy \(g(h,z,u)\). The intended use of habit scoring is ranking feasible histories, not changing the physical transition law or making definitive identity classifications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Action variation is strongly associated with physical prediction error

In the StackCube Physical Kernel, replacing the true action with alternatives increased one-step state error by approximately **10.4 times**. At a fixed initial state, action mixtures produced branching in predicted outcomes, while zero-action sequences remained concentrated.

On DROID proprioception, the predictive spread for incorrect actions was approximately **0.28**, compared with **0.02** for the true action. At 32 adaptation examples, freeze-plus-interface adaptation achieved an MSE of **0.00030**, compared with **0.00039** for scratch training.

RH20T produced a particularly large contrast. Pairing the correct state with another operator’s action increased MSE by approximately **1,362 times**, while unstructured action shuffling increased it by approximately **1,352 times**. This indicates that operator-specific action differences can be manipulated while the task and state remain otherwise comparable.

#### 4.2. Operator habit is detectable, but not equivalent to identity

Phase-level action residuals were used as a behavioral fingerprint. Operator identification achieved an accuracy of **0.52**, compared with a majority baseline of approximately **0.25**. Scrambling the residual fingerprint reduced accuracy to **0.21**.

However, direct user identification remained weak in the reverse-scoring experiments, with approximately **13% top-1 accuracy** compared with a **9% chance level**. The authors therefore interpret habit modeling as a way to rank plausible action histories rather than as a reliable user-recognition mechanism. They also found that raw state information can itself carry stylistic information, making it difficult to claim that physics residuals are completely identity-free.

#### 4.3. Freezing dynamics improves low-shot adaptation under several conditions

In StackCube, with 16 adaptation transitions, freeze-plus-interface training achieved held-out error of **0.00089**, compared with **0.00115** for joint fine-tuning and **0.00169** for scratch training. In a closed-loop StackCube test with 128 demonstrations, freeze-plus-interface achieved a stacked success rate of **0.22 ± 0.09**, compared with **0.10 ± 0.07** for scratch training. It performed better in seven of eight seeds.

When adaptation demonstrations were deliberately corrupted by scaling actions by a factor of two, the frozen-dynamics approach remained ahead of scratch training. In RH20T, freezing the dynamics under corrupted demonstrations preserved clean-physics prediction error by approximately two orders of magnitude relative to fine-tuning. Fine-tuning instead absorbed the corrupted behavior into the dynamics model.

The results do not support indiscriminate transfer of a dynamics model across robots or domains. A StackCube dynamics model transplanted directly to DROID remained only weakly action-sensitive, with a wrong-action-to-true-action ratio of approximately **1 to 1.3**. An in-domain DROID dynamics model with an appropriate action-sensitivity objective restored the desired behavior. Thus, the evidence supports reusing the training and adaptation protocol rather than reusing simulator-specific dynamics weights.

#### 4.4. Appearance changes have smaller effects than action changes

For the DROID pixel model, appearance interventions changed next-joint error by a factor of **0.988 ± 0.054**, effectively leaving structural prediction unchanged. Wrong actions increased error by **9.5 ± 3.7 times**. Evaluation from a second camera was more difficult, with a ratio of **1.42 ± 0.06**, but this remained substantially smaller than the action intervention effect.

In multi-step prediction, wrong-action ratios increased from **1.9 ± 0.2** at one step to **7.3 ± 2.5** at five steps and **13.7 ± 0.6** at ten steps. Appearance ratios remained near one. This supports the interpretation that the pixel model is responding to action-conditioned state transitions rather than merely to visual changes.

#### 4.5. Habit-aware reverse scoring provides limited but measurable benefit

Adding the correct habit prior increased posterior mass on the ground-truth past by **0.040 ± 0.002** compared with physics-only scoring. The correct operator model beat an incorrect operator model in **64.9%** of trials. The effect was therefore useful for ranking candidate histories, but insufficient for reliable operator classification.

#### 4.6. The transfer rule has identifiable failure conditions

An overly coarse command interface can make freeze-plus-interface adaptation appear ineffective because the interface does not contain enough information to express the required action policy. In addition, objectives that maximize wrong-action sensitivity too aggressively can preserve a strong diagnostic ratio while harming low-shot transfer. The authors report that moderate action-sensitivity losses provide a better balance.

The paper also finds weak or inconclusive evidence for cross-task habit transfer. Leave-one-task-out style transfer produced a win rate of approximately **57%**, with a ratio near one. Finally, freezing is inappropriate if operator habit directly changes physical parameters, such as contact properties, because that would violate the assumed causal graph.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It proposes evaluating robot world models through targeted interventions rather than relying only on aggregate prediction error. This provides a way to ask whether a model is sensitive to action-dependent physics while remaining stable under observational changes.

If validated further, the freeze-and-interface strategy could reduce the amount of data required to adapt robot models to new operators, command conventions, or moderately corrupted demonstrations. It may also reduce the risk that adaptation data rewrites a previously learned dynamics model. The pixel experiments suggest that visual representations can be organized around structural joint-state targets rather than direct next-image prediction, potentially improving robustness to camera and appearance changes.

The claims remain bounded by the study’s limitations. Closed-loop evidence is primarily simulated, hardware deployment is not evaluated, and the interventions are proxies for latent habit rather than direct measurements of operator preference. Cross-camera effects are non-negligible, direct identity recognition is weak, and the approach depends on a sufficiently informative command interface and an in-domain, action-sensitive dynamics model. Overall, the paper presents an intervention-based framework for distinguishing action selection, physical transition, and visual rendering, with evidence that this distinction can improve low-shot adaptation when its causal assumptions are approximately satisfied.
