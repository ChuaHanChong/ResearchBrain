# MA-VLA: Multi-Arm Vision-Language-Action Model for Collaboration and Compositional Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> MA-VLA is a multi-arm vision-language-action framework that decomposes a high-level instruction into mid-level atomic prompts assigned per arm, executed by a single flow-matching policy. A training-time Arm Shuffle permutation of each arm's observation, state, prompt, and action breaks arm-identity binding. It reaches 83.3% on four-arm RoboFactory tasks and 13% average success on unseen collaboration patterns where baselines score zero.

## Problem

- Existing VLA models encode language as one global static instruction, providing no explicit mechanism to assign or compose arm-specific subgoals across collaborating arms.
- Division of labor is inferred implicitly from demonstrations, so policies over-specialize to the few collaboration patterns seen in training and transfer brittlely.
- Prior multi-arm work relies on diffusion or reinforcement learning with fixed roles; no benchmark tests recomposing known atomic actions into unseen coordination structures.

## Method

- A VLM-based planner (GPT-4.1, not fine-tuned) maps instruction plus multi-view images onto a finite ==atomic prompt== vocabulary, yielding per-arm stage-wise subgoals in one forward pass.
- A unified **MA-VLA** executor built on a ==flow-matching== backbone consumes concatenated per-arm prompts, global and wrist views, and all proprioception, emitting all arms' actions via multi-head projection.
- ==Arm Shuffle== randomly permutes the arm-to-(state, view, prompt, action) mapping during training; ==View Dropout== masks random camera views, both as data-level augmentations leaving the ==behavior-cloning== loss unchanged.

## Results

- On RoboFactory, MA-VLA reaches **83.5%** average on two-arm tasks and **83.3%** on three-to-four-arm tasks, versus **80.3%** and **76.5%** for the flow-matching baseline.
- On out-of-domain compositional splits every baseline scores **0.0%** while MA-VLA averages **13.0%**; real dual-arm SO101 role-reversed tasks reach **10/20** on stack bowls versus **0/20**.
- Ablation: atomic prompts alone give **0%** out-of-domain, adding Arm Shuffle gives **7.3%**, plus View Dropout **15.3%**; shuffle rate **80%** peaks out-of-domain while in-domain drops only **58%**-to-**53%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Making division of labor an explicit language interface, rather than an implicit statistic of the data, is what buys recomposition into unseen coordination patterns.
> - Permutation augmentation over role-indexed input bundles trades a few points of in-domain accuracy for order-of-magnitude gains in out-of-distribution structural generalization.
> - One shared model beats independent per-arm policies: separate policies score higher in-domain but 0% out-of-domain and cost inference linearly in arm count.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) explores how intelligent systems interact with the physical world through perception, reasoning, and action. Within robotics, a significant area of research is multi-arm manipulation, which enables systems to perform tasks beyond the capability of a single arm through parallel execution and coordinated control. This necessitates robust collaboration mechanisms.

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have shown promise in enabling robots to interpret natural language instructions and translate them into physical actions. However, a limitation of many existing VLA models, particularly in multi-arm contexts, is their tendency to process language as a single, global instruction. This design often leads to implicit inference of task decomposition and division of labor among arms, which can be brittle and over-specialized to specific collaboration patterns observed during training. As a result, these systems frequently struggle to transfer their learned skills to *unseen collaboration patterns*, a challenge the authors term "multi-arm compositional generalization."

Prior work in multi-arm robot manipulation has leveraged imitation learning and reinforcement learning, with frameworks like RoboBallet and RoboFactory addressing multi-arm coordination. Concurrently, VLA models such as RT2, Octo, and OpenVLA have integrated multimodal data for robot control, yet typically treat language as a static input rather than an interactive channel for explicit coordination. While task generalization in robotics has been explored for environmental, visual, or task-level changes (e.g., object variations or parameterized policies), the specific problem of generalizing to novel *collaborative structures* in multi-arm systems, particularly concerning the recombination of known atomic actions, remains less explored.

MA-VLA positions itself by addressing this gap. It proposes a structured approach inspired by human teamwork, where complex goals are broken down into mid-level responsibilities and atomic actions. By explicitly assigning these atomic actions to individual arms and employing training strategies that promote role-agnostic behavior, MA-VLA aims to enhance the compositional generalization capabilities of multi-arm VLA systems, enabling them to adapt to unseen coordination demands.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework, named MA-VLA, capable of robust multi-arm collaboration and compositional generalization to previously unencountered collaboration patterns.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current multi-arm robotic systems and VLA models:

1. **Implicit Task Decomposition:** Existing multi-arm VLA models predominantly interpret a high-level natural language instruction as a monolithic command, expecting the system to implicitly infer the division of labor and sub-task allocation across multiple arms. This implicit learning often results in policies that are highly specialized and brittle, as they overfit to the specific collaboration patterns present in the training data.
2. **Limited Compositional Generalization:** A consequence of implicit task decomposition is the inability to robustly handle novel collaborative scenarios. If a multi-arm system has only observed certain arrangements or sequences of actions for its arms during training, it typically struggles when required to recompose these known actions into an *unseen collaboration pattern*. This limitation severely constrains the scalability and flexibility of multi-arm systems in dynamic real-world environments. The paper refers to this specific challenge as "multi-arm compositional generalization."
3. **Lack of Explicit Coordination Mechanism:** Unlike human teamwork, where complex goals are often achieved through explicit communication and assignment of mid-level responsibilities and atomic actions, most robotic VLA systems lack an explicit interface for specifying or coordinating arm-specific behaviors. This absence hinders structured planning and effective inter-arm coordination.
4. **Reliance on Fixed Arm Identities:** Current models often bind learned behaviors to specific arm identities or fixed spatial roles (e.g., "Arm 1 always does X, Arm 2 always does Y"). This dependency prevents arms from interchangeably performing roles or adapting to dynamic reassignments, further restricting generalization.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of MA-VLA are:

- To introduce a framework that explicitly *decomposes cooperative behavior into mid-level atomic prompts*.
- To establish an explicit mechanism for *assigning these atomic prompts to individual arms*, thereby facilitating clear subgoal specification.
- To enable *compositional reuse* of these atomic actions across diverse tasks, supporting the formation of new strategies from familiar components.
- To implement training strategies that *reduce reliance on fixed arm identities*, thereby enforcing role-agnostic instruction following.
- To facilitate the recomposition of atomic actions into *unseen coordination patterns*, which is central to achieving multi-arm compositional generalization.
- To construct a benchmark for evaluating compositional generalization, where test-time collaboration patterns are absent from the training set.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MA-VLA is a unified Vision-Language-Action framework designed for multi-arm collaboration, employing an explicit atomic action assignment strategy. Its architecture integrates a VLM-based Planner for task decomposition and a VLA Executor for action generation, supported by specialized training strategies.

#### 3.1. System Overview

MA-VLA operates hierarchically:
1. A **VLM-based Planner** interprets a high-level natural language instruction and visual observations to produce a sequence of explicit, mid-level *atomic prompts* for each arm. These prompts delineate arm-specific sub-goals at different temporal stages.
2. A **VLA Executor** then grounds these linguistic, arm-wise sub-goals into continuous control actions for each arm, considering the current scene and state.
3. A *flow-matching expert* (inspired by pi0) is integrated to ensure smooth and physically consistent execution across all arms.

#### 3.2. VLM-based Planner: Task Decomposition via Atomic Prompts

The planner ($P_{\phi}$) is responsible for converting a high-level goal into a structured sequence of per-arm sub-instructions. Instead of directly mapping the high-level instruction to low-level control, the planner predicts a stage-wise assignment of atomic prompts.

- **Input**: High-level instruction `l`, visual observations `I` (multi-view or video frames), and a predefined atomic prompt set `A`.
- **Output**: A sequence of `T` atomic stages `(p_1, ..., p_T)`, where each stage `p_t` is a tuple of `N` atomic prompts `(p_1_t, ..., p_N_t)`, with `p_i_t` representing the prompt for arm `i` at stage `t`. Examples of atomic prompts include "grasp object," "hold bowl," or "place cube."
- **Mechanism**: The planner leverages a large Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically GPT-4.1, to reason over spatial and semantic cues in `I` and `l`. It selects templates from `A` and canonicalizes its outputs to ensure valid atomic instructions. Importantly, the planner is not fine-tuned; it generates language prompts that guide the downstream executor. Stages are executed until task-dependent termination conditions are met.

#### 3.3. VLA Executor: Multi-arm Action Generation

The VLA executor ($\pi_{\theta}$) translates the linguistic atomic prompts from the planner into executable robot control actions.

- **Unified Model**: MA-VLA utilizes a single, unified model to jointly infer actions for all arms in a single forward pass. This design is intended to capture inter-arm dependencies and facilitate coordinated behaviors, rather than having independent policies for each arm.
- **Input**:
 - **Unified Instruction String**: At each timestep `t`, the atomic prompts for all arms (`p_0_t, ..., p_N_t`) are concatenated into a single string `u_t` (e.g., "Arm0: grasp object, Arm1: hold bowl"). This serves as the language input to the executor.
 - **Visual Observations**: Multi-view `I_t = {I_view_t, I_wrist_t}`, including global and egocentric perspectives.
 - **Proprioceptive States**: Concatenated states of all arms `s_t = [s_1_t, ..., s_N_t]`.
- **Output**: A complete multi-arm action vector `[a_1_t, ..., a_N_t]`.
- **Action Generation**: A lightweight *flow-matching formulation* (similar to pi0) is used to predict a denoising vector field. During training, this field maps noisy latent actions towards ground-truth actions, conditioned on visual observations, proprioceptive states, and the unified linguistic context. At inference, actions are obtained by integrating this learned flow field.
- **Multi-head Projection Layer**: A component that separates the shared latent representation within the executor into arm-specific action heads, allowing for differentiated control while leveraging shared context.

#### 3.4. Training Strategies for Compositional Generalization

To enhance generalization, particularly to unseen collaboration patterns and to mitigate reliance on fixed arm identities, two stochastic regularization strategies are applied exclusively during training:

- **Arm Shuffle**: With a probability `p_shuffle`, the correspondence between arms' states, local observations (views), assigned atomic prompts, and ground-truth actions is randomly permuted. This prevents the model from associating behaviors with fixed arm indices (e.g., "left arm" or "Arm 0"), forcing it to learn role-agnostic instruction following based on semantic meaning rather than structural position.
- **View Dropout**: With a probability `p_drop`, a subset of visual inputs (camera views) is randomly masked (replaced with zeros). This encourages the model to leverage redundant spatial cues and develop more robust, multi-view reasoning, making it less sensitive to the availability or quality of any single view.

#### 3.5. Training Objective

Both Arm Shuffle and View Dropout are integrated as stochastic data augmentations under a unified behavioral cloning (BC) objective. The model samples whether to apply shuffle or dropout at each iteration and computes the standard action-level loss, which implicitly regularizes MA-VLA towards permutation-invariant coordination and robust visual grounding.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MA-VLA was conducted across simulation benchmarks (RoboFactory, RoboTwin 2.0) and a real-world dual-arm platform (SO101), assessing both in-domain performance and out-of-domain compositional generalization.

#### 4.1. In-domain Collaboration Results

- **RoboFactory Benchmark**: MA-VLA consistently outperformed competitive imitation learning and VLA baselines (DP, Pi0-Fast, Pi0) across 2-arm, 3-arm, and 4-arm tasks. The performance gains were particularly noticeable with an increasing number of arms, suggesting that explicit conditioning on atomic action instructions effectively clarifies per-arm responsibilities in more complex coordination scenarios. For instance, on 3-arm and 4-arm tasks, MA-VLA achieved average success rates of 83.3%, compared to Pi0's 76.5% and DP's 15.3%.
- **RoboTwin 2.0 (Hard)**: Under challenging visual disturbances including distractors, background variations, and lighting changes, MA-VLA demonstrated robustness, showing consistent improvements over the Pi0 backbone and other diffusion-based baselines. It achieved an average success rate of 49.0% across various dual-arm tasks, outperforming Pi0's 41.1% and DP3's 28.6%.

#### 4.2. Out-of-domain Compositional Generalization Results

- MA-VLA achieved non-trivial success rates in scenarios involving novel role orders and coordination structures unseen during training. These tasks required the recomposition of familiar atomic actions into new collaboration patterns.
- In contrast, prior end-to-end imitation baselines (DP, Pi0-FAST, Pi0) largely failed in these out-of-domain tasks, consistently yielding 0% success rates.
- MA-VLA's success (e.g., an average of 13.0% success across different stacking order tasks and a passing task) indicates that the combination of atomic action conditioning, Arm Shuffle, and View Dropout effectively mitigates reliance on fixed arm identities and promotes instruction-centric coordination, enabling generalization to novel collaborative paradigms.

#### 4.3. Real-world Dual SO101 Results

- On a physical dual-arm SO101 platform, MA-VLA consistently improved in-domain performance compared to Pi0 across tasks like Stack Bowls, Place Cubes, Pass Toys, and Stack Cubes.
- Crucially, MA-VLA achieved non-zero success rates in *role-reversed out-of-domain settings* (e.g., reversed stacking orders, alternative passing orders) where Pi0 recorded 0% success. This result supports that atomic action conditioning, coupled with Arm Shuffle and View Dropout, effectively reduces arm-identity bias and facilitates transfer to unseen real-world collaboration patterns. For example, in the OOD Stack Bowls task, MA-VLA succeeded in 10 out of 20 trials, while Pi0 failed in all 20.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

- **Components**:
 - Introducing atomic actions alone significantly improved in-domain performance.
 - The addition of **Arm Shuffle** was pivotal for out-of-domain generalization, enabling non-zero success where models without it failed (e.g., from 0% to 7.3% for out-of-domain tasks, with atomic actions).
 - **View Dropout** further boosted out-of-domain compositional generalization with only minor degradation in in-domain performance. The full MA-VLA model (atomic actions + Arm Shuffle + View Dropout) achieved 15.3% out-of-domain and 53.0% in-domain success on the Three Robots Stack Cube unseen-order task.
- **Shuffle Probability**: Varying the `p_shuffle` rate from 0% to 100% demonstrated that increasing the shuffle rate significantly boosted out-of-domain performance, confirming its strong positive effect on compositional generalization. In-domain accuracy remained stable with a mild drop.
- **Separate Model**: A baseline of training independent VLAs per arm, where each model processed only its own observations, state, and actions, showed poor compositional generalization (0% out-of-domain success). While it could follow atomic actions for individual arms, it failed to coordinate in unseen multi-arm tasks. This underscores the limitations of independent-arm training and highlights the scalability and coordination benefits of MA-VLA's unified framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of MA-VLA represents a notable contribution to the field of multi-arm robotics and embodied AI, particularly in addressing the challenge of compositional generalization.

1. **Enhanced Multi-arm Collaboration and Generalization**: MA-VLA offers a structured and practical approach for multi-arm systems to move beyond rote execution of trained collaboration patterns. By explicitly decomposing tasks into atomic actions and assigning them to individual arms, the framework enables agents to recompose learned skills into novel coordination strategies. This significantly advances the generalization capabilities of multi-arm robots to unseen scenarios, which is critical for deployment in dynamic, unstructured environments.
2. **Explicit and Interpretable Task Decomposition**: Unlike prior VLA models that rely on implicit inference of task breakdown, MA-VLA introduces an explicit VLM-based planner that generates interpretable, mid-level atomic prompts. This not only makes the robot's intent and division of labor transparent but also provides a clear interface for controlling and debugging complex multi-arm behaviors.
3. **Role-Agnostic Learning**: The introduction of **Arm Shuffle** as a training strategy is significant. It forces the model to learn behaviors independently of fixed arm identities or spatial positions. This promotes a more robust and flexible understanding of roles and actions, allowing arms to interchangeably perform tasks and adapt to changes in their assignments or environment, much like humans can swap roles in a collaborative task.
4. **Robust Perception and Control**: The integration of **View Dropout** enhances the model's perceptual robustness by encouraging it to leverage redundant visual information. This makes the system more resilient to partial occlusions, camera failures, or visual noise, contributing to reliable operation in real-world settings.
5. **Scalability and Efficiency**: The unified VLA executor, which jointly infers actions for all arms within a single model, contrasts with approaches that train separate policies for each arm. This unified design inherently supports coordinated behaviors and potentially offers better scalability with increasing numbers of arms, while reducing inference and deployment overhead.
6. **Bridging Simulation to Reality**: The consistent success of MA-VLA across simulation benchmarks and real-world robot platforms, particularly in out-of-domain tasks, suggests a viable path for transferring learned collaborative policies from simulated environments to physical robots. This is a crucial step towards practical, general-purpose robotic systems.

The MA-VLA framework, with its focus on explicit task decomposition, role-agnostic learning, and robust execution, lays groundwork for future developments in more adaptable, intelligent, and scalable multi-robot systems. It has potential implications for various applications requiring complex manipulation, assembly, or human-robot co-working.
