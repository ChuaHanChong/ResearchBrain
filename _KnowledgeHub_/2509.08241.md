# Sample-Efficient Online Control Policy Learning with Real-Time Recursive Model Updates

> [!summary] Summary
> Recursive Koopman Learning (RKL), a pipeline for sample-efficient and computationally lightweight data-driven control policy learning, integrates Koopman theory, EDMD, RLS, and MPC. The method achieves superior control performance with significantly less data, for example, using 0.8% of the training time for puck balancing on a Soft Stewart Platform compared to reinforcement learning benchmarks, and also provides formal convergence guarantees for online Koopman model updates.

## Problem

- Traditional data-driven control methods like Reinforcement Learning (RL) suffer from low sample efficiency and high computational costs, making them impractical for real-time hardware training, especially for complex or unmodeled systems.
- Existing online model updating approaches, particularly for Koopman models, are computationally intensive (requiring retraining or iterative solvers) or lack formal convergence guarantees for data generated by dynamical systems (Markov chains).
- Control of complex, nonlinear, and often unmodeled robotic systems (e.g., soft robots) requires adaptable policies that can be learned and updated efficiently in real-time directly on hardware.

## Method

- Introduces **Recursive Koopman Learning (RKL)**, an online control policy learning pipeline combining ==Koopman theory==, ==Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)==, ==Recursive Least Squares (RLS)==, and ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)==.
- Employs RLS to perform real-time, computationally lightweight recursive updates of the Koopman model, which has an O(n^2) complexity per update independent of dataset size, avoiding full re-estimation with growing datasets.
- Utilizes MPC to synthesize control policies based on the continuously updated linear Koopman model, enabling rapid adaptation and control for nonlinear systems by minimizing an objective function over a finite horizon.

## Results

- RKL demonstrated superior control performance (lower tracking errors, improved stability) on a simulated planar arm and a hardware Soft Stewart Platform (SSP), requiring significantly less data (e.g., **0.8%** of training time on SSP for puck balancing) compared to leading RL methods.
- Online recursive model updates reduced tracking errors by up to **77.94%** on a simulated arm and enabled robust control for the highly nonlinear SSP, successfully adapting to challenges like static friction and unmodeled dynamics.
- Initializing RKL with high-quality demonstration data or iteratively collected online data (validating the ACG hypothesis) led to optimal performance, showcasing the method's ability to capitalize on informative datasets for rapid and efficient learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The integration of RLS for recursive model updates significantly reduces computational complexity, enabling real-time adaptation within typical robotic control loop frequencies (e.g., <10 ms per update for a 50 Hz loop).
> - A formal convergence analysis establishes sufficient conditions for the almost sure convergence of the estimated Koopman matrix when data is continuously generated by a Markov chain, providing theoretical guarantees for online learning.
> - Data collected while actively attempting a control goal (weak ACG hypothesis) is highly informative, allowing the system to rapidly improve its model and controller performance with minimal additional online data compared to static datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of control policies for nonlinear and dynamic robotic systems presents several challenges. Traditional model-based approaches, while mathematically rigorous, often struggle with systems that are difficult to model from first principles (e.g., soft robots), tasks requiring high dynamism, environments with significant uncertainties, hybrid dynamical systems, and escalating computational demands with increasing constraints.

In contrast, data-driven methods, particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL), have demonstrated advancements in handling uncertainties and complex dynamics, achieving notable performance across various robotic applications such as soft robot control, dexterous manipulation, and locomotion. However, RL algorithms typically exhibit low sample efficiency, necessitating extensive data collection and substantial computational resources, which can hinder their application to hardware-only training scenarios, especially for systems that are difficult to simulate with high fidelity. Furthermore, RL often lacks formal convergence guarantees and mathematical interpretability due to its stochastic nature and reliance on neural networks.

Koopman theory offers an alternative by representing nonlinear systems as linear models in a higher-dimensional observable space. Advances in computational techniques, such as Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD), have made data-driven estimation of Koopman operators feasible. This approach has found applications in diverse fields, including fluid dynamics, physics, and robotics, providing a pathway to leverage the computational advantages of linear systems (convexity, speed, and lighter computations) for nonlinear control.

Within the Koopman-based control landscape, the efficient and real-time updating of models with new data remains an area of active research. While some studies have explored online updates for neural network models, these methods tend to be computationally heavy, slow, and not suitable for real-time performance or sample efficiency. Efforts to update Koopman models have often relied on retraining or iterative solvers, which can be computationally intensive. Recursive Least Squares (RLS)-inspired algorithms have been proposed for faster online updates of linear models, but these approaches have typically lacked comprehensive convergence and complexity analyses, as well as experimental validation on real-world robotic systems.

Moreover, the theoretical understanding of EDMD's convergence properties, particularly for data generated by dynamical systems forming Markov chains, has been incomplete. Prior work has discussed convergence under i.i.d. or ergodic sampling assumptions, often overlooking the correlations inherent in trajectory data or the specific conditions for applying the Strong Law of Large Numbers for Markov chains.

This research addresses these limitations by introducing Recursive Koopman Learning (RKL), a pipeline designed for sample-efficient and computationally lightweight data-driven control policy learning. It integrates Koopman theory, EDMD, RLS, and Model Predictive Control (MPC) to enable rapid control policy learning and real-time recursive model updates. Crucially, the paper provides a formal convergence analysis for EDMD and RLS under continuous data growth within Markov chains, identifying sufficient conditions for convergence that were previously unaddressed. It also offers algorithmic complexity analyses and validates the approach through experiments on both simulated and hardware robotic platforms. This work positions itself at the intersection of model-based and data-driven control, seeking to combine the strengths of both paradigms while overcoming their respective challenges in real-time, resource-constrained environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-driven control policy learning method that is both highly sample-efficient and computationally lightweight. This objective is motivated by several critical challenges in modern robotics:

- **Resource Constraints in Hardware Learning**: Data acquisition and computational resources are often limited when deploying and training control policies directly on robotic hardware. Traditional data-driven methods, such as Reinforcement Learning (RL), typically require vast datasets and significant computational power, making on-hardware learning impractical or extremely time-consuming. This paper aims to alleviate these demands, enabling more feasible hardware implementation.
- **Limitations in Dynamic and Unmodeled Environments**: Many contemporary data-driven control methods struggle with real-time model updates, which limits their performance and adaptability in dynamic environments or for systems whose dynamics are difficult to capture through first principles. Examples include soft robots, which exhibit complex, nonlinear, and often time-varying behaviors that are challenging to simulate accurately. The research seeks a method capable of continuous, real-time adaptation.
- **Inadequacy of Current Online Model Updating Approaches**: While the concept of online model updating exists, previous attempts using neural networks have been described as heavy, slow, and far from real-time performance, in addition to being sample-inefficient. Existing Koopman-based model updates often rely on complete retraining or iterative numerical solvers, which are computationally intensive and slow down adaptation. There was a clear need for a stable, fast, and lightweight real-time model updating algorithm.
- **Lack of Formal Guarantees in Online Koopman Learning**: Earlier RLS-inspired algorithms for online model updates in linear systems, though conceptually faster, lacked comprehensive convergence and complexity analyses. Specifically, a formal analysis of EDMD's convergence properties, particularly when dealing with correlated data from dynamical system trajectories (Markov chains), was absent. Establishing such theoretical guarantees is crucial for ensuring the reliability and predictability of data-driven control systems in practical applications.
- **Leveraging the "Attempting Control Goal (ACG) Hypothesis"**: The researchers are motivated by the idea that data collected while actively trying to achieve a control objective, even with a sub-optimal policy, is highly informative. This "weak ACG hypothesis" suggests that an iterative loop of computing the best policy available and then attempting to control the system could yield valuable data for continuous improvement. Real-time online model updates are essential to effectively capitalize on such dynamically generated, informative data.
- **Harnessing Koopman Theory for Linearity**: The inherent linearity offered by Koopman representations in higher-dimensional observable spaces provides a foundation for developing optimization-friendly and computationally efficient algorithms. This linearity, distinct from local linearization, offers the potential for faster computations and higher sample efficiency, combining benefits from both model-based and data-driven paradigms.

In summary, the motivation stems from the practical demands of robotic control—specifically the need for efficient, adaptable, and formally grounded learning methods that can operate effectively on hardware, without the excessive data and computational requirements or the theoretical opacities of many existing approaches.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Recursive Koopman Learning (RKL) pipeline integrates Koopman theory, Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD), Recursive Least Squares (RLS), and Model Predictive Control (MPC) into a sample-efficient and computationally lightweight framework for online control policy learning.

#### 3.1. Overall RKL Pipeline

The RKL pipeline operates in a continuous loop:
1. **Initialization**: A small initial dataset is collected offline. This dataset is used to compute an initial Koopman model via EDMD. This initial model is then used to initialize the RLS module and the MPC controller.
2. **Online Control and Model Update**: In an iterative loop, the MPC controller uses the current Koopman model to calculate an optimal control input. This input is applied to the robotic system (environment). As the system executes the action, new state-action-next state data is collected. This new data is immediately fed into the RLS module, which recursively updates the Koopman model in real-time. The updated model is then used by the MPC for the next control cycle.

#### 3.2. Koopman Model Formulation

For a discrete-time dynamical system $x_{k+1} = f(x_k)$ with control input $u_k$, the method lifts the state $x$ and control $u$ into higher-dimensional observable spaces using observation functions $\phi(x)$ and $\psi(u)$, respectively. New variables are defined: $\alpha_k = [\phi(x_k), \psi(u_k)]$ and $\beta_k = [\phi(x_{k+1}), \psi(u_k)]$. The Koopman model is then approximated as a linear relationship $\beta_k = K \alpha_k$, where $K$ is the Koopman matrix. This matrix can be partitioned to describe the evolution of the state observable: $\phi(x_{k+1}) = K_z \phi(x_k) + K_g \psi(u_k)$.

#### 3.3. Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)

EDMD is employed for the initial estimation of the Koopman operator. Given data matrices $Y = [\alpha_0 \alpha_1 \cdots]$ and $\bar{Y} = [\beta_0 \beta_1 \cdots]$, the Koopman matrix $K$ is estimated by solving a least-squares problem: $\min_K ||KY - \bar{Y}||_F$. The solution is given by $K = \bar{Y}Y^T (YY^T)^\dagger$, where $F$ denotes the Frobenius norm and $\dagger$ denotes the pseudo-inverse.

#### 3.4. Recursive Least Squares (RLS) for Real-Time Model Updates

RLS is central to RKL's ability to perform real-time model updates. The algorithm generalizes the standard RLS for scalar outputs to update the entire Koopman matrix. Instead of recomputing the full EDMD solution with the growing dataset, RLS incrementally updates the Koopman matrix $K_k$ and the inverse of the "data covariance" matrix $P_k$ (which is effectively $(Y_k Y_k^T)^\dagger$) using rank-1 updates.
Specifically, upon receiving a new data pair $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$, $P_k$ is updated using the Sherman-Morrison formula: $P_{k+1} = P_k - \gamma_k P_k \alpha_k \alpha_k^T P_k$, where $\gamma_k = (1 + \alpha_k^T P_k \alpha_k)^{-1}$. The Koopman matrix $K_k$ is then updated as $K_{k+1} = K_k + \gamma_k (\beta_k - K_k \alpha_k) \alpha_k^T P_k$. This recursive update is mathematically equivalent to retraining the entire model with EDMD on the expanded dataset, but with significantly reduced computational cost.

#### 3.5. Model Predictive Control (MPC)

The control policy is synthesized using an MPC framework. While any MPC technique could be used, the authors primarily utilize Sequential Action Control (MPC-SAC) due to its observed performance advantages over methods like LQR, as reported in prior research. MPC-SAC computes control actions by solving a trajectory optimization problem at each step, minimizing an objective function over a finite horizon. It employs a closed-form expression to determine optimal individual control actions that enhance a tracking objective over a longer time horizon. The Koopman model, $\dot{z}(t) = K_z z(t) + K_u u(t)$, serves as the dynamics model within the MPC optimization.

#### 3.6. Algorithmic Complexity Analysis

The paper provides a formal analysis of the computational complexity for both EDMD and RLS. For EDMD, estimating $K$ with $N$ data points and an observation function of dimension $n$ has a complexity of $O(n^2 N + n^3)$. This dependency on $N$ makes full retraining increasingly slow as the dataset grows. In contrast, each RLS update has a complexity of $O(n^2)$, which is independent of the dataset size $N$. This analysis confirms RLS's suitability for real-time online updates under continuous data growth.

#### 3.7. Convergence Analysis

A novel contribution of this work is the formal convergence analysis of EDMD and RLS under continuous data growth within Markov chains. The analysis establishes sufficient conditions for the almost sure convergence of the estimated Koopman matrix $K_N$ to its optimal mean-square estimate $K^*$. These conditions are:
- The dataset forms an irreducible, aperiodic, and positive recurrent Markov chain with respect to an invariant measure $\mu$ (implying ergodicity).
- The observation function $\phi(s)$ is $\mu$-square-integrable.
- The matrix $P$ (the inverse of the expected outer product of observables) and its finite-data estimate $P_N$ are full-rank.
By applying the Strong Law of Large Numbers for Markov chains, the expected outer product terms in the EDMD formulation are shown to converge almost surely to their true expectation values, thereby proving the convergence of $K_N$.

#### 3.8. Relation to ACG Hypothesis

The convergence analysis is linked to the "Attempting Control Goal (ACG) hypothesis." A high-quality demonstration dataset, or data collected during the iterative process of achieving a control goal, is hypothesized to induce a Markov chain that is nearly ergodic with respect to the target dynamics' invariant measure. This provides a favorable starting point for the RKL algorithm, allowing online model updates to rapidly converge to the optimal Koopman representation as the system attempts to achieve its objective.

#### 3.9. Experimental Validation

The methodology is validated through experiments on two systems: a simulated planar two-link arm and a hardware Soft Stewart Platform (SSP). The planar arm is used for trajectory tracking, and the SSP, a highly nonlinear, hybrid, and time-varying system with soft actuators, is used for puck balancing and trajectory tracking, demonstrating the practical applicability of RKL. The control aspects are implemented in a multi-threaded C++ codebase for real-time performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on a simulated planar two-link arm and a hardware Soft Stewart Platform (SSP) demonstrated the sample efficiency, control performance, and computational advantages of Recursive Koopman Learning (RKL) compared to benchmark methods.

#### 4.1. Superior Sample Efficiency and Control Performance

- **Simulated Planar Two-Link Arm**: RKL-SAC consistently achieved the best overall performance in tracking an '8'-shaped reference trajectory. Notably, RKL-SAC required significantly less data than reinforcement learning (RL) benchmarks. For instance, RKL-SAC (using 3,000 random actions + 500 online steps) achieved an RMSE of 0.8359 cm, while RL-SAC required 2,000,000 steps to achieve an RMSE of 1.6001 cm. This indicates RKL-SAC used less than 5% of the data required by leading RL methods for superior performance. Furthermore, using MPC-SAC as the solver generally improved performance for both RKL and standard Koopman Learning (KL) compared to LQR.
- **Hardware Soft Stewart Platform (SSP)**:
 - **Puck Balancing**: For controlling a puck to balance at desired locations, RKL-SAC outperformed RL-SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) by requiring only 1 minute of initial data plus 20 seconds of online updates (total 8,000 steps), compared to RL-SAC's 2 hours and 46 minutes (100,000 steps). This represents approximately 0.8% of the training time. RKL-SAC also resulted in lower mean errors and significantly lower standard deviations (indicating more stable puck behavior) compared to RL-SAC. RKL also demonstrated a 66% lower average error and 8 times better standard deviation than NN-MPPI, using only 2.6% of the training time.
 - **Trajectory Tracking with Contacts**: In tracking an 'N'-shaped trajectory involving contacts with the platform boundaries, RKL-SAC (4 min initial data) achieved a mean error of 0.0636 m, outperforming KL-SAC (4 min initial data) at 0.1079 m and RL-SAC at 0.0821 m.

#### 4.2. Impact of Online Recursive Model Updates

Comparing RKL to KL (Koopman Learning without updates) consistently demonstrated the benefits of recursive model updates:
- On the simulated planar arm, online updates reduced the RMSE by up to 77.94% and diminished time lag, particularly with smaller initial datasets.
- On the SSP, KL-SAC often struggled with small datasets, sometimes resulting in puck immobilization due to static friction. RKL-SAC, with its online updates, remained robust and stable, indicating that recursive updates helped overcome such challenges by continuously adapting the model.
- The results empirically validated the "weak ACG hypothesis," showing that 20 seconds of online data collected while attempting a control objective could be more effective than several minutes of manually collected initial data in enhancing controller performance.

#### 4.3. Influence of Initial Dataset Quality

The quality of the initial dataset significantly impacted RKL's performance:
- Initializing RKL-SAC with a "500 Demo" dataset (data generated by a high-quality PD controller) on the planar arm yielded the best performance across all methods and datasets tested (0.725 cm RMSE). This supports the ACG hypothesis, as a high-quality demonstration provides an initial model whose invariant measure closely approximates the true dynamics, facilitating rapid convergence with online updates.
- Even when initialized with a deliberately "cropped" dataset (lacking data from the first quadrant of the SSP), RKL-SAC (mean error 0.03 m, std dev 0.0237 m) performed robustly and still exceeded RL-SAC, demonstrating its ability to quickly learn and adapt in previously unobserved state-space regions through online model updates.

#### 4.4. Algorithmic Properties Validation

- **Convergence Analysis**: The paper presented a formal convergence analysis of EDMD and RLS under continuous data growth within Markov chains, identifying sufficient conditions for convergence. This theoretical contribution was supported by experimental observations, where the RKL models exhibited robust and improving performance over time, consistent with the convergence guarantees.
- **Algorithmic Complexity**: The theoretical analysis demonstrating RLS's $O(n^2)$ computational complexity per update (independent of dataset size) was empirically supported by the real-time performance on the SSP, where each RLS update was completed within 10 ms (well within the 50 Hz control loop). This contrasted with the higher complexity of full EDMD retraining.

#### 4.5. Hardware Validation

The successful deployment and superior performance of RKL on the Soft Stewart Platform, a highly nonlinear, hybrid, and time-varying system with soft actuators, demonstrated the practical utility and robustness of the method for challenging real-world robotic systems. The multi-threaded C++ implementation further confirmed the feasibility of real-time operation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of data-driven control, addressing critical limitations in sample efficiency, computational cost, and real-time adaptability for complex robotic systems.

#### 5.1. Enabling Sample-Efficient and Real-Time Hardware Learning

The most immediate impact of RKL is its demonstrated capability to train high-performance control policies with significantly less data and computational resources than state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods. This is particularly crucial for robotic systems that are challenging or costly to simulate accurately, such as soft robots, or for scenarios where data collection on hardware is time-consuming and expensive. RKL's lightweight nature and real-time recursive updates make it feasible to learn and adapt controllers directly on hardware, potentially accelerating the development and deployment cycles of robotic systems.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Control Performance and Stability for Complex Systems

RKL consistently achieved superior control performance, characterized by lower tracking errors (RMSE) and improved stability (lower standard deviation in errors), across both simulated and hardware platforms, including a highly nonlinear, hybrid soft robot. This performance, coupled with its data efficiency, suggests that RKL can provide more robust and precise control for challenging dynamic tasks.

#### 5.3. Advancing the Theoretical Foundation for Online Koopman Learning

The formal convergence analysis of EDMD and RLS for continuous data growth within Markov chains fills a notable gap in the existing literature. By identifying specific sufficient conditions for convergence, the paper provides critical theoretical guarantees for the reliability and predictability of recursive Koopman-based learning. This theoretical rigor enhances confidence in deploying such methods in safety-critical or performance-demanding applications, contributing to the broader understanding of data-driven dynamic systems.

#### 5.4. Guiding Data Collection Strategies

The empirical validation of the "weak ACG hypothesis" and its explanation through the convergence analysis has direct implications for data collection strategies. It suggests that data acquired during iterative attempts to achieve a control objective, even with imperfect policies, is highly informative for model improvement. This insight motivates future research into developing "ergodicity-related metrics" to guide data collection processes, aiming to generate data that is optimally structured for rapid and sample-efficient learning, especially when high-quality demonstration data is scarce.

#### 5.5. Broader Applicability and Reproducibility

While validated in robotics, the principles of sample-efficient, real-time recursive model updates based on Koopman theory could extend to various other domains involving complex nonlinear dynamical systems where real-time adaptation and resource efficiency are paramount. Furthermore, the open-sourcing of the high-performance C++ codebase significantly lowers the barrier to entry for other researchers and practitioners, fostering reproducibility and encouraging further development and application of RKL.

#### 5.6. Addressing Limitations and Future Research

The identified limitations, such as potential dataset imbalance when the system dwells in small regions and the impact of poor initial models on convergence, offer clear directions for future research. This includes developing active learning strategies to ensure dataset diversity or more robust initial model estimation techniques, further enhancing the method's practical utility and broadening its applicability to even more challenging scenarios.
