# EnsembleDAgger: A Bayesian Approach to Safe Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University and UIUC developed EnsembleDAgger, an interactive imitation learning algorithm that utilizes Bayesian deep learning to quantify the novice policy's uncertainty ('doubt'). This algorithm employs a state-dependent decision rule for expert intervention, demonstrating improved learning efficiency and enhanced safety, including zero failures in simple tasks and a better Pareto frontier in complex robotics environments.

## Problem

- Standard imitation learning (IL) algorithms like DAgger are susceptible to data mismatch and distribution shifts, leading to compounding errors and potentially unsafe actions in states outside the expert's demonstrations.
- Existing safety mechanisms, such as SafeDAgger's fixed threshold for expert intervention, are suboptimal because the acceptable deviation from expert behavior is inherently state-dependent; a fixed threshold is either too conservative or too permissive.
- There is a need for a model-free proxy to assess the novice policy's proximity to unsafe regions, which can inform state-dependent safety interventions without requiring an explicit environmental model.

## Method

- **EnsembleDAgger** extends the iterative ==DAgger== framework by incorporating a ==probabilistic decision rule== that considers both the novice policy's agreement with the expert and its own predictive uncertainty.
- It quantifies the novice policy's 'doubt' (==epistemic uncertainty==) by using an ==ensemble of neural networks==, where the variance of actions across ensemble members approximates a ==Gaussian Process== and indicates lack of familiarity with a given state.
- A combined ==decision rule== is introduced: the novice acts only if both its action's discrepancy from the expert's action and its self-measured 'doubt' are below predefined thresholds, otherwise the expert intervenes.

## Results

- In the inverted pendulum domain, the 'doubt rule' achieved superior learning performance with **zero failures** over **six epochs**, while the 'discrepancy rule' exhibited lower performance and non-zero failure rates.
- On the MuJoCo HalfCheetah task, the 'doubt rule' consistently Pareto dominated the 'discrepancy rule,' indicating better novice policy performance for equivalent levels of compromise to the combined expert-novice system.
- The full EnsembleDAgger decision rule, combining both discrepancy and doubt conditions, demonstrated strictly more conservative behavior for the combined system and could lead to further performance improvements given optimal hyperparameter tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A novice policy's epistemic uncertainty, measured by the variance of a deep ensemble, serves as an effective model-free proxy for familiarity with the state space and inversely, the proximity to potential failure states.
> - Guiding expert intervention based on the novice's doubt enables more efficient learning of robust policies with stronger safety guarantees, as the novice is allowed to act primarily in regions where it is confident and familiar.
> - Deep ensembles provide a scalable and practical method for estimating predictive uncertainty in high-dimensional imitation learning settings, making Bayesian uncertainty quantification viable for complex robotic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper addresses a critical challenge in imitation learning (IL): ensuring safety, especially when deploying learned policies in real-world robotic systems. Imitation learning is a powerful paradigm where a novice policy learns by observing expert demonstrations, circumventing the need for explicit reward function design, which is often complex in reinforcement learning (RL) settings. However, a fundamental limitation of standard IL is its susceptibility to "data mismatch" or "distribution shift." This occurs when the novice policy, during deployment, encounters states not adequately represented in the expert's training dataset, leading to compounding errors and potentially unsafe or catastrophic failures.

The paper builds upon the **DAgger (Dataset Aggregation)** algorithm, a seminal iterative approach that mitigates data mismatch. DAgger works by having a novice policy act, observing the states it encounters, querying an expert for the correct actions in these novel states, and then aggregating this new (state, expert action) data into its training set. This iterative process allows the novice to learn a more robust policy across a wider range of states. While DAgger offers theoretical guarantees and online functionality, it explicitly *does not* guarantee safety, as the novice is allowed to act even if its proposed action might lead to an unsafe state.

Prior work, such as **SafeDAgger**, extended DAgger to introduce a notion of safety by employing a decision rule that switches control to the expert if the discrepancy between the novice's and expert's actions exceeds a certain threshold (τ). However, the authors argue that SafeDAgger's single, fixed threshold is suboptimal. An ideal safety mechanism should be *state-dependent*: deviations from expert actions might be permissible in familiar, safe regions of the state space but highly risky near failure states (e.g., obstacles or system limits). A fixed threshold is either too conservative in safe regions (hindering exploration and learning) or too relaxed in dangerous ones (risking failure).

This paper positions itself by addressing this limitation through a novel **Bayesian approach to safety**. It leverages advancements in quantifying uncertainty in deep learning, particularly by approximating Gaussian Processes (GPs) with ensembles of neural networks. GPs are powerful tools for modeling uncertainty, but their computational cost typically limits their application to high-dimensional problems. Deep ensembles offer a scalable way to estimate predictive uncertainty (epistemic uncertainty, reflecting the model's lack of knowledge about unfamiliar states).

By integrating this uncertainty quantification into the DAgger framework, EnsembleDAgger aims to create a more sophisticated and context-aware decision rule than SafeDAgger. It seeks to provide a proxy for safety by measuring the novice's "doubt" or confidence, which is hypothesized to correlate inversely with the proximity to failure states. This work therefore advances the state of the art in safe imitation learning by combining the robustness of DAgger with the interpretability of Bayesian uncertainty, making it more suitable for real-world robotic deployments where safety is paramount.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an imitation learning algorithm that allows a novice policy to maximize its share of actions while strictly constraining the probability of failure. This dual objective is motivated by the inherent challenges of deploying imitation learning in safety-critical domains, such as robotics.

The core motivations and specific objectives can be summarized as follows:

1. **Address the Safety Gap in DAgger:** Standard DAgger, while effective at mitigating data mismatch, does not incorporate safety considerations. Allowing a novice policy to explore freely, even with expert supervision, can lead to costly or irreversible failures in real-world scenarios. The authors aim to introduce a robust safety mechanism without sacrificing DAgger's learning benefits.

2. **Overcome Limitations of Fixed-Threshold Safety Rules (e.g., SafeDAgger\*):** The decision rule in SafeDAgger\*, which relies on a fixed threshold for the discrepancy between novice and expert actions, is identified as a key shortcoming. This approach fails to account for the state-dependent nature of safety. The authors hypothesize that:
 - The magnitude of *permissible perturbation* to an expert action should be inversely related to the system's *distance from unsafe regions*. Near a wall, even a small deviation might be dangerous; far from it, larger deviations might be acceptable.
 - In a model-free setting, there is an *inverse relationship between a state's similarity to expert trajectories and the allowed perturbations*. If the novice is in a familiar state, it's likely safe for it to act; in an unfamiliar state, the risk of failure is higher, and the expert should intervene.

3. **Quantify Novice Confidence as a Proxy for Safety:** To implement a state-dependent safety mechanism without explicit knowledge of safety envelopes (which are often hard to define in model-free settings), the authors propose using the novice policy's *uncertainty* or *confidence* as a proxy for safety. They hypothesize that a novice policy exhibiting high uncertainty in a given state implies a lack of familiarity with that state during training, and thus a higher risk of entering an unsafe region.

4. **Integrate Bayesian Uncertainty into DAgger:** The motivation is to leverage modern Bayesian deep learning techniques to estimate predictive uncertainty in the novice's actions. Specifically, they aim to show that an ensemble of neural networks can effectively approximate a Gaussian Process, providing a measure of variance that quantifies "doubt" about the novice's proposed action.

5. **Develop a Combined Decision Rule:** The ultimate objective is to combine two conditions for novice action: (1) low discrepancy from the expert's action (similar to SafeDAgger\*) and (2) low uncertainty (doubt) in the novice's own action. This combined "EnsembleDAgger Decision Rule" aims to be more conservative and safer than previous DAgger variants while still promoting efficient learning by allowing the novice to act in informative, yet safe, regions.

By achieving these objectives, the authors seek to make imitation learning a more viable and trustworthy approach for real-world robotics, where safety guarantees are non-negotiable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of EnsembleDAgger lies in extending the DAgger framework with a probabilistic decision rule that explicitly considers the novice policy's confidence in its actions, in addition to its agreement with the expert.

**A. DAgger Framework Overview**
EnsembleDAgger operates within the iterative DAgger framework (Algorithm 1), which involves:
1. Initializing a novice policy ($\pi_{nov,0}$) on an initial expert dataset ($D_0$).
2. In each epoch ($i$):
 - Collecting T-step trajectories by allowing a combined expert-novice system to interact with the environment, guided by a **decision rule (DR)**.
 - Recording observations ($o_t$) and corresponding expert actions ($\pi_{exp}(o_t)$) as a new dataset ($D_i$).
 - Aggregating all collected data ($D \leftarrow D \cup D_i$).
 - Retraining the novice policy ($\pi_{nov,i+1}$) on the aggregated dataset $D$.

**B. Quantifying Novice Doubt using Deep Ensembles**
The key innovation is the method for quantifying the novice's uncertainty, referred to as "doubt."
- **Approximating Gaussian Processes:** While Gaussian Processes (GPs) are ideal for uncertainty quantification, their computational cost for high-dimensional state spaces and large datasets is prohibitive. The authors choose to approximate GPs using **ensembles of neural networks**.
- **Ensemble Architecture:** The novice policy ($\pi_{nov}$) is implemented as an ensemble of neural networks. Instead of a single network predicting an action, multiple networks are trained concurrently to perform the same task.
- **Uncertainty Measure:** When queried with a state ($o_t$), each network in the ensemble outputs an action. The **variance of these actions across the ensemble members** is used as the measure of the novice's doubt ($\sigma^2_{a_{nov,t}}$). This variance captures *epistemic uncertainty*—the uncertainty due to a lack of knowledge or data in unfamiliar regions of the state space.
- **Training:** The individual neural networks within the ensemble are trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. An empirical evaluation (Appendix A) demonstrates that this "Vanilla Ensemble" approach effectively estimates predictive uncertainty comparable to a GP, even with noisy-free expert data, and does so more efficiently than other Bayesian approximation methods like Monte-Carlo Dropout or ensembles trained with Negative Log-Likelihood. For the inverted pendulum, 10 MLPs with hidden layers were used. For HalfCheetah, 5 networks with 5 hidden layers (16 neurons each).

**C. EnsembleDAgger Decision Rule (Algorithm 4)**
The proposed EnsembleDAgger decision rule, parameterized by two thresholds, $\tau$ (discrepancy) and $\chi$ (doubt), dictates whether the novice or expert acts at each timestep $t$:
1. **Novice Action and Uncertainty:** For a given observation $o_t$, the ensemble policy $\pi_{nov}$ provides a mean action ($\bar{a}_{nov,t}$) and a variance ($\sigma^2_{a_{nov,t}}$).
2. **Expert Action:** The expert policy provides its action ($a_{exp,t}$).
3. **Discrepancy Calculation:** The squared Euclidean distance between the novice's mean action and the expert's action is computed: $\hat{\tau} = \|\bar{a}_{nov,t} - a_{exp,t}\|_2^2$.
4. **Doubt Calculation:** The variance of the novice's action is directly used as the doubt measure: $\hat{\chi} = \sigma^2_{a_{nov,t}}$.
5. **Decision Logic:**
 - If **both** $\hat{\tau} \le \tau$ (low discrepancy) **AND** $\hat{\chi} \le \chi$ (low doubt), the novice's mean action ($\bar{a}_{nov,t}$) is used to interact with the environment.
 - Otherwise, the expert's action ($a_{exp,t}$) is used.

**D. Underlying Assumptions for Safety Guarantee**
The efficacy of this decision rule hinges on several key assumptions:
1. **Expert Safety Preference:** The expert policy is assumed to prefer trajectories that avoid failure states, implying it rarely visits regions close to danger. Consequently, states that are "unfamiliar" to the novice (i.e., dissimilar to those in the expert's training dataset) are hypothesized to be at higher risk of being near failure states.
2. **Doubt as a Safety Proxy:** The novice's epistemic uncertainty (doubt) is considered a model-free proxy for proximity to failure states. Higher doubt signifies less familiarity and thus higher perceived risk.
3. **Bounded Perturbations:** To constrain the probability of failure, any action taken must be within a certain bound from the expert's action.
4. **State-Dependent Bounds:** Ideally, this bound on discrepancy should be tighter when the system is closer to failure states.
5. **Doubt-Discrepancy Relationship:** Following from assumptions 2 and 4, the bound on permissible discrepancy should decrease as the novice's doubt increases.

While an ideal implementation might involve a functional mapping where the discrepancy bound is inversely proportional to doubt, the authors opted for a simpler, interpretable approach with two independent thresholds ($\tau$ and $\chi$) for the combined decision rule, as visualized in Figure 3. This approach serves as a low-order approximation to the ideal state-dependent safety envelope.

This methodology provides a concrete, model-free way to incorporate a probabilistic notion of safety into imitation learning, aiming to strike a better balance between novice autonomy, learning efficiency, and safety.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted experiments on two distinct robotic control environments to validate their claims: a simple inverted pendulum and the more complex MuJoCo HalfCheetah.

**A. Inverted Pendulum Domain (Simple, 2D State Space)**
- **Setup:** A deterministic, non-linear control problem for stabilizing an inverted pendulum. The novice policy was an ensemble of ten multi-layer perceptrons. The expert control law was derived using feedback linearization. This domain allowed for clear visualization of the state space and decision rule behavior.
- **Experiment 1: Permitted Set Volume Comparison:**
 - **Objective:** To qualitatively compare how the "doubt rule" (novice acts if $\hat{\chi} \le \chi$) and "discrepancy rule" (novice acts if $\hat{\tau} \le \tau$) allocate their "permitted set" (states where the novice is allowed to act) for a fixed volume budget.
 - **Findings (Figure 6):**
 - **Doubt Rule:** The permitted set was concentrated *in the neighborhood of states already represented in the training dataset ($D$)*. This supports the claim that variance (doubt) is a good measure of dissimilarity/familiarity, as uncertainty increases away from learned data. Trajectories guided by the doubt rule led the system to the edge of familiar regions, then deferred to the expert, resulting in a significantly larger "novice basin of attraction" (states from which the novice can converge alone) without entering dangerous territory.
 - **Discrepancy Rule:** The permitted set was haphazardly distributed. The novice was allowed to act in arbitrary regions where its action happened to agree with the expert by chance, potentially including dangerous areas. This haphazard distribution meant the novice rarely explored meaningfully beyond expert trajectories, leading to less informative data aggregation and a smaller novice basin of attraction.
- **Experiment 2 (Appendix C): Fixed Hyperparameter Comparison:**
 - **Objective:** To quantitatively compare the doubt rule and discrepancy rule with fixed, a priori chosen hyperparameters ($\chi$ and $\tau$), as a practitioner would. Metrics included "Learning Performance" (fraction of states novice can control), "Failure Rate" (trajectory leaving expert's basin of attraction), and "Permitted Set Volume."
 - **Findings (Figure 10):**
 - An instance of the doubt rule (with $\chi=10^{-3}$) demonstrated **superior learning performance** and, crucially, **zero failures** over six epochs across 30 repetitions.
 - Instances of the discrepancy rule (with $\tau=10^{-1}$ and $\tau=5 \cdot 10^{-2}$) showed lower learning performance and **non-zero failure rates**. More conservative $\tau$ values reduced failures but at the cost of learning speed.
 - The discrepancy rule consistently had much larger permitted set volumes than the doubt rule, indicating it was less selective about where the novice could act, yet proved less safe and less effective for learning.
 - **Conclusion:** These inverted pendulum experiments strongly supported the claims that novice doubt (variance) is an effective proxy for familiarity/safety, and that the doubt rule is superior to the discrepancy rule in terms of both learning efficiency and safety.

**B. MuJoCo HalfCheetah Domain (Complex, High-Dimensional State/Action Space)**
- **Setup:** An OpenAI Gym environment with 18-dimensional observations and 6-dimensional actions, requiring a stable gait for locomotion. The expert policy was trained using TRPO. The novice was an ensemble of five neural networks.
- **Objective:** To demonstrate the superiority of the doubt rule and the effect of the combined EnsembleDAgger decision rule in a more complex, high-dimensional setting.
- **Metrics:** Performance of the "lone novice" (summed score of novice over epochs) vs. performance of the "combined system" (summed score of expert/novice under decision rule over epochs). These were sampled 100 times.
- **Findings (Figure 8):**
 - **Doubt Rule Pareto Dominates Discrepancy Rule:** The Pareto frontier for the doubt rule consistently achieved better lone novice performance for the same level of compromise to the combined system's performance compared to the discrepancy rule. This quantitatively confirmed the observations from the inverted pendulum: constraining the novice to act only in familiar states allows for more robust learning while maintaining safety.
 - **EnsembleDAgger (Combined Rules):** The full EnsembleDAgger decision rule (combining both $\tau$ and $\chi$ conditions) was shown to be **strictly more conservative** than either component rule alone, leading to improved combined system performance (less compromise). For specific hyperparameter settings (e.g., $(\tau, \chi) = (0.5, 0.05)$), EnsembleDAgger showed a slight improvement in novice performance over the doubt rule alone, suggesting that the combination can be beneficial if hyperparameters are well-tuned.

**Overall Conclusions from Findings:**
The experiments consistently demonstrated that the novice's uncertainty (doubt) serves as an effective model-free proxy for safety. The "doubt rule" is generally superior to the "discrepancy rule" in allowing the novice to learn a more robust policy faster while maintaining a high level of safety. The combined EnsembleDAgger decision rule offers the highest safety guarantees for the combined system and can lead to further performance improvements in complex domains, contingent on appropriate hyperparameter selection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The EnsembleDAgger paper makes significant contributions to the field of imitation learning, particularly in addressing the critical aspect of safety for real-world robotic applications. Its impact can be assessed in several key areas:

**A. Introduction of Probabilistic Safety to Imitation Learning:**
The most significant contribution is the introduction of a principled, probabilistic notion of safety into the DAgger framework. By using a Bayesian approach to quantify the novice policy's uncertainty ("doubt"), EnsembleDAgger moves beyond deterministic, heuristic safety measures. This is crucial for real-world systems where safety is paramount but explicit safety models might be unavailable or intractable. The paper effectively demonstrates that this model-free proxy for safety (familiarity/uncertainty) holds strong validity in typical IL scenarios where the expert maintains a safety margin.

**B. Improved Learning Efficiency and Safety Trade-off:**
The paper rigorously demonstrates that using the novice's doubt to guide expert intervention leads to superior performance compared to previous methods like SafeDAgger\*. The "doubt rule" allows the novice to explore informative regions of the state space without incurring significant risk, accelerating learning of a robust policy while simultaneously maintaining a higher level of safety (e.g., zero failures in the inverted pendulum domain under optimal settings). This ability to achieve better learning performance for the same or even improved safety compromise is a major step forward.

**C. Scalability and Practicality with Deep Ensembles:**
By leveraging deep ensembles to approximate Gaussian Processes, the authors provide a scalable and practical method for uncertainty quantification in high-dimensional state-action spaces. This makes the Bayesian safety approach feasible for complex robotic tasks, overcoming the computational limitations of traditional GP methods. The empirical evaluation of ensemble methods against other Bayesian deep learning approximations (Monte-Carlo Dropout) further contributes to the practical application of these techniques.

**D. Foundation for Future Safe Learning Algorithms:**
EnsembleDAgger provides a strong foundation for future research in safe learning. It highlights the importance of epistemic uncertainty in determining when a learning agent should rely on expert knowledge versus its own learned policy. This principle can be extended to other forms of learning, including reinforcement learning with expert guidance.

**E. Potential Impact on Autonomous Systems:**
The direct application of this research is in developing safer and more reliable autonomous systems, particularly in areas like autonomous driving, drone control, and industrial robotics. By providing a framework that enables learning from demonstration while actively mitigating the risk of unsafe actions, EnsembleDAgger contributes to building trust in AI-driven autonomy. The acknowledgment of industry funding (SAIC Innovation Center) and the citation of follow-up work on real vehicles underscore its practical relevance and immediate impact.

**F. Identified Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper is also significant for clearly articulating its own limitations, which opens avenues for future research:
- **"Safety == Familiarity" Assumption:** The core assumption that risk correlates inversely with familiarity might not hold in all environments, especially those with "bottleneck" states where experts *must* traverse close to unsafe regions to achieve their goal. Relaxing this assumption is a crucial next step.
- **Hyperparameter Selection:** The current method requires manual tuning of hyperparameters ($\chi$ and $\tau$). Developing heuristic or automated strategies for safely discovering optimal hyperparameter settings is vital for broader adoption, an area already being explored in subsequent work by the authors and their collaborators.

In conclusion, EnsembleDAgger represents a substantial advancement in safe imitation learning by elegantly integrating Bayesian uncertainty quantification into the DAgger framework. Its demonstrated ability to improve both learning performance and safety, coupled with its practical scalability, positions it as an influential work that will likely inspire further developments in building robust and trustworthy intelligent autonomous systems.
