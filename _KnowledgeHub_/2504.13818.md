# Not All Rollouts are Useful: Down-Sampling Rollouts in LLM Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed PODS (Policy Optimization with Down-Sampling), a framework that addresses the computational imbalance in large language model reinforcement learning by strategically selecting informative rollouts. This method allows for up to 3.0x faster training convergence and often achieves higher peak accuracy on mathematical reasoning tasks like GSM8K and MATH, utilizing diverse model architectures and hardware configurations.

## Problem

- Large language model (LLM) reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) suffers from computational asymmetry between fast, parallelizable rollout generation and slow, memory-intensive policy updates.
- Policy updates scale poorly with batch size due to memory constraints and communication overhead, leading to hardware underutilization or increased latency.
- Not all generated rollouts equally contribute to model improvement, leading to redundant computation in the policy update phase.

## Method

- ==PODS== decouples the inference and policy-update phases by first generating a large batch of 'n' rollouts and then applying a principled down-sampling rule.
- A ==max-variance down-sampling rule== selects a smaller, more informative subset of 'm' rollouts (where m < n), which prioritizes examples at the extremes of the reward distribution.
- Policy updates are performed exclusively on this selected 'm' subset, significantly reducing the memory and computational burden on the policy update phase.

## Results

- GRPO-PODS consistently achieved faster convergence, reaching baseline peak accuracies at least **1.7x** faster and up to **3.0x** faster across various benchmarks, models (Qwen, Llama), and hardware setups.
- The max-variance down-sampling rule consistently outperformed trivial baselines like random sampling and max-reward sampling, demonstrating its effectiveness in enhancing the learning signal.
- PODS showed robust performance across a wide range of down-sampling ratios, with typical ratios of **2 to 4** (e.g., updating with **16** rollouts from **32** or **64** generated) offering an effective balance between efficiency and accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Maximizing the empirical reward variance within a subset of rollouts provides a stronger contrastive signal for policy learning, effectively distinguishing between highly successful and unsuccessful reasoning paths.
> - For a sorted list of rewards, the variance-maximizing subset of any given size always consists of the highest and lowest rewarded rollouts.
> - Intelligently down-sampling rollouts within each prompt can leverage efficient inference capabilities while mitigating the high costs of policy updates, thereby accelerating LLM RLVR training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of large language model (LLM) training, specifically focusing on Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to enhance reasoning capabilities. RLVR, which includes algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), has been instrumental in recent advancements in LLMs for tasks such as math problem-solving, code generation, and general reasoning.

The paper identifies a computational challenge inherent in current RLVR methodologies: a fundamental asymmetry between the inference phase (rollout generation) and the policy-update phase. The inference phase is characterized as embarrassingly parallel and memory-light, allowing for efficient generation of thousands of rollouts concurrently on modern accelerators. In contrast, the policy-update phase is described as memory- and communication-intensive, scaling poorly with batch size due to the requirements for full-precision optimizer states and cross-device synchronization. This asymmetry leads to computational bottlenecks, as systems either underutilize inference compute or incur significant overheads from memory-saving techniques like gradient accumulation.

Existing related work has explored reinforcement learning for LLMs, with GRPO gaining prominence for reasoning tasks due to its performance and algorithmic simplicity. Furthermore, the broader machine learning community has extensively studied data selection and down-sampling techniques to manage large and complex datasets, evident in areas such as clustering, regression, speech recognition, computer vision, and foundation model training. In reinforcement learning, methods like prioritized experience replay have demonstrated the value of selective sampling from experience buffers. More recently, data selection has also been applied to LLM training through prompt selection and filtering methods such as DAPO, SRPO, and Polaris.

This work specifically advances the area of data selection within LLM reinforcement learning by focusing on down-sampling *rollouts* generated for each prompt, rather than selecting or filtering prompts themselves. By addressing the computational bottleneck arising from the inference-update asymmetry, the proposed method, PODS, complements existing prompt-selection strategies and aims to improve training efficiency and performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the identified computational asymmetry in RLVR for LLMs. Specifically, the authors observe that while the inference phase (rollout generation) can leverage highly parallel hardware for efficient generation of numerous rollouts, the subsequent policy-update phase becomes a bottleneck due to its high memory and communication demands. This mismatch forces practitioners to either underutilize inference capabilities or employ memory-saving techniques (e.g., gradient accumulation) that increase training latency and communication overheads. Empirical evidence provided in the paper illustrates this asymmetry, showing that inference time per token decreases significantly with batching, while policy update time rapidly increases and hits memory limits, necessitating gradient accumulation which slows down training.

A key underlying observation motivating the proposed solution is that "not all rollouts contribute equally to model improvement." The authors suggest that beyond a certain number, additional rollouts may provide diminishing returns or even introduce redundant information, potentially degrading the learning signal. This leads to the hypothesis that selectively training on only the most informative subset of generated rollouts could mitigate the computational bottleneck without compromising, and potentially even improving, learning quality.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this work are:
1. **Decouple rollout generation and policy updates:** To allow the inference phase to operate at its full, parallel capacity, generating a large pool of rollouts, while the policy update phase processes a smaller, more manageable subset.
2. **Reduce policy update costs:** By training on a strategically selected subset of rollouts, thereby reducing memory and communication requirements, and avoiding the latency associated with gradient accumulation.
3. **Maintain or enhance learning quality:** To ensure that the selection process identifies rollouts that provide strong, contrastive learning signals, thus preserving or improving the model's final performance compared to training on all rollouts or a randomly sampled subset.
4. **Develop a principled down-sampling criterion:** To systematically select informative rollouts, moving beyond trivial or arbitrary selection methods.
5. **Provide an efficient implementation:** To ensure the proposed down-sampling method is practical for real-world deployment in large-scale LLM training.

The proposed framework, PODS (Policy Optimization with Down-Sampling), aims to achieve these objectives by generating a large number of rollouts (n) during inference and then selecting a smaller, informative subset (m < n) for policy updates.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the **PODS (Policy Optimization with Down-Sampling)** framework, which modifies the standard two-phase structure of RLVR algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

The paper first outlines the GRPO algorithm, which serves as the base reinforcement learning method for LLM reasoning:
- **Inference phase:** Given an input prompt `p`, the policy $\pi_\theta$ (parameterized by $\theta$) autoregressively samples `n` rollouts $o = (o_1..., o_n)$. Each rollout is a token sequence representing a potential solution. A reward model `R` then evaluates each rollout, yielding rewards $r = (r_1..., r_n)$. These rewards are then used to compute normalized advantage estimates $a_i = (r_i - \mu) / \sigma$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of the rewards.
- **Policy update phase:** The policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated by optimizing the GRPO objective $L_{GRPO}(\theta)$. This objective averages a clipped importance-weighted loss over all tokens and rollouts, designed to encourage increasing probabilities for high-reward tokens and reducing them for low-reward tokens, following a "slow to adopt, quick to abandon" principle.

#### 3.2. PODS Framework

PODS decouples the inference and training phases. Instead of updating on all `n` generated rollouts, PODS selects a smaller subset of size `m < n` using a down-sampling rule `D`. This enables high-throughput inference while reducing the computational burden of policy updates.

- **Down-sampling rule $D(o, r; m)$:** This function takes the `n` rollouts, their rewards `r`, and the desired update size `m`, outputting a set of `m` indices `S` for the rollouts to be retained.
- **Advantage computation:** Advantage estimates $a_{S,i}$ are computed using only the rewards from the selected subset `S`, ensuring consistency between the normalization statistics and the data used for updates.
- **GRPO-PODS Objective:** The policy is then updated using $L_{PODS}(\theta, S)$, which is the GRPO objective applied only to the `m` selected rollouts.

The paper also briefly describes three trivial down-sampling strategies for comparison:
- **Random down-sampling ($D_{rand}$):** Uniformly selects `m` indices.
- **Percentile down-sampling ($D_{perc}$):** Selects `m` rollouts corresponding to evenly spaced quantiles of the reward distribution.
- **Max-reward down-sampling ($D_{maxr}$):** Selects the `m` rollouts with the highest rewards.

#### 3.3. Max-Variance Down-Sampling

The core contribution of the methodology is the introduction of **max-variance down-sampling ($D_{maxv}$)**. This principled criterion selects the subset `S` of size `m` that maximizes the empirical reward variance, $\text{Var}(\{r_i | i \in S\})$. The rationale is that maximizing variance preserves strong contrastive signals, representing both successful and unsuccessful reasoning paths, which is beneficial for learning. This criterion is supported by optimization-theoretic justifications from prior work.

A key theoretical finding is **Lemma 3.1**, which states that for a sorted list of rewards, the variance-maximizing subset of size `m` always consists of `k` highest rewards and (`m - k`) lowest rewards for some `k`. This lemma transforms a combinatorial problem into an efficiently solvable one.

- **Algorithm 2 (Max-Variance Down-Sampling):**
 1. Sort the rollouts by reward.
 2. Iterate `k` from 0 to `m`, constructing a candidate subset by taking the `(m-k)` lowest-reward rollouts and `k` highest-reward rollouts.
 3. Select the subset that yields the maximum variance.

- **Complexity (Theorem 1):** This algorithm is proven to be implementable in $O(n \log n)$ time, dominated by the initial sorting step. Subsequent variance calculations for each `k` can be done in $O(1)$ time using prefix sums of rewards and squared rewards.

- **Special Case (Theorem 2):** For binary rewards and an even `m`, selecting `m/2` highest-reward rollouts and `m/2` lowest-reward rollouts is shown to maximize reward variance. This provides an intuitive understanding of the rule's operation in a common scenario.

#### 3.4. Training Infrastructure and Evaluation

Experiments were conducted across diverse configurations:
- **Benchmarks:** GSM8K, MATH, SciKnowEval (Chemistry subset).
- **Models:** Qwen2.5 (3B, 7B), Llama3.2 (3B).
- **Hardware:** Single L40S GPU (resource-constrained LoRA fine-tuning), 8 H100s, and 8 A100s (full-parameter distributed training with DeepSpeed ZeRO-2).
- **Reward Models:** Rule-based models that score correctness, format compliance, and thinking tag placement, resulting in discrete but non-binary rewards.

The comparison involved:
- **Single-GPU settings (a-d):** GRPO-PODS against vanilla GRPO with matched training batch sizes (m).
- **Distributed settings (e-f):** GRPO-PODS against GRPO with gradient accumulation (GRPO-GA), where the total number of generated rollouts (`effective n`) was matched between methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of PODS demonstrated consistent performance improvements and efficiency gains across various experimental settings.

#### 4.1. Comparison to Baseline GRPO

Across all six tested hardware and model configurations (Table 1), GRPO coupled with max-variance PODS consistently exhibited faster convergence and often achieved higher peak test accuracy compared to baseline GRPO or GRPO-GA.
- **Speedup:** GRPO-PODS achieved the baselines' peak accuracies at least **1.7 times faster** (ranging from 1.7x to 3.0x across settings, as detailed in Section A.4).
- **Final Performance:** In several settings, GRPO-PODS converged to a higher final test accuracy than its respective baseline.
- **Generalizability:** The performance gains were observed across different task domains (mathematics, chemistry), model scales (3B, 7B), model architectures (Qwen2.5, Llama3.2), and deployment scenarios (single-GPU LoRA fine-tuning, multi-GPU full-parameter distributed training). This indicates the broad applicability and practical benefits of PODS.
- **Per-step Runtime:** Accompanying runtime comparisons showed that GRPO-PODS maintained lower per-step training times than GRPO-GA, while allowing inference to leverage parallelism.

#### 4.2. Effect of Rollout and Update Sizes (n, m)

An ablation study on the GSM8K dataset with Qwen2.5-3B (setting a) investigated the impact of the total rollout size (`n`) and the update batch size (`m`) on GRPO-PODS's performance:
- **Rollout Size (n):** Increasing `n` initially improved performance, with an optimal range observed around `n = 64`. Performance degraded beyond `n = 128`, attributed to increased inference runtime as GPU memory saturated and diminishing returns in rollout diversity.
- **Training Batch Size (m):** Performance remained robust across a wide range of `m`, showing minimal degradation until very small values ($m \le 4$). This suggests that max-variance selection effectively preserves learning signals even with aggressive down-sampling ratios (e.g., up to 16:1 when `n = 64, m = 4`).
- **Practical Guidelines:** The results suggest a down-sampling ratio of 2 to 4 provides a balance of performance and efficiency. More aggressive ratios (up to 16:1) can be viable in resource-constrained settings.

#### 4.3. Comparison of Different Down-Sampling Rules

The study compared max-variance down-sampling against max-reward, random, and percentile down-sampling rules on setting (a):
- **Superiority of Max-Variance:** The max-variance rule consistently outperformed all other down-sampling strategies throughout training. This indicates its effectiveness in selecting informative rollouts by providing strong contrastive signals, learning from both highly rewarded and poorly rewarded examples.
- **Max-Reward Limitations:** Max-reward down-sampling, which focuses only on positive examples, performed poorly, suggesting that ignoring low-reward rollouts deprives the policy of crucial negative feedback.

#### 4.4. Advantage Normalization Design Choice

An additional experiment (Section A.3) compared computing advantage normalization statistics (mean and standard deviation) from the down-sampled batch ("After") versus the full rollout batch before down-sampling ("Before"):
- **Consistency is Key:** Computing advantage normalization statistics using the down-sampled batch led to better performance. This suggests that ensuring consistency between the advantage normalization and the actual data used for policy updates is beneficial for learning.

#### 4.5. Robustness Across Test Sets

To verify that performance improvements were not due to overfitting, models from settings (a) and (b) were evaluated on additional test sets (GSM8K Platinum and MATH, detailed in Section A.5):
- **Consistent Outperformance:** GRPO-PODS consistently outperformed GRPO on these additional test sets, reinforcing the robustness of the observed performance improvements and indicating a generalization of learning.

#### 4.6. Average Completion Length

Analysis of average completion length over training time (Sections A.6, Figs. 8-10) generally showed that completion lengths remained relatively stable across most experimental settings, indicating that PODS did not drastically alter the generated response length characteristics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of PODS represents a practical advancement in the field of large language model reinforcement learning, particularly for reasoning tasks. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key areas:

1. **Addressing a Core Bottleneck:** PODS directly addresses the computational asymmetry between rollout generation and policy updates, a fundamental bottleneck in modern RLVR training. By allowing inference to operate at peak parallelism while reducing the compute-intensive policy update batch size, it optimizes hardware utilization and improves overall training efficiency.
2. **Improved Training Efficiency:** The demonstrated speedup of at least 1.7x in reaching peak accuracies signifies a substantial reduction in training time and computational resources. This makes RLVR fine-tuning for LLMs more accessible and cost-effective, potentially accelerating research and deployment cycles.
3. **Enhanced Learning Signal:** The max-variance down-sampling rule is a principled approach that selects the most diverse and informative rollouts. By preserving strong contrastive signals from both high and low-reward examples, it leads to a "cleaner learning signal" that not only speeds up convergence but also often results in higher final model performance. This suggests that "less data, strategically chosen" can be more effective than "all data."
4. **Broad Applicability:** The empirical results across different model architectures (Qwen2.5, Llama3.2), scales (3B-7B), task domains (math, chemistry), and hardware configurations (single GPU, distributed) indicate the method's generalizability. This makes PODS a broadly applicable improvement for GRPO-based RLVR systems.
5. **Complementary to Existing Methods:** PODS focuses on intra-prompt rollout selection, complementing existing prompt-level data selection or filtering strategies. It could be integrated with such methods to achieve further gains in reasoning performance.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The PODS framework is general, allowing for exploration of alternative down-sampling rules that could incorporate additional information (e.g., rollout entropy, similarity) or target specific reward distributions. This opens avenues for further optimization and customization of the learning process.

**Limitations:** The authors explicitly note several limitations:
- **Domain Specificity:** The evaluation focuses on RLVR tasks with verifiable correctness (e.g., math problems). The dynamics and benefits of PODS may differ in domains requiring open-ended dialogue or subjective rewards.
- **Alternative Optimizations:** In scenarios demanding greater prompt diversity, processing more prompts per iteration with fewer rollouts per prompt and accumulating gradients across prompts could be an alternative approach to address the inference-update asymmetry. This is a different axis of optimization that PODS does not directly replace.
- **Off-Policy Behavior:** By selectively down-sampling, PODS alters the training rollout distribution, making it an off-policy method. While demonstrating empirical benefits, it may not be suitable in contexts where strict on-policy guarantees are required.
- **GRPO Specificity:** The empirical and theoretical claims are specific to PODS instantiated within GRPO. Its applicability and benefits to other RL methods (e.g., PPO or value-based variants) would require separate analysis and experimentation.

Despite these limitations, PODS offers a practical, principled, and efficient solution to a critical computational challenge in LLM reinforcement learning, with the potential to significantly impact the development and training of more capable reasoning models.
