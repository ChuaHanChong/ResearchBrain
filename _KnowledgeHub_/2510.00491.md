# Traj2Action: A Co-Denoising Framework for Trajectory-Guided Human-to-Robot Skill Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Traj2Action transfers manipulation skills from human videos to robot policies by using 3D end-effector trajectory as a unified intermediate representation across embodiments. A Trajectory Expert predicts a coarse plan from human and robot data, conditioning an Action Expert that generates precise robot actions via joint flow-matching co-denoising. On a Franka arm it beats a pi-0 baseline by up to 27% success rate on short-horizon and 22.25% task progress on long-horizon tasks.

## Problem

- Human video pretraining methods lack robot action labels and use hand poses directly, failing to bridge the morphological gap to grippers.
- Two-stage human-then-robot pipelines and joint-training methods either constrain final policy quality or only work for kinematically similar embodiments like humanoids.
- Direct pose-alignment or rigid-transformation methods struggle to establish physically meaningful correspondence, or require strictly paired human-robot demonstrations and complex retargeting.

## Method

- ==Unified Trajectory Space== represents both human hand (thumb-index midpoint) and robot end-effector motion as shared 3D cartesian position sequences, abstracting away embodiment-specific details.
- ==Dual-expert co-denoising architecture==: a ==Trajectory Expert== trained on combined human and robot data predicts coarse future trajectory, conditioning an ==Action Expert== trained only on robot data for fine-grained actions.
- ==Hierarchical causal attention mask== enforces Task to Trajectory to Action information flow, plus a wrist-mounted camera for human data collection mimicking the robot's ego-centric view.

## Results

- Full Traj2Action model gains **+27%** success rate on short-horizon tasks and **+22.25%** task progress on long-horizon tasks over the pi-0 baseline on a real Franka arm.
- Adding only the Trajectory Expert (robot data only) already boosts long-horizon stack-cups task progress from **37.75%** to **54.25%**, while naive human-robot co-training without unified trajectory yields just **+2%** success rate.
- Human demonstrations replace robot data efficiently: **240** human demos match **60%** success rate of robot-only training with **20%** less collection time, and a **30 FPS** human / **10 FPS** robot sampling ratio achieves peak **76%** success rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A shared low-dimensional geometric abstraction (trajectory), not raw pose alignment, is what actually unlocks cross-embodiment transfer value from human data.
> - Simply co-training on human and robot action-padded labels barely helps; the representation bridging the embodiment gap matters more than data volume alone.
> - Matching kinematic sampling frequency between faster human motion and slower robot motion is necessary for the shared representation to remain a meaningful guide.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation faces a fundamental challenge in enabling robots to acquire diverse skills. Current policy learning paradigms, particularly imitation learning, are resource-intensive, relying on extensive datasets of expert demonstrations. These datasets are typically gathered through teleoperation, a process that demands specialized hardware, significant operator training, and considerable time investment, thereby bottlenecking the scalability of robotic capabilities.

An alternative data source, human videos, offers abundance and cost-effectiveness. However, their direct application to robotic skill transfer is impeded by a significant morphological disparity between human and robotic embodiments. Prior research has explored various strategies to bridge this gap:
- **Indirect Approaches**: Some methods attempt to learn shared visual representations across human and robot videos, formulate reward functions, or use human demonstrations as high-level context for robot action prediction. These approaches often do not fully leverage explicit motion signals from human actions.
- **Direct Approaches with Limitations**: Other works aim for a unified action space or motion transfer. Two-stage pipelines, where models are first trained on human data and then adapted with robot data, can constrain the final policy quality. Joint-training approaches are often restricted to kinematically similar embodiments (e.g., humanoid robots). Methods that directly align mismatched poses (e.g., human hand to parallel gripper) encounter difficulties in establishing physically meaningful correspondences. Simplified representations like rigid transformations still face misalignments due to fundamental structural differences. Furthermore, some existing methods necessitate strictly paired human-robot demonstrations or complex motion retargeting, which diminishes the cost-benefit of using human data.

Traj2Action positions itself within this landscape by proposing a novel framework that directly addresses the morphological gap through the use of 3D trajectories of the operational endpoint as a unified, abstract intermediate representation. This approach aims to provide a more robust and scalable mechanism for transferring manipulation knowledge from human demonstrations to robots, overcoming the limitations of previous indirect or constrained direct methods. It seeks to leverage the rich motion information in human actions by abstracting away embodiment-specific details, thereby enhancing data efficiency and cross-embodiment learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for developing Traj2Action stems from a critical bottleneck in contemporary robot policy learning: the reliance on expensive and time-consuming teleoperated demonstrations. Modern policy learning methods, particularly those leveraging Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, require large-scale datasets that are prohibitively difficult to acquire for a wide range of complex manipulation skills and environments. While human videos present a scalable and cost-effective alternative, their utility is severely limited by the inherent morphological differences between human hands and robot end-effectors, making direct imitation challenging due to the lack of robot-specific action labels. Existing attempts to bridge this "embodiment gap" often suffer from indirectness, reliance on specific kinematic similarities, or requirements for expensive paired data, thereby failing to fully exploit the potential of abundant human data.

Against this background, Traj2Action was developed with two primary objectives:

1. **Systematic Verification of the Traj2Action Framework's Effectiveness**:
 - To introduce a novel framework that uses the 3D trajectory of the operational endpoint as a unified intermediate representation to bridge the morphological gap between human and robot embodiments.
 - To demonstrate how manipulation knowledge embedded in this trajectory can be effectively transferred to generate precise robot actions. This involves learning to generate a coarse trajectory plan, leveraging both human and robot data, which then conditions the synthesis of robot-specific actions within a co-denoising framework.
 - To empirically validate the architectural design, assess its generalization capabilities across different tasks (both short- and long-horizon), and evaluate its data efficiency.

2. **Revelation of Key Laws Governing Robot Policy Learning with Human Data Integration**:
 - To investigate and quantify the impact of human hand demonstration data on robot policy learning, specifically focusing on aspects such as data collection efficiency characteristics, the effect of scaling human data volume, and the potential for human data to substitute expensive robot teleoperation data.
 - This objective aims to provide a scalable paradigm for human-to-robot skill transfer that is robust to morphological discrepancies and offers practical benefits in terms of data acquisition cost and efficiency.

The overarching goal is to establish a foundation for leveraging high-fidelity human demonstration data to enhance robot learning, with future potential for scaling to large-scale, low-cost internet video datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Traj2Action framework is designed to facilitate human-to-robot skill transfer by addressing the embodiment gap and integrating knowledge from diverse data sources. The methodology centers on a unified trajectory representation and a dual-expert policy architecture trained with a joint co-denoising objective.

**A. Unified Trajectory Space**
A core component of Traj2Action is the establishment of a unified representation for motion, addressing the morphological differences between human hands and robot end-effectors. The framework defines trajectory as a sequence of 3D Cartesian positions (T ∈ R³). For human demonstrations, this trajectory is derived from the midpoint between the thumb and index finger keypoints. For robot demonstrations, it corresponds to the 3D position of the end-effector. This representation abstracts away specific morphological details (e.g., individual finger articulation versus gripper width), focusing instead on the high-level operational intent of a task, thereby creating a common ground for knowledge transfer.

**B. Traj2Action Framework**
The framework employs a coarse-to-fine action prediction paradigm implemented via a dual-expert policy architecture, trained with a joint denoising objective.

1. **Policy Architecture**:
 - **Backbone**: Traj2Action builds upon a pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, π₀, which includes a Vision-Language Model (VLM) for processing inputs and an action expert for generating control signals.
 - **Trajectory Expert (g_T)**: An additional expert is introduced, mirroring the architecture of π₀'s action expert and initialized with its weights. This expert is trained on a combined dataset of both human (D_h) and robot (D_r) demonstrations. Its function is to predict a coarse future 3D trajectory (T_t+1:t+H), acting as a high-level motion planner.
 - **Action Expert (π_θ)**: This expert learns exclusively from robot data (D_r). Its critical function is to translate the high-level spatial plan generated by the Trajectory Expert into fine-grained, robot-specific actions. Each action vector (a_t ∈ R⁷) comprises a 3D end-effector position delta, a 3D axis-angle rotation delta, and a 1D gripper state.

2. **Joint Denoising for Trajectory-Conditioned Action Generation**:
 - **Flow Matching Objective**: Both experts are trained jointly using a flow matching objective.
 - **Trajectory Denoising Loss (L_traj)**: The Trajectory Expert is trained to denoise a noisy trajectory (T_τ = τ · T\*_t+1:t+H + (1−τ)z, where T\* is ground truth and z is Gaussian noise). The loss aims to predict the original trajectory, conditioned on visual observations (I_t), language instruction (P), and current trajectory state (q'_t ∈ R³).
 - **Action Denoising Loss (L_action)**: The Action Expert is trained to denoise a noisy action (a_τ = τ · a\*_t+1:t+H + (1−τ)z), conditioned on both the noisy action a_τ and the noisy trajectory T_τ. This conditioning mechanism integrates the manipulation prior from the unified trajectory. The loss aims to predict the original action sequence, conditioned on I_t, P, and the full robot proprioceptive state (q_t ∈ R⁸, including 3D position, 4D quaternion orientation, and 1D gripper width).
 - **Total Loss**: The overall training objective is L = L_traj + L_action. During inference, both trajectory and action sequences are generated in parallel using an ODE solver, ensuring no additional latency.
 - **Hierarchical Attention Mask**: A hierarchical attention mask is applied to control information flow. Action-level states are prevented from attending to trajectory-level or task-level representations, maintaining a Task → Trajectory → Action hierarchy and preventing information leakage.

**C. Data Collection for Human and Robot**
A specialized data collection pipeline was developed to acquire cross-embodiment demonstrations (D = D_h ∪ D_r).

1. **Human Hand Motion Capture System**:
 - **Hardware**: A calibrated multi-camera system (three static, one wrist-mounted ego-view camera) operating at 30 Hz. The wrist-mounted camera mimics the robot’s ego-centric view to enhance cross-embodiment training consistency.
 - **3D Hand Pose Estimation**: Google MediaPipe detects 2D hand keypoints from multiple views. The parametric MANO 3D hand model is then fitted to these 2D keypoints via an iterative optimization process that minimizes reprojection error. This process reconstructs accurate 3D hand motion, from which the operational endpoint's trajectory is extracted.

2. **Robot Data Collection System**:
 - **Hardware**: A Franka Research 3 arm equipped with a UMI Gripper. Teleoperation is performed by an expert using a 6-DoF SpaceMouse.
 - **Data Recording**: Proprioceptive data (3D end-effector position, 3D orientation, gripper state) and images from both a static third-person camera and a wrist-mounted camera are recorded at 30 Hz. The robot's end-effector 3D position directly forms its trajectory.
 - **Initial State Randomization**: To enhance policy robustness and generalization, the robot's starting pose is randomized for each demonstration, sampling offsets from a uniform distribution around a canonical start pose.

**D. Camera Calibration**: A custom calibration setup with five AprilTag markers strategically distributed across the workspace ensures robust extrinsic camera parameter determination for all cameras using the Kalibr toolbox, meeting the precision requirements for manipulation tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Traj2Action involved extensive real-world experiments on a Franka robot across four distinct manipulation tasks categorized as Short-Horizon (pick up the water bottle, pick up the tomato and put it in the yellow/blue tray) and Long-Horizon (stack the rings on the pillar, stack the paper cups).

**A. Data Collection Efficiency Analysis**
A significant finding relates to data collection efficiency. Human expert demonstrations were approximately 3.5 times faster per demonstration than robot teleoperation (average 5.09s vs. 17.98s per demonstration). This efficiency advantage was more pronounced in complex, long-horizon tasks (e.g., 3.85 times faster for "stack the paper cups"), indicating a scalable path for acquiring data for challenging skills.

**B. Performance Analysis**
The performance of Traj2Action was compared against a baseline (π₀ architecture fine-tuned with robot data).

1. **Contribution of Trajectory Expert**:
 - **Architectural Improvement**: Simply adding the trajectory expert to the baseline architecture, even when trained solely on robot data, resulted in performance improvements. For instance, on the "stack the paper cups" (SPC) task, Task Progress increased from 37.75% (baseline) to 54.25% (+16.50%). This suggests that the explicit decomposition into a high-level planning (trajectory) and low-level execution (action) framework provides benefits by simplifying the action expert's task.
 - **Necessity of Unified Representation**: A naive approach of co-training the action expert directly on both human and robot data (without the trajectory expert or unified representation) yielded only a marginal improvement (+2%) on the "pick up the tomato and put it in the tray" (PTT) task compared to the baseline. This demonstrates that merely adding human data is insufficient; the unified trajectory space is critical for effectively bridging the embodiment gap.

2. **Contribution of Human Data (Full Traj2Action)**: Integrating human demonstrations with the full Traj2Action model consistently provided substantial performance gains:
 - **Short-Horizon Tasks**: +28% on "pick up the water bottle" (reaching 76.00% Success Rate) and +26.00% on "pick up the tomato and put it in the tray" (reaching 76.00% Success Rate) over the baseline.
 - **Long-Horizon Tasks**: +23.50% on "stack the paper cups" (reaching 61.25% Task Progress) and +21.00% on "stack the rings on the pillar" (reaching 44.75% Task Progress) over the baseline.
 These results indicate that long-horizon tasks particularly benefit from the diversity of human motion data, which can include agile re-orientations or awkward angles difficult to achieve via teleoperation, leading to more robust and generalizable plans.

**C. Ablation Studies**

1. **Impact of Human Data Scale**: Experiments showed a positive correlation between the volume of human demonstrations and policy performance. For the PTT task, the Success Rate increased from 50% (no human data) to 76% with 460 demonstrations. For the SPC task, Task Progress rose from 37.75% to 61.25% with 460 demonstrations. This empirically validated the hypothesis that human demonstrations are a rich data source that the model can effectively leverage.

2. **Human Data as an Effective Substitute for Robot Data**: Traj2Action demonstrated that low-cost human data could partially replace expensive robot data. For the PTT task:
 - Leveraging 240 human demonstrations and less robot data achieved a 60% success rate, identical to a robot-only model, but with a 20% reduction in total data collection time.
 - Increasing human data to 635 demonstrations (total collection time comparable to robot-only setup) resulted in performance surpassing the baseline (62% success rate).
 This suggests that human data offers an economically viable solution for scaling robot learning.

3. **Impact of Trajectory Sampling Frequency**: An optimal performance of 76% Success Rate was achieved for the PTT task when human trajectories were sampled at 30 FPS and robot trajectories at 10 FPS (a 3:1 ratio). This indicated that aligning the kinematic speeds through differential sampling frequencies is beneficial for effective cross-embodiment policy learning. A mismatch between the robot trajectory's planning frequency and the robot actions' execution frequency was observed to degrade performance.

**D. Zero-Shot Generalization to Unseen Tasks**
In a zero-shot setting for the "pick up the tomato and put it in the blue tray" task (robot trained only on "yellow tray" placements, but human data included both), the robot policy achieved a 12% success rate. This non-zero success rate provides evidence that the Trajectory Expert, enriched by diverse human data, can generate plausible trajectories for unseen goals, guiding the Action Expert towards generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Traj2Action addresses a significant constraint in contemporary robotics: the data-hungry nature of robot policy training and the associated cost and scalability limitations of teleoperated demonstrations. By introducing a framework that effectively transfers skills from readily available human videos, the work offers a path towards more efficient and accessible robot learning.

The primary significance of this research lies in its novel approach to bridging the morphological gap between human and robotic embodiments. By establishing the 3D trajectory of the operational endpoint as a unified, abstract intermediate representation, Traj2Action provides a robust mechanism for knowledge transfer. The coarse-to-fine co-denoising framework enables the integration of diverse human motion planning with precise robot-specific action execution.

The findings demonstrate that Traj2Action can significantly outperform baselines that rely solely on robot data, with notable improvements observed across both short- and long-horizon tasks. This indicates enhanced planning capabilities and improved robustness in complex sequential operations. Crucially, the research validates several key aspects:
- The trajectory prior effectively enhances high-level planning for robotic tasks.
- Policy performance scales positively with the amount of human demonstration data provided.
- Low-cost human data can serve as an effective substitute for a substantial volume of expensive robot data, reducing both data collection costs and time requirements.

The implications of these findings are substantial. By lowering the financial and operational barriers to large-scale data collection, Traj2Action offers a practical and economically viable paradigm for developing more capable and generalizable robot policies. This work contributes to accelerating the deployment of versatile robotic manipulators in real-world environments by leveraging abundant human knowledge more effectively.
