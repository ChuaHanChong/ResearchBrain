# WAM-TTT: Steering World-Action Models by Watching Human Play at Test Time

> [!summary] Summary
> WAM-TTT introduces a framework that adapts robot foundation models at test time using unlabeled human videos, significantly improving task success rates in novel environments. The method achieved an average progress of 46.2% on manipulation tasks in unseen household environments, outperforming the pretrained backbone by 13.7 percentage points, while preserving the foundation model's generalization capabilities.

## Problem

- Robot foundation models (RFMs) exhibit rigidity post-pretraining, necessitating expensive retraining or new robot data for adaptation to novel tasks or user preferences.
- Leveraging human demonstrations for robot control is challenging due to the embodiment gap, and existing methods for integration can lead to catastrophic forgetting or reduced generalization.
- Efficient, deployment-time adaptation of RFMs using easily accessible, unannotated human videos without modifying core model parameters is a critical unmet need.

## Method

- A two-stage framework involving meta-training with paired human-robot data and ==test-time training (TTT)== that utilizes only unlabeled human videos.
- Augments a frozen ==World Action Model (WAM)== with residual **Video TTT layers** incorporating a ==fast-weight memory== mechanism.
- Employs a meta-training objective to calibrate TTT branches, aligning human visual cues with robot behaviors using human video prediction and a ==key-value memory reconstruction loss==.

## Results

- WAM-TTT achieved an average progress of **46.2%** across nine manipulation tasks in unseen household environments, surpassing the pretrained LDA backbone by **13.7 percentage points**.
- The framework demonstrated strong robustness to distribution shifts, maintaining a **0.76** meaningful retention ratio when transitioning to novel environments, unlike other methods that experienced catastrophic drops.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical roles of test-time adaptation, the specific TTT memory architecture, and the meta-training phase, while showing that noisy pseudo-action supervision from human videos was detrimental to performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rapid adaptation of a frozen robot foundation model is achievable at test time by updating only lightweight, fast-weight memory using unlabeled human videos.
> - A meta-training stage that explicitly aligns human visual cues with robot behaviors is crucial for effectively bridging the embodiment gap and enabling the fast-weight memory to steer action generation.
> - Decoupling task adaptation into a specialized memory component preserves the foundation model's generalization capabilities and robustness to distribution shifts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a key challenge in the development and deployment of general-purpose robot foundation models (RFMs): the difficulty of adapting their learned behaviors to new task variants or user-preferred strategies efficiently and without extensive retraining.

Current RFMs typically embed knowledge into fixed model parameters during large-scale pretraining. Once deployed, their behavior is often rigid, determined by these fixed weights and limited conditioning interfaces such as language instructions or goal images. This rigidity necessitates costly collection of new robot demonstrations or fine-tuning of the entire model for each new adaptation, limiting the flexibility and reusability of RFMs in dynamic, open-ended environments.

Human demonstrations offer a natural and scalable interface for teaching robots, as users can simply show how a task should be performed without needing to specify robot-specific actions. However, directly leveraging human videos for robot control presents several challenges:
1. **Embodiment Gap:** Human motion is often not directly executable by a robot, requiring complex techniques like pose estimation, 3D motion tracking, or motion retargeting, which can be noisy and expensive to obtain.
2. **Training-Time Integration Issues:** Existing methods often integrate human data through co-training or fine-tuning with robot data, which can lead to catastrophic forgetting of previously learned skills or reduce the generalization ability of the pre-trained model.
3. **In-Context Learning Limitations:** Conditioning robot policies directly on raw human videos, while appealing, can lead to rapidly growing context lengths as demonstrations accumulate and may be fragile under distribution shifts.

WAM-TTT positions itself at the intersection of World Action Models (WAMs), Test-Time Training (TTT), and learning from human demonstrations. WAMs, which couple future visual prediction with action generation, provide a predictive substrate for grounding policies in imagined state transitions. This work builds upon these models (specifically LDA) by integrating a novel TTT framework. Instead of re-training the entire WAM or relying solely on in-context conditioning, WAM-TTT proposes to adapt a lightweight, adaptive memory *within* a frozen WAM at test time. This approach aims to provide efficient and reusable steering from human demonstrations, address the embodiment gap without explicit retargeting, and preserve the generalization capabilities of the foundation model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables efficient and reusable steering of robot foundation models (RFMs) towards new task variations or user-preferred behaviors using raw human videos, without requiring additional robot demonstrations, human-side annotations, or full model fine-tuning at deployment.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations in current RFM deployment:
- **Limited Adaptability Post-Pretraining:** Existing RFMs primarily encode knowledge into fixed parameters. Adapting them to new scenarios (e.g., new task variants, previously unseen objects, or specific interaction strategies) typically requires either collecting costly, task-specific robot demonstrations or fine-tuning the entire model. Both approaches are resource-intensive and restrict the flexibility and reusability of RFMs in practical, open-ended environments.
- **Challenges with Human Demonstrations:** While human videos are a natural and scalable source of task information, their direct use for robot control is complicated by the embodiment gap. Current methods that utilize human data often rely on expensive or noisy intermediate representations (e.g., hand poses, 3D motion, retargeted trajectories) or co-training approaches that can compromise the foundation model's pre-trained knowledge through catastrophic forgetting. In-context conditioning with human videos also faces scalability issues due to growing context lengths and vulnerability to visual distribution shifts.
- **Need for Efficient Deployment-Time Adaptation:** There is a demand for methods that can rapidly adapt an RFM's behavior at the point of deployment, using readily available, unannotated human demonstrations, without modifying the core, pre-trained model parameters. This would allow users to quickly specify new behaviors without needing deep robotics expertise or extensive computational resources.

WAM-TTT aims to overcome these issues by:
- Formulating human-video-based steering as a test-time training problem.
- Absorbing unlabeled human videos into a lightweight, adaptive memory within a *frozen* WAM through self-supervised video prediction.
- Introducing a meta-training stage that aligns human demonstrations with robot behaviors using paired human-robot data and a key-value memory reconstruction objective, making the adaptive memory useful for control.
- Enabling adaptation solely from human videos at test time, thus avoiding robot actions, human-side annotations, or task-specific fine-tuning.
- Preserving the generalization ability of the foundation model by isolating adaptation to a specialized memory component.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

WAM-TTT implements a two-stage training and deployment pipeline: a meta-training phase using paired human-robot data, followed by a test-time training (TTT) phase where only unlabeled human videos are used to adapt a fast-weight memory within a frozen World Action Model (WAM).

#### 3.1. Architecture

The framework is built upon a pre-trained World Action Model (WAM), specifically LDA, which is a diffusion transformer (DiT) based model. This WAM architecture comprises coupled "video expert" and "action expert" components within each diffusion transformer block. The video expert processes visual latent tokens, while the action expert operates on robot action tokens, with communication between them facilitated by joint attention.

WAM-TTT augments this WAM by adding residual branches, termed "Video TTT layers," exclusively to the video expert within each diffusion transformer block. The output of a TTT-augmented block's video stream is modified as $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell+1)} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell+1)} + \Delta\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_{\text{TTT}}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell+1)}$ is the original WAM output and $\Delta\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_{\text{TTT}}$ is the TTT residual. The action expert's output $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell+1)}$ remains unchanged, ensuring that test-time human-video adaptation primarily influences the visual dynamics.

Each TTT layer incorporates a fast-weight memory mechanism. It consists of:
- **Slow projections** ($\theta^{(\ell)}_K, \theta^{(\ell)}_V, \theta^{(\ell)}_Q, \theta^{(\ell)}_O$): These are learnable parameters that project input tokens into keys, values, and queries.
- **Fast-weight network** ($f_W^{(\ell)}$): This network's weights ($W$) are dynamically updated during inner-loop adaptation.

The TTT residual $\Delta\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_{\text{TTT}}$ is computed as $\theta^{(\ell)}_O \mathbf{f}_{W}^{(\ell)}(\theta^{(\ell)}_Q(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}))$, where $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}$ are the current video tokens. This structure allows the fast weights to store information from context tokens (human videos) and steer action generation through the WAM's shared visual-action dynamics.

#### 3.2. Human-Robot Meta-Training

This offline stage aims to calibrate the TTT branches so that human visual cues align with robot behaviors.
- **Data Synchronization:** A dataset of paired human video clips ($u_h$) and synchronized robot trajectories (actions and observations) is used. Synchronization is performed by matching the normalized phase ($t/T_r$) between robot and human episodes.
- **Inner-Loop Adaptation of Fast Weights:** For each training example, the fast weights $W^{(\ell)}$ for each TTT layer are initialized from learnable parameters $W^{(\ell)}_{\text{init}}$. These fast weights are then updated via inner-loop Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) for $N$ iterations, minimizing a combined human-side objective $L_{\text{adapt}}(W_i)$.
 - **Human Video Prediction Loss ($L^{\text{human}}_{\text{vg}}$):** This is the standard LDA video-prediction loss applied to the final video latents derived from the human video $u_h$.
 - **Key-Value Memory Reconstruction Loss ($L^{(\ell)}_{\text{KVM}}$):** For each TTT layer $\ell$, synchronized human tokens are projected into keys ($K^{(\ell)}_h$) and values ($V^{(\ell)}_h$) using the slow projections $\theta^{(\ell)}_K$ and $\theta^{(\ell)}_V$. This loss measures how well the fast-weight network reconstructs human values from human keys: $\frac{1}{BL_h d} \| \mathbf{f}_{W}^{(l)}_i(\mathbf{K}^{(\ell)}_h) - \mathbf{V}^{(\ell)}_h \|_2^2$. This term encourages the fast weights to encode the human-side information.
- **Outer Loss Optimization:** After the fast weights $W^{(\ell)}_N$ are adapted, they are used by the robot side. Robot video tokens are projected into queries ($Q^{(\ell)}_r = \theta^{(\ell)}_Q(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_r)$), and the adapted fast weights produce the residual $\Delta\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_{\text{TTT}}$. The outer training objective, $L_{\text{meta}} = L^{\text{robot}}_{\text{WAM}}$, is the standard WAM multitask loss (combining video diffusion and action diffusion targets) applied to the paired robot data. Gradients are backpropagated through the TTT residual and the inner fast-weight update to optimize the slow parameters: the WAM parameters ($\Theta_{\text{WAM}}$), the TTT slow projections ($\theta_{\{K,V,Q,O\}}$), and the fast-weight initializations ($W_{\text{init}}$). After each training example, the adapted fast weights are discarded and reinitialized.

#### 3.3. Test-Time Training from Human Video

During deployment, the WAM parameters ($\Theta_{\text{WAM}}$), TTT slow projections ($\theta$), and $W_{\text{init}}$ are *frozen*.
- **Input:** A small batch of unlabeled, action-free human videos ($B_h$) from the target domain or task.
- **Fast-Weight Adaptation:** The model operates in video-generation mode. Only the video-side TTT fast weights $W^{(\ell)}$ are optimized. This is done by running inner SGD for $N$ steps, minimizing an objective $L_{\text{TTT}}(W_i)$ that has the *same form* as $L_{\text{adapt}}$ from meta-training (i.e., human video prediction plus key-value memory reconstruction). No robot-side supervision or actions are required at this stage.
- **Robot Execution:** After adaptation, the fixed, adapted fast weights $W_N$ are used during robot rollout to steer action generation. The TTT residual $\Delta\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_{\text{TTT}}$ is computed using queries from the robot's observations and the adapted $W_N$, influencing the WAM's visual dynamics and subsequently its action output.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated WAM-TTT on real-robot manipulation across three embodiments (Unitree G1, Galbot gripper, Galbot sharpa) and nine manipulation tasks, under both standardized "Orig." (in-cubicle) and "New" (unseen household environments with combined out-of-distribution shifts) settings. Performance was measured as progress (%) over 25 trials using a partial-credit subgoal completion metric.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results (Main Comparisons)

- **Superior Performance in Unseen Environments (New setting):** WAM-TTT achieved an average progress of 46.2% across the nine tasks in the challenging "New" setting. This significantly outperformed all baselines:
 - LDA (pretrained WAM backbone, no human data): 32.5% (+13.7 percentage points)
 - WAM-COTRAIN (WAM fine-tuned with paired human data, no TTT): 25.3% (+20.9 pp)
 - EGOSCALE (VLA trained on egocentric human data): 15.0% (+31.2 pp)
 - π_0.5 (open-world-generalization VLA): 14.8% (+31.4 pp)
 - WAM-ICL (in-context human video conditioning): 7.1% (+39.1 pp)
- **Performance in Standardized Environments (Orig. setting):** WAM-TTT also led in the "Orig." setting, averaging 61.1%. It improved over the LDA backbone (50.2%, +10.9 pp) and WAM-ICL (48.4%, +12.7 pp). WAM-COTRAIN performed poorly (29.8%), indicating that simply mixing human data without an explicit alignment mechanism can degrade in-distribution performance.
- **Robustness to Distribution Shift:** Analysis of "Orig." to "New" performance retention showed WAM-TTT maintained a high meaningful retention ratio (0.76), losing 14.9 pp, while WAM-ICL experienced a catastrophic collapse (0.15 retention, lost 41.3 pp), highlighting the fragility of in-context conditioning under scene perturbations.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

- **Value of Test-Time Adaptation:** Removing TTT entirely ("w/o TTT") significantly reduced performance (e.g., Table Bussing dropped from 100.0% to 40.0%), confirming the importance of test-time human-video adaptation.
- **Importance of TTT Memory Structure:** Replacing TTT fast weights with generic parameter-efficient LoRA ("WAM-LoRA") resulted in poor performance (e.g., Table Bussing 30.0%), indicating that the specific TTT memory structure and its dedicated training are crucial.
- **Role of Meta-Training:** Removing the human-robot meta-training stage ("w/o Meta Training") severely degraded performance (e.g., Table Bussing 9.0%), emphasizing the necessity of explicitly aligning human visual cues with robot behaviors.
- **Contribution of Memory Reconstruction Loss:** Removing the key-value memory reconstruction loss ("w/o Memory Recon.") decreased performance (e.g., Table Bussing 66.7%), demonstrating the benefit of this structured objective for writing information into fast weights.

#### 4.3. Generalization Preservation

- WAM-TTT demonstrated strong performance under various perturbations (lighting, spatial shifts) on the "Deliver Drink" task, outperforming baselines. This suggests that the TTT mechanism adapts to specific tasks without undermining the foundation model’s pre-trained robustness.
- Experiments on direct generalization to unseen lab scenes (without in-scene human videos) showed that the meta-trained slow projections alone provided a useful skill prior, indicating that the calibration learned during meta-training generalizes to new visual statistics.
- Further tests across six axes of in-scene distribution shift (object, lighting, height, chassis, affordance, object-position generalization) demonstrated WAM-TTT's robustness. This robustness is attributed to the residual fast-weight TTT, which decouples human-side updates from the WAM's core parameters, preventing domain shift in human videos from corrupting the pre-trained policy.

#### 4.4. Data-Ratio and Model-Architecture Ablations

- **Data-Ratio:** Adding 100 paired human episodes to 100 robot episodes significantly improved average performance (+14.6 pp), demonstrating the marginal value of human data. At an iso-budget of 200 episodes, (100 robot, 100 human) performed comparably to (200 robot, 0 human), suggesting human data can effectively substitute for robot data. Performance saturated around 100 human episodes.
- **Model Architecture:** Ablations confirmed the critical role of the VLM backbone, its pre-training, and joint adaptation (unfrozen VLM) in the DiT for optimal performance.

#### 4.5. Action-Pseudolabel Ablation

- Adding a forward-dynamics (FD) loss based on retargeted pseudo-actions derived from human videos (using MediaPipe and MANO hand pose estimation) was uniformly detrimental, dropping average performance from 72.3% to 28.9%. This suggests that the noise and inaccuracies in current single-view hand tracking and motion retargeting pipelines make pseudo-action supervision unreliable for learning, and that relying on action-free video prediction is a more robust strategy for human-side supervision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

WAM-TTT presents a significant step towards more adaptable and reusable robot foundation models (RFMs) in real-world scenarios. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating an effective method for steering RFMs at deployment time using unannotated human videos, which directly addresses a critical limitation of current general-purpose robotic systems.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**
- **Efficient Deployment-Time Adaptation:** The framework allows for rapid adaptation of robot behavior to new task variants or user preferences using readily available human demonstrations. This bypasses the need for costly and time-consuming collection of additional robot demonstrations or full model fine-tuning, thereby increasing the practical utility and deployment speed of RFMs.
- **Preservation of Foundation Model Generalization:** By freezing the pre-trained World Action Model (WAM) and restricting adaptation to lightweight, fast-weight memory within the TTT branches, WAM-TTT effectively mitigates the risk of catastrophic forgetting. This ensures that the broad generalization abilities and extensive knowledge accumulated during RFM pretraining are preserved, leading to more robust and versatile systems.
- **Reduced Annotation Burden:** The method operates on raw, unlabeled human videos, eliminating the need for complex and error-prone human-side annotations such as hand poses, 3D motion, or retargeted trajectories. This simplifies the data acquisition process and makes human demonstrations a more accessible and scalable source of information for robot learning. The action-pseudolabel ablation explicitly supports this, showing that noisy pseudo-actions can be detrimental.
- **Novel Architectural Paradigm:** WAM-TTT introduces a "plug-and-play" TTT memory as an architectural component within WAMs. This concept suggests a future direction for RFM design, where models could incorporate explicit "adaptation seats" that practitioners can drive with various forms of side information, making foundation models more customizable and extensible.
- **Bridging the Embodiment Gap:** Through a meta-training stage that aligns human demonstrations with robot behaviors using a key-value memory reconstruction objective, the framework effectively translates human intent into robot actions without direct imitation or complex cross-embodiment mapping, which is a longstanding challenge in imitation learning.

**Limitations:**
- **Phase Alignment Sensitivity:** The meta-training phase relies on accurate synchronization between human and robot episodes. Misaligned demonstrations can degrade the inner adaptation signal, potentially limiting performance in scenarios where precise synchronization is difficult to achieve.
- **Bounded Adaptability:** The deployment-time adaptation is constrained by the expressiveness of the fast-weight network and the fixed slow projections from meta-training. Large shifts between the meta-training distribution and deployment tasks might reduce the effectiveness of adaptation, and the empirical boundaries of this limitation are not fully characterized.
- **Information Input Modality:** The current deployment-time interface exclusively uses egocentric human RGB frames. The framework does not yet leverage other potentially rich cues like hand-pose, contact information, or 3D scene geometry, which could offer additional avenues for robust skill transfer, though the action-pseudolabel ablation indicated that current methods for extracting such cues may be too noisy.

**Future Directions:**
- Future research could focus on developing more robust and automated techniques for phase alignment, particularly in unconstrained environments.
- Empirical studies to characterize the limits of adaptation and degradation under severe domain shifts would be valuable for understanding the method's practical applicability.
- Exploring the integration of other auxiliary modalities, beyond visual data, to drive parallel fast-weight branches could enhance the versatility of the TTT framework.
- Further development of "adaptation seats" within RFM backbones could lead to more modular and flexible architectures capable of incorporating diverse forms of deployment-time information for highly customizable robot behaviors.
