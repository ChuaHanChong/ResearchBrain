# MCU: An Evaluation Framework for Open-Ended Game Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> Minecraft Universe (MCU) introduces a comprehensive evaluation framework for open-ended AI agents in Minecraft, providing a diverse set of over 3,400 atomic tasks and an automated VLM-based evaluation system (AutoEval). AutoEval achieves over 90% agreement with human judgments across multiple performance dimensions, demonstrating a scalable and efficient method for assessing agent generalizability.

## Problem

- Existing Minecraft AI benchmarks suffer from insufficient task diversity, low task quality, and a lack of scalable automatic evaluation mechanisms.
- Evaluating open-ended agents is challenging due to the absence of clear success signals and reliance on costly, non-scalable human judgment.
- The research landscape lacks a unified and standardized benchmark for comparing agent generalizability across complex, dynamic virtual environments.

## Method

- Introducing Minecraft Universe (**MCU**), a framework featuring ==3,452 atomic tasks== across 11 categories, dynamically configured by an ==LLM-powered pipeline== to enhance intra-task and inter-task diversity.
- Developing ==AutoEval==, a ==VLM-based multi-dimensional automated evaluation system==, which assesses agent performance from recorded video trajectories against task-specific criteria generated by an LLM.
- Implementing a robust ==task configuration generation pipeline== that incorporates LLM self-verification and simulator validation to ensure task quality, solvability, and diverse environmental conditions.

## Results

- AutoEval demonstrated an **84.0%** F1 score in comparative evaluations against human judgments and an average agreement rate of **91.5%** with human assessments across six evaluation dimensions, proving **8.1x** more cost-effective and **4.8x** more efficient than human labeling.
- Evaluations of foundation agents (e.g., VPT, STEVE-I, GROOT) using MCU revealed significant performance degradation for compositional tasks and in 'hard' environmental modes, highlighting challenges in inter-task and intra-task generalization.
- Agents consistently showed lower performance in subjective dimensions like 'Creativity,' 'Error Recognition,' and 'Efficiency' across diverse tasks and difficulty levels, indicating areas for future AI development.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An LLM-driven task configuration generator, combined with self-reflection and simulator validation, can effectively create diverse, high-quality, and configurable open-ended tasks in complex game environments.
> - VLM-based automated evaluation (AutoEval) achieves high alignment with human judgment (over 90% agreement) for multi-dimensional assessment of agent performance in open-ended tasks, offering substantial cost and time efficiency.
> - Current state-of-the-art agents struggle with generalizability across varied task conditions (intra-task diversity) and exhibit specific limitations in creative problem-solving, error recognition, and overall efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of AI agents capable of operating in dynamic, open-world environments to solve diverse tasks represents a long-standing challenge in Artificial Intelligence. Such environments require agents to exhibit strong generalizability, robustness, and the ability to handle complex, long-horizon tasks. Games, particularly open-world video games like Minecraft, have emerged as prominent platforms for studying AI agents. These environments offer programmable simulators that present real-world challenges within a controlled, safe, and efficient testing ground, supporting long-horizon planning and diverse interactions. This contrasts with other digital environments like web/app interfaces or mobile platforms, and offers greater control complexity compared to Atari-like environments, while being more accessible than physical robotics.

Previous research in Minecraft has explored tasks such as navigation, block placement, combat, and resource gathering. More recent efforts have focused on the full human action space, encompassing complex activities like inventory management and crafting. However, existing benchmarks for Minecraft agents have limitations that impede comprehensive evaluation of agent generalizability. These include insufficient task diversity, low task quality (e.g., intractable or repetitive tasks), and a lack of scalable automatic evaluation mechanisms, often relying on human judgment or manually designed metrics. This has led to a fragmented evaluation landscape where agents are often assessed on distinct, non-unified task sets.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing Minecraft Universe (MCU), a comprehensive evaluation framework designed to provide a standardized and scalable benchmark for open-ended game agents. MCU aims to advance the field by offering a highly diverse and high-quality task set, coupled with an automated evaluation system, thereby enabling more robust assessment of agent capabilities and facilitating the development of more generalist AI. It leverages recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) for both task generation and evaluation, aligning with the "LLM-as-a-Judge" paradigm to enhance consistency and scalability compared to traditional human-intensive evaluation methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish Minecraft Universe (MCU), a comprehensive and scalable evaluation framework for open-ended AI agents operating within the Minecraft environment. This framework is designed to overcome the limitations of existing benchmarks and drive progress in developing agents capable of generalizable intelligence in complex, dynamic virtual worlds.

The motivation for developing MCU is multifaceted:

- **Addressing Scalability Limitations in Evaluation:** Evaluating open-ended agents is inherently difficult due to the lack of clear success signals and the reliance on human evaluation or manually designed metrics, which are not scalable for a large number of diverse tasks and agent trajectories. There is a need for an automated system that can reliably assess agent performance.
- **Enhancing Generalizability Assessment:** The ultimate goal of AI agent development is to create systems that can robustly solve tasks across drastically different situations in real-world, open-ended environments. Existing benchmarks often lack the diversity and complexity required to thoroughly test an agent's generalizability across a broad spectrum of skills and environmental conditions.
- **Improving Task Quality and Diversity:** Prior Minecraft benchmarks have been identified as having issues with low task quality (e.g., infeasible or extremely difficult tasks), insufficient diversity (e.g., repetitive tasks), and a lack of mechanisms to introduce variability into task conditions. A robust benchmark requires a large, high-quality, and diverse set of tasks that accurately mirror real gameplay scenarios and challenge various agent capabilities.
- **Establishing a Unified Benchmark:** The current landscape of Minecraft agent research suffers from a lack of a unified evaluation standard, where different agents are often evaluated on their distinct, proprietary task sets. This fragmentation hinders direct comparison and consistent progress tracking across the field. A standardized benchmark is crucial for collaborative advancement.
- **Creating Enduring Challenges:** To remain relevant as a benchmark over time, the evaluation framework must continuously provide new and increasingly complex challenges that push the boundaries of AI capabilities. This necessitates mechanisms for generating novel tasks with varying difficulty levels.

To address these motivations, MCU was designed based on four key principles:

1. **High Task Diversity:**
 - **Intra-task diversity:** An LLM-based generator dynamically configures task initialization conditions (biome, weather, player states) to introduce variability within the same atomic task, simulating real gameplay.
 - **Inter-task diversity:** A collection of 3,452 atomic tasks, spanning 11 major categories and 41 subcategories (e.g., precise control, logical reasoning, knowledge application), provides a broad range of challenges. These atomic tasks can be composed combinatorially to create an exponential number of new, complex tasks.
2. **High Task Quality:** Tasks are rigorously filtered from multiple data sources to ensure solvability and adherence to high-quality standards. The LLM-based task configuration generator incorporates soft constraints and a self-reflection refinement mechanism, validated by the Minecraft simulator, to prevent intractable or erroneous task instantiations.
3. **Automated Evaluation (AutoEval):** A VLM-based system is introduced to provide scalable, multi-dimensional assessment of open-ended tasks. AutoEval aims to align closely with human judgments and offers fine-grained insights beyond binary success rates by evaluating across dimensions such as task progress, action control, and creative attempts.
4. **Enduring Challenges:** The framework supports tasks of varying difficulty and enables the composition of atomic tasks into more intricate scenarios, ensuring that MCU remains a long-term benchmark for future agent development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Minecraft Universe (MCU) framework integrates several components to achieve its objectives of scalable task generation and automated evaluation for open-ended game agents.

#### 3.1. Minecraft Environment

The chosen environment is an unmodified Minecraft version 1.16.5, running in survival mode. Agents interact with the environment using mouse and keyboard inputs, receiving observations as 640x360 RGB images. This environment is selected for its vast state space, which includes expansive maps, numerous block types (approx. 500), diverse entities (30+ mob types, 30+ animal types, villagers with professions/levels), and complex inventory states (36 slots, 500+ item types). The paper estimates a final state space of approximately 10^10^20, far exceeding the number of atoms in the universe, providing immense capacity for generalizability testing. Minecraft also supports high complexity tasks involving over 20 sub-goals and long-horizon planning, as well as open-endedness for creative challenges. Its dynamic nature, with day-night cycles and emergent entities, requires adaptive agent behaviors.

#### 3.2. Integration with MineStudio

MCU is built upon MineStudio, an open-source software package designed for Minecraft AI agent development. MineStudio provides APIs for environment initialization and agent interaction. MCU leverages MineStudio’s `CommandsCallback`, `SummonMobsCallback`, and `FastResetCallback` for task environment setup, and `RecordCallback` for consistently formatting agent trajectories into video, enabling model-agnostic evaluation. `RewardsCallback` is used for user-defined metrics, including AutoEval.

#### 3.3. Atomic Tasks: Fundamental Testing Units

The concept of "atomic tasks" is central to MCU. An atomic task `T` is defined solely by its goal `g`, independent of the specific methods, tools, or environmental conditions. This allows for isolated evaluation of specific agent capabilities. For example, "mine stone" is an atomic task, but its instantiations could vary (e.g., "mine stone with a wooden pickaxe," "mine stone during a rainy day").

MCU comprises 3,452 atomic tasks, represented as textual descriptions, categorized into 11 major and 41 sub-categories. These tasks are collected from four sources:
- **Existing Benchmarks:** Tasks distilled from MineDojo and SkillForge, with deduplication and filtering of overly complex or unsolvable tasks.
- **Minecraft Wiki:** Executable tasks extracted from the official Minecraft Wiki, particularly the Advancement page.
- **In-Game Data Synthesis:** Systematic generation of tasks based on item properties from the Minecraft simulator (e.g., "craft item X if X is craftable").
- **LLM and Expert Brainstorming:** Creative and open-ended tasks generated in collaboration with human experts and LLMs.

Atomic tasks can also be combined to form more complex tasks using logical operators (AND, OR) and constraints (when, where, how), enabling exploration of a vast combinatorial task space.

#### 3.4. Task Configuration Generation

To execute atomic or compositional tasks, the environment must be configured with specific initial conditions (e.g., spawn biomes, player states, inventories, surrounding mobs). This process, formalized as sampling an initial state `s0` from a task-specific distribution `P(s0|T)`, is handled by a scalable, LLM-powered pipeline using GPT-4o.

- **Config Generator:** GPT-4o is prompted with a task description and few-shot examples to generate executable `custom_init_commands` for MineStudio callbacks. This includes necessary resources (e.g., diamond ores and an iron pickaxe for "mine diamonds with an iron pickaxe") and random factors to enhance diversity (e.g., ore placement). Soft constraints are embedded in prompts to mitigate LLM limitations regarding game rules or numerical insensitivity.
- **Verification:** A two-stage verification mechanism ensures the quality and validity of generated configurations:
 1. **LLM Self-Verification (Reflxion):** The LLM re-validates its generated configuration. If errors are detected (e.g., summoning cows for a "hunt sheep" task), it regenerates the configuration.
 2. **MineStudio Simulator Validation:** The configuration is executed in the MineStudio Simulator to ensure executability. Error logs from the simulator guide further regeneration if needed.

#### 3.5. Automatic Evaluation (AutoEval)

AutoEval is a VLM-based multi-dimensional evaluation framework designed to score agent performance from recorded video trajectories.

- **Criteria Generation:** To ensure objective and detailed assessment, GPT-4o first generates task-specific criteria for six predefined dimensions:
 1. **Task Progress:** Critical steps and factors for task completion.
 2. **Action Control:** Avoidance of unrelated or unnecessary actions.
 3. **Material Usage:** Selection and application of materials.
 4. **Task Efficiency:** Minimizing repetitions and optimizing strategies.
 5. **Error Recognition:** Ability to identify and correct mistakes.
 6. **Creative Attempts:** Recognition of innovative approaches.
- **Scoring with Criteria:** Agent rollout trajectories, recorded as videos, are sampled (one frame every 30 frames). These sampled frames, along with the task-specific criteria, are fed into a VLM (GPT-4o with vision). The VLM assesses each dimension, providing supporting evidence and explanations, and assigns a score from five intervals: very poor (0), poor (0.25), fair (0.5), good (0.75), and excellent (1).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents empirical results validating AutoEval and assessing the capabilities of state-of-the-art agents using the MCU framework.

#### 4.1. Validity of Automatic Evaluation (AutoEval)

To confirm the reliability of AutoEval, the system's assessments were compared against human annotations in two settings:

- **Comparative Evaluation:**
 - Using a dataset of 236 trajectory pairs from 60 tasks (agent rollouts and human gameplay), participants voted on which of two trajectories was better.
 - AutoEval (GPT-4o) achieved an average F1 score of **84.0%** in predicting the better human-annotated trajectory across different task categories. This significantly outperformed the MineCLIP baseline, which scored **34.6%**.
 - Across all six evaluation dimensions, AutoEval demonstrated an average agreement rate of **91.5%** with human assessments, indicating its effectiveness in multi-dimensional evaluations. Performance was slightly reduced for intricate tasks requiring precise attention to detail.
- **Absolute Rating:**
 - For 227 individual trajectory ratings across 50 distinct tasks, AutoEval showed strong positive correlations with human ratings.
 - Pearson and Kendall correlation coefficients were calculated between AutoEval and human ratings. Objective dimensions, such as "Task Progress," exhibited a high Pearson correlation of **0.78**. More subjective dimensions, like "Creative Attempts," showed a lower correlation of **0.63**.
 - Inter-rater agreement among human evaluators for the same trajectory also mirrored this pattern, with "Task Progress" having a Pearson correlation of 0.83 and "Creative Attempts" a correlation of 0.69.

These results suggest that AutoEval is a robust and scalable method for evaluating open-ended tasks, achieving substantial alignment with human judgment while being more cost-effective (8.1x) and efficient (4.8x) than human labeling.

#### 4.2. Evaluating Existing Agents with MCU

The study evaluated four foundation agents: VPT (BC), VPT (RL), STEVE-I, and GROOT, all compatible with MineStudio. Experiments were conducted on a subset of 30 atomic and 5 compositional tasks for illustrative purposes, with a more extensive evaluation of 150 tasks deferred to the appendix.

- **Inter-task Generalization:**
 - Agents often struggled to reliably complete tasks across diverse categories (e.g., combat, building). However, AutoEval's "task progress" metric, a process-supervised score, indicated partial progress even in cases of non-full completion, providing more granular insights than a binary success rate.
 - VPT (RL), fine-tuned for a specific objective (obtaining diamonds), performed well on tasks aligned with this objective (e.g., crafting a crafting table from scratch) but significantly underperformed on unrelated tasks, underscoring the importance of inter-task diversity for assessing true generalizability.
 - Generalist agents like GROOT and Steve-I demonstrated better open-endedness but still faced difficulties with specific tasks (e.g., crafting particular items).
 - Compositional tasks generally yielded lower task progress scores than atomic tasks, highlighting the persistent challenge of multi-step, complex objectives.
 - Multi-dimensional performance analysis (Figure 1c) revealed that current agents exhibit notable limitations in "Creativity," "Error Recognition," and "Efficiency."

- **Intra-task Generalization:**
 - For GROOT, selected tasks were evaluated in both "simple" and "hard" modes, which introduced slight changes in task situations or environmental complexity.
 - Performance, particularly in "Task Progress" and "Action Control," significantly degraded in hard mode. For example, the "sleep in bed" task, simple in direct conditions, became challenging when the bed was inside a house with distractors, leading to agent failures (e.g., interacting with a chest, leaving the house). This indicates that the agent did not learn the skill robustly, a phenomenon not widely reported previously.
 - Some tasks (e.g., "mine iron ore," "craft to cake") showed relatively stable performance across difficulty levels, suggesting that certain task categories are more sensitive to environmental variations than others.
 - Overall, enhancing intra-task diversity proved beneficial for testing robust generalization.

#### 4.3. Large-Scale Inference and MCU-Turbo Baselines (Appendix E & F)

A larger evaluation of 90 atomic tasks (in hard mode) and 60 compositional tasks confirmed the findings. STEVE-1 showed performance decline in compositional tasks, attributed to its reliance on short prompts and resulting out-of-distribution (OOD) issues with longer or unseen instructions. Low performance in creativity and error recognition remained consistent across all agents for hard atomic and compositional tasks. The MCU-Turbo benchmark, a curated subset of 100 tasks with structured difficulty settings, was introduced to provide standardized baselines. It demonstrated that performance consistently declines in hard mode, and creative tasks are significantly harder than programmatic tasks for agents (e.g., a 15.8% drop in task progress for STEVE-1 on creative tasks).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Minecraft Universe (MCU) carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of AI agent development, particularly for open-ended environments.

Firstly, MCU addresses a critical gap in the current research landscape by providing a **scalable, comprehensive, and high-quality evaluation framework** for open-ended game agents. Existing benchmarks in Minecraft have been limited by insufficient task diversity, low task quality, and the lack of automated evaluation. MCU's expansive collection of 3,452 atomic tasks, which can be dynamically composed, ensures an ever-expanding and challenging task space, thereby overcoming these limitations. This task diversity, encompassing both inter-task and intra-task variability, is crucial for rigorously testing the generalizability of AI agents.

Secondly, the **Automated Evaluation (AutoEval) system** is a key innovation. Its demonstrated high alignment with human judgment (over 90% agreement) and its multi-dimensional assessment capabilities provide a reliable and efficient alternative to labor-intensive human annotations. This scalability is expected to accelerate research by enabling more frequent and extensive evaluation cycles, allowing researchers to iterate on agent designs more rapidly. The multi-dimensional scoring (e.g., Task Progress, Action Control, Creativity) offers fine-grained insights into agent behaviors, moving beyond simple success rates to understand *how* an agent performs, which is vital for complex, open-ended tasks.

Thirdly, the empirical results derived from evaluating state-of-the-art foundation agents with MCU provide **clear directions for future research**. The findings reveal that even advanced agents struggle with the increasing diversity and complexity of tasks, particularly in areas like "Creativity," "Error Recognition," and "Efficiency." The observation that agents may not learn skills robustly even for simple tasks when confronted with slight environmental variations (intra-task generalization challenges) highlights a fundamental area for improvement in current AI models. These insights can guide the development of new architectures and training methodologies focused on building more robust, adaptive, and genuinely generalist agents.

Fourthly, the release of MCU, and specifically the curated subset MCU-Turbo, has the potential to **standardize benchmarking** in the Minecraft agent community. A unified benchmark facilitates direct comparison of different agent designs, fostering collaborative progress and providing a common ground for assessing advancements. This standardization can help prevent fragmented evaluations where agents are tested on disparate task sets, ultimately leading to more transparent and impactful research outcomes.

Finally, the work contributes to the broader understanding of **adaptive and autonomous artificial intelligence** in dynamic environments. By pushing the boundaries of what agents can achieve in a highly complex and open-ended virtual world like Minecraft, this research indirectly influences other domains requiring similar AI capabilities, such as robotics, real-world decision-making, and interactive entertainment. The framework's ability to generate novel and challenging scenarios ensures that MCU will remain a relevant and enduring benchmark as AI capabilities continue to evolve.
