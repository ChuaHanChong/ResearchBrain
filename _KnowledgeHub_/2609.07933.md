# SPOT: Spatial Perception-Oriented Long-Horizon Humanoid Teleoperation

> [!summary] Summary
> SPOT is a VR teleoperation system for long-horizon humanoid loco-manipulation that widens the operator's perceptual horizon. A robot-mounted binocular fisheye camera is rendered on a virtual hemisphere, so head rotation inspects the wide stereo view without commanding the robot, while IMU-driven rotation compensation stabilizes it. Across four perception-critical tasks it cuts completion, search, and recovery times versus exocentric and egocentric baselines.

## Problem

- Prior humanoid teleoperation work optimizes motion retargeting and whole-body control, treating egocentric video as passive feedback rather than an explicit interface design objective.
- Narrow onboard camera fields of view miss peripheral events, hide one manipulator during bimanual work, and lose objects that fall or appear outside the view.
- Head-coupled active cameras entangle looking around with robot actuation, and robot-mounted views rotate during locomotion, forcing costly viewpoint realignment.

## Method

- ==Egocentric binocular view==: a robot-mounted fisheye stereo stream is sampled per eye via ==equisolid projection== and rendered on a hemispherical display surface, preserving wide peripheral coverage and disparity.
- ==Viewpoint-decoupled free-looking== plus ==IMU-driven visual stabilization== that rotates the display by the relative camera orientation, so head motion only moves gaze and robot rotation does not shift the visual frame.
- Teleoperation pipeline: a streaming network estimates torso orientation from headset and controller poses, then ==Cartesian retargeting== with arm-length rescaling feeds a ==target-conditioned control policy== trained in simulation.

## Results

- Across **10** operators and **20** trials per task, SPOT cut drop-recovery completion to **19.69 s** versus **25.73 s** exocentric and **32.12 s** egocentric, with **98%** success.
- Visual search time fell to **0.11 s** versus **0.96 s** egocentric; light-switch alignment time dropped to **0.39 s** versus **1.52 s**, and exocentric succeeded only **8%** of the time.
- Ablations show wide field of view matters most for bimanual retrieval (**9.76 s** to **14.06 s**) and stabilization most for light switch (**11.54 s** to **18.43 s**); users rated comfort **4.0** versus **1.4** for egocentric.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perception transfer is a distinct bottleneck from motion transfer: interface design that preserves spatial awareness can improve teleoperation throughput without touching the controller.
> - Decoupling gaze from actuation is the cheap win, letting operators inspect a wide passive view removes the head-versus-robot conflict without an actuated neck.
> - Interface components address separable failure modes: field of view fixes multi-object awareness, stereo fixes depth judgment, stabilization fixes reorientation cost after turning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose humanoid robots relies significantly on high-quality demonstration data. Teleoperation systems are a common method for collecting such data, particularly for complex tasks involving mobility, bimanual interaction, recovery from unexpected events, and precise spatial alignment in long-horizon loco-manipulation scenarios.

Prior research in humanoid teleoperation has made substantial advancements in the technical aspects of motion transfer. This includes progress in motion tracking, retargeting human motion to robot motion, and developing whole-body control strategies. Many contemporary systems leverage Virtual Reality (VR) for its accessibility and ability to capture operator motion, facilitating an intuitive interface for controlling humanoid robots.

However, the authors identify a critical limitation in existing teleoperation interfaces: a constrained "perceptual horizon." This term describes the extent to which an operator can reliably maintain task-relevant spatial awareness over time, including object locations, environmental context, and the robot's pose and limb configurations. Current systems, whether exocentric or egocentric, present several challenges in this regard:
- **Exocentric interfaces**, which provide a third-person external view of the robot, often suffer from weakened depth perception and difficulties when the operator changes their viewing angle relative to the robot.
- **Conventional egocentric interfaces**, utilizing robot-mounted cameras, frequently provide narrow views that may miss peripheral events crucial for task execution. These robot-mounted cameras can also experience instability during robot locomotion, balancing, or recovery actions, making it challenging for the operator to maintain a consistent spatial reference. Furthermore, in many egocentric systems, the operator's head movements, intended for visual exploration, are coupled to the robot's head, camera, or torso movements, potentially interfering with robot actuation commands.

The current work distinguishes itself by explicitly addressing the problem of the limited perceptual horizon. Unlike systems that primarily optimize motion retargeting and treat visual feedback as a passive element, this research treats spatial perception as a primary objective in interface design. It proposes that improving the transfer of spatial perception from the robot to the human operator is a crucial, yet often overlooked, complement to accurate motion transfer for scalable and efficient long-horizon humanoid teleoperation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a VR teleoperation system, named SPOT, that significantly enhances the operator's spatial perception during long-horizon humanoid loco-manipulation tasks. This enhancement aims to facilitate the collection of high-quality demonstration data, thereby addressing a central bottleneck in training general-purpose humanoid robots.

The motivation for SPOT stems from several identified deficiencies in existing humanoid teleoperation systems:
- **Need for Sustained Spatial Awareness**: As humanoid robots are tasked with increasingly complex and long-duration operations that involve both locomotion and manipulation, operators require a continuous and comprehensive understanding of the robot's surroundings, the precise locations of objects, the robot's own configuration, and its relative pose within the environment. This sustained spatial awareness, termed the "operator’s perceptual horizon," is crucial for efficient and convenient task execution.
- **Limitations of Current Visual Feedback Paradigms**:
 - **Narrow Field of View**: Many egocentric camera setups offer a restricted field of view, causing important peripheral events (e.g., objects falling, robot limbs moving into the workspace) to be missed, thereby shortening the perceptual horizon.
 - **Visual Instability**: Robot-mounted cameras, when subjected to the robot's inherent movements like locomotion, balancing, or recovery from perturbations, transmit unstable visual feedback. This instability makes it difficult for operators to maintain a stable mental model of the environment and the robot's state.
 - **Coupled Viewpoint Control**: In systems where an operator's head movements directly control the robot's head or camera, the act of visually exploring the environment (looking around) can inadvertently trigger robot movements. This coupling interferes with precise control and can be counterproductive, particularly when the robot's head movement is not required for the task.
- **Consequences of Limited Perception**: A restricted perceptual horizon leads to several operational inefficiencies, including:
 - Increased time spent searching for objects.
 - Slower recovery from unexpected events.
 - Greater difficulty in achieving precise spatial alignment for manipulation tasks.

To address these issues, SPOT's design is specifically motivated by the goal of extending the operator’s perceptual horizon. This involves providing visual feedback that is wide, stable, and actively inspectable, while simultaneously decoupling visual exploration from robot actuation. By doing so, the system aims to improve task efficiency, accuracy, and recovery speed, thereby contributing to more effective and user-friendly data collection for complex humanoid behaviors. The work also aims to formally conceptualize the "perceptual horizon" as a critical factor complementary to motion transfer in the context of humanoid teleoperation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPOT integrates a robot-centric immersive perception interface with a VR-based teleoperation pipeline. The core principle of SPOT's methodology is to enhance the transfer of spatial perception from the robot to the human operator.

#### 3.1. Immersive Perception Interface

The perception interface is designed to provide operators with a wide, stable, and actively inspectable view of the robot's environment:
- **Egocentric Binocular View**:
 - A robot-mounted binocular fisheye camera captures a wide-field stereo observation of the environment.
 - This camera stream is transmitted to a VR headset, where it is rendered onto a virtual hemispherical display surface.
 - An equisolid projection model is used to accurately map viewing directions within the virtual hemisphere to the corresponding pixels in the fisheye images, preserving wide peripheral coverage.
 - The left and right halves of the stereo image are sampled independently for each eye, maintaining binocular disparity, which is critical for accurate depth perception, especially for objects or robot limbs entering the operator's peripheral vision.
- **Viewpoint-Decoupled Observation**:
 - Unlike conventional egocentric systems where head movements control the robot's camera, SPOT renders the egocentric stereo observation onto a virtual hemisphere that surrounds the operator.
 - This design allows the operator to rotate their head naturally to inspect different parts of the wide-field observation *within the virtual environment*, without commanding any corresponding movement of the robot's physical camera, head, or torso.
 - This decoupling ensures that visual exploration does not interfere with robot actuation, providing a more flexible and intuitive control experience, particularly for robots not equipped with actuated necks.
- **Visual Stabilization**:
 - To counteract visual disturbances caused by robot motion (locomotion, balancing, recovery), SPOT implements visual stabilization.
 - The rendered observation is dynamically rotated to compensate for the physical camera's rotational movements. This is achieved by applying a transformation based on the relative camera motion from an initial orientation.
 - In the implementation, a lightweight Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) attached to the stereo camera provides the necessary orientation estimates for driving this stabilization transform.
 - The result is a visually stable scene for the operator, providing a consistent spatial reference despite robot movements.

#### 3.2. Teleoperation Pipeline

The teleoperation pipeline translates human motion into robot control targets:
- **Human Motion Acquisition**:
 - Commands are acquired using an OpenXR-compatible VR headset and two handheld controllers (tested with Meta Quest and PICO).
 - The system queries the tracked poses (position and orientation) of the headset and both controllers at a frequency of 50-90 Hz.
 - Additional inputs include 2D joystick movements for locomotion commands and button states, with controller triggers directly mapped to robot gripper commands.
 - These signals are serialized and streamed via UDP to the robot control system.
- **Upper-body Estimation**:
 - To prevent the operator's free head motion (for visual exploration) from directly controlling the robot's torso, a lightweight streaming neural network is used.
 - This network estimates the operator's torso orientation from a short historical sequence of the three tracked VR poses (headset and two controllers). It is trained using VR motion sequences paired with torso orientations recorded by a body-mounted IMU.
 - The estimated torso orientation, combined with fixed headset-to-neck and neck-to-torso offsets, yields the robot's torso position, resulting in sparse upper-body targets (estimated torso and two hands).
- **Cartesian Retargeting**:
 - Following the ExtremControl framework, the estimated human upper-body poses (torso and two wrists) are directly mapped to robot-side SE(3) Cartesian targets. This approach avoids the need for online inverse kinematics or joint-space retargeting.
 - A one-shot neutral-pose calibration establishes initial heading alignment, rotational offsets between controllers and wrists, and human-to-robot arm length ratios.
 - During runtime, each hand target is expressed relative to the torso frame and scaled according to the pre-calibrated arm-length ratios.
- **Target-conditioned Control Policy**:
 - A target-conditioned control policy is trained in simulation to track the generated Cartesian hand targets and locomotion commands.
 - The policy takes robot proprioceptive data, target commands, and recent action history as input.
 - Training involves standard motion-tracking objectives and domain randomization techniques to ensure robustness during real-world deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SPOT involved a suite of perception-critical humanoid loco-manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against exocentric and conventional egocentric teleoperation baselines. Ten operators (three experienced, seven novice) participated, completing 20 trials per task for each interface.

**Quantitative Evaluation Across Four Tasks (Table 1):**
1. **Unexpected Drop and Recovery**: SPOT demonstrated superior efficiency, with an average completion time of 19.69 ± 4.04 seconds and a recovery time of 11.12 ± 3.69 seconds. This was significantly faster than both the Exocentric (25.73 ± 5.48 s completion, 15.07 ± 7.80 s recovery) and Conventional Egocentric (32.12 ± 10.03 s completion, 20.28 ± 8.06 s recovery) baselines. Success rates were high for SPOT (98%) and Conventional Egocentric (96.5%), while Exocentric was slightly lower (79.5%).
2. **Peripheral Object Retrieval**: SPOT achieved the fastest completion time (7.43 ± 1.49 s) and remarkably low visual search time (0.11 ± 0.03 s). This compared favorably to Exocentric (9.18 ± 2.68 s, 0.00 ± 0.00 s search, noting this baseline doesn't explicitly involve search in the same manner as egocentric views) and Conventional Egocentric (11.16 ± 2.04 s, 0.96 ± 0.29 s search), highlighting SPOT's ability to quickly re-localize out-of-view objects. All methods showed high success rates (99-100%).
3. **Multi-object Bimanual Retrieval**: SPOT facilitated faster task completion (9.76 ± 2.82 s) and required fewer grasp attempts (2.11 ± 0.33) than Exocentric (13.64 ± 4.47 s, 3.11 ± 0.74 attempts) and Conventional Egocentric (15.45 ± 3.67 s, 2.56 ± 0.58 attempts). This suggests improved bimanual coordination and accuracy.
4. **Light Switch**: SPOT significantly reduced completion time (11.54 ± 3.39 s) and viewpoint realignment time (0.39 ± 0.08 s), achieving a 100% success rate. The Exocentric baseline failed frequently (8% success rate), and Conventional Egocentric was slower (18.15 ± 4.45 s completion, 1.52 ± 0.41 s alignment).

**Qualitative Observations:**
- **Awareness of Unexpected Events and Out-of-View Objects**: SPOT's wide-field immersive interface enabled operators to rapidly re-localize dropped or peripheral objects, maintaining robot-centered spatial consistency. This contrasts with conventional egocentric views where objects leaving the narrow field of view required additional search time. The system also demonstrated real-time tracking of dynamic objects.
- **Bimanual Spatial Awareness**: SPOT allowed for continuous awareness of both manipulators and nearby objects, leading to more efficient bimanual interaction. Conventional egocentric interfaces often required operators to sequentially switch focus between manipulators due to limited visibility. Qualitative examples included bimanual rope knotting and toy sword insertion, demonstrating sustained awareness during complex coordinated actions.
- **Spatial Consistency Across Locomotion and Manipulation**: SPOT's decoupled viewpoint and visual stabilization maintained a stable visual reference frame, preventing abrupt viewpoint changes during robot locomotion or turning. This minimized viewpoint realignment time for operators, a significant advantage over conventional egocentric systems where the visual frame rotates with the robot. The exocentric baseline often led to operators losing awareness of the robot after reorientation, resulting in task failures.

**Ablation Study (Appendix A.1):**
An ablation study confirmed the contribution of each design component:
- **w/o Stereo**: Removing binocular rendering increased completion times in bimanual and light switch tasks, indicating the importance of depth perception.
- **w/o Wide FoV**: Restricting the field of view led to the largest performance degradation, especially in bimanual tasks, as operators lost visibility of objects or manipulators.
- **w/o Stabilization**: Removing visual stabilization significantly impacted the light switch task, where robot turning continuously altered the visual reference, requiring more viewpoint corrections.

**User Study (Appendix A.2):**
A user study with 10 participants on the Multi-object Bimanual Retrieval task showed that SPOT received the highest ratings for Visual Perception (3.4/5) and Manipulation Ease (3.1/5). While Exocentric teleoperation scored highest in Comfort (4.8/5) due to not requiring immersive head-mounted viewing, SPOT was rated substantially more comfortable (4.0/5) than Conventional Egocentric (1.4/5), suggesting that stabilization improves the subjective experience of immersive teleoperation.

**Experienced and Novice Operators (Appendix A.3):**
SPOT maintained competitive performance across both experienced and novice participants, generally achieving the lowest completion times in most tasks for both groups.

In summary, the results indicate that SPOT's design, focused on extending the operator's perceptual horizon through a wide-field, stereoscopic, stabilized, and viewpoint-decoupled robot-centric interface, improves task efficiency, accuracy, and recovery speed in perception-critical humanoid teleoperation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper carries several significant implications for the field of robotics and teleoperation:

Firstly, the explicit formulation of the "operator's perceptual horizon" as a critical bottleneck in long-horizon humanoid teleoperation represents a notable conceptual contribution. This highlights that effective teleoperation requires more than accurate motion transfer; it necessitates sustained and task-relevant spatial awareness. This perspective can guide future research towards a more holistic understanding of human-robot interaction in teleoperation.

Secondly, the introduction of SPOT provides a principled design for VR-based teleoperation interfaces that prioritizes spatial perception. The system's key innovations—a wide-field stereoscopic robot-centric view, viewpoint-decoupled free-looking, and visual stabilization—address long-standing challenges in providing comprehensive and stable visual feedback to operators. This design demonstrates an effective approach to enhancing an operator's ability to understand, search, and maintain spatial context within complex robot environments.

Thirdly, the demonstrated improvements in task efficiency, accuracy, and recovery speed for perception-critical tasks suggest a direct impact on the quality and scalability of demonstration data collection for humanoid robots. By enabling operators to perform complex loco-manipulation tasks with greater ease and reliability, SPOT can facilitate the generation of higher-quality training data, which is essential for advancing machine learning-based control policies for general-purpose humanoids. This directly addresses the "central bottleneck" in training such robots.

Finally, the system's focus on user-friendliness through features like viewpoint decoupling and visual stabilization can potentially broaden the accessibility and appeal of humanoid teleoperation. Reduced cognitive load and improved comfort could allow a wider range of users, including those with less prior experience, to effectively control complex robotic systems over extended periods.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as integrating other sensory modalities (tactile, force, audio), reducing camera streaming latency, and evaluating the impact on large-scale downstream policy learning, indicates promising avenues for further development and validation of the principles established by SPOT. This research underscores the importance of explicitly considering spatial perception as a fundamental interface design objective for the continued advancement of teleoperation technology.
