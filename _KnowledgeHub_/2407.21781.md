# Berkeley Humanoid: A Research Platform for Learning-based Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The Berkeley Humanoid, a mid-scale robot developed at UC Berkeley, provides an affordable and reliable platform for learning-based control by minimizing the simulation-to-reality gap. It achieves robust dynamic locomotion, including navigating varied terrains and rejecting disturbances, with a measured sim-to-real forward velocity tracking error of 0.058 m/s compared to 0.051 m/s in simulation.

## Problem

- Developing affordable, reliable, and accessible humanoid robotics platforms for research and learning-based control strategies.
- Bridging the simulation-to-reality gap, which hinders the deployment of policies trained in simulation onto physical humanoid robots.
- Achieving dynamic bipedal locomotion on humanoids using relatively simple, adaptable control approaches.

## Method

- Designed the **Berkeley Humanoid**, a lightweight, mid-scale (85cm, 10 DoF) robot optimized for stability, dynamic capability, and cost-effectiveness.
- Developed custom quasi-direct drive (QDD) actuators with planetary gear reduction and high-bandwidth torque control, communicating via EtherCAT for precise, low-latency motion.
- Implemented a hierarchical control architecture where high-level reinforcement learning policies, trained in ==NVIDIA Isaac Lab== with domain randomization, control low-level precise motor actions.

## Results

- Demonstrated robust omnidirectional walking across diverse indoor/outdoor terrains (grass, gravel, sloped surfaces), plus dynamic capabilities like hopping and a successful 710-meter long-distance walk through varied elevation on the UC Berkeley campus.
- Achieved excellent disturbance rejection, with the robot maintaining balance and recovering its gait when subjected to external pushes.
- Quantitatively showed a narrow sim-to-real gap, with real-world forward velocity tracking errors (**0.058** m/s) closely matching simulated errors (**0.051** m/s).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A carefully integrated hardware design, custom actuation, and high-fidelity simulation modeling can effectively narrow the sim-to-real gap, enabling successful policy transfer for humanoids.
> - Mid-scale humanoids can achieve robust and dynamic locomotion with relatively simple learning-based controllers and proprioceptive sensing.
> - Custom quasi-direct drive (QDD) actuators optimized for torque, backdrivability, and precise control are crucial for high-performance humanoid locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics faces a significant challenge: developing affordable, reliable, and accessible platforms that can effectively implement learning-based control strategies. The Berkeley Humanoid addresses this gap by providing a mid-scale humanoid robot specifically designed to minimize the simulation-to-reality gap while maintaining low cost and high reliability for research purposes.

Developed by researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, this platform enables the exploration of dynamic locomotion using relatively simple learning-based controllers. While full-scale humanoids often require complex hardware and control systems, and miniature robots struggle with dynamic movements due to scaling issues, the Berkeley Humanoid offers a middle ground that balances capability with accessibility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The platform specifically targets the challenges of transferring policies trained in simulation to real-world hardware—a persistent obstacle in learning-based robotics. By carefully addressing mechanical design, actuation, communication, and control architecture, the Berkeley Humanoid achieves impressive locomotion capabilities including walking on varied terrain, responding to disturbances, and even performing dynamic maneuvers.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Berkeley Humanoid is designed with an 85cm height, 16cm foot length, and 22cm hip width, positioning it as a mid-scale humanoid. This size provides an optimal balance between stability, dynamic capability, and cost. The robot features a lightweight construction that enhances agility while maintaining structural integrity.

The robot's structure comprises 10 degrees of freedom (DoF)—3 DoF per leg and 2 DoF per arm—which is sufficient for bipedal locomotion while maintaining hardware simplicity. Key components include:

- An onboard computer (Intel NUC) for high-level control
- An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) for state estimation
- A battery system providing approximately 30 minutes of operation
- Custom-designed actuators optimized for torque density and control precision

By eliminating unnecessary complexity while retaining essential functionality, the design achieves a remarkable balance between capability and simplicity. The hardware architecture also accommodates expansion for future perception capabilities, though the current implementation relies solely on proprioceptive feedback (joint encoders and IMU).

#### 3.1. Actuation System

A critical contribution of the Berkeley Humanoid is its custom actuation system, which addresses several limitations of off-the-shelf components. The researchers developed quasi-direct drive (QDD) actuators that balance the high torque requirements of humanoid locomotion with backdrivability and precise control.

These actuators feature:

- Planetary gear reduction (10:1 ratio) providing sufficient torque while limiting backdriving friction
- Integrated hollow shaft design allowing for cable routing and compact joint design
- Precise position sensing through magnetic encoders directly on the output shaft
- Custom motor drivers enabling high-bandwidth torque control

The actuation units are fabricated with computer numerical control (CNC) machining, ensuring precision and durability. The team also implemented a collection of actuators with varying specifications to optimize for different joint requirements, as shown in Figure 9.

The communication architecture leverages EtherCAT protocol for deterministic, high-speed control with minimal latency (1ms control cycles), significantly outperforming alternative communication methods and contributing to the narrow sim-to-real gap.

#### 3.2. Control Architecture

The Berkeley Humanoid implements a hierarchical control architecture that balances computational requirements with control performance. The system comprises:

1. A high-level policy network running at 50Hz on the onboard computer
2. A mid-level controller translating policy outputs to joint-level commands
3. Low-level controllers within each actuator running at 1kHz for precise tracking

This architecture allows the learning-based policy to focus on high-level behaviors while delegating precise motor control to specialized hardware. The system uses minimal sensing—primarily joint encoders and an IMU—to reduce complexity and cost while maintaining sufficient state estimation for effective control.

The robot's simplified dynamics model used in both simulation and control further reduces the sim-to-real gap. By carefully modeling actuation dynamics, friction, and contact physics, the simulation environment closely approximates real-world behavior, enabling effective policy transfer.

#### 3.3. Learning Based Control Implementation

The locomotion capabilities of the Berkeley Humanoid are achieved through reinforcement learning (RL), which offers advantages over traditional model-based approaches by learning directly from experience and adapting to unmodeled dynamics.

The policy training utilizes:

- The NVIDIA Isaac Lab simulator for physics modeling and policy training
- Domain randomization to enhance robustness to modeling errors
- Model-free RL algorithms with appropriate reward functions for locomotion tasks

The training process emphasizes simplicity and generalization rather than task-specific optimization. The resulting policy can handle a wide range of terrains and disturbances without explicit modeling of these conditions, demonstrating the effectiveness of the learning-based approach.

The policy network itself is relatively compact, enabling real-time execution on modest computational hardware. This contrasts with approaches requiring extensive computational resources, making the Berkeley Humanoid more accessible for research and development.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The Berkeley Humanoid demonstrates impressive locomotion capabilities across various real-world scenarios. Experimental evaluations showcase:

The robot successfully navigates diverse environments including:

- Various indoor surfaces (hardwood, tile, carpet)
- Outdoor terrains (grass, gravel, sloped surfaces)
- Urban obstacles (curbs, crosswalks, uneven pavements)

The platform also demonstrates dynamic capabilities beyond static walking:

A particularly impressive achievement is the robot's ability to walk continuously for extended periods. One test documented a 710-meter walk through the UC Berkeley campus with significant elevation changes (shown in Figure 7), demonstrating both the robustness of the control system and the mechanical reliability of the hardware.

The robot also demonstrates excellent disturbance rejection. When subjected to external pushes (Figure 8), the Berkeley Humanoid maintains balance and recovers its gait without falling, highlighting the robustness of the learned control policy.

#### 4.1. Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap

A key contribution of this work is effectively addressing the simulation-to-reality gap that often plagues learning-based robotics. The Berkeley Humanoid achieves this through:

1. Hardware design choices that prioritize controllability and predictable dynamics
2. High-fidelity actuator modeling in simulation, capturing backlash, friction, and latency
3. Consistent control loop timing through EtherCAT communication
4. Domain randomization during training to enhance robustness

The effectiveness of these approaches is quantitatively demonstrated in Figure 7, which compares the velocity tracking performance between simulated and real robots:

The data shows remarkably similar steady-state errors between simulation and reality—0.051 m/s vs. 0.058 m/s for forward velocity and 0.086 m/s vs. 0.116 m/s for lateral velocity. This close correspondence validates the approach to bridging the sim-to-real gap and explains the successful transfer of policies from simulation to the physical robot.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Berkeley Humanoid represents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics by providing a research platform specifically designed for learning-based control. By carefully addressing the challenges of hardware design, actuation, and the simulation-to-reality gap, the researchers have created a system that achieves impressive locomotion capabilities using relatively simple control approaches.

Key achievements include:

- Successful omnidirectional walking on diverse terrains
- Robust disturbance rejection without explicit modeling
- Dynamic maneuvers including hopping
- Extended operation demonstrating mechanical reliability
- Close correspondence between simulated and real-world performance

The platform's focus on accessibility, through both cost-effective design and open-source policies, positions it to accelerate progress in humanoid robotics research by lowering barriers to entry. The Berkeley Humanoid demonstrates that mid-scale humanoids can achieve dynamic, robust locomotion when hardware design and control strategies are carefully integrated to minimize the sim-to-real gap.

This work provides a foundation for future research in learning-based humanoid control, potentially enabling applications from service robotics to exploration of challenging environments where humanoid form factors offer distinct advantages.

#### 5.1. Practical Impact and Applications

The Berkeley Humanoid offers several advantages for the research community:

1. **Accessibility**: With its focus on cost-effectiveness and open-source components, the platform democratizes humanoid robotics research, allowing more institutions to participate in this field.

2. **Reliability**: The robust mechanical design withstands falls and impacts, reducing maintenance requirements and enabling extended experimentation.

3. **Versatility**: The platform supports diverse research directions, from locomotion control to human-robot interaction, without requiring specialized expertise in hardware development.

4. **Scalability**: The modular design allows for incremental improvements and adaptations to specific research needs without complete redesigns.

By providing an accessible, reliable platform with a narrow sim-to-real gap, the Berkeley Humanoid enables researchers to focus on algorithmic development rather than hardware troubleshooting, potentially accelerating progress in learning-based humanoid robotics.
