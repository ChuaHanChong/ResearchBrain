# SAIL: Faster-than-Demonstration Execution of Imitation Learning Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The Georgia Institute of Technology researchers developed SAIL, a full-stack framework enabling visuomotor policies to execute tasks faster than human demonstrations. The framework achieves up to a 4x speedup in simulation and 3.2x in real-world scenarios while maintaining high success rates by addressing challenges like altered robot dynamics and system latencies.

## Problem

- Offline imitation learning policies typically execute tasks at the same slow speed as human demonstrations, limiting task throughput.
- Increasing execution speed fundamentally alters robot dynamics, leading to out-of-distribution states and compounding errors for policies trained on slower data.
- Real-world system latencies and the need for precision during critical task phases complicate continuous, high-speed, and accurate robot control.

## Method

- **SAIL** incorporates ==Error-Adaptive Guidance (EAG)== to adjust guidance based on end-effector tracking error, and trains policies to predict controller-invariant 'reached poses' executed by a high-fidelity tracking controller, mitigating dynamics-induced distribution shifts.
- Adaptive speed modulation dynamically adjusts the execution speed based on motion complexity and gripper events, allowing for slowing down in critical phases and speeding up during simpler motions.
- Action scheduling addresses system latencies by executing actions from previous policy inferences during delays, ensuring continuous real-time control at high speeds.

## Results

- SAIL achieved up to a **4x** speedup over demonstration speed in simulation and up to **3.2x** in real-world scenarios across diverse manipulation tasks, maintaining high success rates.
- Ablations show Error-Adaptive Guidance enhanced motion consistency and task success during accelerated execution, while controller-invariant action targets and the tracking controller reduced distribution shifts, improving success rates by up to **55%**.
- Adaptive speed modulation proved vital for precision tasks, preventing up to a **31%** drop in success rates compared to uniform speedup, and SAIL consistently improved throughput-with-regret on 6 out of 7 real-world tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Achieving faster-than-demonstration execution requires a full-stack approach integrating policy-level adaptations with system-level engineering, crucially decoupling policy learning from the dynamics of the demonstration controller by training on reached poses.
> - Dynamically adjusting execution speed based on task demands and motion complexity allows for balancing high throughput with precision in complex manipulation tasks.
> - Mitigating the impact of tracking errors and system latencies through adaptive guidance and intelligent action scheduling is essential for stable and continuous high-speed robot operation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a novel and critical problem within the rapidly evolving field of robot learning: achieving **faster-than-demonstration execution** of visuomotor policies derived from offline imitation learning (IL).

- **Offline Imitation Learning (IL):** Traditional offline IL methods, such as Behavior Cloning (BC) and more recent generative models like Diffusion Policy (DP) and ACT, have significantly advanced the capability of robots to acquire complex manipulation skills from human demonstrations. These methods excel at learning high-fidelity behaviors from diverse datasets. However, a fundamental limitation, which this paper explicitly highlights, is that learned policies typically mimic the speed of the demonstrations. Since human demonstrations are often slow and deliberate for clarity, policies inherit this sluggishness, limiting the task throughput crucial for industrial automation and real-world efficiency.
- **"Better-than-Demonstration" vs. "Faster-than-Demonstration":** Previous lines of work have explored "better-than-demonstration" imitation learning, where policies aim to surpass expert performance in terms of success rate or robustness (e.g., T-REX). Some approaches incorporate online learning or reinforcement learning with task-specific rewards to achieve modest speed increases (e.g., 1.1-1.3x), but these often require interactive learning in the environment and may compromise success rates. SAIL distinguishes itself by focusing purely on the *offline* setting and explicitly targeting *speed* as the primary objective without sacrificing success, proposing a comprehensive system-level solution rather than relying on online interaction.
- **System Integration for Imitation Learning:** Recent years have seen a growing recognition of the importance of full-stack system optimization for robot learning, moving beyond purely algorithmic innovations. Works like Mobile-ALOHA and UMI demonstrate the immense value of tightly integrating low-level robot controllers with learning algorithms. SAIL extends this trend by identifying and tackling the unique challenges that arise when deliberately varying execution speed between demonstration and policy deployment, a problem largely unaddressed by prior system integration efforts.
- **Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) and Riemannian Motion Policies (RMPs):** These state-space frameworks theoretically support temporal modulation of motion, offering insights into how speed might be varied. While some approaches have incorporated such techniques into end-to-end imitation learning, the field has largely gravitated towards simpler, end-to-end deep learning algorithms. SAIL, while leveraging modern generative policies like Diffusion Policy, introduces system-level and policy-level adaptations to achieve speed modulation, effectively bringing some of the benefits of temporal control to these advanced learning paradigms.
- **The identified challenges—robot dynamics alteration, state-action distribution shifts, execution fidelity, and real-world latencies—are central to bridging the gap between impressive lab demonstrations and practical, high-throughput robotic applications.** SAIL's holistic approach to these issues positions it as a significant contribution towards making learned manipulation policies viable for industrial and everyday scenarios where speed is paramount.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable **faster-than-demonstration execution of learned visuomotor policies** while maintaining high task success rates. This directly addresses the critical need for increased task throughput in real-world robot learning applications, especially in domains like industrial automation where efficiency is key.

The motivation stems from several core observations and challenges:

- **Inherited Sluggishness from Demonstrations:** Current offline imitation learning methods, despite their success in acquiring complex skills, produce policies that typically execute tasks at the same speed as the human or teleoperated demonstrations. These demonstrations are often slow and deliberate, leading to policies with inherently sluggish motions.
- **Altered Robot Dynamics at Higher Speeds:** Increasing execution speed fundamentally alters robot dynamics. This introduces greater tracking errors, dynamic effects, and critically, shifts the robot's observation distribution into "out-of-distribution" (OOD) states. Policies, trained on slower demonstration data, are ill-equipped to handle these shifts, leading to compounding errors and task failures.
- **Execution Fidelity and Precision Requirements:** Achieving high speeds can compromise precision, especially during critical task phases like grasping, insertion, or delicate object manipulation. A uniform speedup throughout a task is often suboptimal or leads to failure.
- **Real-world System Latencies:** Practical robotic systems are subject to irreducible inference latency, sensor delays, and control bandwidth limitations. These impose hard physical constraints on the maximum achievable speed and necessitate careful action scheduling to maintain real-time, continuous control.

In essence, the paper aims to answer the question: "How can we speed up the execution of learned visuomotor policies beyond demonstration speed?" by developing a "full-stack" solution that integrates algorithmic advancements with system-level engineering to overcome these intertwined challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SAIL (Speed Adaptation for Imitation Learning) is presented as a full-stack framework designed to tackle the problem of faster-than-demonstration policy execution. Its approach integrates four tightly-connected components, addressing both policy-level and system-level challenges:

1. **Consistent Action Prediction via Error-Adaptive Guidance (EAG):**
 - **Challenge:** Faster execution exacerbates tracking errors, pushing the robot into Out-of-Distribution (OOD) states. When the policy replans in a receding-horizon manner, conditioning on an OOD "tail" of the previously planned (and potentially poorly executed) trajectory can lead to inconsistent and jerky motions.
 - **Approach:** SAIL uses **Error-Adaptive Guidance (EAG)**, an extension of Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG). CFG usually blends a conditional prediction (conditioned on the end of the previous action sequence) with an unconditional one to enforce temporal consistency. EAG *adaptively* adjusts the guidance weight (`w`) based on the current end-effector tracking error (`e`).
 - If tracking error is low (`e ≤ ρ`), indicating reliable conditioning, standard CFG is applied (`w > 0`).
 - If tracking error is high (`e > ρ`), indicating an OOD state and potentially unreliable conditioning, guidance is disabled (`w = 0`), and the policy relies solely on the unconditional prediction.
 - This adaptive mechanism ensures temporal consistency when beneficial, while preserving robustness by avoiding misleading guidance from inaccurate state information.

2. **Reducing Controller Shift via Controller-invariant Action Target:**
 - **Challenge:** Policies are often trained to predict *commanded* poses (`x_d`) from teleoperation data, which are influenced by the (often low-gain) teleoperation controller. Executing these commanded poses at faster speeds with different, higher-gain controllers alters dynamics, creating a distribution shift for the policy.
 - **Approach:** SAIL changes what the policy predicts and how it's executed.
 - **Policy Target:** The policy is trained to predict the *reached poses* (`x`) directly from demonstration data. Reached poses represent the robot's actual achievable trajectory and are largely invariant to the specific dynamics or compliance of the teleoperation controller used during data collection.
 - **Execution Controller:** During deployment, the original teleoperation controller is replaced with a dedicated, **high-fidelity tracking controller (K_e)**, optimized for fast and accurate motion. This high-gain controller tracks the policy's predicted reached poses, decoupling policy execution from the variable dynamics of the data collection phase and significantly reducing controller-induced distribution shift.

3. **Adaptive-Speed Policy Execution:**
 - **Challenge:** Not all parts of a task require the same execution speed. High-precision phases (e.g., grasping, insertion) benefit from slowing down, while simpler motions (e.g., reaching, transport) can be accelerated.
 - **Approach:** SAIL dynamically adjusts the instantaneous speedup factor (`c_t`) based on task demands. It identifies "critical actions" using two methods:
 - **Motion Complexity Analysis:** Offline analysis (e.g., DBSCAN clustering on waypoints extracted from demonstrations using AWE) flags segments of geometrically complex motion. The policy learns to predict, alongside its actions, whether the current action falls into a complex phase.
 - **Gripper Event Detection:** A simpler runtime heuristic detects changes in the predicted gripper state (opening/closing) within the policy's action sequence, indicating potential interaction phases.
 - Based on a binary critical action flag (`k_t`), the speedup factor (`c_t`) is modulated between preset `c_slow` (for critical actions) and `c_fast` (otherwise). This allows SAIL to automatically slow down for precision and speed up during simpler, less critical motions.

4. **Maintaining Real-Time Control at High Speed Under System Latency:**
 - **Challenge:** Inherent sensing-to-action delays (`δ_delay`) and computation latencies can lead to gaps in action availability, causing pauses or unstable control, especially at high speeds.
 - **Approach:** SAIL employs **action scheduling** to ensure continuous motion.
 - **Latency Handling:** During the `δ_delay` interval (between sensor data request and new action sequence arrival), actions from the *previous* policy inference step are executed. Once the new sequence arrives, the system smoothly transitions to it, discarding outdated actions.
 - **Upper Bounding Speedup:** To prevent "action exhaustion" (running out of commands before the next inference cycle completes), the actual execution interval (`δ_t`) is lower-bounded by a factor (`δ_lb`) derived from the prediction horizon, conditioning length, and sensing-inference delay. This ensures that the adaptively chosen speedup factor never pushes the system beyond its physical capacity for continuous operation.

By combining these four components, SAIL provides a comprehensive solution for robust, faster-than-demonstration execution of visuomotor policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SAIL was conducted across 12 tasks in both simulation and on two distinct real robot platforms (Franka Emika Panda and Bimanual UR5), demonstrating its effectiveness and robustness. The key findings are:

- **SAIL Achieves Significantly Higher Task Throughput (H1 & H2 Validation):**
 - **Simulation:** SAIL achieved up to a **4× speedup** over demonstration speed (SOD) while maintaining high success rates. In tasks like "Lift" and "Stack," SAIL showed up to a 3-4x SOD and significantly higher Throughput-with-Regret (TPR) compared to the baseline Diffusion Policy (DP) and other baselines. For "Can" and "Stack" tasks, SAIL achieved up to a 3× throughput of baselines without sacrificing success rate.
 - **Real World:** On physical robots, SAIL achieved up to a **3.2× speedup** across 7 challenging manipulation tasks (e.g., Stacking Cups, Wiping Board, Baking, Folding Cloth, Plate Fruits, Pack Chicken, Bimanual Serve), consistently improving TPR and SOD on 6 out of 7 tasks.

- **Error-Adaptive Guidance (EAG) Enhances Motion Consistency and Performance (H1 Validation):**
 - Qualitative and quantitative analysis (using SPARC metric) showed that EAG significantly enhances motion consistency, leading to smoother trajectories. This, in turn, improved task success rates during accelerated execution.
 - Ablation studies confirmed that removing consistency-preserving trajectory generation reduced performance.
 - EAG's adaptive nature, particularly the adaptive tracking error cutoff, was crucial for effective conditional guidance, preventing misleading guidance when the robot state was OOD due to large tracking errors.

- **Controller-Invariant Action Targets and High-Fidelity Tracking Reduce Controller Shift (H2 Validation):**
 - Training policies to predict *reached poses* (rather than commanded poses) proved critical for high-speed execution. Ablation showed that using commanded poses resulted in a significant performance degradation (e.g., 55% drop in success rate for "Square" and 0.08 reduction in average TPR across tasks).
 - Using a high-gain controller to track these reached poses enabled consistent behavior across execution speeds, outperforming low-gain controllers and scenarios using commanded poses.

- **Adaptive Speed Modulation is Crucial for Precision Tasks (H2 Validation):**
 - Ablation studies (SAIL vs. SAIL(-AS)) revealed that adaptive speed modulation (AS) was vital, especially for tasks requiring precision. Without AS, success rates significantly dropped (up to 31% degradation on "Square" and "Mug" tasks), highlighting the importance of dynamically adjusting speed based on motion complexity or gripper events.

- **SAIL Overcomes Common DP Failure Modes in Real-World Scenarios (H3 Validation):**
 - **Action Depletion:** SAIL's action scheduling maintained constant motion, overcoming DP's tendency to pause when inference lagged.
 - **Imprecise Grasping:** Adaptive speed modulation in SAIL helped slow down critical grasping phases, enhancing success rates in tasks like plating fruits and packing chicken.
 - **Low-Fidelity Tracking:** SAIL's high-fidelity tracking controller substantially reduced tracking errors, improving performance in precision-critical stages like cloth folding and baking.
 - **Jerky Motion:** EAG contributed to smoother trajectories, particularly evident in the bimanual serving task, leading to significantly higher throughput and success rates.
 - **Limitation:** A minor decrease in performance was observed for the "Wiping Board" task, hypothesized to be due to the high-gain controller's difficulty adapting to new robot-object dynamics (sustained contact) at higher speeds, indicating a direction for future work.

- **Generalizability:** SAIL demonstrated generalizability across diverse manipulation tasks and two distinct robot platforms (Franka and UR5), underscoring its broad applicability.
- **ACT Compatibility:** The core components of SAIL (excluding EAG specific to Diffusion Policy) were shown to be applicable to other generative policy models like ACT, indicating the modularity of the framework.

Overall, the findings robustly validate SAIL's full-stack approach in enabling practical, high-throughput robot manipulation by addressing fundamental challenges associated with accelerating learned visuomotor policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "SAIL: Faster-than-Demonstration Execution of Imitation Learning Policies" makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact:

- **Formalization of a Novel Problem:** The paper formally introduces and defines the problem of "faster-than-demonstration execution" for visuomotor policies. By identifying and categorizing the fundamental challenges (robot dynamics shifts, state-action distribution shifts, execution fidelity, system latencies), it establishes a crucial new research direction for the robotics community.
- **Enabling High Task Throughput for Robot Learning:** This work directly addresses a critical bottleneck preventing the wider adoption of learned policies in industrial and real-world applications: the inherently slow execution speed inherited from human demonstrations. By achieving up to 4x speedup in simulation and 3.2x in real-world scenarios while maintaining high success rates, SAIL demonstrates a practical pathway to significantly increase robot task throughput, making learned skills economically viable for automation.
- **Full-Stack Solution for Complex Robotics Problems:** SAIL's strength lies in its comprehensive, "full-stack" approach, integrating policy-level algorithmic innovations (Error-Adaptive Guidance, controller-invariant targets, adaptive speed modulation) with system-level engineering solutions (latency-aware action scheduling, high-fidelity tracking controllers). This holistic perspective is essential for tackling the multifaceted challenges of deploying advanced robot learning in complex, dynamic environments.
- **Improved Robustness to Distribution Shift and Dynamic Changes:** The core components of SAIL—especially the use of reached poses as controller-invariant targets and the adaptive nature of EAG—significantly enhance the robustness of policies to the inevitable distribution shifts and altered dynamics that occur when executing at higher speeds. This mitigates compounding errors and makes learned behaviors more reliable.
- **Enhanced Trajectory Smoothness and Precision:** EAG contributes to smoother, more temporally consistent action predictions, which is crucial for stable and efficient high-speed operation. Adaptive speed modulation allows the robot to dynamically trade off speed for precision during critical task phases, improving overall success rates in manipulation tasks.
- **Paving the Way for Industrial Adoption:** The ability to execute complex manipulation tasks reliably and at significantly higher speeds than demonstrated directly aligns with the demands of industrial automation. SAIL's contributions can accelerate the transition of advanced robot learning research from laboratory settings to practical factory floors and logistics centers.
- **Future Research Directions:** The limitations identified by the authors, such as the need to explicitly address robot-object interaction dynamics at higher speeds (e.g., maintaining contact during wiping or preventing objects from being thrown due to momentum), open up fertile ground for future research. This includes incorporating explicit dynamics modeling, leveraging simulation for training, and co-optimizing low-level control with anticipated dynamic effects.

In conclusion, SAIL represents a significant step forward in making learned robot manipulation policies not only capable but also *efficient* and *practical* for real-world deployment, thereby broadening the impact of imitation learning in robotics.
