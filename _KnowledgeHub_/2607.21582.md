# Scale Up Strategically: Learning Compositional Generalization via Bias-Aware Evaluation and Data Collection for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Northeastern University and NVIDIA developed a diagnostic framework to uncover "instruction factor bias" in robot policies, revealing consistent patterns of how policies over-rely on certain instruction components. They then proposed a bias-aware data collection strategy that significantly improves compositional generalization and sample efficiency in robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Robot policies struggle with compositional generalization, failing to recombine learned primitives to execute novel instructions, even with large-scale pretraining.
- Modern policies often exhibit "shortcut learning," over-relying on superficial visual cues or correlations in training data rather than fully grounding natural language instructions, termed "instruction factor bias."
- Existing diagnostic methods for robot foundation policies are typically coarse, indicating *if* a policy fails but not *why* it struggles with specific instruction components, hindering targeted improvements.

## Method

- Introduced a diagnostic framework using ==Factor Dominance Rate (FDR)== and ==Factor Dominance Hierarchy (FDH)== to quantify and rank how strongly policies rely on individual instruction factors (e.g., color, verb, object).
- Utilized a Vision-Language Model (Gemini-2.5-Flash) to classify robot rollouts, inferring which instruction factor the policy overfit to when faced with ambiguous or contradictory cues.
- Proposed a bias-aware data collection strategy (**V-strategy**) that reallocates a limited demonstration budget towards "under-grounded" instruction factors identified by the FDH, improving sample efficiency and compositional generalization.

## Results

- In simulation, the V-strategy matched or outperformed baselines, showing gains of up to **+14 percentage points** in success rate for specific factor combinations, and surpassed all baselines using only **half** the demonstration budget in the "All Factor" setting.
- Real-robot experiments demonstrated that the V-strategy consistently outperformed baselines across three tasks, achieving an average improvement of **+28.7 points** over the L-strategy and **+16.7 points** over the Random strategy.
- The V-strategy with **n=100** demonstrations (**54.2%** success) outperformed both L (**37.5%**) and Random (**47.9%**) strategies even when they used **n=200** demonstrations, showcasing significant sample efficiency in real-world scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot policies exhibit consistent instruction factor biases across different backbones, with color being the most dominant factor and verb and size being relatively overlooked.
> - A broadly consistent Factor Dominance Hierarchy (color "," object "," spatial "," verb "," size) was identified across diverse foundation models, suggesting a shared vulnerability to certain perceptual or semantic cues.
> - Strategically distributing data collection based on identified factor biases significantly enhances compositional generalization and sample efficiency, outperforming uniform or random sampling, especially when data is scarce.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to several interconnected areas within artificial intelligence and robotics, primarily focusing on compositional generalization, shortcut learning, and efficient data collection in robotic manipulation.

**Compositional Generalization in Robotic Manipulation:** The ability of robots to recombine learned primitives to execute novel instructions is a long-standing challenge. Prior work has explored this through explicit structural priors like modular architectures, symbolic planners, and programmatic policies, as well as implicitly via large-scale multitask learning in foundation models such as RT-1/2, Openpi, and Open X-Embodiment. These models are trained on extensive datasets like DROID and Open X-Embodiment. This paper addresses a previously under-measured failure mode—instruction factor bias—that specifically bottlenecks compositional generalization in these pretrained robot policies.

**Evaluation of Shortcut Behaviors in Robot Foundation Models:** It is recognized that pretrained robot policies often exhibit "shortcut behaviors," where they defer to visually salient cues rather than deeply grounding language instructions. Existing analyses of these shortcuts typically measure aggregate success rates under varying conditions, such as dataset diversity, language perturbation, or visual robustness. While these studies identify *if* a policy fails, they often do not elucidate *why*, making it difficult to pinpoint which specific parts of an instruction are overlooked or overemphasized, and how to address these failures. This work introduces a systematic framework for fine-grained analysis of shortcut behaviors at the level of individual instruction factors, aiming to diagnose failure behaviors more precisely.

**Data Collection Strategy in Robotic Manipulation:** Data collection is a significant bottleneck in imitation learning for robotics. Efforts to mitigate this cost include scalable teleoperation, motion capture, sim-and-real co-training, and active learning. Complementary to increasing data quantity and efficiency, other studies highlight that task coverage and the *distribution* of data, rather than just its size, are critical for generalization. Building on this, and on principles of compositional generalization over environmental factors, this research proposes a bias-aware data collection strategy. This strategy shifts the focus from merely acquiring more data to strategically allocating a limited budget to address identified biases in how policies interpret instruction factors.

In essence, this work bridges the gap between identifying generalization failures in large robot policies and developing actionable strategies to overcome them, by offering a diagnostic tool and a data collection methodology rooted in fine-grained understanding of policy biases towards language instruction components.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations of this research stem from the observed limitations of current robot learning policies, particularly their struggle with compositional generalization despite extensive training data.

**Problem Identification: Shortcut Learning and Instruction Factor Bias:** The core motivation is the pervasive issue of "shortcut learning" in modern robot policies. Policies trained on large, diverse datasets often succeed by latching onto superficial cues or correlations in the training data, rather than truly understanding and grounding natural language instructions. For instance, a policy might prioritize an object's color over the action verb, leading to incorrect actions when the instruction's components are recombined in an unfamiliar way (e.g., "Push the blue cube" failing if the policy primarily associates "blue" with "picking up" from training). This behavior, termed "instruction factor bias," means fine-tuned policies tend to over-rely on "dominant" instruction factors as shortcuts, while "under-grounding" other crucial components.

**Limitations of Existing Analyses:** Current diagnostic methods for robot foundation policies are often coarse. They typically report aggregate success rates under various conditions (e.g., dataset diversity, language perturbations). While these metrics indicate *whether* a policy fails, they do not provide sufficient insight into *why* the failure occurred. This lack of fine-grained understanding makes it difficult for researchers and developers to identify precisely which semantic components of an instruction a policy struggles with, how strongly it relies on specific cues, and consequently, how to effectively intervene and improve policy robustness and generalization.

**Need for Fine-Grained Diagnosis:** To address this gap, the researchers aimed to systematically study shortcut behaviors at the level of "instruction factors." These factors are defined as reusable and independent semantic components of an instruction, such as descriptive color, action verbs, object types, size, and spatial attributes. The objective was to develop a diagnostic framework capable of localizing failure to individual factors and quantifying the instruction factor bias.

**Actionable Insights for Data Collection:** A significant motivation was to translate this diagnostic understanding into practical, actionable strategies for improving policy learning. Given that exhaustive coverage of the combinatorially expanding instruction space is often infeasible, particularly in real-robot scenarios, the researchers sought to develop a model-agnostic data collection strategy. This strategy would leverage the identified biases to reallocate a fixed demonstration budget towards under-grounded factors, aiming to enhance sample efficiency and compositional generalization.

In summary, the key objectives were to:
1. **Identify and formalize** the problem of instruction factor bias as a systematic failure mode in pretrained robot policies.
2. **Develop a quantitative framework** (FDR and FDH) to diagnose and measure this bias at the level of individual instruction factors.
3. **Reveal consistent patterns** of factor dominance across different foundation policies.
4. **Propose and validate** a bias-aware data collection strategy that improves compositional generalization and sample efficiency by strategically allocating demonstrations based on the diagnosed factor hierarchy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research involves a multi-stage approach encompassing problem formalization, diagnostic framework development, and a bias-aware data collection strategy, validated through both simulation and real-robot experiments.

**Problem Formulation:**
The experiments are conducted in a multi-task tabletop manipulation setup within the ManiSkill environment. Natural language instructions follow a template: "[Verb] the [Size] [Color] [Object] on the [Spatial Attribute] of the table."
- **Instruction Factors:** Five common factors are defined:
 - **Verb:** `grasp, lift, push, pull, rotate, slide`
 - **Color:** `red, yellow, blue, orange, green, black`
 - **Object:** `cube, sphere, cup, car, pyramid, star`
 - **Size:** `small, large, smaller, larger, smallest, largest`
 - **Spatial Attribute:** `left, right, middle, front, behind`
The Cartesian product of these factors yields 6,480 unique instructions, defining a comprehensive instruction space. Scenes include a target object and up to three random distractors.

**Evaluation of Factor Bias:**
A diagnostic framework is introduced to quantify factor bias using two metrics: Factor Dominance Rate (FDR) and Factor Dominance Hierarchy (FDH).

- **Factor Dominance Rate (FDR) for Pairwise Factor Bias Evaluation:**
 - **Setup:** To measure bias between a factor pair (f1, f2), policies are finetuned on a training distribution where f1 and f2 are deliberately correlated (e.g., specific f1 values are always paired with specific f2 values). All other factors and spatial layouts are randomized.
 - **Evaluation:** Policies are then evaluated on unseen, "off-diagonal" combinations of f1 and f2, where the correlation established during training is broken. The goal is to observe which factor the policy "overfits" to.
 - **Inference Scene Construction:**
 - **Verb vs. an object-centric factor:** The scene presents two objects with the same color: the instructed target object and an object that was paired with the instructed verb during training. Overfitting to the verb means acting on the verb-paired object (ignoring the instructed object). Overfitting to the object means applying that object's training-paired verb to the instructed target (ignoring the instructed verb).
 - **Two object-centric factors:** An impossible instruction is given (e.g., "grasp the blue cup" when neither a blue object nor a cup exists simultaneously). The scene contains two objects, each satisfying one of the instructed attributes (e.g., a blue star and a red cup). The object the policy acts on indicates which factor (color or object) it prioritized.
 - **Scoring:** A rule-based success metric is insufficient for diagnosing *why* a failure occurred. Therefore, Gemini-2.5-Flash, a Vision-Language Model (VLM), is used to classify each agent-view rollout as "success," "overfitting to f1," "overfitting to f2," or "other." The VLM judge's classifications demonstrate high consistency (88.7% overall agreement) with human annotations. FDR is calculated as:
 `FDR(f1, f2) = (N_f1 - N_f2) / (N_f1 + N_f2 + ε)`
 where `N_f1` and `N_f2` are the counts of overfitting to f1 and f2, respectively. A positive FDR indicates dominance by f1, negative by f2, and values near 0 suggest balanced grounding.
 - **FDR Experiments:** 300 demonstrations are used for training and 400 rollouts for evaluation for each pairwise FDR score across six foundation policies (π0.5, π0, OpenVLA-oft, GR00T-N1.7, XVLA, Genie-Envisioner).

- **Factor Dominance Hierarchy (FDH):**
 - **Aggregation:** Pairwise FDR scores are aggregated into a global ranking using Copeland ranking. Each factor `fi` receives a score based on how many other factors it dominates (FDR > a tie threshold τ=5%). Factors are then ranked in descending order of their Copeland scores.

**Strategic Data Collection with Factor Bias (V-strategy):**
The observed consistent FDH is used to guide data collection.
- **Principle:** Reallocate a limited demonstration budget towards "under-grounded" factors (those ranked lower by FDH). The strategy is model-agnostic, using a single global FDH (color ≥ object ≥ spatial ≥ verb ≥ size) rather than re-estimating for each setting.
- **Strategy (V-Ours):** For two factors, it collects samples from the "leftmost column" (fixing the under-grounded factor and varying the dominant one), augmented with diagonal and sub-diagonal combinations to ensure some joint coverage. For three or more factors, additional "columns" are added for each under-grounded factor, prioritizing those lowest in the hierarchy.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Complete:** Exhaustive coverage of all factor combinations (upper-bound, often infeasible).
 - **Random:** Uniform random sampling of factor combinations.
 - **L:** Fixes each factor at one value and uniformly samples the others (forms an "L" shape on a 2D grid).
- **Metrics for Comparison:**
 - **Factor Change (f):** Measured by Hamming Distance between consecutive configurations, reflecting task-switching cost and configuration variety. This is equalized across methods for fair comparison by adding a small number of random configurations.
 - **Demonstration Budget (n):** The total number of collected demonstrations.

**Experiments:**
- **Simulation Experiments:** Conducted on π0, π0.5, and GR00T-N1.7 in ManiSkill, covering pairwise factor-change, Verb-Color-Object, and All-Factor settings. Each setting involves a specific instruction subspace.
- **Real-robot Experiments:** Conducted on a UR5 robot with a custom gripper, agent-view (RealSense D455), and in-hand (FLIR) cameras, using GELLO for demonstrations. Three tasks (Bun, Pizza, Cup) are designed, each varying two instruction factors in a 4x4 grid. GR00T-N1.7 is used, with matched factor change (f=12) and two demonstration budgets (n=100, n=200).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding factor bias and the efficacy of the proposed data collection strategy.

**Factor Dominance Rate (FDR) and Factor Dominance Hierarchy (FDH):**
The analysis of six foundation robot policies revealed consistent patterns of instruction factor bias:
- **Finding 1: Color is the most dominant instruction factor.** Across all evaluated backbones, color consistently exhibited the highest dominance. This is attributed to color being a highly salient low-level visual cue, reliably captured by vision encoders, and prone to inducing shortcut correlations in large-scale training data.
- **Finding 2: Verb and size are relatively overlooked.** Compared to other factors, verb and size consistently showed the weakest dominance across most policy backbones (with OpenVLA-oft being an exception, showing stronger verb and size grounding). This suggests these factors are intrinsically harder to learn consistently, possibly due to size depending on relative geometric relationships and insufficient diversity in existing datasets regarding these aspects.
- **Finding 3: Different models share FDH consistency.** Except for OpenVLA-oft, the established factor hierarchy was broadly consistent across architectures: `color ≥ object ≥ spatial ≥ verb ≥ size`. This consistency suggests that factor bias is a shared property of many policy backbones, supporting the development of model-agnostic data collection strategies.

**Simulation Experiment Results:**
The bias-aware data collection strategy (V-strategy) was evaluated against baselines (L and Random) under matched factor-change (`f`) and demonstration budgets (`n`):
- **Improved Performance:** The V-strategy generally matched or outperformed the L and Random baselines across a majority of settings.
- **Largest Gains on Dominant Factors:** The performance improvements were most significant for tasks involving strongly dominant factors. For instance, on color-dominated pairs like "Color Object" and "Color Spatial," the V-strategy showed gains of +10 and +7 percentage points, respectively, over the best baseline.
- **Broader Impact:** The advantage was not limited to color; the V-strategy also improved spatial-size grounding on GR00T by +14 points.
- **Sample Efficiency and Scalability:** In the "All Factor" setting, the V-strategy surpassed all baselines using only half the demonstration budget (n=400 vs. n=800), indicating both sample efficiency and scalability for more complex instruction spaces.
- **Limitations for "Hard" Factors:** An exception was observed in the "Verb Size" setting on π0.5, where the V-strategy (51%) trailed Random (68%) at `f=18`. This is attributed to the inherent difficulty of grounding "size" due to its dependence on relative geometry, suggesting that even additional demonstrations might not be reliably beneficial for intrinsically hard-to-learn factors under certain conditions.

**Real-robot Experiment Results:**
The V-strategy was validated on a physical UR5 robot across three manipulation tasks (Bun, Pizza, Cup) with varying instruction factors:
- **Consistent Outperformance:** The V-strategy consistently outperformed both L and Random baselines across all three tasks, showing an average improvement of +28.7 points over L and +16.7 points over Random.
- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency:** On the "Bun" task, the V-strategy with `n=100` demonstrations (54.2% success) already surpassed both L (37.5%) and Random (47.9%) strategies even when they used `n=200` demonstrations. This demonstrates that bias-aware allocation can achieve higher success rates with half the number of demonstrations, highlighting significant sample efficiency.
- **Scaling with Budget:** As the demonstration budget increased from `n=100` to `n=200`, all methods improved, but the V-strategy maintained a clear lead.
- **Partial Coverage vs. Complete Coverage:** Interestingly, under a small demonstration budget (`n=100`), the V-strategy significantly outperformed the "Complete" strategy (which exhaustively covers the instruction space). This is attributed to the "Complete" strategy spreading demonstrations too thinly, leading to undersampling for each instruction, whereas the V-strategy concentrates the budget, allowing for more reliable learning of behaviors for a subset of instructions and generalization to held-out combinations.
- **Structured Generalization:** Visualizations confirmed that while the Random strategy scattered its successes across the instruction grid, the V-strategy concentrated its data collection in a structured manner, leading to more consistent and successful generalization on out-of-distribution cells.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and has potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation and general robot learning:

**Systematic Diagnosis of Shortcut Failures:** The work introduces a novel, fine-grained diagnostic framework (FDR and FDH) that moves beyond aggregate success rates to identify and quantify specific "instruction factor biases" in pretrained robot policies. This is a crucial advancement for understanding *why* policies fail to generalize compositionally, rather than just *if* they fail. This systematic approach provides a deeper understanding of the underlying learning mechanisms and shortcuts employed by large-scale robot foundation models.

**Consistent Patterns of Factor Bias:** The discovery of a broadly consistent Factor Dominance Hierarchy across diverse policy backbones (color ≥ object ≥ spatial ≥ verb ≥ size) is a notable finding. This consistency suggests that certain perceptual or semantic cues are inherently more salient or easier for current vision-language-action models to latch onto, irrespective of architectural differences. This insight is valuable for designing more robust models and training methodologies.

**Actionable and Model-Agnostic Data Collection Strategy:** The proposed bias-aware data collection strategy (V-strategy) provides a practical and effective method to improve compositional generalization and sample efficiency. By strategically reallocating a limited demonstration budget towards under-grounded instruction factors, the strategy demonstrates that *shaping the data distribution* based on diagnostic insights can be more impactful than simply increasing data quantity or diversity. This is particularly significant for real-robot systems where data collection is costly and time-consuming.

**Improved Sample Efficiency and Generalizability:** The experimental results, particularly the real-robot validation, confirm that the V-strategy can achieve higher task success with substantially fewer demonstrations (e.g., half the budget). This translates directly into reduced data collection costs and faster deployment of capable robotic policies, making advanced robot capabilities more accessible. The enhanced generalizability to out-of-distribution instructions through structured, bias-aware sampling is also critical for robots operating in diverse and dynamic environments.

**Guidance for Future Research and Model Development:**
- The findings highlight areas where current models struggle (e.g., grounding verb and size factors), providing clear directions for future architectural improvements or specialized training techniques.
- The framework can serve as a benchmark for evaluating future robot policies, allowing for more precise comparisons of their compositional generalization capabilities and vulnerability to factor bias.
- The concept of bias-aware data allocation opens avenues for more sophisticated active learning or curriculum learning strategies that dynamically adjust data collection based on real-time policy performance and identified biases.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The current work's evaluation is primarily conducted in tabletop manipulation settings with a controlled factor space. Its generalization to more complex open-world scenarios, richer object diversity, or multi-step tasks remains an open question for future investigation. Additionally, the V-strategy is presented as one instance of bias-aware allocation; exploring a principled search over coverage policies, perhaps through active learning or advanced curriculum design, could further optimize data collection.

In conclusion, this research provides both a diagnostic lens and a practical solution for a fundamental challenge in robot learning. By systematically uncovering and addressing instruction factor bias, it contributes to the development of more intelligent, generalized, and sample-efficient robotic agents.
