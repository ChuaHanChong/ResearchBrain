# A Survey of Continual Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey paper provides a comprehensive review of Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL), introducing a novel taxonomy that categorizes methods based on whether they primarily store and transfer knowledge related to policies, experiences, environment dynamics, or reward signals. It identifies the unique challenges of CRL, such as the stability-plasticity-scalability dilemma, and outlines future research directions and applications across various domains.

## Problem

- Traditional Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents suffer from "catastrophic forgetting," losing previously acquired knowledge when learning new tasks.
- DRL often requires extensive training data and computational resources, limiting its efficiency and generalization in dynamic, real-world, non-stationary environments.
- Agents struggle to continuously learn and adapt to new tasks while retaining knowledge from prior experiences, necessitating relearning from scratch for each new task.

## Method

- ==Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL)== aims to enable agents to learn new tasks sequentially while retaining knowledge and leveraging prior experiences more efficiently.
- This survey proposes a novel ==taxonomy== that classifies CRL methods based on what knowledge is stored and transferred (==policy==, ==experience==, ==dynamics==, ==reward==) to provide a structured understanding of the field.
- The paper provides a comprehensive review of existing CRL methods within this new framework, standardizes ==scenario settings== and ==evaluation metrics==, and outlines open challenges.

## Results

- The survey classified existing CRL methods into four main categories based on knowledge management: policy-focused (e.g., reuse, decomposition, merging), experience-focused (e.g., direct, generative replay), dynamic-focused (e.g., direct, indirect modeling), and reward-focused (e.g., shaping, intrinsic rewards).
- Key challenges and future research directions were identified, including task-free CRL, the need for improved evaluation and benchmarks, interpretable knowledge, integration with large-scale pre-trained models (including LLMs), and application to embodied agents.
- Common application areas for CRL were summarized, spanning robotics, game playing, natural language processing, medical imaging, finance, and autonomous driving.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) extends traditional Continual Learning by explicitly addressing a "triangular balance" among plasticity (learning new tasks), stability (retaining old knowledge), and scalability (resource efficiency for many tasks).
> - A novel taxonomy categorizes CRL methods based on the type of knowledge they manage for transfer and retention: policy-focused, experience-focused, dynamic-focused, and reward-focused.
> - Standardized scenario settings (Lifelong Adaptation, Non-Stationarity Learning, Task Incremental Learning, Task-Agnostic Learning) and evaluation metrics (average performance, forgetting, transfer, efficiency) are essential for consistent assessment of CRL systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated significant advancements, particularly with the integration of deep neural networks. However, its widespread applicability in dynamic and real-world scenarios is constrained by several factors, including its reliance on extensive training data, substantial computational resources, and limited generalization capabilities across diverse tasks. This often necessitates retraining from scratch when environments or goals change, which is impractical for persistent autonomous agents in fields like robotics and self-driving systems.

Continual Learning (CL) emerged as a paradigm to address these limitations in machine learning generally, focusing on systems that learn incrementally, adapt to new tasks, and retain previously acquired knowledge without catastrophic forgetting. Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL), also known as Lifelong Reinforcement Learning (LRL), specifically applies CL principles to sequential decision-making problems within the RL framework. CRL aims to develop agents that can continuously accumulate knowledge, efficiently adapt to new tasks, and maintain performance on previously mastered tasks, mirroring human-like learning abilities.

This survey positions itself within this evolving landscape by providing an updated and comprehensive examination of CRL. Previous surveys on CL often offered only a general overview, encompassing both supervised learning and RL without deep dives into the unique complexities of CRL. While Khetarpal et al. (2022) provided a foundational survey on CRL from a non-stationary RL perspective, it lacked detailed comparisons and discussions on critical aspects such as scalability and various scenario settings. Furthermore, the field of CRL has experienced rapid growth in methodologies and benchmarks in recent years, rendering older reviews potentially incomplete. This current work addresses these gaps by offering a contemporary analysis, introducing a novel taxonomy, and highlighting the latest advancements and emergent challenges, thereby serving as a foundational resource for guiding future research in CRL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey stems from the inherent limitations of conventional Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and the rapidly expanding yet unorganized state of Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) research.

**Motivation:**
1. **Addressing DRL Limitations:** Traditional DRL systems, despite their successes, demand vast amounts of data and computational power, struggle with generalizing across tasks, and are prone to "catastrophic forgetting"—losing previously acquired skills when learning new ones. This necessitates restarting training for every new task or environmental change, which is unsustainable for real-world deployments in robotics, autonomous systems, and agentic software.
2. **Bridging the Human-AI Learning Gap:** Humans exhibit a natural ability to continuously learn, adapt to novel situations, and transfer knowledge across experiences while retaining old skills. Modern DRL agents largely lack this capacity, motivating the development of CRL as a pathway to more intelligent and adaptive AI systems.
3. **Synthesizing Rapid Progress in CRL:** The field of CRL has seen an acceleration in research, leading to a proliferation of methods, benchmarks, and theoretical discussions. This rapid development creates a need for a structured and comprehensive review to consolidate knowledge, clarify terminology (e.g., unifying "lifelong" and "continual" RL), and identify overarching trends.
4. **Identifying Gaps in Previous Surveys:** Existing surveys either broadly covered continual learning without specific focus on RL's unique challenges or provided partial views, overlooking crucial aspects like scalability, diverse scenario settings, and comprehensive methodological comparisons.

**Objectives:**
Based on these motivations, the survey sets out to achieve the following key objectives:
1. **Comprehensive Examination:** To provide an in-depth analysis of CRL, covering its foundational concepts, inherent challenges, and diverse methodologies.
2. **Detailed Review of Existing Works:** To systematically organize and analyze current research, including discussions on evaluation metrics, specific task types, established benchmarks, and various scenario settings.
3. **Propose a Novel Taxonomy:** To introduce a new, structured classification system for CRL methods. This taxonomy categorizes approaches based on the type of knowledge (policy, experience, dynamics, reward) that is stored, reused, or transferred across tasks, offering a clearer framework for understanding the field.
4. **Highlight Unique Challenges:** To emphasize the critical "triangular balance" among plasticity (the ability to learn new tasks), stability (the ability to retain old knowledge), and scalability (the ability to learn many tasks within resource constraints), which is central to successful CRL.
5. **Provide Practical Insights and Future Directions:** To identify open research questions, emerging trends (such as task-free CRL, integration with large-scale pre-trained models), and adjacent research areas, thereby guiding future efforts in advancing the field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a systematic survey methodology to synthesize and structure the current state of Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) research. Their approach involved several key steps:

1. **Literature Review and Curation:** The methodology began with a comprehensive review of existing literature, encompassing seminal works, recently published articles, and promising preprints. This broad scope aimed to capture the most current advancements in the rapidly evolving field of CRL. The authors tracked research trends over time (e.g., Figure 4 in the paper) to illustrate the development trajectory of different method classes.

2. **Definition and Scope Clarification:** The survey clarifies the definition of CRL, unifying terms like "lifelong reinforcement learning" under a common "continual reinforcement learning" umbrella. It formally characterizes CRL within the framework of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), adapting the general continual learning update step to the RL context where task data represents interaction experience. This formalization helps in distinguishing CRL from related paradigms like Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (MTRL) and Transfer Reinforcement Learning (TRL).

3. **Identification of Core Challenges:** The authors systematically identified and articulated the fundamental challenges unique to CRL. This included defining the "triangular balance" among plasticity (adapting to new tasks), stability (retaining prior knowledge), and scalability (operating within resource budgets), emphasizing scalability as a particularly critical factor in RL settings.

4. **Analysis of Evaluation Components:**
- **Metrics:** A detailed section is dedicated to current evaluation metrics, explaining how per-task scores (episodic return, success rate) are aggregated to quantify overall performance, forgetting, and transfer (forward and backward). The paper also addresses the nascent state of scalability metrics, noting proxies like model size, memory footprint, and computational cost.
- **Benchmarks:** The survey provides a comparative overview of prominent CRL benchmarks (e.g., Continual World, L2Explorer, CORA, COOM), outlining their characteristics (e.g., 3D, partial observability, multi-agent, image observations) and the specific metrics they employ. Challenges in designing comprehensive and standardized benchmarks are also discussed.
- **Scenario Settings:** CRL scenarios are formally categorized into Lifelong Adaptation, Non-Stationarity Learning, Task Incremental Learning, and Task-Agnostic Learning, based on the nature of environmental changes and the availability of task identities.

5. **Development of a Novel Taxonomy:** A central methodological contribution is the proposal of a new taxonomy for organizing CRL methods. This taxonomy is driven by the guiding question: "What knowledge is stored and/or transferred?" This led to four main categories aligned with core components of RL:
- **Policy-Focused Methods:** Sub-categorized into Policy Reuse, Policy Decomposition, and Policy Merging.
- **Experience-Focused Methods:** Sub-categorized into Direct Replay and Generative Replay.
- **Dynamic-Focused Methods:** Sub-categorized into Direct Modeling and Indirect Modeling.
- **Reward-Focused Methods:**
 Each category and sub-category is detailed with representative examples, algorithmic principles, and discussions on their mechanisms for knowledge transfer and retention.

6. **Scalability Analysis within Taxonomy:** For each method family in the new taxonomy, the authors provide a qualitative summary of its scalability profile, noting trends in memory growth, compute growth, and task-scaling ability, along with brief bottleneck notes. This integrates the challenge of scalability directly into the discussion of methods.

7. **Identification of Future Research Directions:** The survey concludes by outlining open challenges and promising future research directions. These include fundamental conceptual shifts like Task-Free CRL, leveraging advancements in Large-Scale Pre-Trained Models (both using CRL for PTMs and PTMs for CRL), and exploring adjacent research areas such as Unsupervised RL, Meta-RL, Transfer and Negative Transfer, Representation-Based Continual Learning, Sim-to-Real, Continual Domain Randomization, and In-Context RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey provides a structured overview of Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) by synthesizing existing research and proposing a new categorization framework. Key findings and results include:

1. **Clarified Definition and Scope of CRL:** The survey establishes a unified definition for CRL, encompassing "lifelong reinforcement learning" and modeling it as an iterative update process in time-indexed Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). It differentiates CRL from Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (MTRL) by emphasizing sequential task arrival and the management of past knowledge, and from Transfer Reinforcement Learning (TRL) by foregrounding catastrophic forgetting as a key concern.

2. **Identification of Core Challenges: The Triangular Balance:** The paper highlights that CRL agents must navigate a critical "triangular balance" among three intertwined objectives:
- **Plasticity:** The ability to efficiently learn new tasks and leverage prior knowledge (forward transfer).
- **Stability:** The ability to retain knowledge from previously learned tasks and avoid catastrophic forgetting.
- **Scalability:** The ability to learn a growing number of tasks using limited computational and memory resources, which is particularly salient for RL.

3. **Standardized Metrics and Benchmarks:** The survey details a set of standardized metrics crucial for evaluating CRL algorithms, including average performance, forgetting, forward transfer, and backward transfer. It also reviews prominent CRL benchmarks (e.g., Continual World, CORA, L2Explorer), discussing their characteristics (e.g., 3D, multi-agent, partial observability, image-based) and their role in advancing the field. A key observation is the ongoing challenge in developing comprehensive and standardized benchmarks for CRL due to the complexity of designing appropriate task sequences.

4. **Categorization of Scenario Settings:** CRL problems are classified into four main scenarios based on the nature of environmental non-stationarity and task identity availability: Lifelong Adaptation, Non-Stationarity Learning, Task Incremental Learning, and Task-Agnostic Learning. This categorization helps to delineate the scope and assumptions of various CRL methods.

5. **Novel Taxonomy of CRL Methods:** The survey introduces a new taxonomy that classifies CRL methods based on the type of knowledge they primarily store, transfer, or leverage. The four main categories are:
- **Policy-Focused Methods:** These approaches manage policy or value functions.
 - *Policy Reuse* involves initializing new policies with prior knowledge or composing existing policies (e.g., MAXQINIT, CSP).
 - *Policy Decomposition* breaks down policies into shared and task-specific components using factor, multi-head, modular, or hierarchical structures (e.g., PG-ELLA, OWL, SANE, HLifeRL).
 - *Policy Merging* combines multiple policies into a single model to reduce memory footprint, often employing distillation, hypernetworks, masks, or regularization techniques (e.g., EWC, DisCoRL, HN-PPO).
- **Experience-Focused Methods:** These methods concentrate on managing and replaying past interactions.
 - *Direct Replay* explicitly stores and reuses experiences in buffers, often with prioritization (e.g., CLEAR, CoMPS).
 - *Generative Replay* utilizes generative models to synthesize past experiences, reducing memory requirements (e.g., RePR, SLER).
- **Dynamic-Focused Methods:** These methods learn and leverage environmental dynamics.
 - *Direct Modeling* explicitly models the environment's transition function (e.g., MOLe, HyperCRL).
 - *Indirect Modeling* uses abstract representations or latent variables to infer dynamics, often coupled with intrinsic rewards (e.g., LILAC, Continual-Dreamer).
- **Reward-Focused Methods:** These approaches adapt or shape reward signals to facilitate learning (e.g., SR-LLRL, IML).

6. **Scalability Profiles for Method Families:** The survey provides an assessment of the scalability (memory, compute, task-scaling) for each method family, identifying common bottlenecks. For example, policy reuse can suffer from growing policy libraries, while experience replay might face memory limitations, and dynamic modeling can incur high computational costs.

7. **Emerging Research Directions:** The paper identifies several key areas for future research:
- **Task-Free CRL:** Learning in continuously changing, non-stationary environments without explicit task boundaries.
- **Large-Scale Pre-Trained Models (PTMs):** Exploring how CRL can enhance PTMs (e.g., for aligning LLMs with human preferences) and how PTMs can serve as knowledge bases for CRL.
- **Adjacent Directions:** Including Unsupervised RL, Meta-RL, Transfer and Negative Transfer, Representation-Based Continual Learning, Sim-to-Real, Continual Domain Randomization, and In-Context RL.
- **Beyond Traditional CRL:** Areas like task detection, integration of offline RL and imitation learning, and multi-agent CRL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey on Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) holds significant implications for both fundamental research in artificial intelligence and the development of practical autonomous systems.

1. **Advancing General-Purpose AI:** CRL is presented as a pivotal paradigm for moving beyond the limitations of single-task, data-intensive Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). By enabling agents to learn continuously, adapt to novel situations, and retain a vast repository of skills over time, CRL directly contributes to the realization of more robust, autonomous, and general-purpose AI systems that can operate effectively in dynamic, open-ended environments. This addresses a fundamental challenge in AI: creating intelligence that truly "learns for life."

2. **Enabling Real-World Autonomous Systems:** The principles and methodologies outlined in this survey are crucial for the deployment of intelligent agents in practical, real-world applications such as robotics, autonomous driving, and complex control systems. These applications cannot afford to reset and retrain from scratch for every change; they demand agents that can persistently adapt and improve while managing computational and memory constraints. CRL offers a framework for developing such persistent, adaptive agents.

3. **Structuring and Guiding Research:** By providing a comprehensive review and a novel taxonomy, the survey offers a much-needed organizational framework for the rapidly expanding field of CRL. The proposed categorization of methods (policy-, experience-, dynamic-, and reward-focused) based on knowledge storage and transfer mechanisms helps clarify diverse approaches and their underlying principles. This structured understanding can guide new researchers, facilitate comparative studies, and promote a more systematic development of CRL algorithms.

4. **Highlighting Critical Challenges and Future Directions:** The survey's emphasis on the "triangular balance" of plasticity, stability, and scalability brings to the forefront the core trade-offs that CRL algorithms must address. By articulating open challenges, such as task-free learning, integration with large-scale pre-trained models, and multi-agent coordination, the paper effectively signposts critical areas for future research. This proactive identification of research gaps can stimulate innovation and direct collective efforts toward the most impactful problems.

5. **Fostering Interdisciplinary Connections:** The discussion of adjacent research directions (e.g., Unsupervised RL, Meta-RL, Sim-to-Real, In-Context RL) underscores the interdisciplinary nature of CRL and its potential to draw insights and techniques from various subfields of AI. This broad perspective encourages cross-pollination of ideas, which can lead to more holistic and effective solutions for lifelong learning challenges.

In summary, this survey not only consolidates the current understanding of Continual Reinforcement Learning but also provides a forward-looking perspective, positioning CRL as a critical enabler for persistent, intelligent systems and a significant step toward general artificial intelligence.
