# Anomaly detection for generic failure monitoring in robotic assembly, screwing and manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A semi-supervised anomaly detection framework trains autoencoders, chiefly a variational autoencoder, on nominal multi-modal time series (end-effector pose, wrench, velocity, gripper state) and flags failures when per-feature reconstruction error exceeds thresholds derived only from nominal data. Evaluated on three contact-rich industrial tasks under diffusion-policy, position, and force control, it reaches AUROC above 0.96 on cabling and screwing failures.

## Problem

- Prior anomaly detection studies validate feasibility only within single robotic tasks, never showing transfer across different task types or robot control strategies.
- Existing datasets favor external disturbances with large signal deviations, omitting subtle process-related failures such as misaligned parts under tight tolerances.
- No benchmark covers learning-based controllers, whose high execution variance may break the temporal assumptions that earlier reconstruction methods rely on.

## Method

- Sliding windows of length 100 advancing one timestep per run, windowed independently per trajectory to prevent leakage, feed a dense ==VAE== with ==SELU== layers and latent dimension 15.
- Training minimizes ==L2 reconstruction loss== plus ==KL divergence== on nominal runs only; compared variants include plain ==autoencoder==, ==undercomplete autoencoder==, and a convolutional ==SWCVAE==.
- A ==failure-data-free thresholding rule== sets each feature's threshold to the maximum mean absolute reconstruction error over held-out nominal windows, with min-max scaled combined score for online ==ROS== deployment.

## Results

- Cabling reached AUROC **1.00** for human disturbance and above **0.98** for misaligned and incorrect connectors, but only **0.78** for incorrect target pose; screwing scored **0.96-0.97** on two failures, **0.52** on missing screw.
- Polishing detected only severe cases, **0.93** for human disturbance and **0.71** for simulated defect, while surface roughness fell to **0.27** given high nominal force variance.
- Ablations: **10** of **20** demonstrations gave the best AUROC **0.86** versus **0.73** with two; window size **500** beat **50** and **100**; all input modalities combined beat subsets; VAE beat AE and SWCVAE.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Detectability tracks the ratio of anomaly force deviation to nominal signal variance, not failure severity, so variable compliant tasks resist reconstruction-based monitoring.
> - More nominal demonstrations can hurt detection by widening the learned normal distribution, so data efficiency peaks well before the full training set.
> - High AUROC does not imply deployable thresholds; nominal-only threshold rules drift into false positives, and reconstruction error rarely localizes the causal modality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The application of robots in less structured environments frequently results in novel, out-of-distribution states, which can lead to unpredictable behavior, task failure, or potential damage. Anomaly detection (AD) serves as a mechanism to identify deviations from expected patterns in data, enabling the initiation of failsafe behaviors or recovery strategies. Prior research has successfully applied data-driven AD to time series data for specific robotic tasks, such as pick-and-place operations, demonstrating the capability to detect task- and robot-related anomalies using force/torque signals, motor currents, and task states.

However, existing literature indicates limitations in the transferability of AD approaches between diverse robot control strategies and task types. Some studies suggest that AD in contact-rich scenarios may be less accurate, and comprehensive datasets for such tasks remain scarce. While previous work has primarily focused on establishing the general feasibility of AD for specific tasks, a gap exists in evaluating its applicability across a wide range of contact-rich industrial robotic tasks with varying signal characteristics and control methods. Additionally, the role of learning-based control setups, which can introduce different variance characteristics compared to traditional position-controlled systems, has not been thoroughly investigated in the context of AD.

This research addresses these limitations by evaluating the generalizability of data-driven AD methods across three distinct industrial robotic tasks: plug insertion (cabling), screwing, and polishing. Each task involves multi-modal time series data and features several types of anomalies, including subtle failures characterized by low-magnitude force deviations. The study incorporates multiple robot control strategies, specifically diffusion policy-controlled, position-controlled, and impedance-controlled approaches, to assess AD performance in scenarios with varying process times and robot trajectory variations common in real-world applications. By benchmarking data-driven AD methods, including an analysis of detection latency and factors influencing performance such as data volume and model configuration, this work aims to demonstrate the potential for online execution of AD and its integration with recovery behaviors or planning processes. This extends previous work by providing a more comprehensive evaluation of AD transferability and robustness across diverse robotic manipulation contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations guiding this research are multifaceted, aiming to advance the practical application and reliability of anomaly detection in industrial robotics:

Firstly, a key objective is to address the challenge of unpredictable robot behavior and task failure arising from out-of-distribution states in unstructured environments. By identifying deviations from expected data patterns, the research seeks to enable the triggering of failsafe behaviors and recovery strategies, thereby improving success rates and mitigating the risk of damage to robotic systems and workpieces.

Secondly, the study is motivated by the recognized limitation in prior work regarding the transferability of anomaly detection approaches. Existing data-driven AD methods have primarily been demonstrated for specific robotic tasks and control strategies. This research aims to evaluate whether a single AD approach can be effectively applied across a wide range of robotic tasks with diverse signal characteristics and varying control methods, including diffusion policy-controlled, position-controlled, and impedance-controlled robots. Achieving such transferability is considered crucial for the broad deployment of AD in industrial settings.

Thirdly, the research seeks to develop AD methods that are data-efficient. Robotic tasks can exhibit widely varying signal characteristics and requirements for AD. The motivation is to identify approaches that can be applied consistently across a spectrum of tasks without requiring extensive, task-specific data or specialized knowledge for implementation. This also includes investigating the impact of data volume on detection performance.

Fourthly, the study intends to validate the integration of AD in complex industrial tasks that often involve tighter tolerances and inherent variations from both the robot and its environment. This includes focusing on contact-rich tasks, which have been less comprehensively studied in previous AD feasibility assessments, and incorporating subtle failure types characterized by low-magnitude force deviations, which pose a greater challenge for detection compared to severe, easily identifiable anomalies.

Finally, the researchers aim to provide a quantitative assessment of AD performance, including metrics such as data efficiency, detection latency, and an understanding of task characteristics that support robust detection. The goal is to demonstrate the potential for online execution of AD systems, paving the way for their future integration with automated recovery behaviors or within broader planning processes in industrial automation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a data-driven, semi-supervised anomaly detection framework based on autoencoder architectures. The methodology encompasses data acquisition, processing, model training, threshold computation, and extensive experimental evaluation across three distinct industrial robotic tasks.

**Experimental Setups and Data Collection:**
Three industrial tasks were selected to represent diverse contact-rich scenarios and control strategies:
1. **Cabling (Plug Insertion):** Involves a Franka Emika Panda robot grasping and inserting a female plug into a male connector. Robot control utilizes a diffusion policy trained via imitation learning, using camera inputs and TCP pose.
2. **Screwing:** An UR16e robot picks up, places, inserts, and tightens a screw in vehicle battery housing assembly. Robot operation is position-controlled, based on predefined reference points.
3. **Polishing:** An aluminum casting part is polished by a robot-mounted tool with sandpaper. A Cartesian force controller maintains desired contact force along a predefined trajectory. The AD model monitors polishing progress and detects over-polishing or surface defects.

For each application, multi-modal time series data were recorded at 500 Hz, including the robot's end-effector (TCP) wrench forces/torques (F_x, F_y, F_z), TCP positions (x, y, z), TCP velocities (v_x, v_y, v_z), and gripper positions (if applicable). The dataset comprises both nominal task executions and runs with various simulated or induced failure scenarios. Temporal labels for anomalies were manually provided by an operator for evaluation.

**Data Processing:**
Raw time series data `X` is processed using a sliding window approach. Windows `w_t` of a specified length `T` (default 100) advance by a single time step to ensure maximal overlap and preserve temporal fidelity. Each trajectory is windowed independently to prevent data leakage between runs. Data normalization (mean/variance) was tested but not ultimately used as it did not yield performance improvements.

**Autoencoder Architectures:**
Several autoencoder-based models were compared for their anomaly detection capabilities:
1. **Variational Autoencoder (VAE):** This was the primary model investigated. VAEs learn a probabilistic latent representation of the input data by minimizing a combined loss function consisting of a reconstruction loss (Euclidean distance, L2-loss) and the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. The KL divergence regularizes the learned latent distribution towards a standard normal distribution. The VAE encoder consists of three fully connected layers with 30 units each, using SELU activation functions, followed by a latent bottleneck. The decoder mirrors this architecture.
2. **Autoencoder (AE):** A simpler autoencoder that optimizes only the reconstruction loss, maintaining the same encoder-decoder structure as the VAE without the KL divergence term.
3. **Undercomplete Autoencoder (UAE):** An AE variant where the input dimension explicitly exceeds the latent dimension, following parameters from prior work.
4. **SWCVAE:** A VAE model modified with two convolutional layers preceding the flattening step in the encoder, designed to better handle time series data with specific input shapes.

**Training:**
Models are trained exclusively on nominal process data. During training, the autoencoder learns to project time sequences from the input data into a lower-dimensional latent space and reconstruct them. The optimization objective is to minimize the reconstruction error between the input window `w` and its reconstruction `w'`. For VAEs, the KL-divergence term is additionally minimized. An anomaly is detected when the reconstruction loss for a given input significantly increases, indicating a deviation from the learned nominal behavior.

**Threshold Computation (Novel Approach):**
Given the absence of explicit temporal relationships between individual windows, a novel feature-wise thresholding method was proposed. After training on nominal data, the model reconstructs windows `w` from unseen nominal samples. For each feature `i`, the mean absolute reconstruction error `L_i(w)` is computed across the window. The anomaly threshold `ϵ_i` for that feature is then determined as the maximum `L_i(w)` observed across all nominal windows `W`. This sets the upper limit for expected behavior under normal conditions, accounting for task variance. For online validation, a combined loss score is used, derived by applying per-feature min-max scaling.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The performance of the AD system was evaluated using standard metrics, including Precision, Recall (TPR), F1-score, False Positive Rate (FPR), Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, and Area Under the ROC curve (AUROC). Additionally, industrial metrics such as data efficiency (AUROC vs. training data volume) and average detection latency were assessed.

**Implementation Details:**
The system was implemented in Python 3.11 using JAX as the machine learning framework. Robot Operating System (ROS) Noetic and ROS 2 Humble were utilized for experimental deployment and data acquisition. A ROS node handles real-time inference, processing incoming sensor data via sliding windows, and halting robot motion upon anomaly detection. Hyperparameters included a window size of 100, batch size of 25, latent dimension of 15, and 500 training epochs.

**Ablation Studies:**
To understand the factors influencing detection performance, ablation studies were conducted on the Cabling task (specifically for the "Incorrect Target Pose" error). These studies investigated the impact of:
- Different model architectures (VAE, AE, UAE, SWCVAE).
- Various input features (end-effector position, force, velocity, gripper position, time).
- Different window sizes (50, 100, 500).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provided insights into the generalizability, failure-specific performance, and the impact of various parameters on the anomaly detection framework.

**Generalizability Across Applications (Figure 3, Table IV):**
The proposed VAE model demonstrated varying detection performance across the three industrial tasks:
- **Cabling Task:** Four out of five failure types were reliably detected. "Human Disturbance" and "Missing Plug" achieved near-perfect detection with AUROC exceeding 0.99. "Misaligned Plug" and "Incorrect Plug" also showed robust detection with AUROC above 0.98. However, the "Incorrect Target Pose" failure was more challenging, exhibiting a lower AUROC of 0.78.
- **Screwing Task:** Two out of four failure types, "Misaligned Screwdriver" and "Obstructed Screw Hole," were reliably detected with AUROC above 0.96. "Missing Thread" achieved an AUROC of 0.84. The "Missing Screw" failure proved challenging, with an AUROC of 0.52, indicating performance close to random guessing.
- **Polishing Task:** "Human Disturbance" yielded the highest AUROC at 0.93, followed by "Simulated Defect" at 0.71. Subtle failures such as "Different Surface State" (AUROC 0.27) and "Displaced Object" were not reliably detectable. The authors attribute this to high force variance in the nominal polishing task relative to the small force deviations caused by these subtle anomalies.

**Failure-Specific Performance (Table IV):**
The study highlighted that detection performance is highly dependent on the nature and severity of the failure. More severe failures, such as "Human Disturbance," typically resulted in higher AUROC values due to significant deviations from nominal execution. While high AUROC values were observed for several failures, F1-scores were sometimes low, indicating challenges in selecting an optimal threshold without prior failure data, potentially due to an imbalance between true positives and false positives. Optimizing thresholds per failure scenario generally yielded the best F1-scores, but the proposed thresholding method, which does not require failure data, performed comparably to thresholds optimized across entire failure datasets.

**Impact of Data Volume, Model Parameters, and Architecture (Figure 4, 5):**
- **Data Efficiency (Figure 4, right):** For the Cabling task, the highest AUROC (0.86) was achieved using 10 demonstrations (50% of the available training data). Training with the full dataset (20 demonstrations) or 75% of it resulted in a slightly lower AUROC of 0.78. This suggests that incorporating more nominal data beyond a certain point might increase variance in the dataset, potentially reducing detection sensitivity. Using fewer demonstrations (e.g., 2) significantly reduced performance (AUROC 0.73).
- **Model Architectures (Figure 5, 1):** The proposed VAE model generally outperformed the simpler AE and the convolutional SWCVAE for the "Incorrect Target Pose" error in the Cabling task. The Undercomplete Autoencoder (UAE) exhibited comparable performance to the VAE but with lower variance.
- **Input Features (Figure 5, 2):** An ablation study demonstrated that incorporating all available features (end-effector position, force, velocity, and gripper state, along with time from task start) yielded the highest AUROC. This indicates the benefit of multi-modal sensing for comprehensive anomaly detection.
- **Window Sizes (Figure 5, 3):** Larger window sizes contributed to better detection. A window size of 500 achieved the highest AUROC (0.78), while smaller window sizes (50 and 100) resulted in lower mean AUROC values (below 0.4) and increased variance.

**Detection Latency (Table IV):**
Detection latency, measured as the time from the labeled anomaly to the model's first detection, varied across failure types. Some failures (e.g., "Human Disturbance" and "Misaligned Screwdriver") exhibited very low latency (below 3 ms), although this could be influenced by false positives in cases with lower F1-scores. Other failures, such as "Missing Plug" and "Missing Screw," showed zero latency, which was attributed to potential inconsistencies in manual labeling where anomalies were sometimes annotated before the actual failure event.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic automation by demonstrating the feasibility and applicability of a unified anomaly detection framework across a variety of industrial robotic tasks and control strategies. The key significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

Firstly, the study shows that a single data-driven anomaly detection approach, specifically using a VAE model with a novel feature-wise thresholding method, can generalize across diverse industrial applications, including cabling (diffusion policy-controlled), screwing (position-controlled), and polishing (impedance-controlled). This indicates a step towards more scalable and versatile AD systems that do not require extensive task-specific model redesign.

Secondly, the methodology of training exclusively on nominal sensor data is crucial for practical industrial deployment. This semi-supervised approach significantly reduces the data collection burden by eliminating the need for large, labeled datasets of failure scenarios, which are often difficult and costly to acquire in real-world settings. This enhances the efficiency and scalability of integrating AD into existing and new robotic systems.

Thirdly, the detailed evaluation across various failure types and their characteristics provides valuable insights into the limits and capabilities of autoencoder-based AD. The findings reveal that while severe anomalies with large signal deviations (e.g., human disturbances) are reliably detectable, subtle failures or those occurring in tasks with high nominal process variance (e.g., polishing) remain challenging. This understanding can guide future research towards more robust methods for detecting subtle deviations and adapting to inherent task variability.

Fourthly, the ablation studies on data volume, model architecture, feature modalities, and window size offer practical guidance for system design. The observation that optimal performance may not always require the entire training dataset suggests opportunities for data efficiency, while the importance of multi-modal data and appropriate window sizes underlines critical design choices for robust detection.

Finally, this work holds significant potential for enhancing the safety and reliability of machine learning (ML)-driven robot control systems, which can be particularly susceptible to unpredictable behavior in out-of-distribution states. By reliably detecting such anomalies and potentially triggering human intervention or automated recovery strategies, the proposed AD framework can facilitate the broader adoption and integration of ML approaches within industry. This could lead to extended robot autonomy, reduced downtime, and improved process quality in various manufacturing and assembly operations. Future work on pinpointing the specific modality contributing to a failure and developing stage-dependent thresholding strategies could further enhance the effectiveness and practical utility of such systems.
