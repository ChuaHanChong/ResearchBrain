# SARM2: Multi-Task Stage Aware Reward Modeling for Self Improving Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A reward modeling approach, SARM2, combines action-primitive stage estimation with a multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts to generate dense, accurate per-step rewards across diverse long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. This model underpins SPIRAL, a self-improvement framework that iteratively refines manipulation policies using autonomous robot rollouts, reducing reliance on human demonstrations.

## Problem

- Developing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies for long-horizon robotic manipulation requires expensive human demonstrations and supervised fine-tuning, limiting generalization and scalability.
- Current task-specific reward models are not generalizable across tasks, necessitating extensive re-annotation and retraining for each new skill.
- General Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based reward models provide coarse and noisy signals, insufficient for the fine-grained, per-step supervision needed for complex multi-stage tasks.

## Method

- A multi-task ==stage-aware reward== model, **SARM2**, is introduced, integrating an action-primitive based stage estimator with a multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE) value decoder to generate dense and generalizable progress estimates.
- SARM2's stage estimator classifies action primitives from visual and proprioceptive input, while the MMoE decoder specializes value predictions based on these primitives, overcoming interference from diverse dynamics.
- The SPIRAL framework utilizes SARM2's dense rewards within an on-policy residual reinforcement learning loop, enabling iterative policy refinement and self-improvement through autonomous robot interactions.

## Results

- SARM2 demonstrated superior reward modeling accuracy, achieving lower demonstration MSE (**0.006** on S1) and higher rollout classification (ρ=**0.667**) compared to VLM-based baselines that showed over-optimistic estimation.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the action-primitive stage estimator and the multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts design significantly contribute to SARM2's ability to capture task structure and improve accuracy.
- The SPIRAL framework, leveraging SARM2's dense rewards, consistently improved policy success rates across tasks (e.g., 12/12 successful trials on Folding Shorts Flat) over multiple rounds of autonomous self-improvement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing long-horizon tasks into a small, recurring vocabulary of action primitives provides a robust, task-agnostic intermediate representation for stage estimation.
> - A multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE) architecture, conditioned on predicted action primitives, effectively specializes value prediction for diverse primitive dynamics, overcoming limitations of monolithic value heads.
> - Dense and accurate per-step reward signals are critical for enabling stable and effective policy self-improvement through on-policy reinforcement learning, significantly surpassing the capabilities of sparse rewards or coarse VLM-based signals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which facilitate end-to-end policy learning. However, these models often exhibit limitations when applied to long-horizon tasks. Extending VLA capabilities to such complex tasks typically necessitates fine-tuning on in-domain data, primarily through supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This process is costly, requiring substantial volumes of high-quality human demonstrations, and often leads to policies that remain close to the demonstration distribution, rendering them susceptible to out-of-distribution (OOD) failures.

To mitigate this dependence on extensive demonstrations, reward models have been explored. These models can reweight training samples or provide dense supervision for on-robot reinforcement learning (RL). Existing approaches to reward modeling in robotic manipulation generally fall into two categories:

1. **Task-specific reward models:** These models (e.g.,) are trained on narrow sets of demonstrations and are capable of high accuracy within their specific task domain. Stage-aware variants, such as SARM, improve accuracy on long-horizon tasks by decomposing them into sub-stages. However, a significant limitation of these models is their single-task nature; each new task requires retraining, and some, like SARM, necessitate task-specific stage annotations, restricting their scalability and general applicability as the diversity of tasks increases.

2. **General-purpose Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based reward models:** Leveraging the broad priors of pretrained VLMs (e.g.,), these models offer broad applicability across tasks. Despite their semantic generalization, their predictions are often coarse-grained and noisy at the step level. This characteristic renders them unsuitable as a dense supervisory signal for fine-grained progress estimation in long-horizon manipulation tasks.

A gap exists in the current research landscape: no single reward model simultaneously satisfies the requirements of being dense, accurate, and general enough to reliably supervise long-horizon tasks across diverse domains. This limitation hinders the scalable application of VLA-RL, as existing models either lack generalization or provide insufficient detail for complex manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a multi-task stage-aware reward model, named SARM2, capable of generating dense, accurate, and general per-step rewards for long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. This development is motivated by several challenges and limitations observed in the current state of robotic manipulation research:

- **Dependence on Costly Demonstrations:** Current VLA policies for long-horizon manipulation largely rely on behavior cloning, which mandates costly, high-quality human demonstrations. This dependence confines policies to the distribution of demonstrated actions and hinders their ability to generalize or self-improve in novel or varied scenarios.
- **Inadequacy of Existing Reward Models:**
 - **Task-Specific Models:** While accurate for their designated tasks, these models require extensive per-task annotations and retraining. This manual annotation process is time-consuming and expensive, posing a scalability barrier as the number of tasks grows. The lack of transferability across tasks necessitates a complete redevelopment of the reward mechanism for each new robotic skill, which is not conducive to developing generalist robots.
 - **General VLM-Based Models:** Despite their broad applicability, VLM-based reward models provide a coarse and often noisy signal at the fine-grained step level required for long-horizon tasks. Their design, often optimized for broad semantic alignment, does not typically capture the subtle, motion-level temporal discrimination critical for precise manipulation. This makes them unsuitable for dense supervision in complex, multi-stage tasks where accurate real-time feedback is necessary.
- **Need for Policy Self-Improvement:** Current offline approaches, even those leveraging reward models, are fundamentally bounded by the initial demonstration distribution. They lack mechanisms for continual policy refinement through on-robot interaction. Realizing autonomous, self-improving robotic systems requires a dense, reliable reward signal that can guide reinforcement learning from cheap, autonomous robot rollouts, thereby moving beyond the limitations of pre-recorded data.

To address these motivations, the research aims to:
1. **Develop SARM2:** A novel reward model that integrates a general action-primitive-based stage estimator with a multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE) value head. This architecture is designed to capture fine-grained task progress across diverse tasks without requiring task-specific stage annotations.
2. **Propose SPIRAL:** A self-improvement framework that utilizes SARM2's dense rewards. SPIRAL is intended to enable VLA policies to continually refine themselves through on-policy reinforcement learning from autonomous robot interactions, thereby establishing a stable robot data flywheel with minimal human intervention.

These objectives collectively aim to advance the capabilities of robotic manipulation by providing a robust and generalizable reward signaling mechanism that facilitates scalable policy learning and autonomous self-improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper introduces SARM2, a multi-task stage-aware reward model, and SPIRAL, a self-improvement framework built upon SARM2's capabilities.

#### 3.1. SARM2: Multi-Task Stage-Aware Reward Model

SARM2 is designed to provide dense, accurate, and generalizable progress estimates for long-horizon manipulation tasks. It comprises two main components: an Action-Primitive Stage Estimator and a Multi-Gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE) Value Decoder.

#### 3.1.1. Action-Primitive Stage Estimator

This component addresses the limitation of task-specific stage estimators by using action primitives as a task-agnostic intermediate representation.
- **Motivation:** Analysis of over 10,000 hours of annotated manipulation data indicated that tasks decompose into recurring action primitives, which possess a relatively small vocabulary and exhibit semantic similarities.
- **Dataset Construction:** A dataset, D_AP, was curated from 200 hours of real-world manipulation data spanning 100 tasks. This dataset was balanced to ensure each of the K=21 identified action primitives contributed approximately 3 hours of segments, alongside an additional 'null' class for remaining long-tail samples, totaling 66 hours.
- **Model Architecture:** Stage estimation is framed as a temporal classification problem over K+1 (22) classes. At each timestep, the model receives N=6 recent frames (sampled at a stride of Δ=30 steps, approximately 6 seconds of context) from three camera views (left wrist, right wrist, top-down) and proprioceptive state. These inputs are encoded by a frozen SigLIP-2 encoder and a separate MLP for proprioceptive state. All tokens are processed by a 4-layer causal Transformer (f_θ) followed by a linear head (g_θ) to produce class logits. The model is trained using cross-entropy on D_AP.

#### 3.1.2. MMoE Value Decoder

This component builds on the stage estimator to provide dense, stage-conditioned goal-distance estimates, overcoming interference issues associated with monolithic value heads for diverse primitive dynamics.
- **Motivation:** Different action primitives exhibit distinct visual dynamics and progress signatures, making a single value head less effective. MMoE is employed to specialize along primitive groups while sharing a common expert pool.
- **Architecture:** The value model uses a 6-layer causal Transformer backbone, trained separately from the stage estimator but sharing the frozen SigLIP-2 encoder and its cached frame embeddings for inference efficiency.
- **Input:** At timestep 't', it receives multi-camera frames, proprioceptive state, task-name text embedding, and the predicted-primitive embedding (e_prim) from the stage estimator. The input frame sequence includes the first episode frame (visual anchor), six recent frames, and up to three rewinding frames as implicit negatives.
- **MMoE Mechanism:** The K+1 primitives are semantically grouped into M+1=8 categories based on shared visual/motion patterns (Appendix 6.4). Each group has a dedicated gate G_m over E=10 shared experts (each a 3-layer MLP). The active gate is selected by the predicted primitive, routing the fused token through the top-k (k=2) experts to produce the progress estimate. Auxiliary losses (L_balance, L_entropy) are applied to prevent router collapse and encourage balanced expert utilization.
- **Objective:** The model predicts normalized remaining steps to completion, r*t ∈ [-1, 0]. The total objective combines an MSE term with the auxiliary losses.

#### 3.2. SPIRAL: Dense-Reward-Enabled Robot Self-Improvement Framework

SPIRAL (Self-Policy Improvement via Reward-Aligned Learning) is a closed-loop framework that leverages SARM2's dense rewards for iterative policy refinement through on-policy residual RL.
- **Underlying RL Substrate:** SPIRAL adopts the distribution contractive RL finetuning scheme of DICE-RL, which uses multi-sample latent noise for action generation and residual learning for policy modification.
- **Key Modifications for SPIRAL:**
 - **Dense Rewards:** The TD3 objective for the critic uses dense per-step rewards from SARM2, rather than sparse terminal rewards.
 - **Hybrid Objective:** A combined objective (J_critic(ϕ) = J_TD(ϕ) + α ⋅ J_MC(ϕ)) is used, where J_TD provides fine-grained local shaping and J_MC (from sparse episode-level rewards) imposes a global preference for efficient, successful trajectories.
 - **Critic and Actor:** The critic is an ensemble of N=5 chunk-based Q-functions to mitigate overestimation. The residual actor is trained with a TD3+BC-style objective, minimizing Q_ϕ(s,a) while a BC regularizer keeps the residual policy close to the pretrained policy.
 - **Multi-Sample Training:** At each state, κ=4 latent samples are drawn to form candidate actions, and the critic and actor objectives are averaged over these candidates, promoting diverse exploration. Best-of-κ action selection is used at deployment.
- **Pipeline Stages (Algorithm 1):**
 1. **Behavior Cloning (BC) Fine-tuning:** An initial VLA policy (π_1) is fine-tuned on human demonstrations.
 2. **One-time Reward Model Adaptation:** Rollouts (n=50–100) from the initial policy (π_1) are collected and human-annotated. This data is mixed with a subsample of the reward model's pretraining data to fine-tune RM_1 into RM_2, adapting it to the rollout distribution and capturing human preferences (a 2-3 hour effort).
 3. **Initial Offline SPIRAL Update:** An offline SPIRAL update is performed using the pretrained RM_1 and the demo data to train an improved policy (π_2).
 4. **Autonomous Self-Improvement Loop:** This iterative loop involves: (a) collecting new rollouts from the current policy, (b) labeling them with RM_2 (without human intervention), and (c) refining the policy through SPIRAL updates. This process continues until a success rate threshold is met.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SARM2 and the SPIRAL framework focused on reward model accuracy and policy improvement across a 10-task benchmark and two specific real-world manipulation tasks: Folding Shorts and Cleaning Whiteboard.

#### 4.1. Reward Model Evaluation (Section 4.1, Table 1, Figure 7)

- **SARM2 Performance:** SARM2 exhibited robust performance, achieving the lowest demonstration Mean Squared Error (MSE) across tasks (0.006 on S1, 0.031 on S2, 0.020 combined). It also yielded the highest rollout classification score on the Cleaning Whiteboard task (ρ = 0.667). These results indicate SARM2's capability for dense and accurate value estimation.
- **Comparison with VLM-based Baselines:** General-purpose VLM-based reward models, such as TOPReward and Robometer, consistently underperformed SARM2. They showed 4-6 times higher demo losses on S1 and poor rollout classification scores. Visualizations (Figure 7) indicated an over-optimistic estimation, where these models saturated near the maximum progress early in trajectories. Even with LoRA fine-tuning (Robometer-FT), these models, despite being significantly larger (∼4B parameters vs. SARM2's ∼200M), did not surpass SARM2 or other in-domain baselines. This suggests that VLM pretraining optimizes for different temporal discrimination capabilities than required for dense robotic reward modeling.
- **Benefits of Stage Estimation and MMoE:** Ablation studies confirmed the individual and synergistic benefits of the Action-Primitive Stage Estimator and the Multi-Gate MMoE design. Removing the stage estimator (w/o SE) increased overall demo loss from 0.020 to 0.034, while removing the multi-gate design (w/o MG) raised it to 0.026. This indicates that both components contribute to capturing task structure and improving accuracy.
- **Impact of Training Distribution:** SARM2 trained on a broader task distribution (S1 and S2) achieved better performance on S1 (0.006 demo loss) compared to a variant trained solely on S1 (0.010 demo loss). This suggests positive transfer from more complex tasks in S2, which likely exposed the model to a richer set of action primitives and visual contexts beneficial for disambiguating progress signals.
- **MoE Routing Health:** SARM2 demonstrated a healthy and balanced MoE routing, with a low MoE Density score of 0.10. In contrast, the 'w/o SE' ablation showed routing collapse (0.87), and 'w/o MG' was concentrated (0.42). This correlation between balanced routing and performance supports the effectiveness of the primitive-conditioned multi-gate design in mitigating router collapse.
- **MoE Placement (Appendix 6.10, Table 8):** An ablation comparing MoE placement showed that SARM2's "MoE-Decoder" architecture, where experts are in the output head, significantly outperformed an "MoE-FFN" variant where experts are within the Transformer's feed-forward network. MoE-Decoder more than halved demo MSE and roughly tripled rollout scores, suggesting that specializing the prediction mapping in the output head is more effective than specializing intermediate representation building in the FFN for multimodal reward modeling.

#### 4.2. Policy Learning with Self-Improvement (Section 4.2, Table 2, Figure 4, Figures 8, 9)

- **SPIRAL as an Effective Offline RL Backbone:** On demo-only training, the RL-Dense configuration (SPIRAL with SARM2 dense rewards) achieved superior performance compared to Behavior Cloning (BC), RA-BC, and RL-Sparse. For example, it reached 7/12 successful trials on Folding Shorts Flat (compared to 1/12 for BC and 4/12 for RL-Sparse) and 10/20 at 81.3% average progress on Cleaning Whiteboard. This indicates that dense rewards combined with a value bootstrap provide a stronger optimization signal than sparse rewards or reward-weighted imitation learning.
- **Reward-Aligned Self-Improvement:** Starting from the RL-Dense checkpoint, the SPIRAL framework with SARM2 dense rewards led to continuous policy improvement over three rounds of self-improvement. It achieved 12/12 successful trials on Folding Shorts Flat, 8/12 on Crumpled, and 18/20 at 97.5% progress on Cleaning Whiteboard. These gains accumulated monotonically (Figure 4), demonstrating that autonomous on-policy rollouts can significantly enhance policy capabilities beyond offline RL alone.
- **Criticality of Reward Model Quality:** The quality of the reward model was identified as a critical bottleneck for the data flywheel.
 - Sparse terminal rewards led to a regression in performance on Folding Shorts Crumpled (from 4/12 to 2/12), highlighting the challenge of credit assignment with a single endpoint signal over long horizons.
 - While Robometer (FT) with dense rewards showed improvement, it still significantly trailed SARM2 (e.g., 5/12 vs 8/12 on Crumpled), indicating that reward density alone is insufficient. The ability of the reward model to faithfully reflect current rollout states—distinguishing true progress, corrective adjustments, and mistakes—is essential (Figures 8 and 9 visually illustrate these differences).
- **Hybrid Objective Efficacy (Appendix 6.14, Table 10):** An ablation confirmed the benefits of the hybrid critic objective (TD + MC) in the offline RL stage. The mixed objective achieved the best results on both tasks, affirming that the dense TD term provides fine-grained shaping, while the sparse MC term offers a stable global preference for efficient trajectories.
- **Influence on Policy Style (Appendix 6.14, Figure 13):** The one-time human annotation for reward model adaptation allowed the injection of human preferences (e.g., preferred grasp for whiteboard stabilization), leading to policies that distilled these preferences and avoided suboptimal behaviors observed in initial policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation:

- **Advancement in Reward Modeling:** SARM2 addresses a critical limitation in current robotic manipulation research by providing a reward model that is simultaneously dense, accurate, and general across multiple long-horizon tasks. This is achieved by effectively integrating an action-primitive-based stage estimator with a multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts architecture, which allows for specialization while maintaining a unified model. This represents an improvement over prior task-specific models that require extensive re-annotation and retraining, and over general VLM-based models that offer insufficient temporal granularity for complex tasks.

- **Enabling Autonomous Policy Self-Improvement:** The SPIRAL framework, built upon SARM2, introduces a robust, reward-aligned, on-policy self-improvement loop for VLA policies. This framework substantially reduces the reliance on costly human demonstrations and interventions, moving towards a more autonomous data collection and policy refinement paradigm. The demonstrated ability to consistently improve policy success rates from autonomous rollouts highlights a pathway to scalable and continuous learning for robotic systems.

- **Validation of Architectural Design Choices:** The empirical results provide strong validation for the specific architectural choices in SARM2, including the use of action primitives for stage estimation, the multi-gate MoE for value prediction, and the benefits of training on diverse task distributions. The ablations and comparisons, particularly the analysis of MoE placement, contribute to a deeper understanding of effective network design for multimodal robotic learning.

- **Implications for VLM Integration:** The findings suggest that while large VLM-based reward models offer broad semantic generalization, their current formulations may not be directly suitable for the fine-grained temporal discrimination required for dense reward signals in robotic manipulation. This insight could guide future research in adapting or integrating VLMs more effectively for robotics by focusing on mechanisms that can extract or learn precise temporal progress signals.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards future research avenues:
1. **Embodiment Scope:** The action-primitive vocabulary is derived from a bimanual tabletop dataset. Extending SARM2 to other embodiments (e.g., mobile manipulation) would require developing a new primitive set and retraining the stage estimator, indicating a need for more generalized primitive definitions or learning approaches.
2. **Residual RL Ceiling:** SPIRAL, based on residual RL, is primarily effective for correcting action-level and modality-wise errors. It may not address errors at higher levels, such as intention or task decomposition. Future work could explore integrating mechanisms beyond residual RL, such as VLM fine-tuning, to tackle these higher-order policy deficiencies.
3. **Sample Efficiency:** While reducing human intervention, on-policy rollout collection still requires an operator for routine environment resets and safety supervision. Enhancing automation in data collection and environment resetting could further improve the sample efficiency and autonomy of the self-improvement loop.
4. **Complexity of Tasks:** For highly complex tasks with significant state variation, such as "Folding Shorts from Crumble," the current reward model still faces challenges in providing sufficiently granular and accurate feedback, especially during highly unstructured stages. This suggests a need for even finer-grained reward modeling or alternative approaches for tasks with extreme deformability and state diversity.

In conclusion, SARM2 and SPIRAL represent a step towards more autonomous and scalable robotic learning, establishing a robust reward modeling foundation for self-improving manipulation policies.
