# Self-Evolving Agents as Dynamic Graph Transformation: A Survey and New Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey reframes LLM-agent self-evolution as dynamic graph transformation: memories, tools, skills, workflows and inter-agent relations become typed nodes, edges and subgraphs edited by schema-constrained double-pushout rewrites. It organizes 46 dynamic-graph agent methods into four patterns (node/feature, edge/topology, subgraph activation, cross-component cascade), maps nine dynamic-graph-learning families onto agent capabilities, and proposes five graph-aware evaluation and governance protocols plus six open challenges.

## Problem

- Graph-agent surveys treat graphs as static support structures for memory, retrieval and coordination, never as substrates that persistently evolve with the agent's state.
- Self-evolving-agent surveys centre on component mechanisms like reflection and self-training, leaving topology evolution and dependency coupling between memories, tools, skills and workflows unmodelled.
- Evaluation stays task- or answer-centred, so correct answers built on stale memories, unsafe propagated updates or untraceable rewrites pass undetected.

## Method

- A **dynamic agent graph** formalism with four node types (memory, tool, skill, agent) and dependency, communication and provenance edge families, evolved by ==double-pushout rewrite rules==.
- ==Typed rewrite operators== (INSERT, DELETE, FEATUREUPDATE, MERGE, LINK, UNLINK, REWIRE, EDGEFEATUREUPDATE, read-only ACTIVATE, timestamped cascade traces) defining the ==four-branch taxonomy A.1-A.4==.
- A ==capability mapping== from nine dynamic-graph-learning families to agent control points, plus graph-aware metrics: ==support-subgraph accuracy== (Dice-based M1), ==rewrite locality== M2, ==deletion success==, ==audit faithfulness==.

## Results

- Taxonomy covers **46 representative methods** across **four branches**; Table I projects representative mechanisms to trigger, rewrite operator, affected scope and persistence (long/medium/temporary).
- Table II names naive failure modes per dynamic-graph family, temporal leakage, stale text embeddings, forgotten rare skills, rollback damaging shared skills, across **nine** families with **two** representative methods each.
- No experiments are reported: the authors state the mapping is conceptual and provides no empirical evaluation, contributing **five** evaluation-governance protocols and **six** open challenges instead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating agent updates as typed graph rewrites makes affected-scope estimation explicit: local edits gain provenance, so revalidation is targeted rather than global.
> - Separating read-only activation from committed rewrites prevents mistaking transient retrieval for persistent learning, a distinction most agent designs blur.
> - End-task success hides state-level failure; trust needs valid support subgraphs, localized rewrites, leakage-free temporal splits, deletion of derived influence, faithful audits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Long-running LLM agents change as they work. They update memories and skills, change tools and workflows, and alter how agents coordinate. These changes are coupled: a local edit can affect downstream components. A correct answer alone may not reveal stale evidence, an unsafe update, or a change that cannot be traced. The authors' central move is to treat agent state as a dynamic graph that evolves through persistent changes. The survey organizes **46 representative methods** into four rewrite patterns and maps **nine dynamic-graph-learning families** to agent-evolution capabilities. It also discusses graph-aware evaluation and governance. These are classifications and proposals, not experimental evidence that the framework improves agents.

Self-evolving-agent surveys focus on changing agent components, graph-agent surveys on graph-supported capabilities, and dynamic-graph surveys on evolving graph structures. The figure then presents the authors' unified graph-transformation framing and the survey's contributions.

### 2. From component edits to graph changes

Prior surveys ask different questions. [*Graphs Meet AI Agents*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2506.18019) emphasizes how graphs empower agents. [[2507.21046|*A Survey of Self-Evolving Agents*]] organizes evolution around what changes, when it changes, and how. Xu et al. argue that neither perspective makes the coupling between persistent agent-state changes and graph-topology changes its central lens.

In the graph, typed nodes represent components such as memories, tools, skills, and agents. Typed edges represent dependencies, communication, and provenance. Schema-constrained rewrites describe persistent changes. The agent proposes changes, and feedback can lead to acceptance, rejection, or rollback.

The authors identify four patterns:

- **Node/feature evolution** revises, adds, merges, or removes a component or its attributes.
- **Edge/topology evolution** changes relations, such as workflow or communication links.
- **Subgraph activation** selects task-relevant support temporarily. It is read-only unless the selection is written back as a persistent change.
- **Cross-component co-evolution** is a dependent cascade spanning at least two component types, not merely several unrelated edits.

A full system may use several patterns. For example, a changed tool contract can make a skill that calls the tool, a workflow containing the skill, and a team relying on that workflow candidates for revalidation or rollback. The authors identify exposed cascade traces and affected-scope estimation as open problems.

### 3. Dynamic-graph learning as proposed infrastructure

The authors propose reusing dynamic-graph-learning methods for agent evolution. For example, text, update history, and graph connections could help rank a current branch-policy memory over a text-similar but stale one. Such scores can guide retrieval or activation, but they do not themselves authorize persistent changes. Under the proposed division of responsibility, learning methods provide signals or candidate proposals. Validated, localized, auditable graph rewrites govern persistent state changes. The authors describe this mapping as conceptual and say it has no empirical evaluation.

A conceptual infographic, not a plotted result. The top row names nine families, B.1–B.9. The middle row shows combinations of these families, such as B.1 + B.2 + B.7, connected to the bottom row's nine example tasks, including memory update, cascade prediction and revalidation, and privacy deletion and rollback. The diagram proposes possible uses; it does not show that they have been implemented or validated.

### 4. Judging the state, not just the answer

The proposed evaluation adds two graph-level checks. **Support-subgraph accuracy** compares the evidence activated for a decision with acceptable, temporally valid support. It rewards coverage while penalizing overly broad activation. **Rewrite locality** checks whether a proposed update fixes the target behavior while leaving unrelated behavior unchanged. Both checks require instrumentation. The first requires support-subgraph labels or logs, and the second requires rewrite traces and locality probes. Without them, graph counts are diagnostics, not primary metrics.

The governance checks also have conditions. Chronological splits should prevent future events from entering a decision. Deletion checks should look for residual influence in derived artifacts, not only for removal of the original node. The proposed deletion score is not a certified unlearning guarantee. Audit faithfulness requires replay or masking that preserves executable structure. Otherwise, explanations remain qualitative artifacts.

### 5. Make change traceable

The paper's contribution is a structural lens and research agenda. Persistent adaptation affects future behavior through dependencies, so the authors make state changes and evidence paths explicit for evaluation and governance to inspect. Its proposals remain conceptual and are not demonstrated improvements.
