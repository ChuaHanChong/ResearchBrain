# Learning Dexterous Manipulation Skills from Imperfect Simulations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley developed DexScrew, a framework that facilitates learning complex, contact-rich dexterous manipulation skills such as nut-bolt fastening and screwdriving. It combines simplified simulation for fundamental finger gaits with real-world, multisensory data collected via skill-based teleoperation, resulting in policies achieving up to 98.75% progress ratio on diverse nut shapes and 95.00% on screwdriving tasks.

## Problem

- Existing sim-to-real transfer methods struggle with accurately modeling complex contact dynamics and tactile sensing, creating a significant sim-to-real gap for dexterous manipulation tasks.
- Collecting large, diverse real-world datasets for dexterous multi-fingered hands via traditional teleoperation is challenging due to the inherent morphological differences between human and robot hands.
- Learning robust and generalizable manipulation skills for contact-rich tasks like fastening and screwdriving is difficult when a perfectly accurate, high-fidelity simulation is not feasible.

## Method

- **DexScrew** trains reinforcement learning policies for fundamental finger rotation gaits in a simplified simulated environment, abstracting intricate contact physics.
- Utilizes these simulation-trained policies as ==skill primitives== within a human-assisted teleoperation system to efficiently collect real-world, multisensory data, including crucial ==tactile feedback==.
- Applies ==behavior cloning== to the collected real-world dataset, training an autonomous policy that integrates proprioceptive and tactile feedback with ==temporal observation history== for robust execution.

## Results

- The DexScrew framework achieved high success rates on nut-bolt fastening (up to **98.75%** progress ratio on cross-shaped nuts) and screwdriving (**95.00%** progress ratio), outperforming direct sim-to-real and expert replay baselines.
- Policies incorporating both tactile feedback and temporal observation history consistently demonstrated superior performance, leading to improved progress ratios and faster task completion times across diverse object geometries.
- Learned policies exhibited robustness to external perturbations, such as dragging fingers away or opposite rotations, consistently recovering stable fastening behavior by re-establishing contact and correcting rotational direction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Imperfection in simulation can be effectively leveraged; simplified physics models are sufficient for learning foundational motion primitives, with real-world data bridging remaining dynamics and sensing gaps.
> - Real-world tactile sensing, when combined with temporal observation history, is critical for achieving robust and stable contact-rich dexterous manipulation, enabling policies to infer object state and recover from perturbations.
> - Skill-based assisted teleoperation offers an efficient and practical method for collecting high-quality, multisensory datasets for complex dexterous hands, mitigating the challenges of direct multi-finger teleoperation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic dexterous manipulation has seen advancements through reinforcement learning (RL) and sim-to-real transfer. Policies trained in simulation with techniques such as domain randomization have demonstrated a degree of robustness and generalization in real-world environments. However, two primary limitations persist:

1. **Complexity of Physics Simulation**: Accurately modeling complex contact dynamics, especially for intricate tasks, remains challenging. As tasks become more dynamic, the sim-to-real gap—the discrepancy between simulated and real-world physics—increases, rendering simulation alone insufficient for high fidelity.
2. **Sim-to-Real Gap in Sensing Modalities**: While visual sensing can be partially addressed with domain randomization, reliable tactile sensing simulation is difficult to achieve. Existing approaches to tactile simulation or proxy representations often do not fully leverage the capabilities of real-world tactile feedback.

Conversely, imitation learning and teleoperation approaches bypass simulation limitations by directly learning from real-world interactions and sensorimotor signals. However, teleoperating dexterous multi-fingered hands is challenging due to inherent morphological differences between human and robot hands, making it difficult to collect sufficiently large and diverse datasets for robust behavior and generalization.

This work, DexScrew, positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of both sim-to-real transfer and traditional imitation learning. It proposes a hybrid framework that leverages the strengths of large-scale simulation for learning motion primitives while utilizing real-world data to bridge the gaps in dynamics and sensing, particularly tactile feedback. The paper contrasts its approach with prior work that focuses on specialized high-fidelity simulation, extensive domain randomization, or real-world policy refinement (e.g., through residual models, stability controllers, or human interventions for simpler end-effectors). By combining simulation-trained skills with real-world tactile data collection via skill-based teleoperation, DexScrew aims to enable dexterous manipulation in contact-rich tasks that have traditionally been difficult to achieve autonomously.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that current reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer methods face significant challenges in dexterous manipulation tasks that involve complex contact dynamics and require multisensory feedback, particularly tactile information. The difficulty in accurately simulating these phenomena in high fidelity creates a substantial "sim-to-real gap" that limits the complexity of tasks achievable by robots. While teleoperation and imitation learning can bypass simulation, collecting sufficient, high-quality data for dexterous hands is impeded by the inherent differences between human and robotic morphology.

The key objectives of the DexScrew framework are:

1. **To enable learning dexterous manipulation skills for contact-rich tasks (specifically nut-bolt fastening and screwdriving) even when a perfectly accurate simulation of the environment is not feasible.** The research hypothesizes that the fundamental motion primitives for such tasks can be learned in a simplified simulation, even if the detailed physics (e.g., screw threads) are not perfectly modeled.
2. **To develop a method for efficiently learning transferable rotational finger gaits in simulation using simplified object models.** This aims to reduce the computational cost and complexity associated with simulating fine-grained contact interactions, allowing policies to focus on the core rotational behaviors required.
3. **To facilitate the collection of real-world, multisensory data, including crucial tactile and proprioceptive information, by integrating simulation-trained policies into a skill-based teleoperation system.** This objective addresses the challenge of directly teleoperating complex dexterous hand movements by abstracting them into high-level skill primitives that a human operator can activate, while controlling the broader arm motion.
4. **To train an autonomous behavior cloning (BC) policy that effectively coordinates arm and finger movements, incorporating real-world tactile feedback to achieve robust performance and generalization.** The goal is for this policy to overcome the dynamics and sensing mismatches inherent in the initial simplified simulation, enabling stable and reliable execution even on novel objects and under external perturbations.

By addressing these objectives, the researchers aim to provide a scalable path towards more complex dexterous manipulation abilities, thereby supporting the broader deployment of general-purpose robot hands in varied and unstructured environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DexScrew framework comprises three sequential stages designed to bridge the gap between imperfect simulation and real-world dexterous manipulation.

#### 3.1. Stage A: Training a Reinforcement Learning Policy in Simulation

This initial stage focuses on learning fundamental finger manipulation skills in a simplified simulated environment.

1. **Simplified Object Modeling**: Instead of modeling intricate details like thread structures, objects are simplified to facilitate efficient learning of rotational behaviors. For nut-bolt tasks, a thick triangular shape attached via a revolute joint to a fixed cylindrical base is used. For screwdriving, spherical, octagonal, or dodecagonal handles are used, also connected via a revolute joint. This abstraction reduces simulation complexity, focusing on inducing desired finger gaits for rotation.
2. **Training Pipeline**: A two-stage RL training process is employed:
 - **Oracle Policy Training**: An oracle policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with access to "privileged information." This includes ground-truth environment and object properties (e.g., position, scale, mass, friction coefficients), hand pose, finger configurations, and low-level controller parameters. The robot's proprioceptive state (joint positions, previous target positions) is also provided. The policy outputs relative target joint positions for the hand, converted to torque via a low-level PD controller.
 - **Sensorimotor Policy Training**: A sensorimotor policy is subsequently trained using DAgger. This policy operates without privileged information, instead inferring a predicted embedding (ˆz_t) from a 30-timestep history of proprioceptive states (h_t) using a prediction module. It is optimized to minimize the L2 loss between its actions and the oracle's actions, as well as between its predicted privileged embedding and the ground-truth privileged embedding.
3. **Reward Function**: The reward function for the simulation policy includes terms for task completion (encouraging positive angular velocity and finger-object proximity), energy penalties (for large joint torques and excessive joint power), and stability penalties (for deviations from an initial finger configuration and excessive angular velocity).
4. **Randomization**: Domain randomization is applied during training to enhance policy robustness. This involves randomizing object mass, center of mass, friction coefficient, size, and PD gains, along with adding observation and action noise.
5. **Termination Conditions**: Episodes terminate if fingers drift too far from the object, if the object remains stagnant, or if contact forces are near zero for a sustained period. This prevents the policy from getting stuck in unproductive states.

#### 3.2. Stage B: Real-World Data Collection with Learned Policy

This stage bridges the gap between the simplified simulation and real-world complexities, particularly those difficult to simulate accurately (like thread interactions and tactile feedback).

1. **Skill-Based Assisted Teleoperation**: The simulation-trained finger rotation policy is utilized as a "skill primitive." A human operator controls the robot arm's wrist movement (using a Quest VR controller joystick for yaw, pitch, and vertical translation) and decides when to activate the pre-trained finger rotation skill. This approach delegates the complex fine-grained finger movements to the robust simulation-trained policy, while the human provides high-level guidance for arm positioning and task progression (e.g., downward motion for fastening).
2. **Multisensory Data Recording**: During teleoperation, the system records two types of actions: the hand joint positions generated by the RL policy (or current joint positions when inactive) and the arm joint positions commanded by the human. Crucially, it also records multisensory observations, including all joint positions and raw tactile signals from the XHand's pressure-based tactile sensors (120 elements per fingertip across three axes).

#### 3.3. Stage C: Behavior Cloning with Multisensory Data

The final stage involves training an autonomous policy using the real-world dataset collected in Stage B.

1. **Neural Network Architecture**: A feedforward neural network is used. It processes a concatenation of past `K` timesteps of observations (joint positions and tactile signals). Tactile signals are first flattened and passed through a dedicated Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). The combined features are then processed by an hourglass encoder, which outputs action predictions.
2. **Action Chunking**: To improve temporal consistency, the policy predicts a sequence of `H` future actions (an action chunk) rather than a single next action. The paper uses `K=5` and `H=16`.
3. **Training**: The behavior cloning policy is trained using supervised learning, minimizing the L2 loss between the predicted action chunks and the corresponding expert action sequences from the collected real-world dataset. The Adam optimizer is used, and observations are normalized. Vision is explicitly excluded from this policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the DexScrew framework across two contact-rich tasks: nut-bolt fastening and screwdriving, using a UR5e robot arm and a 12-DoF XHand equipped with tactile sensors.

#### 4.1. Nut-Bolt Experiments

- **Baseline Limitations**: Direct sim-to-real transfer policies, while capable of rotating a nut, could not drive it downward, highlighting the gap in simulating thread interactions and coordinated arm movements.
- **Impact of Observation History**: Policies incorporating a short temporal history of observations showed substantial improvements in both progress ratio and execution time across all nut geometries (square, triangular, hexagonal, cross-shaped). This indicates that temporal cues aid in tracking rotational progress and discerning geometric features.
- **Importance of Tactile Information**: The inclusion of tactile input generally improved the progress ratio, particularly on complex and challenging nut shapes (e.g., triangular, cross-shaped nuts, where progress ratios increased from approximately 30% to 65% and 60% to 80%, respectively, when tactile sensing was added). This result underscores the role of tactile feedback in maintaining stable contact and detecting effective rotation.
- **Combined Modalities (Tactile + History)**: The combination of tactile sensing and temporal history yielded the highest overall performance. For instance, progress ratios reached 97.5% for square nuts, 96.25% for triangular, 95% for hexagonal, and 98.75% for cross-shaped nuts, with generally faster completion times.
- **Observed Failure Modes**: Policies without observation history struggled with object shape inference and generalization. Non-tactile policies frequently drifted into unstable contact states, losing alignment and preventing continuous rotation, with limited recovery capabilities. Tactile policies, in contrast, could recover through wrist and force adjustments.

#### 4.2. Screwdriving Experiments

- **Sim-to-Real and Expert Replay Baselines**: A direct sim-to-real policy achieved a 41.60% progress ratio, demonstrating basic rotational ability but never completing the task. Replaying collected expert data (Expert Replay) yielded a higher progress ratio of 50.80% but still failed to complete tasks fully, indicating a lack of adaptability.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) Performance**: The baseline behavior cloning policy (without tactile or history) achieved a 69.20% progress ratio, outperforming direct expert replay. This is attributed to the "filtered behavior cloning" effect, where only successful trajectories contribute to training.
- **Impact of Modalities**:
 - Adding observation history alone resulted in a comparable 67.63% progress ratio, suggesting its role in tracking progress and recovering from partial failures.
 - Tactile information alone provided limited benefit without history, but became complementary when history was included.
 - **Combined Modalities (Tactile + History)**: The policy with both tactile feedback and temporal history demonstrated the strongest performance, achieving a 95.00% progress ratio and significantly reducing the average rotation time to 187.87 seconds.
- **Observed Failure Modes**: Open-loop baselines commonly failed due to gradual handle slipping and accumulated orientation drift. BC policies without observation history struggled to stabilize the screwdriver. Non-tactile policies were unable to detect subtle torque imbalances and often lost stable contact under perturbations. The combined BC policy compensated for these issues by adjusting wrist orientation and applied forces.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Experiments

- **Out-of-Distribution Robustness**: The learned policies exhibited robustness to external perturbations not seen during training, such as dragging fingers away or rotating the object in the opposite direction. The policy consistently recovered to stable fastening behavior by re-establishing contact and correcting the rotational direction.
- **Tactile Visualization**: Analysis showed that tactile signals provide structured spatiotemporal patterns which the policy utilizes to infer contact phases. Stable contact activation signatures emerged during correct engagement, and deviations triggered corrective actions like re-engagement.

#### 4.4. Simulation Ablations

- **Role of Privileged Information**: Ablation studies in simulation confirmed that providing privileged information during oracle policy training leads to significantly higher rewards and more stable episode lengths. Removing privileged information (e.g., in an asymmetric actor-critic setup or entirely) resulted in noticeable performance degradation, highlighting its importance for effective policy learning.
- **Sensorimotor Policy Effectiveness**: The sensorimotor policy, when provided with proprioceptive history, was observed to approach the performance of the oracle policy, indicating the efficacy of the distillation process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DexScrew framework presents a significant contribution to the field of dexterous robotic manipulation, particularly for contact-rich tasks. Its main significance lies in offering a practical and scalable approach to overcoming the limitations of both direct sim-to-real transfer and pure imitation learning from teleoperation.

**Key areas of significance include:**

- **Effective Sim-to-Real Bridge for Complex Tasks**: The framework demonstrates that even imperfect simulations, when appropriately designed to capture essential motion primitives, can serve as a valuable bootstrap for learning complex real-world skills. By leveraging a simplified simulator to learn fundamental rotational finger gaits, the method avoids the computational burden and difficulty of creating high-fidelity simulations for intricate contact dynamics.
- **Validation of Tactile Sensing and Temporal History**: The research strongly validates the critical importance of real-world tactile sensing, especially when integrated with temporal observation history. Policies that combine these modalities consistently outperform those relying solely on proprioception or single-step observations. This highlights tactile feedback as an indispensable component for maintaining stable contact, inferring object state, and recovering from perturbations in contact-rich manipulation.
- **Efficient Real-World Data Collection**: The skill-based assisted teleoperation system significantly reduces the complexity of collecting real-world data for dexterous hands. By allowing a human operator to control high-level arm movements and activate pre-trained finger skills, it mitigates the challenge of directly controlling a multi-fingered hand, thereby enabling the efficient acquisition of diverse, multisensory datasets.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization**: The resulting behavior cloning policies demonstrate robust performance on diverse, including unseen, object geometries and under external perturbations. This level of generalization and resilience is crucial for deploying dexterous manipulators in unstructured and dynamic real-world environments.
- **Scalable Path for Dexterous Manipulation**: By outlining a staged pipeline that effectively combines the strengths of simulation for skill acquisition and real-world data for refinement, DexScrew offers a scalable solution for expanding the capabilities of general-purpose robot hands. This could accelerate the development of autonomous systems capable of performing a wider array of complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks.

**Potential impacts of this work include:**

- **Advancing Robotic Assembly**: The ability to reliably perform tasks like nut-bolt fastening and screwdriving autonomously could directly impact automated assembly processes in manufacturing, enabling robots to handle more intricate components and adapt to variations.
- **Robotics in Unstructured Environments**: The robustness and generalization capabilities demonstrated could facilitate the deployment of dexterous robots in less controlled environments, such as service robotics, disaster response, or household tasks, where object variability and unexpected contacts are common.
- **Future Research Directions**: The framework provides a foundation for future research into combining simulated and real-world learning. It highlights the need for further exploration into autonomous data collection methods, integration of vision with tactile and proprioceptive feedback, and the application of similar staged learning pipelines to even longer-horizon and more complex manipulation tasks involving diverse tools and objects.

The identified limitations, such as the continued reliance on human teleoperation for data collection and the focus on pre-installed/inserted objects, also provide clear avenues for future research, underscoring that while significant progress has been made, the journey towards fully autonomous, general-purpose dexterous manipulation continues.
