# Jetson-PI: Towards Onboard Real-Time Robot Control via Foresight-Aligned Asynchronous Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> Jetson-PI presents a framework for deploying Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on low-power edge devices, overcoming challenges of high inference latency and perception-execution misalignment for real-time robot control. This framework integrates a foresight-aligned asynchronous correction mechanism with confidence-based scheduling and system-level accelerations, boosting control frequency by 8.66x on Jetson Orin while maintaining task accuracy for complex manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- High computational complexity of VLA models requires high-end GPUs, leading to significant power consumption and reduced battery life for robotic systems.
- Deployment on low-power onboard devices results in substantial inference latency and low control frequencies (e.g., 0.7 Hz for ","0.5 on Jetson Orin), impeding real-time robot control and causing pauses.
- Asynchronous inference methods suffer from perception-execution misalignment, where VLA predictions are based on outdated visual inputs due to environmental changes during the inference period.

## Method

- A ==foresight-aligned asynchronous correction== mechanism uses a lightweight module to predict future ==VLM hidden states== conditioned on committed actions, mitigating ==perception-execution misalignment==.
- ==Confidence-based scheduling== dynamically skips VLM invocations when the future prediction confidence is high, reducing reaction time by leveraging the correction module's estimates.
- System-level optimizations within a ==llama.cpp==-based framework, including ==computation graph reuse==, ==GPU-resident intermediate buffering==, and ==flow matching unroll==, accelerate VLA inference on edge GPUs.

## Results

- Jetson-PI achieved an **8.66x** improvement in control frequency on Jetson Orin (from **0.70 Hz** to **6.06 Hz**) and a **3.48x** improvement on Jetson Thor, compared to naive PyTorch inference of π0.5.
- In simulation experiments on the LIBERO benchmark, Jetson-PI demonstrated a **14.8%** higher average success rate compared to VLASH and **3.9%** higher than RTC, especially with increased inference latency.
- Real-world robot experiments showed Jetson-PI enabled a robot to successfully complete a complex clothes folding task with accuracy comparable to **RTX 4090** deployments, significantly outperforming naive asynchronous inference on Jetson Orin.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting compressed, action-conditioned future VLM states is an efficient way to align VLA model predictions with the future environment, even with varying inference latencies.
> - Dynamically managing VLM invocations based on prediction confidence allows for an adaptive balance between low reaction time and prediction accuracy on resource-constrained devices.
> - Specific system-level optimizations that exploit VLA model characteristics (e.g., deterministic token length, iterative expert calls) are crucial for achieving real-time performance on bandwidth-bound edge hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant advancement in embodied artificial intelligence, demonstrating proficiency in a range of complex robotic tasks by translating raw observations and natural language instructions into action sequences. These models, often based on Vision-Language Models (VLMs), leverage extensive pre-trained knowledge and fine-tuning on robot demonstration datasets. State-of-the-art VLA models, such as the π series (e.g., π0 and π0.5), typically employ a "VLM + action expert" architecture where the VLM processes visual and linguistic inputs to generate contextual information (KV cache) for the action expert, which then uses flow matching for iterative action prediction.

However, the high computational complexity and large parameter counts of VLA models pose substantial challenges for their deployment on real-world robotic systems, particularly those relying on low-power onboard devices. Current practices often involve deploying VLA models on high-end GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 4090), which provide ample computational resources. This approach, however, introduces considerable power consumption, significantly reducing battery life and limiting the robot's operational duration and autonomy. Such setups confine many VLA applications to laboratory environments or necessitate online server deployment, which then introduces network latency and restricts the robot's physical activity area.

The deployment on resource-constrained onboard devices, such as the NVIDIA Jetson Orin, typically results in substantial inference latency (e.g., 1.4 seconds for π0.5 on Jetson Orin), leading to low control frequencies (e.g., 0.7 Hz). This low frequency translates to slow reactions to environmental changes and noticeable pauses between action chunks, impeding real-time robotic control.

Asynchronous inference has been explored as a strategy to mitigate this latency by overlapping the execution of a current action chunk with the inference for the next. Previous asynchronous approaches, such as SmolVLA and RTC, aim to mask latency or smooth action transitions. However, they frequently encounter two primary issues:
1. **Perception-Execution Misalignment:** The environment continues to evolve during the VLA model's inference period. By the time a new action chunk is predicted, the visual input that initiated the inference is outdated, leading to predictions that are misaligned with the current environmental state. This problem becomes more pronounced with increasing inference latency.
2. **Long Reaction Time:** Even with asynchronous inference, the robot's ability to react quickly to dynamic environmental changes remains bounded by the VLA inference time. The reaction time typically ranges from ∆ to ∆+L (where ∆ is inference latency and L is action chunk length), leading to suboptimal responsiveness, especially for complex, dynamic tasks. VLASH attempts to address misalignment by predicting future robot states, but this alone often proves insufficient to capture broader environmental changes, especially under longer latencies.

Another related area of research involves World Action Models (WAMs), which utilize predictive world modeling to inform action generation. These models often predict future images or latent state representations to guide current actions or define desired final states. Jetson-PI's approach shares conceptual similarities but differentiates itself by introducing a lightweight future correction module that dynamically predicts environment representations at an action step granularity, explicitly designed to address perception-execution misalignment in the context of varying VLA inference latencies on edge devices.

Jetson-PI positions itself within this landscape by specifically targeting the efficient and real-time deployment of VLA models on low-power onboard devices. It addresses the identified limitations of asynchronous inference by introducing a foresight-aligned mechanism and system-level optimizations, aiming to bridge the gap between VLA model capabilities and the practical constraints of embodied robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable robust and real-time control of robots by efficiently deploying computationally intensive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on low-power onboard devices. This objective is motivated by several critical challenges and limitations in current VLA model deployment strategies:

1. **Addressing Power Consumption and Portability Issues of High-End GPUs:** Current VLA models frequently require high-end GPUs, such as the NVIDIA RTX 4090, for adequate performance. While these GPUs offer substantial computational power, they consume significant energy (e.g., 450W for RTX 4090), which severely drains robot batteries (reducing battery life by up to 6.0× compared to onboard devices). This limits the robot's operational duration, restricts its activity area to tethered or localized environments, and increases overall operational costs. The motivation is to shift VLA deployment to low-power onboard devices (e.g., Jetson Orin, 30-50W) to enhance robot autonomy, portability, and applicability in diverse real-world scenarios.

2. **Overcoming High Inference Latency and Low Control Frequency on Onboard Devices:** When VLA models are deployed on resource-constrained onboard devices, their computational demands lead to high inference latency. For instance, a state-of-the-art VLA model like π0.5 exhibits an inference latency of approximately 1.4 seconds on a Jetson Orin, resulting in a low control frequency of around 0.7 Hz. Such low frequencies cause noticeable pauses between action chunks and slow reaction times to dynamic environmental changes, making it challenging for robots to perform complex manipulation tasks efficiently and responsively. The motivation is to significantly reduce inference latency and increase control frequency to enable smoother, more responsive, and real-time robot control.

3. **Mitigating Perception-Execution Misalignment in Asynchronous Inference:** Asynchronous inference, while partially masking latency by parallelizing action execution and subsequent inference, introduces a critical issue: perception-execution misalignment. During the inference period for the next action chunk, the robot continues to execute committed actions, causing the environment to change relative to the initial image input. This renders the VLA model's input observation outdated, leading to inaccurate predictions and a decrease in task success rates, particularly as inference latency increases. Existing solutions like VLASH, which predict future robot states, are often insufficient to account for complex environmental dynamics. The motivation is to develop a mechanism that can accurately anticipate future environmental states, conditioned on committed actions, to align action predictions with the actual environment at the time of execution.

4. **Reducing Long Reaction Times for Improved Robot Responsiveness:** Even with asynchronous inference, the robot's reaction time to novel environmental changes remains constrained by the VLA model's inference latency. The reaction time typically spans from ∆ (inference latency in action steps) to ∆+L (where L is the number of actions in a chunk). Longer average reaction times lead to degraded task success rates. The motivation is to reduce the effective reaction time without reintroducing pauses or compromising accuracy, thereby allowing the robot to respond more promptly and effectively to dynamic and unpredictable task environments.

In summary, the research aims to make VLA models practical for mobile robotics by developing an efficient, low-latency, and accurate deployment method for onboard devices, directly addressing the core challenges of power consumption, inference speed, perception-execution misalignment, and reaction time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Jetson-PI proposes a comprehensive approach for efficient VLA model deployment on low-power onboard devices, combining a novel foresight-aligned asynchronous correction mechanism with confidence-based scheduling optimization and system-level accelerations. The methodology is structured to address the identified challenges of perception-execution misalignment and long reaction times.

#### 3.1. Foresight-Aligned Asynchronous Correction

This core component aims to mitigate perception-execution misalignment by providing the action expert with information about the future environment.

- **Requirements for the Future Correction Module:**
 - **Action-conditioned prediction:** The module must predict future environment states based on the specific actions that have been committed for execution, as these actions directly influence environmental evolution.
 - **Lightweight design:** To avoid exacerbating latency, the module must have minimal computational overhead.
 - **Adaptability to varying ∆ (inference latency):** Inference latency varies across different hardware and power modes; thus, the module must effectively operate across a range of `∆` values.
- **Module Design:**
 - Instead of predicting future images or directly correcting the entire KV cache, the module predicts a compressed representation of the VLM's final-layer output at a future time `t + ∆`.
 - Inputs to the module are the compressed final-layer output of the VLM at time `t` and the sequence of committed actions from `t` to `t + ∆ - 1`.
 - The module consists of an MLP+Transformer block for processing action sequences, a Q-Former block for compressing the VLM state at `t`, followed by concatenation, projection, and two Transformer blocks.
 - It outputs a "correction item" (the predicted compressed VLM final-layer hidden state at `t + ∆`) to the action expert, enabling it to predict actions starting from `t + ∆`, aligning with the future environment.
 - Additionally, it outputs a scalar confidence estimate `c` for the future prediction, used for scheduling. The module has a small parameter size (40M, ~1% of the VLA model), ensuring negligible latency addition.
- **Two-stage Correction-aware Training Pipeline:**
 - **Stage 1:** Focuses on enabling the action expert to utilize future hidden states. The compressor and action expert are trained using the ground-truth VLM final-layer state at `t + ∆` and an action loss, teaching them to extract relevant information from future environmental representations.
 - **Stage 2:** Trains the future correction module itself. It takes the compressed VLM final-layer output at `t` and the action sequence from `t` to `t + ∆ - 1` to predict the compressed VLM final-layer output at `t + ∆`. The loss function includes an L2 norm between predicted and ground-truth future states (`L_predict`) and a term that penalizes discrepancies between predicted confidence and prediction accuracy (`λ||c + L_predict||^2`). `∆` is randomly sampled during training to enhance adaptability.

#### 3.2. Confidence-based Scheduling Optimization

This approach aims to reduce reaction time by dynamically managing the invocation frequency of the VLM and action expert.

- **Rethinking Roles:** The VLM is primarily for observing the current environment and ensuring accuracy. The action expert is for real-time action prediction. The key is to balance their invocations.
- **Scheduling Mechanism:** The confidence `c` predicted by the future correction module serves as the scheduler:
 - **High Confidence (`c > θ`):** If the future environment prediction is deemed reliable, the VLM is skipped. The action expert directly uses the *predicted* future environment representation (`h_t+∆_ae`) from the correction module to generate the next action chunk. This significantly reduces reaction time as the VLM's latency is bypassed.
 - **Low Confidence (`c ≤ θ`):** If confidence is low, indicating potential prediction error accumulation, the VLM is explicitly invoked. It processes the *actual* current observation (`o_t`), updating the KV buffer for the action expert and the hidden state buffer for the correction module. The correction module then predicts the future state based on this refreshed VLM output, and the action expert uses it for prediction.
- **Justification for Avoiding VLM/Action Expert Parallelization on Onboard Devices:** Roofline model analysis indicates that on onboard devices like Jetson Orin, both the VLM and action expert are bandwidth-bound. Attempting to run them in parallel would lead to severe contention for shared memory bandwidth, causing both components to slow down rather than speed up. This contrasts with high-end GPUs where VLM might be compute-bound, allowing for effective parallelization.

#### 3.3. System Design (Accelerations on llama.cpp)

Jetson-PI builds upon llama.cpp and incorporates system-level optimizations tailored for VLA inference on edge platforms to further reduce reaction time.

- **Exploited VLA Characteristics:**
 - **Deterministic Token Length:** VLA inference, given fixed camera configurations and image resolutions, typically maintains a constant token length, unlike generative LLMs where token length grows. Language instructions are also usually short and stable.
 - **Cross-Component Communication Overhead:** Traditional frameworks often transfer intermediate results (e.g., ViT output to LLM, LLM KV cache to action expert) via CPU memory, incurring latency.
 - **Repeated Action Expert Invocations:** The flow matching formulation of the action expert requires multiple denoising steps, each involving an identical computation graph, leading to repetitive graph invocation overhead.
- **Optimizations Implemented:**
 - **Computation Graph Reuse:** For the deterministic token length, the CUDA graph constructed during the first inference is reused for subsequent inferences, saving graph construction time. Language instructions are padded to a fixed length to maintain constant token length.
 - **GPU-Resident Intermediate Buffer:** Fixed-size buffers are pre-allocated in GPU memory for intermediate results between ViT, LLM, and the action expert. This avoids costly data transfers back and forth to CPU memory.
 - **Flow Matching Unroll:** The iterative denoising steps within the action expert's flow matching process are unrolled and fused into a single, unified computation graph. This reduces the number of graph invocation calls, despite a one-time increase in initial graph construction time, which is then amortized by reuse.

These methodological components collectively contribute to Jetson-PI's ability to achieve real-time, accurate, and efficient VLA model control on resource-constrained edge devices.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Jetson-PI includes simulation experiments, latency evaluation on different hardware, real-world robot experiments, and an analysis of the confidence-based scheduling.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted on the LIBERO benchmark using the π0.5 model, comparing Jetson-PI against synchronous inference (Sync.), RTC, and VLASH.

- **Success Rate (Table 3):**
 - Jetson-PI, both with Foresight-Aligned Asynchronous Correction alone ("Ours") and with Confidence-based Scheduling Optimization ("+Sched"), consistently outperformed VLASH and RTC across four sub-datasets (LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, LIBERO-10) and varying inference latencies (∆ from 1 to 9 action steps).
 - On average, Jetson-PI demonstrated a 14.8% higher success rate compared to VLASH and 3.9% higher compared to RTC.
 - As ∆ increased, indicating more severe perception-execution misalignment, VLASH and RTC exhibited significant performance degradation. In contrast, Jetson-PI maintained high accuracy. For ∆ = 9, Jetson-PI achieved success rates that were 45.6% higher than VLASH and 7.0% higher than RTC, averaged across the four datasets.
 - Compared to synchronous inference, Jetson-PI achieved comparable accuracy while eliminating the pauses between action chunks.

#### 4.2. Latency Evaluation

Inference latency and control frequency were measured on Jetson Orin and Jetson Thor devices, using the π0.5model.

- **Reaction Time and Control Frequency (Table 4):**
 - **Confidence-based Scheduling Optimization:** Reduced reaction time by 2.11× on Jetson Orin and 1.87× on Jetson Thor compared to naive π0.5 inference, primarily by selectively skipping VLM invocations.
 - **Computation Graph Reuse:** Further reduced reaction time by 2.96× on Jetson Orin by eliminating per-inference graph construction overhead.
 - **GPU-Resident Intermediate Buffering & Flow Matching Unroll:** Achieved 1.50× and 1.59× acceleration for the action expert component on Jetson Orin and Thor, respectively, by optimizing data transfer and reducing graph invocation calls.
 - **Overall Performance:** Jetson-PI achieved a substantial 8.66× improvement in control frequency (from 0.70 Hz to 6.06 Hz) on Jetson Orin and a 3.48× improvement (from 2.18 Hz to 7.59 Hz) on Jetson Thor, compared to naive PyTorch inference of π0.5.
 - When compared to `vla.cpp`, Jetson-PI also demonstrated a 5.41× improvement in control frequency on Jetson Orin.

#### 4.3. Real-world Experiments

Real-world validation was conducted using PrimeBot Research Institute's X2-W robot equipped with a Jetson Orin, performing a complex clothes folding task.

- **Task Success (Figure 7):** Jetson-PI enabled the robot to complete the complex clothes folding task (divided into three subtasks: picking, unfolding/folding, placing) with negligible accuracy loss when compared to deployments using an RTX 4090. It demonstrated clear improvements in success rates compared to naive asynchronous inference on the Jetson Orin. For example, in the "folding" subtask, naive async achieved 0/10 successes, while Jetson-PI achieved 8/10, close to the RTX 4090's 7/10.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Confidence-Based Scheduling

An analysis was performed to illustrate the adaptive nature and robustness of the confidence-based scheduling.

- **Confidence Evolution (Figure 9):** An example task ("pick up the black bowl...and place it on the plate") showed that the predicted confidence remained high for most of the task duration. However, at critical manipulation steps, such as grasping and placing, confidence temporarily dropped, triggering VLM invocations. This demonstrated the system's ability to adaptively balance real-time responsiveness with accuracy by invoking the VLM only when necessary.
- **Ablation on Confidence Threshold (Figure 8):** Varying the confidence threshold `θ` showed that Jetson-PI's performance was robust. While larger `θ` values led to more frequent VLM invocations and a slight decrease in control frequency, and smaller `θ` values reduced reaction time at the cost of slightly larger prediction errors, the method consistently achieved improvements in both success rate and control frequency compared to a configuration without scheduling optimization.

Collectively, these results indicate that Jetson-PI effectively enhances the efficiency, responsiveness, and accuracy of VLA model deployment on resource-constrained onboard devices, making real-time robot control feasible in challenging embodied tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Jetson-PI presents a method for deploying VLA models on low-power onboard devices, addressing several fundamental challenges that currently limit the widespread adoption of these advanced models in robotics. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Enabling Practical Onboard Robot Control:** A primary impact is enabling the deployment of computationally intensive VLA models directly onto robots using low-power edge hardware like the Jetson Orin. This capability moves VLA models beyond laboratory settings and high-end GPU clusters, which are constrained by power consumption, heat dissipation, and physical size. By achieving efficient onboard processing, Jetson-PI facilitates the development of truly autonomous and mobile robotic systems.

2. **Improving Robot Autonomy and Operation Duration:** By allowing VLA models to run efficiently on low-power devices, Jetson-PI directly addresses the significant battery life limitations imposed by high-end GPUs. Robots equipped with Jetson-PI can operate for extended periods without external power, expanding their functional range and utility in diverse applications such as field robotics, logistics, and assistive robotics, where energy efficiency is critical.

3. **Enhancing Real-Time Responsiveness and Control Frequency:** The substantial improvements in control frequency (e.g., 8.66× on Jetson Orin) and reduction in reaction time allow robots to interact with their environment more dynamically and smoothly. This is crucial for tasks requiring precise and timely responses to changing conditions, such as dexterous manipulation, fast navigation, and human-robot interaction. The elimination of pauses between action chunks contributes to a more fluid and natural robot behavior.

4. **Mitigating Key Asynchronous Inference Limitations:** Jetson-PI effectively addresses the critical issues of perception-execution misalignment and long reaction times, which have historically hampered asynchronous inference strategies. The Foresight-Aligned Asynchronous Correction module provides a mechanism to maintain prediction accuracy even under significant inference latencies, while confidence-based scheduling intelligently balances speed and accuracy, thereby overcoming common drawbacks of prior asynchronous approaches.

5. **Establishing a Foundation for Edge VLA Deployment:** The system-level optimizations (computation graph reuse, GPU-resident intermediate buffering, flow matching unrolling) implemented within a `llama.cpp`-based framework provide a practical blueprint for accelerating VLA inference on edge devices. These optimizations are complementary to other model-level techniques (e.g., quantization, pruning) and can serve as a foundation for future research and development in efficient edge AI for robotics.

6. **Expanding Application Scenarios for VLA Models:** By making VLA models deployable on resource-constrained devices, Jetson-PI broadens the scope of applications. Robots can be equipped with sophisticated vision-language capabilities in environments where network connectivity is unreliable or non-existent, or where immediate, on-device decision-making is paramount. This could accelerate the adoption of VLA models in manufacturing, healthcare, service industries, and exploration.

In summary, Jetson-PI represents a significant step towards practical, real-world deployment of advanced VLA models on mobile robotic platforms. Its contributions in addressing latency, power, and perception-action alignment challenges pave the way for more capable, autonomous, and versatile robots.
