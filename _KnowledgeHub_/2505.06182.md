# Apple: Toward General Active Perception via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The APPLE framework enables autonomous agents to learn general active perception policies by integrating reinforcement learning with supervised learning, optimizing for information-gathering actions and prediction accuracy across various tactile tasks. This approach achieves high performance in classification, regression, and pose estimation, demonstrating improved sample efficiency and robustness over previous methods.

## Problem

- Existing active perception methods are often algorithmically tailored to specific tasks and environments, relying on hand-crafted heuristics that limit their generality.
- Traditional reinforcement learning approaches for active perception can be sample-inefficient, hindering their scalability and practical deployment in real-world robotics.
- A unified framework is lacking that can accommodate diverse sensory inputs and prediction goals without significant task-specific modifications, impeding adaptable robotic capabilities.

## Method

- Introduces APPLE (==Active Perception Policy Learning==), an off-policy reinforcement learning framework designed to learn general active perception policies for various tasks.
- Formulates active perception as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) and optimizes a joint objective combining a policy gradient term with a differentiable supervised prediction loss gradient.
- Employs a transformer-based architecture for processing sequences of observations, enabling shared representations across Q-networks, action policies, and prediction policies.

## Results

- APPLE successfully learned exploration strategies and achieved high performance across five diverse tactile benchmarks, including classification (e.g., **89%** accuracy on TactileMNIST) and regression/pose estimation (e.g., **1.9cm**/13° error on Toolbox).
- APPLE methods consistently outperformed a random exploration baseline and the on-policy Haptic Attention Model (HAM), demonstrating significantly better sample efficiency and prediction accuracy.
- APPLE-CrossQ showed enhanced robustness by maintaining high performance when transferred to new environments without specific hyperparameter re-tuning and reduced training time by **53%** compared to APPLE-SAC.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly training an information-gathering policy and a prediction module using a unified objective, which combines RL rewards with a differentiable perception loss, is effective for learning task-agnostic active perception.
> - Off-policy actor-critic methods, particularly adaptations like APPLE-SAC and APPLE-CrossQ, offer superior sample efficiency and robustness for active perception compared to on-policy baselines.
> - The use of a transformer architecture for sequential observation processing proves more effective and stable than LSTM-based models for learning complex active exploration behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Active perception is a fundamental area of research in robotics, addressing the challenge of dealing with uncertainty in partially observable environments through deliberate information gathering. This capability is considered essential for autonomous agents operating in unstructured settings where sensor data may be sparse, noisy, or incomplete.

Historically, active perception has been explored across various sensory modalities. Vision-based active perception has received substantial attention, particularly for tasks such as object search and next-best-view planning, where a single observation can provide broad information coverage. In contrast, tactile sensing presents distinct challenges due to its inherently local and sparse nature; each contact provides limited information, necessitating purposeful interaction for effective information gathering.

Prior work in active tactile perception has focused on specific tasks, including shape estimation, texture recognition, and grasping. Many of these approaches rely on algorithmic designs tailored to specific objectives, often employing greedy information-gain heuristics, Gaussian Processes, or Bayesian optimization. Common limitations include assumptions such as stationary objects and reliance on sparse contact points, as well as the need for additional sensing modalities like vision in some cases. These methods are typically constrained by their task-specific objectives and underlying assumptions, limiting their general applicability.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a framework for acquiring more dynamic and general active perception policies, enabling agents to learn sequential decision-making strategies directly from interaction. Previous RL-based methods for active perception in the tactile domain have utilized algorithms such as REINFORCE, PPO, and DQN. Noteworthy among these is the Haptic Attention Model (HAM), which employed REINFORCE to learn exploration strategies for haptic object classification by jointly optimizing perception and action. However, on-policy RL approaches, such as REINFORCE and PPO, are often characterized by sample inefficiency, which can restrict their scalability and transferability to new tasks. Furthermore, the generality of active perception methods has not been extensively studied, with most existing approaches lacking a unified formulation applicable across diverse tasks and objectives.

This work addresses the aforementioned limitations by proposing a novel framework, APPLE (Active Perception Policy Learning), which aims to provide a general, task-agnostic, and RL-based solution for active perception. It positions itself by integrating reinforcement learning with supervised learning to learn information-gathering policies that are not bound to specific tasks or heuristics, thus contributing to the development of more versatile and robust robotic capabilities in environments requiring active tactile exploration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a principled, Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based algorithm capable of discovering active perception policies. This algorithm is intended to rely solely on a ground-truth label and a differentiable loss function during training, moving away from the need for hand-crafted, task-specific exploration heuristics prevalent in much of the existing literature.

A broader objective is to demonstrate the generality of such an approach, showing its applicability across a diverse range of active perception problems. This includes tasks requiring different output types, such as classification and regression, without modifications to the core framework or the necessity for specialized exploration strategies for each problem.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations identified in the current landscape of active perception research:

- **Lack of Generality:** Many existing active perception methods are algorithmically tailored to specific tasks (e.g., shape reconstruction, texture recognition, grasping) and environments. They often make strong assumptions (e.g., stationary objects, simplified contact models) that restrict their applicability to broader or more dynamic scenarios.
- **Reliance on Heuristics:** Traditional approaches frequently depend on hand-crafted optimization criteria or greedy information-gain heuristics to guide exploration. While effective in narrow settings, these heuristics can be brittle and do not readily generalize to novel tasks or environmental conditions.
- **Sample Inefficiency of On-Policy RL:** While RL has been explored for active perception, particularly on-policy methods like REINFORCE and PPO, these can be sample-inefficient. This limitation impedes their scalability and practical deployment, especially in real-world robotics where data collection is costly.
- **Need for a Unified Formulation:** The absence of a unified framework that can accommodate different sensory inputs and prediction goals without significant task-specific modifications hinders progress toward truly autonomous and adaptable robots.

By framing active perception within the context of Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) and integrating supervised learning, the authors aim to address these challenges. The intention is to create a system that can learn how to actively gather information by optimizing its actions to minimize a perception loss, thereby reducing uncertainty about specific environmental properties (e.g., object class or pose). This approach focuses purely on evaluating the agent's ability to perceive actively, deferring the integration of active perception into broader downstream manipulation tasks for future investigation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces APPLE (Active Perception Policy Learning), a framework designed to address various active perception problems by integrating reinforcement learning with supervised learning. The core methodology is based on optimizing a joint objective that trains both an information-gathering policy and a perception module.

**Problem Formulation:**
Active perception is formally defined as a special case of a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The hidden environment state `~h_t` is decomposed into `(h_t, y*_t)`, where `y*_t` represents the ground-truth value of the target property (e.g., object class, pose) that the agent aims to infer. The agent's action space `~a_t` is composed of control actions `a_t` (e.g., sensor movement) and a current estimate `y_t` of the desired environment property.

The overall reward function `~r` combines a differentiable prediction loss `ℓ` and an RL reward `r`:
`~r(h_t, y*_t, a_t, y_t) = r(h_t, a_t) - ℓ(y*_t, y_t)`.
Here, `ℓ` could be a cross-entropy loss for classification or Euclidean distance for pose estimation. The RL reward `r` is primarily used for regularizing the agent's control actions `a_t` and does not need to be differentiable. The objective is to find a policy `π(a_t | o_0:t)` that maximizes the expected discounted return `J(π)`.

**Optimization Objective:**
The gradient of the objective function `J(π)` is shown to decompose into two main components: a policy gradient term and a negative supervised prediction loss gradient term. This decomposition allows for joint optimization of the action policy and the prediction model. The policy gradient component guides the learning of information-gathering actions, while the prediction loss gradient directly optimizes the accuracy of the property estimation.

**Architecture and Input Processing:**
APPLE utilizes a transformer-based architecture inspired by Video-Vision-Transformer (ViViT) for processing sequences of observations.
1. **Image Encoding:** For high-dimensional visual or tactile image inputs (e.g., tactile images), a Vision Transformer (ViT) generates embeddings for each image.
2. **Sequence Processing:** These image embeddings are then concatenated with scalar inputs (e.g., sensor position) and processed over time by a sequence transformer. This transformer generates an embedding `m_t` for each time step.
3. **Shared Backbone:** The embeddings `m_t` are shared across the Q-networks, the action policy `π(a_t | o_0:t)`, and the prediction policy `π(y_t | o_0:t)`. This sharing is intended to promote a unified representation for both decision-making and perception.

**APPLE Variants (RL Algorithms):**
Two variants of APPLE are proposed, based on off-policy actor-critic RL methods, which are adapted for the active perception setting:
1. **APPLE-SAC:** Based on Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), an off-policy algorithm that learns a policy and two Q-networks by minimizing the Bellman residual and maximizing the expected return (with an entropy regularization term). SAC employs target networks for stability.
2. **APPLE-CrossQ:** Based on CrossQ, which is similar to SAC but replaces target networks with BatchRenorm layers in the Q-networks for stabilization. This is stated to offer computational advantages by reducing the number of forward passes.

**Adaptations for Active Perception:**
The standard SAC and CrossQ algorithms are adapted in three ways for APPLE:
1. **Partial Observability:** Instead of a complete state `s_t`, the policy and Q-networks receive a trajectory of past observations `o_0:t`.
2. **Dynamic Reward for Q-Networks:** During Q-network training, the total reward for computing the Bellman residual dynamically includes the differentiable prediction loss `ℓ(y*_t, y_t)`, resulting in `r_t - ℓ_π(y*_t, o_0:t)`.
3. **Augmented Policy Gradient:** The policy update is augmented by the prediction loss gradient, directly incorporating the supervised learning objective into the policy optimization.

**Experimental Setup:**
The framework is evaluated across five active perception benchmarks, primarily in the tactile domain, from the Tactile MNIST Benchmark Suite:
- **CircleSquare:** A 2D classification task using low-dimensional glimpses of simple shapes.
- **MHSB Classification:** A tactile classification task with four blocks, adapted from prior work.
- **TactileMNIST Classification:** High-dimensional tactile image classification of 3D MNIST digits.
- **TactileMNIST Volume Estimation:** Regression task to estimate the volume of 3D MNIST digits using tactile input.
- **Toolbox:** A 2D pose (position and orientation) estimation task for a wrench using tactile sensing.

**Baselines:**
- **APPLE-RND:** A random policy baseline, where actions are sampled uniformly, but the perception module is still trained. This helps evaluate the necessity of active exploration.
- **HAM (Haptic Attention Model):** An LSTM-based, on-policy REINFORCE method for tactile classification, adapted to use negative prediction loss as a reward for comparability.

Hyperparameters for all methods were tuned using the HEBO Bayesian optimizer. Metrics included average and final prediction accuracy for classification tasks, and average and final prediction error for regression tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of APPLE and its variants yielded several findings regarding its efficacy, generality, and comparison to existing methods:

**Overall Efficacy and Generality:**
- APPLE successfully learned exploration strategies across all evaluated active perception tasks, encompassing classification (MHSB, CircleSquare, TactileMNIST) and regression (TactileMNISTVolume, Toolbox).
- The framework demonstrated its ability to jointly optimize an action policy for information gathering and a prediction model for inference without requiring task-specific heuristics.

**Comparison to Baselines:**
- **APPLE-RND (Random Baseline):** The consistently lower performance of the APPLE-RND baseline across all tasks (e.g., 68% accuracy on CircleSquare, 74% on TactileMNIST, higher errors on regression tasks) underscored the necessity of active, structured exploration, indicating that APPLE's learned policies went beyond random behavior.
- **HAM (Haptic Attention Model):**
 - On the MHSB classification task, all APPLE approaches, including APPLE-RND, achieved near 100% accuracy around 250K steps, while HAM reached 68% at 1M steps and required 5M steps to converge to good performance. This highlighted HAM's sample inefficiency and a limitation of the MHSB task due to its small observation space.
 - On the CircleSquare task, HAM failed to achieve prediction accuracy beyond random guessing, despite extensive tuning and long training times (10M steps). In contrast, APPLE-SAC and APPLE-CrossQ achieved high accuracies (97% and 96%, respectively).

**Task-Specific Performance:**
- **MHSB Classification:** APPLE-SAC and APPLE-CrossQ approached 100% final accuracy.
- **CircleSquare Task:** APPLE-SAC (97% final accuracy) and APPLE-CrossQ (96% final accuracy) demonstrated strong performance, learning to follow a background gradient to efficiently locate and classify objects.
- **TactileMNIST Classification:** Both APPLE-SAC (87% final accuracy) and APPLE-CrossQ (89% final accuracy) achieved high performance. They gathered information more quickly and exhibited higher certainty in label predictions compared to the random baseline (74% final accuracy).
- **TactileMNIST Volume Estimation (Regression):** APPLE-SAC (0.99 cm³ final error) and APPLE-CrossQ (1.05 cm³ final error) outperformed APPLE-RND (1.07 cm³ final error). The task was noted as more challenging, with higher variances and performance fluctuations.
- **Toolbox Task (Pose Estimation):** APPLE-CrossQ achieved a final error of 1.9cm and 13° on average, significantly outperforming APPLE-SAC and APPLE-RND, even without specific hyperparameter tuning for this task.

**Robustness and Efficiency:**
- **Cross-Task Robustness:** APPLE-CrossQ exhibited greater robustness, maintaining high performance when transferred to new environments (e.g., Toolbox) without specific hyperparameter re-tuning.
- **Computational Efficiency:** APPLE-CrossQ, by avoiding target networks, required approximately half the transformer forward passes during training, leading to an average 53% reduction in training time compared to APPLE-SAC, without sacrificing performance.

**Learned Exploration Behaviors:**
- The policies learned by APPLE demonstrated intuitive exploration strategies. In CircleSquare, agents learned to follow the background gradient to locate the object and then stabilize their glimpse.
- In the Toolbox task, APPLE-CrossQ learned a circular search pattern to locate the wrench and then transitioned into a sliding motion along its handle to disambiguate its orientation.
- For TactileMNIST tasks, agents typically moved towards the center, then followed local edges or strokes of the digit, relying on aggregating multiple local cues for the final prediction. These emergent behaviors highlight the adaptability of APPLE's exploration strategies to environmental demands.

**Ablation Studies (CircleSquare):**
- **Model Architecture:** Replacing the transformer backbone with an LSTM model resulted in slower convergence and reduced performance, indicating the transformer's effectiveness.
- **Pure RL (without prediction loss gradient):** Training agents using only the combined reward `~r` (where `y_t` was part of the action space to be optimized by pure RL) resulted in highly inefficient and unstable learning, underscoring the critical role of the differentiable supervised loss term.
- **Static Grid-Search Policy:** A heuristic grid-search policy performed better than random but stagnated at 73% accuracy, confirming that the learned adaptive policies were more effective for tasks with limited time budgets requiring efficient information seeking.
- **Sparse Rewards:** APPLE variants successfully learned to solve tasks even when the perception reward was only provided at the final time step, indicating adaptability to sparse label availability.
- **Perception for Downstream Tasks:** In a "CircleSquareHideAndSeek" variant, the regular APPLE-CrossQ agent, which leveraged the prediction loss, significantly outperformed a pure RL agent, suggesting that the active perception bias can be beneficial for downstream tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of APPLE represents a significant step toward developing general active perception capabilities in robotics, offering several implications for future research and applications:

- **Advancing General Active Perception:** The primary significance lies in providing a unified, task-agnostic framework that moves beyond task-specific heuristics. By demonstrating efficacy across diverse problems (classification, regression, pose estimation) and sensor types (low-dimensional glimpses, high-resolution tactile images), APPLE contributes to a more generalized understanding and implementation of active perception. This addresses a key limitation in prior research, which often produced specialized solutions.

- **Principled Information Gathering:** The methodology of jointly training a perception module and a decision-making policy with a unified optimization objective (combining policy gradients with a supervised prediction loss gradient) offers a principled way to learn how to actively gather information. This approach avoids the need for manual design of exploration strategies, allowing robots to discover efficient information-seeking behaviors autonomously.

- **Enhanced Robotic Autonomy in Unstructured Environments:** Equipping robots with general active perception is crucial for their deployment in real-world, unstructured, and partially observable environments. For modalities like touch, where information is inherently local and sparse, actively probing the environment is often the only practical way to acquire sufficient information. APPLE's success in tactile benchmarks, including complex tasks like object volume and pose estimation, indicates progress toward more capable and adaptable robotic manipulation and interaction.

- **Robustness and Efficiency of Off-Policy RL:** The empirical results highlight the advantages of off-policy reinforcement learning (SAC and CrossQ) over on-policy methods (HAM), particularly in terms of sample efficiency. The robustness demonstrated by APPLE-CrossQ, maintaining high performance across tasks without re-tuning hyperparameters, suggests a practical framework for real-world applications where extensive tuning might be infeasible. Its computational efficiency, relative to other complex RL methods, further contributes to its practical impact.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for several future research directions:
 - **Sample Efficiency:** Addressing the high data requirements of transformer-based RL, potentially through pre-trained models or advanced sample-efficient RL techniques, is critical for real-world deployment.
 - **Real-World Robotics:** Transferring APPLE to actual robotic systems, potentially leveraging advancements in sim-to-real transfer and accurate tactile simulation, is a key practical goal.
 - **Multi-Modal and Multi-Fingered Perception:** The model's architectural design supports extension to multi-fingered robotic hands and integration of multiple sensing modalities (e.g., vision and touch), opening avenues for more comprehensive and dexterous manipulation tasks.
 - **Active Perception for Downstream Tasks:** Further investigating how active perception, guided by a differentiable loss, can serve as a beneficial inductive bias for solving larger downstream tasks (e.g., finding and retrieving objects) offers a promising research direction.

In summary, APPLE represents a significant contribution by providing a versatile, data-driven framework for active perception that can learn sophisticated exploration strategies across a range of tasks. Its potential impact lies in enhancing robotic intelligence and autonomy, particularly in scenarios where active information acquisition is paramount.
