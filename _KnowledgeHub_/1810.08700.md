# Safe Reinforcement Learning with Model Uncertainty Estimates

> [!summary] Summary
> A safe reinforcement learning framework for dynamic collision avoidance around pedestrians that estimates model uncertainty with MC-Dropout plus a bootstrapped ensemble of LSTM collision predictors. A model predictive controller penalizes predicted collision probability, its variance, and time-to-goal, so the agent cautiously avoids obstacles whose observations are novel, yielding fewer collisions than an uncertainty-unaware baseline in simulation.

## Problem

- Deep networks driving autonomous navigation are overconfident on out-of-distribution observations and fail silently, which is fatal in safety-critical pedestrian collision avoidance.
- Bayesian neural networks and variational or MCMC approximations that expose model uncertainty demand architectural restructuring and computation too costly for onboard real-time robot control.
- Prior safe RL uses external novelty detectors that abruptly switch to a safety controller, or reachability bounds too conservative for collaborative pedestrian environments; earlier uncertainty-aware RL handled only static obstacles.

## Method

- Collision prediction network: a **bootstrapped ensemble** of ==LSTMs==, each trained on resampled experience, predicts collision probability for each of 11 discrete motion primitives from masked variable-length observation histories.
- Uncertainty estimation: ==MC-Dropout== at test time (keep probability 0.7) samples each ensemble member repeatedly, giving sample mean and variance of collision probability over 100 parallelizable forward passes.
- ==MPC== action selection with adaptive variance: cost combines weighted variance, mean collision probability, and time-to-goal, with the variance weight annealed from negative to positive to encourage early exploration.

## Results

- Mean predicted-collision variance rose from **0.363** on the training distribution to **0.820** with added noise, **1.37** masked velocity, **1.93** dropped observations, and **2.41** masked position.
- The uncertainty-aware policy caused fewer collisions than the uncertainty-unaware baseline under added noise, masked position, and masked velocity, matching it on the training distribution and dropped observations.
- Annealing the variance penalty escaped a local minimum where the constant-penalty policy collided in **80%** of runs; 100 samples per step took **32 ms** on a 2-vCPU cloud instance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining bootstrapped ensembles with MC-Dropout gives spatially localized uncertainty: variance rises specifically for actions pointed at the novel obstacle, not just globally.
> - Feeding uncertainty into the controller cost, rather than tripping a binary safety-controller switch, produces smooth cautious behavior that preemptively steers clear of uncertain regions.
> - Caution: dropout-based uncertainty stays overconfident on some genuinely novel behaviors, so an uncertainty-averse penalty must be annealed or it suppresses exploration during training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a critical challenge in the deployment of autonomous systems, particularly those relying on deep neural networks (DNNs) in safety-critical applications: the overconfidence of DNNs in predictions for unseen or out-of-distribution data. This issue can lead to unpredictable and potentially hazardous outcomes, especially in domains like autonomous navigation and collision avoidance.

Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches, while achieving state-of-the-art results in various control and prediction tasks, often lack the inherent capability to quantify the uncertainty associated with their predictions. When confronted with data significantly different from their training distribution (out-of-distribution data), DNNs tend to provide confident, yet incorrect, predictions. This limitation is particularly problematic for safety-critical tasks such as avoiding pedestrians with autonomous vehicles, where misinterpretations of novel situations can have severe consequences.

Existing research in Safe Reinforcement Learning (Safe RL) has explored various strategies to mitigate these risks. Some approaches focus on external novelty detectors or internal modifications to identify environment or model uncertainty. Risk-sensitive RL (RSRL) often targets environment uncertainty, aiming to detect and avoid high-risk events that are known from training data but have low probability. However, many RSRL methods designed for Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are not directly applicable to neural networks. Furthermore, external novelty detectors typically trigger an abrupt switch to a safety controller, which can result in uncomfortable or unpredictable system behavior.

Another significant area of related work involves methods for extracting model uncertainty from neural networks. Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) are generally considered the state-of-the-art for this purpose. However, BNNs, even in their approximate forms (e.g., Markov Chain Monte Carlo, variational methods), often entail substantial computational costs and require significant architectural modifications, making them intractable for many real-time applications. Alternative, more computationally tractable methods, such as Monte Carlo Dropout (MC-Dropout) and bootstrapping, have emerged as practical alternatives for approximating Bayesian inference and generating uncertainty measures without extensive network restructuring. These methods have been previously applied in RL to guide exploration and for static obstacle avoidance.

This paper builds upon these foundational concepts by integrating computationally tractable uncertainty estimation methods (MC-Dropout and bootstrapping) into a Safe RL framework. It distinguishes itself by specifically addressing *model uncertainty*—the uncertainty arising from how well a model fits all possible observations, which can be high with limited or out-of-distribution training data. Unlike environment uncertainty, which accounts for inherent partial observability, model uncertainty specifically highlights situations where the network's predictions should not be trusted due to unfamiliarity with the input. The work extends previous uncertainty-aware RL frameworks, such as that by Kahn et al., by adapting them to more complex dynamic environments involving pedestrian collision avoidance, incorporating exploration-aiding mechanisms, analyzing regional uncertainty increases in novel dynamic scenarios, utilizing LSTMs, and enabling goal-guided actions. The paper thus contributes to the ongoing effort to make DNN-based autonomous systems more robust and reliable in the face of unforeseen real-world conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Safe Reinforcement Learning framework that enables autonomous systems to exhibit uncertainty-aware navigation, particularly in dynamic, safety-critical environments involving pedestrians. The core motivation stems from the inherent limitations of deep neural networks (DNNs) in producing reliable predictions when confronted with data that deviates from their training distribution.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing DNN Overconfidence in Safety-Critical Applications:** Deep neural networks, despite their performance, often generate overconfident predictions on unseen or out-of-distribution data. In safety-critical applications like collision avoidance for autonomous vehicles, an incorrect but confident prediction can lead to catastrophic failures. The paper is motivated by the need for systems that "know what they do not know" to prevent such failures.
- **Developing a Robust Collision Avoidance Policy:** The work aims to create a collision avoidance policy that is robust to novel observations. This involves identifying when observations are novel and subsequently taking actions that cautiously mitigate risk, rather than failing ungracefully due to misgeneralization.
- **Employing Computationally Tractable Uncertainty Estimates:** State-of-the-art methods for model uncertainty extraction, such as Bayesian neural networks, are often computationally expensive and impractical for real-time safety-critical systems. A key objective is to leverage computationally tractable and parallelizable methods, specifically MC-Dropout and bootstrapping, to provide real-time uncertainty estimates without significant restructuring of the neural network architecture.
- **Integrating Uncertainty into a Safe RL Framework:** The research seeks to embed these uncertainty estimation methods directly into a Safe Reinforcement Learning framework. This integration allows the learning agent to explicitly reason about the uncertainty in its predictions and adapt its behavior accordingly, particularly by avoiding regions of high uncertainty.
- **Generalizability from Simulation to Real-World Scenarios:** Autonomous navigation in environments with human pedestrians presents a particular challenge due to the complexities of human behavior and the difficulty of fully capturing all possible interactions in training data. The project aims to develop a framework that improves generalizability from simulated environments to the real world by identifying and reacting cautiously to novel pedestrian behaviors (e.g., uncollaborative pedestrians).
- **Enhancing Exploration in RL:** A secondary objective is to use uncertainty estimates to aid exploration during the training process. By adaptively penalizing highly uncertain actions, the framework can encourage the agent to explore directions of high model uncertainty in early training stages, thereby escaping local optima and converging to a more robust optimal policy.
- **Demonstration in a Simulated Dynamic Environment:** The research aims to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed uncertainty-aware framework in a simulated environment, specifically focusing on dynamic collision avoidance around pedestrians. This involves showing that the algorithm can detect novel observations, act more cautiously than uncertainty-unaware baselines, and achieve safer outcomes.

In summary, the motivation is to enhance the safety and reliability of autonomous systems, particularly in human-robot interaction scenarios, by equipping them with the ability to quantify and act upon their predictive uncertainties in the presence of novel or out-of-distribution data, using practical and scalable methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates model uncertainty estimates into a Safe Reinforcement Learning framework for dynamic collision avoidance. The core idea is to enable an agent to detect novel observations and react cautiously by avoiding areas of high predictive uncertainty. The approach consists of several interconnected components, as illustrated in the system architecture in Fig. 2.

**A. System Architecture Overview**
The system comprises an agent that observes its environment (pedestrian position/velocity, goal), feeds these observations into a Collision Prediction Network (an ensemble of LSTM networks), which then predicts collision probabilities for a set of potential motion primitives. Uncertainty estimates (mean and variance of collision probabilities) are derived from these predictions using MC-Dropout and bootstrapping. Alongside, a simple model estimates time-to-goal for each primitive. A Model Predictive Controller (MPC) then selects the optimal motion primitive based on a cost function that incorporates expected collision probability, uncertainty, and time-to-goal. The chosen action is executed, and collision labels are collected to update the network weights in an iterative observe-act-train cycle.

**B. Collision Prediction Network**
An ensemble of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks is employed to predict collision probabilities for a predefined set of motion primitives. LSTMs are chosen due to their demonstrated effectiveness in predicting pedestrian paths by capturing temporal intentions.
- **Input:** For each forward pass `i`, the network takes as input the history of observations (`o_t-l:t-1`) and actions (`u_t-l:t-1`), the current observation (`o_t`), and the evaluated motion primitive (`u_t:t+h`). Observations include the pedestrian's position, velocity, and radius, as well as the relative goal position of the RL agent.
- **Output:** The network outputs a collision probability `P_i_coll` (1 if collision, 0 otherwise) for the given motion primitive.
- **Motion Primitives:** The agent considers a set `U` of 11 discrete motion primitives, each defined by a heading angle `α ∈ [-π/6, π/6]` and a length `h=1` (one time step).
- **Variable Length Input:** Masking is used to handle variable length observation histories, allowing the LSTMs to process inputs of different lengths by deactivating irrelevant cells.

**C. Uncertainty Estimates with MC-Dropout and Bootstrapping**
To compute stochastic estimates of model uncertainty, `Var(P_coll)`, two methods are combined:
- **Bootstrapping:** Multiple networks (an ensemble) are trained independently. Each network is initialized randomly and trained on distinct, overlapping subsets of a larger experience dataset, sampled with replacement. This design ensures that networks agree on common data but diverge, exhibiting higher sample variance, in regions of uncommon or novel data. This process can be parallelized for computational efficiency.
- **MC-Dropout:** Dropout is activated during test time for each network in the bootstrapped ensemble. In each forward pass, network units are randomly deactivated based on a Bernoulli distribution with a keeping probability `p` (set to 0.7). By performing multiple forward passes with different dropout masks for each network, a distribution of predictions is obtained, approximating Bayesian inference in deep Gaussian processes.
- **Combined Uncertainty:** For `n_d` dropout samples per network and `n_b` networks in the ensemble, a total of `N = n_d * n_b` forward passes are sampled for each motion primitive. From these `N` predictions, the sample mean (`E(P_coll)`) and sample variance (`Var(P_coll)`) of the collision probabilities are calculated.

**D. Selecting Actions with Model Predictive Control (MPC)**
The agent selects the "safest" motion primitive `u*_t:t+h` by minimizing a joint cost function:
`u*_t:t+h = argmin_u∈U (λ_v * Var_N(P_i_coll) + λ_c * E_N(P_i_coll) + λ_g * t_goal)`
- **Cost Components:**
 - `Var_N(P_i_coll)`: The variance of collision probability, representing model uncertainty. This term encourages avoiding actions with high uncertainty.
 - `E_N(P_i_coll)`: The expected collision probability, representing the direct risk.
 - `t_goal`: The estimated time-to-goal for each motion primitive, encouraging progress towards the goal.
- **Weighting Factors:** `λ_v`, `λ_c`, and `λ_g` are weighting factors for uncertainty, collision cost, and goal cost, respectively. `λ_g` and `λ_c` are chosen such that the predicted collision cost `λ_c * E_N(P_i_coll)` is generally greater than the goal cost `λ_g * t_goal`, prioritizing collision avoidance.
- **Regional Avoidance:** Unlike some prior works that penalize velocity, this approach focuses on identifying and avoiding uncertain regions in the ground plane, allowing for more nuanced cautious behavior.

**E. Adaptive Variance Penalty (`λ_v`)**
During training, a constant, high penalty on uncertainty (`λ_v`) could hinder exploration, leading to suboptimal policies. To address this, `λ_v` is adaptively increased over time. Initially, `λ_v` might be set to a low or even negative value to encourage exploration in directions of high model uncertainty. As training progresses, `λ_v` is linearly increased (e.g., from -50000 to 200 in the experiments) to converge to an uncertainty-averse behavior during policy execution. This strategy allows the policy to explore effectively in early stages and then act cautiously in later stages.

**F. Collecting the Dataset (Experience Replay)**
- **Episode Roll-out:** The selected action is executed in the simulation environment. At the end of each episode, a collision label (`1_coll`) is returned (1 for collision, 0 otherwise).
- **Experience Storage:** The history of observations and actions for the entire episode, along with the collision label, is stored in an experience dataset.
- **Training Cycle:** After accumulating several episodes, random subsets from the full experience dataset are drawn to train the ensemble of networks. This observe-act-train cycle is crucial for allowing the agent to learn the reactions of dynamic obstacles to its own learned policy, going beyond a purely supervised learning approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that the proposed uncertainty-aware Safe RL framework is capable of regionally detecting novel obstacle observations, acting more cautiously, and achieving fewer collisions than an uncertainty-unaware baseline in dynamic scenarios.

**A. Regional Novelty Detection in 1D**
- **Observation:** In a simplified 1D test case using a two-layer fully-connected network with MC-Dropout and bootstrapping, the model accurately predicted collision/no-collision labels with low uncertainty for training distribution data (obstacles on the right-hand side of the agent).
- **Novelty Identification:** When presented with novel out-of-training observations (obstacles on the left-hand side), the network failed to generalize, yielding both collision and no-collision predictions for actions that would collide. However, crucially, it identified these regions of false predictions with high model uncertainty (light colors in Fig. 3b).
- **Cautious Action:** The MPC, leveraging these high uncertainty values, preferred conservative actions that were "certainly safe" (dark green lines in Fig. 3b) over false-positive "safe" but uncertain predictions. This resulted in the agent cautiously avoiding the novel obstacle while confidently approaching known obstacles (Fig. 4).

**B. Novelty Detection in Multi-Dimensional Observations**
The findings extended to a more complex multi-dimensional environment using a one-layer 16-unit LSTM model trained in a gym-based simulation.

- **Experiment Setup:** The agent was trained to avoid RVO-policy dynamic obstacles. Test scenarios introduced novel observations such as Gaussian noise (μ=0, σ=.5) on position/velocity, randomly dropped observations (20% probability), masked position, or masked velocity information. These manipulations were not present during training.
- **Regional Novelty Detection (Fig. 5, 6):** The model continued to regionally identify novel obstacle observations. For instance, when an uncertainty-aware agent encountered a dynamic obstacle with added sensor noise, collision predictions for actions directed towards the obstacle exhibited higher uncertainty than actions into free space. This led the uncertainty-aware agent to select actions with lower uncertainty, cautiously steering away from the noisy obstacle without collision (Fig. 6b). In contrast, an uncertainty-unaware baseline agent, unable to generalize to the noise, collided with the obstacle (Fig. 6a).
- **Novel Scenario Identification with Overall Uncertainty (Table I):** Across all tested novel scenarios (added noise, dropped observations, masked velocity, masked position), the overall model uncertainty (mean and standard deviation of `Var(P_coll)`) was significantly higher than in the training distribution test set. The magnitude of this uncertainty was interpreted as an indicator of the novelty of the observations for the model, with masked position information leading to the highest uncertainty.
- **Fewer Collisions in Novel Scenarios (Fig. 7):** The uncertainty-aware model consistently demonstrated greater robustness to novel scenarios, leading to fewer collisions compared to the uncertainty-unaware baseline. While both models performed similarly on training distribution samples, the uncertainty-aware model caused fewer collisions in cases with added noise, masked position, and masked velocity information. In the case of dropped observations, both models had similar collision rates, but the uncertainty-unaware model took longer to reach the goal. This indicates that the regional increase in uncertainty prompted the model to prefer more cautious avoidance actions.

**C. Using Uncertainty to Escape Local Minima**
- **Adaptive Variance Penalty:** The approach of increasing the variance penalty `λ_v` over time during training was shown to facilitate exploration and help the agent escape local minima.
- **Challenging Avoidance Case (Fig. 8):** In a challenging training scenario where a goal was blocked by a static obstacle and the agent was initially headed towards it, a policy with a constant `λ_v` often got stuck in a local minimum, resulting in collisions in 80% of the runs. The proposed policy, with an adaptively increasing `λ_v`, exhibited more explorative behavior in early stages and converged to a better minimum with significantly fewer collisions. In an easier avoidance case, both approaches performed similarly well.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the field of Safe Reinforcement Learning and the broader application of deep neural networks in safety-critical autonomous systems. Its significance and potential impact can be understood in several key areas:

- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness for Autonomous Systems:** The primary impact is the development of a framework that addresses a fundamental limitation of DNNs: their overconfidence on out-of-distribution data. By enabling autonomous agents to quantify and react to model uncertainty, the system can operate more safely in unpredictable real-world environments. This is particularly crucial for applications like autonomous driving, robotics interacting with humans, and critical infrastructure control, where unexpected scenarios can lead to severe consequences.
- **Practicality of Uncertainty Estimation:** The paper demonstrates the effective use of computationally tractable methods (MC-Dropout and bootstrapping) for extracting model uncertainty. Unlike more resource-intensive Bayesian neural networks, these methods are shown to be parallelizable and feasible for real-time applications, making the integration of uncertainty awareness more practical for deployed systems.
- **Proactive Risk Mitigation:** Instead of relying on abrupt transitions to safety controllers when novelty is detected, this framework allows the agent to proactively avoid uncertain regions. This leads to more graceful and predictable cautious behavior, potentially increasing user comfort and trust in autonomous systems.
- **Improved Generalization and Out-of-Distribution Detection:** The ability to detect novel observations through increased model uncertainty offers a mechanism for improving the generalization capabilities of RL policies. Autonomous systems can better handle scenarios not explicitly seen during training, such as unusual pedestrian behavior or sensor anomalies, by recognizing their unfamiliarity and responding conservatively.
- **Advanced Exploration Strategies in RL:** The finding that adaptively increasing the variance penalty (`λ_v`) during training can aid exploration and prevent convergence to local minima is a valuable contribution to RL training methodologies. This mechanism can lead to more robust and optimal policies, particularly in environments with complex reward landscapes or challenging initial conditions.
- **Foundation for Future Safety Research:** This work provides a foundation for further research into more accurately calibrated uncertainty estimates and stronger guarantees on model uncertainty in neural networks. The challenges identified, such as the observed overconfidence of dropout in certain novel scenarios (e.g., uncollaborative agents), highlight areas for continued development, pushing towards more rigorous safety assurances for deep learning models.
- **Bridging Simulation-to-Real Gap:** By enabling the agent to identify and cautiously react to novelties, the framework helps mitigate the challenges of deploying policies trained in simulation to the real world, where unforeseen situations are common. This step is critical for unlocking the vast capabilities of deep neural networks for practical, safety-critical tasks.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond simply detecting collisions to actively understanding *when* the model's predictions might be unreliable. By fostering this self-awareness in autonomous agents, it represents a significant step towards developing more intelligent, robust, and ultimately safer AI systems for real-world deployment.
