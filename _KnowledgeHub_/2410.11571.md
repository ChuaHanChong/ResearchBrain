# SDS -- See it, Do it, Sorted: Quadruped Skill Synthesis from Single Video Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for quadrupedal robots learns diverse locomotion skills from a single video demonstration, using a VLM (GPT-4o) and novel prompting to synthesize executable reward functions. This system achieved high-fidelity, stable, and zero-failure locomotion on a Unitree Go1 robot, while also generalizing to morphologically different platforms.

## Problem

- Quadrupedal robot skill acquisition typically involves high engineering overhead, such as costly motion capture (MoCap) systems, extensive manual reward function design, or precise controller tuning.
- Traditional methods and prior learning pipelines suffer from limitations in scalability, generalization, and transferability of learned skills across different robotic platforms or tasks.
- Existing large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) face challenges in applying to complex, high-dimensional, and contact-rich locomotion tasks due to limitations in temporal coherence, spatial reasoning, or reliance on specific descriptions.

## Method

- A fully automated pipeline, **SDS**, leverages ==GPT-4o== for multimodal reasoning and executable reward function synthesis from a single video demonstration.
- Novel prompting mechanisms, ==Grid-Frame Prompting (G_v)== and multi-agent ==SUS Prompting==, were developed to enhance the VLM's temporal understanding and decompose complex visual information into semantically structured subgoals.
- A 7-step closed-loop training process incorporates an iterative ==reward function evolution== mechanism that refines generated rewards using self-supervised signals from training footage (G_s) and performance metrics (Contact Plots).

## Results

- The framework achieved high imitation fidelity (DTW distances on the order of **10^-6**) and **100%** contact sequence matching for pace, trot, hop, and bound gaits on a Unitree Go1 robot, with stable, zero-failure real-world deployment.
- SDS successfully generalized to a morphologically different simulated ANYmal-D robot, achieving an average DTW score of **157 	imes 10^-6** and **95%** average contact sequence match for trotting and bounding skills.
- The method outperformed five state-of-the-art baselines in data efficiency (single video input), training time (**1 day** on a single RTX-4090 GPU), and engineering effort (fully automated reward generation).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Both Grid-Frame Prompting and SUS Prompting are critical for overcoming VLM limitations in temporal coherence and structured reasoning, enabling effective and meaningful reward function generation for complex motions.
> - The closed-loop reward evolution, guided by simulated footage and contact pattern feedback, is essential for progressively aligning generated reward functions with desired demonstrated behaviors, leading to accurate and stable gait imitation.
> - A general-purpose VLM, when combined with specialized prompting and an iterative self-supervised refinement loop, can fully automate the synthesis of executable, behavior-grounded reward functions directly from a single visual demonstration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of diverse and robust locomotion skills for quadrupedal robots represents a significant challenge in robotics. Traditional approaches often rely on methods such as costly motion capture (MoCap) systems for expert data extraction, extensive manual engineering of reward functions for reinforcement learning, or precise tuning of phase-scheduled controllers. These methods typically suffer from limitations including high computational cost, time-consuming development cycles, lack of scalability, and reduced generalization capabilities across different robotic platforms or tasks. They are often brittle, specific to a particular morphology, and demand significant human expertise.

Recent advancements in large-scale foundational models, particularly large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), have introduced new possibilities for robotic control by enabling perception-action alignment and goal-driven reasoning. While initial progress in leveraging these models has largely focused on manipulation tasks, their application to complex, high-dimensional, and contact-rich locomotion remains an area of active research. Prior work has explored using LLMs for automated reward function generation (e.g., Eureka), but these often require natural language descriptions and manual task-specific adjustments. VLMs have also been utilized for one-shot policy learning (e.g., RoboCLIP, VLM-RM), yet their generalization has been limited to manipulator-based tasks, and they often face challenges such as limited spatial reasoning or sensitivity to visual realism. Other approaches, such as physics-informed 3D reconstruction from monocular videos, can be computationally intensive and demand significant hardware resources for real-time operation.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing an automated framework that leverages the multimodal reasoning capabilities of a general-purpose VLM (GPT-4o) to synthesize executable reward functions directly from a single video demonstration. The paper aims to eliminate the need for MoCap data, handcrafted rewards, or predefined gait schedules, focusing specifically on periodic locomotion gaits, which are particularly sensitive to morphology-specific coordination demands. By introducing novel prompting techniques and a closed-loop evaluation and evolution mechanism, the SDS framework positions itself as an advancement in intuitive, data-efficient, and generalizable robot skill acquisition from visual demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automated and scalable framework, termed SDS ("See it. Do it. Sorted."), that enables quadrupedal robots to acquire diverse locomotion skills from a single video demonstration. The authors aim to achieve this without requiring extensive manual effort, such as reward engineering, motion capture, or specific gait schedules.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in current robot skill learning paradigms:
- **High Engineering Overhead**: Traditional methods for teaching quadrupedal robots new gaits necessitate considerable human intervention, either through laborious motion capture data collection, manual design and tuning of complex reward functions, or intricate scheduling of phase-based controllers. This makes the process time-consuming, expensive, and difficult to scale to a wide range of behaviors.
- **Lack of Generalization and Transferability**: Many existing pipelines produce skills that are brittle and specific to a particular robot morphology or task. Transferring learned skills to novel tasks or different robotic platforms often requires significant re-tuning, limiting their practical utility.
- **Complexity of Quadrupedal Locomotion**: Quadrupedal dynamics are inherently high-dimensional, contact-rich, and heavily dependent on the robot's morphology. These characteristics make it difficult to define appropriate reward signals or control strategies manually.
- **Leveraging Foundational Models**: Recent progress in LLMs and VLMs has unlocked new possibilities for aligning perception with action and enabling goal-driven reasoning in robotics. The authors are motivated to extend these capabilities to the challenging domain of mobile robot locomotion, moving beyond manipulator-centric applications.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of SDS are:
- To design a general framework for quadrupedal gait learning that operates solely from a single video demonstration.
- To introduce a novel multimodal prompting system for a VLM (GPT-4o) that can synthesize semantically grounded and executable reward functions. This involves developing methods to overcome VLM limitations in temporal coherence and visual reasoning for complex motion.
- To implement a closed-loop reward evolution mechanism that refines the generated reward functions using self-supervised signals from training footage and performance metrics, eliminating the need for predefined fitness metrics.
- To demonstrate the sim-to-real deployment of multiple diverse gaits (pace, trot, hop, bound) on a Unitree Go1 robot, validating the framework's ability to achieve high fidelity, stability, and zero-failure locomotion.
- To evaluate the framework's generalization capabilities across morphologically different quadrupedal platforms, such as the ANYmal robot.
- To demonstrate superior performance compared to prior state-of-the-art methods in terms of data efficiency, training time, and reduction of engineering effort.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SDS framework operates as an automated pipeline for quadrupedal skill acquisition from single video demonstrations, primarily leveraging GPT-4o for multimodal reasoning and code synthesis. The method is structured into two main components: novel prompting techniques and a 7-step closed-loop training pipeline.

**Prompting Techniques:**
The core of SDS's interaction with GPT-4o relies on two novel prompting mechanisms:
- **Grid-Frame Prompting (G_v)**: To address GPT-4o's limitations in native video understanding and temporal coherence, the demonstration video `V` is processed into a grid of adaptively sampled frames, `G_v`. Frames are sampled at an interval `τ` inversely proportional to the quadruped's velocity, `v`, resulting in denser sampling for slower movements to capture finer details. These `n` frames are arranged into a spatially uniform grid (`h=w=sqrt(n)`), which preserves temporal consistency while reducing token budget and enabling parallel visual processing by the VLM. To mitigate potential hallucinations and provide structured motion context, the grid inputs are augmented with ViTPose++ keypoints, mapping reward generation directly to the robot's movement.
- **SUS (See it. Understand it. Sorted.) Prompting**: This multistage, multi-agent technique aims to decompose complex visual information into semantically structured subgoals, enhancing the quality of generated reward functions. Given `G_v`, SUS employs a multi-agent framework where four specialized GPT-4o agents collaborate:
 - **Task-Descriptor Agent**: Identifies the primary task demonstrated.
 - **Gait-Analyzer Agent**: Analyzes contact sequences and potential gait patterns.
 - **Task-Requirement Agent**: Specifies key characteristics required to successfully replicate the demonstration.
 - **SUS-Prompt-Generator Agent**: Compiles the information from the preceding agents into a final, comprehensive SUS prompt, which is then used for generating reward functions.

**SDS Training Pipeline:**
The training process is an iterative, closed-loop system comprising seven steps:
1. **Reward Function (RF) Generation**: GPT-4o is prompted to generate a set of `n=8` candidate executable Python reward functions (`RF_i`). This generation is conditioned on `G_v`, the SUS prompt, and environment-specific code for NVIDIA IsaacGym. Each `RF_i` is a dictionary of individual sub-rewards (e.g., foot contacts, joint angles/velocities) and an aggregated total reward. A structured system prompt defines the expected code format and reward structure, while a user prompt injects task-level objectives. Candidate `RF_i` are validated for Python syntax, API compatibility, and dynamic importability; invalid ones are discarded.
2. **Isaac Gym PPO Training**: Each validated `RF_i` drives an independent PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) training run within IsaacGym. Policies are optimized using 4000 parallel quadruped agents over 1000 iterations. Sub-reward components are logged to monitor their contribution. Training incorporates PhysX for feasibility, torque clipping, and normalized rewards, with adaptive rescaling for unbounded reward terms.
3. **Reward Function Evaluation**: Post-training, each policy's behavior is evaluated by deploying it in a 1000-timestep simulation rollout. Keypoint trajectories are extracted using ViTPose++ and rendered into a temporally ordered image grid (`G_s`). Simultaneously, a binary foot-ground contact sequence is recorded and visualized as a Contact Plot (CP). A structured chat-style prompt is constructed using `G_s`, `CP`, and `G_v`, instructing GPT-4o to assess the behavior based on criteria like postural stability, gait periodicity, and trajectory adherence. GPT-4o assigns a numerical score (0-10) for each criterion, which are summed to determine the rollout's aggregate performance. The `RF_i` associated with the highest aggregate score is selected as `RF*`.
4. **Reward Function Evolution**: `RF*` serves as the seed for the next iteration's RF candidates. Its refinement is guided by an updated structured prompt that incorporates the `RF*` code, natural language summaries of scalar sub-reward statistics from PPO training logs, and feedback regarding execution success or errors. This iterative, conversational approach allows for gradient-free reward optimization, continuously improving reward function quality through behavior-grounded and simulation-informed refinement.

This closed-loop system aims to progressively align the generated reward functions with the desired demonstrated behavior, ultimately leading to policies that accurately imitate the skills from the single video input.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The SDS framework was evaluated on four distinct quadrupedal gaits: pace, trot, hop, and bound, representing skills of increasing dynamic complexity. Experiments involved 5 SDS iterations per skill, with training completed within one day using an NVIDIA RTX-4090 GPU. The learned policies were subsequently transferred zero-shot to a Unitree Go1 robot for real-world validation.

**Skill Learning Evaluation:**
- **Task Imitation (Dynamic Time Warping - DTW)**: DTW was used to quantify the frame-wise similarity between the demonstration footage and the SDS-learned policies. Keypoint sequences, extracted via ViTPose++ and spatially aligned with ICP, showed strong trajectory correspondence. All skills achieved DTW distances on the order of 10^-6, indicating high imitation fidelity. The bounding skill exhibited a slightly higher DTW value, which was attributed to tracking errors from motion blur during high-speed hind limb movements.
- **Gait Imitation (Contact Sequences)**: The fidelity of gait imitation was assessed by comparing simulated and real-world foot-ground contact sequences with the demonstrated patterns. All skills, both in simulation and real-world deployment, achieved 100% contact sequence matching over 1-minute evaluation runs, confirming the framework's ability to reproduce specific gait patterns accurately.
- **Locomotion Stability**: Real-world stability was evaluated by tracking base height fluctuations using motion capture and OpenCV. Pacing and trotting gaits exhibited low height fluctuation variances (3.6 × 10^-5 m and 5.6 × 10^-5 m, respectively). More dynamic gaits like bounding and hopping showed slightly larger, though still contained, fluctuations (2.2 × 10^-4 m and 3.6 × 10^-4 m). Robustness was further demonstrated by maintaining stable control under increased ground friction and 50–110N lateral pushes, with an average Stability-to-Speed ratio (StS) of 1.77 out of a maximum of 2 across all skills and perturbations. The policies demonstrated stable locomotion with zero failures during real-world tests.

**Learning Generalization & Component Ablations:**
- **G_v and SUS Prompting Ablation**: Removing either the Grid-Frame Prompting (G_v) or the SUS Prompting mechanism resulted in complete task learning failure, characterized by 0% contact pattern alignment and DTW distances exceeding 150. This highlights the criticality of both components: SUS ensures meaningful reward evolution and task-specific adaptation, while G_v provides temporal coherence and efficient visual processing.
- **RF Evolution (G_s, CP) Ablation**: Ablating the simulation footage grid (G_s) led to unnatural movements and a significant reduction in contact sequence matching (average 4%), indicating its role in enforcing global trajectory and motion structure. Removing the contact plot (CP) resulted in disrupted foot placement and instability, with an average 19% contact matching. Ablating both components led to complete task fidelity collapse, with 0% matching and DTW distances above 100, underscoring their necessity for accurate skill acquisition. The progressive alignment between generated RFs and task behavior across SDS iterations further validated the evolution framework.
- **Generalization to Morphologically Different Quadruped**: SDS was successfully generalized to a simulated ANYbotics ANYmal-D, a robot significantly different in weight, dimensions, and joint configuration (inverted rear knee joints) compared to the Unitree Go1. For trotting and bounding skills, ANYmal-D achieved an average DTW score of 157 × 10^-6 and a 95% average contact sequence match, demonstrating robust skill transfer across distinct robot morphologies.

**Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods:**
SDS was compared against five state-of-the-art methods (Eureka, DrEureka, RoboCLIP, SloMo, VLM-RM). SDS demonstrated superior performance across multiple metrics:
- **Data Efficiency**: SDS requires only a single video demonstration, eliminating the need for MoCap or extensive reward shaping.
- **Training Time**: SDS trained a single skill within one day on an RTX-4090, outperforming methods like SloMo (8 days on 8 RTX 3080 GPUs).
- **Engineering Effort**: SDS achieved fully automated reward generation and policy learning with no human intervention in reward design or additional task inputs, unlike methods requiring manual fitness design or specific task descriptions.
- **Imitation Accuracy**: SDS achieved a DTW distance of 1.3 × 10^-6 for the trotting skill, significantly outperforming other methods that showed DTW values greater than 10 or complete failure (∞).
- **Deployment**: SDS enabled fully onboard and real-time execution in the real world, a capability not universally shared by compared methods.

In summary, the results indicate that SDS can synthesize high-fidelity, stable, and generalizable quadrupedal locomotion skills directly from single video demonstrations, effectively addressing the limitations of prior approaches regarding data efficiency, engineering effort, and transferability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SDS framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic skill acquisition, particularly for quadrupedal locomotion. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating a fully automated pipeline for learning complex, periodic gaits from a single video demonstration, eliminating the need for extensive manual reward engineering, motion capture data, or predefined gait schedules. This approach leverages the multimodal capabilities of a general-purpose VLM (GPT-4o) through novel prompting techniques (G_v and SUS) and a closed-loop reward evolution mechanism, marking a departure from labor-intensive, domain-specific methods.

The potential impacts of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Democratization of Robot Learning**: By simplifying the process of teaching robots new skills, SDS could significantly lower the barrier for entry for researchers and practitioners. Non-experts might be able to impart complex behaviors to robots simply by demonstrating them, accelerating research and development in robotics.
- **Increased Autonomy and Scalability**: The automated nature of reward function generation and evolution, coupled with its ability to learn from single videos, enables robots to acquire new skills more autonomously and efficiently. This could lead to more adaptable robots capable of learning diverse behaviors in various environments without constant human oversight.
- **Enhanced Performance and Robustness**: The demonstrated high imitation fidelity (DTW in the order of 10^-6), 100% gait matching, and robust real-world stability with zero failures suggest that SDS can produce high-quality, deployable policies. The generalization to morphologically different robots like ANYmal further underscores its potential for broad applicability.
- **Advancement in VLM-Robot Interaction**: The novel prompting strategies (G_v and SUS) provide a blueprint for effectively utilizing general-purpose VLMs for complex physical tasks, overcoming their inherent limitations in temporal understanding and structured reasoning. This could inspire further research into more sophisticated human-robot interaction through natural demonstrations.
- **Reduced Development Costs and Time**: By minimizing human intervention and computational resources (1-day training on a single GPU), SDS offers a more cost-effective and time-efficient solution for robot skill learning compared to existing methods.

Despite these promising impacts, the paper also acknowledges several limitations that inform future research directions:
- **Demonstration Constraints**: The current system relies on side-view demonstrations with clear limb visibility. Generalization to arbitrary viewpoints, occluded settings, or animals with less distinct limb structures (e.g., furry animals) remains a challenge.
- **Skill Scope**: The method shows lower success for static or less dynamic skills due to reduced temporal variation and feedback signals. Its current focus on periodic locomotion would need expansion for non-periodic or trajectory-conditioned behaviors.
- **Environmental Assumptions**: The validation was conducted on flat terrain, implicitly assuming environmental similarity between simulation and demonstration. Real-world deployment in diverse, unstructured terrains would require further robustness enhancements and domain adaptation.
- **Simulator State Access**: The reliance on full simulator state may not be feasible in real-world scenarios, necessitating exploration of learning from onboard sensing alone.
- **Scalability for Multi-Skill Learning**: The current approach trains one skill per SDS run, indicating a need for hierarchical or skill-conditioned architectures for multi-skill integration within a single policy.
- **Morphological Generalization**: While demonstrating generalization to ANYmal, effectiveness on entirely different morphologies (e.g., humanoids) remains untested.

Addressing these limitations would further broaden the applicability and impact of the SDS framework, potentially leading to more versatile and intelligent robotic systems capable of navigating and interacting with complex real-world environments.
