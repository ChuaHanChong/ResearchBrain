# TaSA: Two-Phased Deep Predictive Learning of Tactile Sensory Attenuation for Improving In-Grasp Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Waseda University developed TaSA, a two-phased deep predictive learning framework that enables robots to perform tactile sensory attenuation for in-grasp manipulation. The system learns to distinguish between self-contact and external object contact, leading to significantly higher success rates in contact-rich precision insertion tasks, with absolute improvements ranging from 24% to 32% over a baseline method.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to accurately interpret tactile feedback during multi-contact in-hand manipulation, often conflating self-contact (fingers touching each other) with external object contact.
- Current robotic manipulation systems typically ignore self-contact or treat all tactile input uniformly, limiting their generalization and robustness in complex environments.
- The absence of a mechanism akin to human sensory attenuation leads to misinterpretations, unstable grasps, and potential damage during delicate tasks.

## Method

- A two-phase deep learning framework (TaSA) is introduced, starting with a 'Self-Touch Learning Phase' where a Fully Connected Network (FCN) predicts self-generated tactile signals based on robot joint positions.
- In the subsequent 'Motion Learning Phase,' a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network integrates the frozen self-touch FCN's predictions, allowing it to attenuate predictable self-touch signals from raw tactile input.
- The framework is applied to an Allegro Hand equipped with uSkin tactile sensors and evaluated on three precision insertion tasks: paper clip fixing, coin insertion, and pencil lead insertion.

## Results

- The self-touch FCN accurately predicted self-generated tactile signals during contact, with high correlation coefficients (r 

0.96 for thumb, r 

0.98 for index), demonstrating effective self-touch cancellation.
- TaSA (RT+Self) substantially improved task success rates over a baseline (RT only): 95% vs. 70% for paper clip fixing, 92% vs. 68% for coin insertion, and 58% vs. 26% for pencil lead insertion.
- Principal Component Analysis showed that attenuating self-touch reduced nuisance variance in the feature space, leading to clearer separation and increased inter-centroid distance between different object states and placements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mimicking human sensory attenuation, where predictable self-generated sensory input is filtered out, is critical for robots to robustly perceive and interact with external objects during contact-rich manipulation.
> - Explicitly modeling and learning self-touch dynamics allows a deep learning system to effectively disambiguate internal robot-robot contact from external object-robot contact.
> - Integrating a predictive self-touch model into a recurrent neural network for motion control enhances the clarity and discriminability of external tactile features, improving manipulation performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has made significant strides, yet achieving human-level dexterity, especially in complex in-hand tasks, remains a grand challenge. A critical hurdle is the robot's ability to interpret tactile feedback accurately when multiple contacts occur simultaneously – specifically, distinguishing between self-contact (fingers touching each other or the palm) and external contact (fingers touching an object). Most existing robotic manipulation systems either ignore self-contact by constraining motion or treat all tactile input uniformly, which severely limits their generalization to realistic, contact-rich environments and can lead to unstable grasps or damage.

This paper directly addresses this fundamental limitation by drawing inspiration from **human sensory attenuation (SA)**. In humans, SA is a well-understood neuroscientific principle where the brain predicts and down-weights the sensory consequences of its own actions, thereby making unexpected or externally generated stimuli more salient. Previous work in robotics has explored SA for body ownership, predictive coding, and self-calibration, but its application to fine, multi-finger in-hand manipulation with explicit self-touch disambiguation has been largely unexplored.

Current deep learning approaches for manipulation, while powerful, often fall short in explicit self-touch modeling. Many focus on object state estimation or general temporal robustness, treating all tactile inputs as external. Advances in high-resolution tactile sensors, such as the uSkin used in this study, have created the necessary hardware capabilities to capture the fine-grained contact data required for such disambiguation, yet the software frameworks to effectively leverage this information were lacking.

TaSA, therefore, fits into the broader landscape by bridging a crucial gap between biologically inspired principles and practical robotic dexterity. It moves beyond merely collecting or interpreting tactile data to actively filtering and contextualizing it, enabling robots to "perceive" contact in a more human-like, intelligent manner. This work directly pushes the boundaries of dexterous robotic manipulation by providing a robust framework for handling inevitable self-contacts, which is essential for complex tasks like precision assembly, tool use, and adaptive grasping in unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind TaSA stems from a recognized deficiency in current robotic manipulation: the inability of robotic hands to robustly perform in-hand manipulations that involve simultaneous contact between the object and multiple fingers, and crucially, between the fingers themselves (self-contact). Humans effortlessly navigate such scenarios by employing sensory attenuation to filter out predictable self-touch signals, allowing novel object stimuli to stand out. Robots, lacking this predictive filtering, risk misinterpreting self-touch as object contact, leading to errors, unstable grasps, or even damage to the object or the robot itself.

The **key objectives** of this research are:

1. **Develop a mechanism for explicit self-touch learning:** The framework aims to first explicitly model and learn the dynamics of self-touch, understanding how a robot's own joint movements generate predictable tactile feedback when its fingers or palm make contact with each other. This is crucial for distinguishing "expected" self-generated sensations from "unexpected" external object interactions.
2. **Integrate learned self-touch into motion learning for improved perception:** Once the self-touch model is established, the objective is to incorporate this predictive capability into the robot's motion learning phase. This integration allows the robot to "attenuate" or effectively filter out self-generated tactile signals, thereby emphasizing and clarifying the tactile information that arises from interaction with external objects.
3. **Enhance robustness and precision in contact-rich manipulation tasks:** The ultimate goal is to demonstrate that by implementing sensory attenuation for self-touch, robotic manipulation policies can achieve significantly higher success rates, greater stability, and improved generalization in tasks that demand fine tactile discrimination and inevitably involve complex contact dynamics.

In essence, the research seeks to equip robots with a more sophisticated understanding of their own tactile sensory experience, moving beyond a passive reception of contact signals to an active, predictive, and biologically inspired interpretation that prioritizes relevant external stimuli over predictable self-generated ones.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TaSA (Two-Phased Deep Predictive Learning of Tactile Sensory Attenuation) employs a novel, two-phase deep learning framework to achieve its objectives.

**Phase 1: Self-Touch Learning Phase**
This initial phase focuses on explicitly modeling the relationship between the robot hand's joint configuration and the resulting tactile feedback generated purely by self-contact, i.e., finger-finger or finger-palm interactions.
- **Model:** A Fully Connected Network (FCN) is utilized.
- **Inputs:** At each timestep, the FCN receives the current joint positions ($q_t$) and the desired joint positions from the previous timestep ($q_{des_{t-1}}$) for the 8 active joints (4 for the index finger, 4 for the thumb). The input dimension is 16.
- **Output:** The FCN is trained to predict the self-touch tactile state ($\hat{s}_t$). The tactile sensors employed are XELA Robotics' uSkin sensors, which provide high-resolution tri-axial force measurements (Fx, Fy, Fz) from 30 taxels per fingertip. With two fingertips (index and thumb), this results in a tactile data dimension of $60 \times 3 = 180$. The FCN also outputs an auxiliary joint state ($\hat{q}_t$) as a consistency check.
- **Training:** This phase is critical and is conducted solely under free-space or self-contact motions, meaning no external objects are present. This ensures that all tactile input during this phase is correctly attributed to self-touch. The FCN is optimized to minimize the prediction error between the predicted ($\hat{s}_t$) and measured ($s_t$) self-touch tactile signals. The training data includes a wide range of hand postures, from open-pinch to closed-pinch and rubbing motions, enabling the model to learn diverse self-contact patterns.

**Phase 2: Motion Learning Phase**
After the FCN for self-touch prediction is trained and its weights are frozen, this second phase integrates the learned self-touch model into a recurrent neural network for manipulation learning.
- **Model:** A Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is employed to capture temporal dependencies.
- **Inputs ($x_t$):** The LSTM receives a concatenated input vector comprising the current joint positions ($q_t$), the raw tactile sensor output ($T_t$), and critically, the *predicted self-touch tactile signal* ($\hat{s}_t$) generated by passing $q_t$ and $q_{des_{t-1}}$ through the *frozen* FCN from Phase 1. Commanded joint positions ($q_{cmd_t}$) are also included to inform the intended motion trajectory.
- **LSTM Outputs:** The LSTM predicts the next joint configuration ($\hat{q}_{t+1}$), the desired joint configuration for task execution ($\hat{q}_{des_t}$), and the predicted raw tactile feedback ($\hat{T}_{t+1}$).
- **Self-Touch Model Integration:** The predicted next-step postures ($\hat{q}_{t+1}$ and $\hat{q}_{des_t}$) from the LSTM are then passed through the *frozen* self-touch FCN to generate a forward prediction of the next self-touch signal ($\hat{s}_{t+1}$). This acts as a differentiable forward model of self-contact, enabling the network to reconstruct raw tactile output while explicitly maintaining a separation between external and self-induced contact.
- **Hardware and Data Collection:** The experiments are conducted on an Allegro Hand, a four-fingered robotic hand equipped with uSkin tactile sensors on the index and thumb fingertips. Training data is collected through a leader-follower teleoperation system, where a human operator controls a leader structure, and joint positions are transmitted to the Allegro Hand.
- **Tasks and Evaluation:** TaSA is evaluated on three precision insertion tasks: paper clip fixing, coin insertion, and pencil lead insertion. These tasks were specifically chosen because they are contact-rich and require fine tactile discrimination where self-contact and object contact can be ambiguous. The evaluation compares TaSA (referred to as "RT+Self" or "st-rnn") against a baseline method that uses only raw tactile input ("RT only" or "t-rnn"). Train-test splits are carefully designed for each task to probe generalization capabilities across varying object sizes, positions, and orientations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TaSA demonstrates significant improvements in robotic manipulation performance, particularly in contact-rich precision tasks, validating the hypothesis that sensory attenuation for self-touch is crucial for dexterous control.

**A. Self-Touch Prediction Accuracy**
In the self-touch learning phase, the trained FCN accurately predicts self-generated tactile signals. Across held-out test episodes, the predicted self-touch ($\hat{s}$) closely aligned with the raw tactile (RT) measurements for both the thumb and index fingertips. The error channel between predicted and raw self-touch remained near zero during sustained finger-finger contact, typically within a few arbitrary units (a.u.). Small error spikes (8–10 a.u. for the thumb, 25–30 a.u. for the index) occurred during rapid force transitions or transient re-grasp events, which are still significantly smaller than the typical self-contact magnitudes (40–70 a.u.). These localized deviations were interpreted as potentially beneficial, as they highlight unexpected changes that the controller might need to monitor. High correlation coefficients (r ≈ 0.96 for thumb, r ≈ 0.98 for index) confirmed the model's effectiveness in predicting and, by extension, canceling out self-touch.

**B. Task-Level Performance (Success Rates)**
TaSA (RT+Self) consistently and substantially outperformed the baseline (RT only) across all three precision insertion tasks:

- **Paper Clip Fixing:**
 - Baseline (RT only): 70% success (42/60 trials).
 - TaSA (RT+Self): 95% success (57/60 trials), representing a 25% absolute improvement.
 - TaSA achieved 100% success on the unseen "middle" placement for both clip sizes, demonstrating strong generalization. The improvement was particularly notable in the "front" placement, where self-touch often mimicked successful completion in the baseline.
- **Coin Insertion:**
 - Baseline (RT only): 68% success (61/90 trials).
 - TaSA (RT+Self): 92% success (83/90 trials), a 24% absolute improvement.
 - TaSA achieved 100% success on the unseen "middle" slot for all coin sizes, showcasing superior generalization and alignment capabilities. Failures in the baseline were concentrated with larger coins in the "left" slot, where edge grazing frequently occurred.
- **Pencil-Lead Insertion:** This was identified as the most demanding task due to the fine geometric constraints.
 - Baseline (RT only): 26% success (66/250 trials).
 - TaSA (RT+Self): 58% success (146/250 trials), demonstrating the largest absolute improvement of 32%.
 - TaSA showed improved robustness across both trained and unseen test angles, and for various lead diameters. Larger diameters (1.3mm, 2.0mm) had higher success rates than thinner ones (0.7mm, 0.9mm) due to stronger tactile signatures, but TaSA still provided significant gains across the board.

**C. PCA Feature-Space Analysis**
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the feature space provided qualitative evidence supporting TaSA's mechanism.
- In the **Paper Clip Fixing** task, the RT-only features showed overlapping clusters between different placements due to the influence of grasp preload and finger-finger shear, obscuring placement-specific differences. With RT+Self, this nuisance variance was reduced, leading to clearer class boundaries and increased inter-centroid distance, especially for challenging placements.
- For **Coin Insertion**, adding self-touch prediction resulted in a stabilized embedding. Clusters for different coin types and slots tightened and translated coherently, reducing variance from finger-finger preload. While visually similar coin thicknesses still partially merged, TaSA significantly reduced their covariance, leading to better distinction.
- In **Pencil Lead Insertion**, while the diameter pattern was somewhat visible with RT only, thinner leads showed significant overlap. RT+Self preserved diameter separation more clearly and also revealed some angle-based grouping, explaining the improved success rates even with the most challenging thin leads and larger angles.

Overall, the findings conclusively demonstrate that by explicitly learning and attenuating self-touch, TaSA provides a cleaner and more informative representation of external object contact, leading to substantially improved and more robust performance in complex, contact-rich robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TaSA framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, addressing a fundamental challenge that has long hampered the development of truly dexterous robots. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Human-like Dexterity:** By successfully implementing the principle of human sensory attenuation, TaSA allows robots to intelligently filter out predictable self-generated tactile signals. This is a crucial step toward achieving manipulation capabilities that rival human dexterity, where the ability to distinguish between self-touch and object contact is paramount for fine motor control and intelligent interaction.
- **Improved Robustness and Generalization in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The consistent and substantial improvements in success rates across diverse precision insertion tasks, especially in generalization to unseen object configurations, highlight TaSA's ability to enhance robotic systems. This increased robustness is vital for deploying robots in real-world, unstructured environments where self-contact is inevitable and external contacts require precise interpretation.
- **Foundation for Multi-Fingered Manipulation:** The paper explicitly positions TaSA as a framework extensible to multi-fingered manipulation. In such high-DOF systems, finger-finger and finger-palm interactions are even more frequent and complex. TaSA's approach of predicting and attenuating these structured self-touch patterns offers a robust method to disambiguate forces, which is critical for stable grasps, coordinated re-grasps, and intricate in-hand adjustments, thereby preserving object integrity and preventing damage.
- **Enhanced Tactile Perception and Control:** TaSA redefines how tactile information is processed in robotics. Instead of treating all tactile input equally, it provides a structured method for understanding the origin of contact signals. This leads to a clearer "perception" of the external object, allowing for more precise control responses and adaptive behaviors during manipulation. The PCA analysis vividly demonstrates how attenuating self-touch clarifies the feature space, making object-specific characteristics more discernible.
- **Broader Impact on Robotics Applications:** The ability to perform delicate insertion and manipulation tasks with high precision and reliability has wide-ranging implications. This could revolutionize applications in:
 - **Manufacturing and Assembly:** Automating tasks requiring fine motor skills, such as inserting small components or handling delicate parts.
 - **Service Robotics:** Enabling robots to interact more safely and competently with objects in human environments.
 - **Medical Robotics:** Potential for more precise surgical instruments or prosthetic hands that can accurately sense their environment.
- **Inspiring Future Research:** TaSA opens new avenues for research into biologically inspired sensory processing for robots. Future work could explore integrating other sensory modalities (e.g., proprioception, vision) with self-attenuation, adapting the framework for unknown objects, or extending it to more dynamic and unstructured manipulation scenarios. The concept of explicitly extracting and representing "external touch" as a separate input, briefly mentioned as a future direction, also holds significant promise for even finer-grained control.

In conclusion, TaSA is not just an incremental improvement but a fundamental step toward building more intelligent, adaptive, and human-like robotic manipulators by endowing them with a crucial aspect of biological sensory intelligence.
