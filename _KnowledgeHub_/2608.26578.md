# TrapVLA: Trapping Vision-Language-Action Models in Configured Failure Modes

> [!summary] Summary
> TrapVLA introduces Configured Failure Trapping, a backdoor attack where a natural-sounding textual prefix makes a vision-language-action policy fail in an attacker-specified manner (early gripper close/open, offset grasp/release). It pairs a replay-based data engine, a fidelity evaluation suite, and a Target Residual Steering loss that supervises trigger-induced action residuals, reaching 94-99 AVE while preserving clean success.

## Problem

- Prior VLA attacks treat failure as binary, targeting whether the task fails rather than how, so the adversary cannot specify the failure manner.
- Unconstrained trajectory deviations from adversarial or jailbreak attacks look abnormal, making them easier to spot than plausible errors resembling ordinary execution mistakes.
- Vanilla backdoor training suffers sparse action deviation: trigger spans the whole trajectory while only a short interval carries the deviating actions, so supervision is swamped.

## Method

- **TrapEngine**, a ==configure-then-replay data engine==, replays verified expert demos with modified action segments using geometric metadata, then pairs trajectories with GPT-generated, perplexity-ranked stealthy prefixes.
- **Target Residual Steering** adds an ==L1 loss== matching the predicted clean-versus-triggered output residual to the true residual on deviation data, while penalizing residuals beyond a tolerance on task-consistent data.
- **TrapEval** defines ==Configured Attack Success Rate==, checking that end-effector displacement at the first gripper transition matches the configured offset within tolerance, plus AVE combining attack rate with clean-success preservation.

## Results

- On Trap-LIBERO with OpenVLA-OFT, TrapVLA reaches AVE **98.7**/**98.9**/**95.8**/**94.1** across Object/Spatial/Goal/Long, versus **60.0-81.1** for DropVLA and **68.0-79.3** for vanilla backdoor baselines.
- Real-world pi-0.5 on a 6-DoF arm scores AVE **97.0** (eggplant) and **99.2** (cup), beating DropVLA by **44.7** and **43.3** points while keeping clean success at **93.3%** and **100%**.
- ONION detects only **2.7%** of **148** triggered prompts at its default threshold and never fully removes a trigger; a language-model judge flags **0%**, and synonym substitution costs only **0.8-1.6** points of attack rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When supervision is temporally sparse inside a long matched trajectory, contrasting paired outputs to supervise residuals recovers the signal that absolute-action losses drown out.
> - Attack ratio ablation shows more poisoned data is not the fix: success swings non-monotonically from 68.6 to 10.2 to 93.2, so supervision placement dominates quantity.
> - Fluent, semantically compatible text prefixes evade perplexity filters and language-model judges, so instruction-level defenses for embodied policies need behavioral rather than lexical checks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an advancement in embodied intelligence, integrating perception, language understanding, and robotic control. Models such as Octo, OpenVLA, and the π series have demonstrated capabilities in unifying these domains, moving towards general-purpose robotic agents. Concurrently, the deployment of such systems necessitates a focus on their safety and security.

Prior research into VLA security has largely concentrated on inducing generic task failures. These studies typically fall into three categories: adversarial perturbations (manipulating inputs at inference time to disrupt predictions), backdoor injections (compromising training to implant trigger-dependent malicious behaviors), and semantic jailbreaking (exploiting crafted instructions to bypass safety constraints). While these works have identified vulnerabilities, they commonly view failure as a binary outcome, assessing only whether a task succeeds or fails. This perspective overlooks the potential for adversaries to control the *manner* in which a robot fails, rather than simply causing arbitrary disruption.

This work addresses this specific gap by introducing "Configured Failure Trapping," a novel backdoor attack task. Unlike prior methods that treat any task failure as a successful attack, this research focuses on enabling an attacker to dictate the specific mode of failure, making the attack more precise, stealthy, and potentially harder to detect because the induced failures can resemble naturally occurring errors. It extends the understanding of VLA vulnerabilities from generic disruption to fine-grained control over malicious robot behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and investigate "Configured Failure Trapping," a novel backdoor attack task specifically designed for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This task aims to activate precise, attacker-specified failure modes in robots through stealthy textual triggers, while largely preserving the model's performance on benign, untriggered tasks.

The motivation for pursuing this line of research is multifaceted:

- **Limitation of Existing Attack Paradigms:** Current VLA security studies primarily focus on causing generic task failures, viewing failure as a binary event. This overlooks a more sophisticated threat where an adversary seeks to control *how* a robot fails, rather than simply making it fail unpredictably. This binary view is insufficient for understanding the full spectrum of potential malicious manipulations.
- **Need for Fine-Grained Control over Failures:** The paper argues that an adversary might desire precise control over robotic failures. For instance, causing a robot to grasp with a specific positional offset, release an object prematurely, or close its gripper too early. Such detailed control necessitates a different approach to attack development and evaluation.
- **Stealthiness and Detectability:** Configured failure modes are designed to be "behaviorally plausible." This means the induced failures can resemble errors that naturally occur during normal task execution, making them substantially more stealthy and difficult to detect compared to unconstrained or random failures. An attack that merely disrupts a task without control over the failure mode might be more easily identified.
- **Practical Implications for VLA Deployment:** As VLA models become more integrated into real-world applications, especially in safety-critical domains like manufacturing or healthcare, understanding and mitigating such fine-grained, controlled sabotage becomes crucial. The ability to induce specific, predictable failure patterns could have more severe and targeted consequences than random errors.
- **Addressing the "Sparse Action Deviation" Problem:** A significant technical challenge in developing such a fine-grained attack is that the textual trigger is present throughout an entire trajectory, but the *actual* action modifications that define the configured failure only occur over a small, localized interval. This "sparse action deviation" can cause the learning signal from the critical, deviant actions to be diluted and overshadowed by the numerous task-consistent actions, making it difficult to reliably associate the trigger with the intended failure behavior. The research is motivated to develop a method that can overcome this challenge.
- **Developing Comprehensive Evaluation Tools:** To properly study and benchmark such an attack, new metrics and data generation mechanisms are required that go beyond simple task success rates. This motivates the development of a data engine for synthesizing high-quality target trajectories and an automated suite for measuring configured-failure fidelity.

In summary, the work is motivated by the need to explore a more sophisticated and stealthy form of adversarial manipulation in VLA models, enabling an attacker to precisely dictate the nature of robotic failures, which poses a significant and underexplored security risk.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research defines Configured Failure Trapping as a backdoor attack on VLA models, characterized by two main criteria: (i) a predefined textual trigger induces target actions leading to a configured failure mode (high Configured Attack Success Rate, C-ASR), and (ii) the model maintains a high task success rate (SR) on clean data without the trigger. A "failure mode" is defined as a structured and plausible pattern of task failure.

To concretize this, four representative failure modes were instantiated, each parameterized by a configurable offset vector ∆ ∈ R³:
1. **Early Close (EC):** Gripper closes prematurely at `e_obj + ∆_ec`, preventing a valid grasp.
2. **Grasp Deviation (GD):** End effector is steered to `e_obj + ∆_gd` for an off-target grasp.
3. **Early Open (EO):** Gripper opens prematurely at `e_goal + ∆_eo`, releasing the object before the nominal placement.
4. **Release Deviation (RD):** End effector is steered to `e_goal + ∆_rd` for an off-target release.

### 4. Data Engine (TrapEngine)

TrapEngine is proposed for automatically constructing high-quality backdoor data, following a "configure-then-replay" strategy:
1. **Meta Data Collection:** Expert demonstrations are replayed, and only successful trajectories are retained. Time-aligned geometric metadata (timestep `t`, end-effector position `e_t`, target object position `e_obj`, and target placement position `e_goal`) is recorded for configuring attacks.
2. **Target Trajectory Generation:** For each successful benign trajectory and a given attack configuration, specific benign actions are replaced with target actions to induce a failure mode. This involves identifying the relevant manipulation phase and modifying corresponding segments via demonstration replaying. The resulting trajectories are categorized into "task-consistent data" (normal execution) and "target-deviation data" (intentional deviations).
3. **Backdoor Dataset Assembly:**
 - **Trajectory Verification:** Human inspection filters out infeasible or invalid target trajectories (e.g., unintended collisions).
 - **Trigger Selection:** GPT-5.4 generates candidate textual prefixes conditioned on each task prompt to ensure contextual compatibility and linguistic naturalness. These candidates are then ranked by perplexity (PPL/NLL) using an open-source language model, with the top-M lowest-perplexity candidates selected as stealthy textual triggers.

### 5. Evaluation Metrics (TrapEval)

To measure the activation and specificity of configured failure modes, TrapEval introduces:
- **Configured Attack Success Rate (C-ASR):** Measures whether the induced failure strictly conforms to the configured failure mode. It calculates the displacement `d = e_t* - e_target` at the first relevant gripper-transition event (`t*`). An attack is successful if `||d - ∆||₁ < γ`, where `∆` is the configured offset and `γ` is a tolerance threshold (set to 0.03).
- **Attack Validity and Effectiveness (AVE):** A composite metric that jointly assesses clean-task preservation and attack effectiveness. It is calculated as `0.5 * (min(1, SR_bd / SR_benign) + (1/n) * Σ C-ASR_j)`, where `SR_bd` is the success rate of the backdoored model on clean instructions, `SR_benign` is the baseline success rate, and `n` is the number of configured failure modes.
- Standard Success Rate (SR) for clean tasks.

### 6. TrapVLA Method

TrapVLA addresses the "sparse action deviation problem," where the learning signal from localized action modifications (instantiating failure) is overwhelmed by task-consistent actions across the trajectory.
1. **Problem Formulation:** The vanilla backdoor injection objective (`L_bad`) directly adds a term for imitating backdoor trajectories to the clean imitation learning objective. However, this approach struggles with sparse deviations because the trigger is present throughout the entire trajectory, even when actions are benign.
2. **Target Residual Steering (TRS):** TrapVLA introduces TRS to explicitly model and amplify the learning signal for trigger-induced action changes.
 - It operates on a `paired dataset` (`D_pair`), where each clean sample is aligned with one or more backdoor samples for the same task.
 - `D_pair` is partitioned into `target-deviation data (S)` (where salient action modifications occur) and `task-consistent data (S_c)` (where benign and target actions are similar).
 - The objective `L_trs(θ)` is defined:
 - For `p_i ∈ S`, it minimizes `||∆a_i - ∆ˆa_i||₁`, where `∆a_i = a*_i - a_i` is the target action residual and `∆ˆa_i = f_θ(I*_i, T*_i) - f_θ(I_i, T_i)` is the predicted residual. This aligns predicted deviations with intended failure deviations.
 - For `p_i ∈ S_c`, it minimizes `[||∆ˆa_i||₁ - ε]₊`, suppressing unnecessary trigger-induced residuals on task-consistent data while allowing minor variations within a tolerance `ε`.
3. **Overall Objective:** The final TrapVLA training objective is `L(θ) = L_bad(θ) + λ_trs * L_trs(θ)`, where `λ_trs` balances the contribution of TRS. This joint learning of absolute actions and trigger-induced residuals strengthens the association between the trigger and configured failure, improving reliability and clean-task preservation.

### 7. Benchmarks and Models

TrapVLA was evaluated on two new benchmarks:
- **Trap-LIBERO:** Built on LIBERO, including Object, Spatial, Goal, and Long suites.
- **Trap-RoboTwin:** Built on RoboTwin 2.0, including bimanual Shoes and Fan tasks.
Victim models included OpenVLA-OFT and π₀.₅. Baselines compared were Benign (clean-trained), DropVLA (triggers only at deviation data), Vanilla-I (vanilla backdoor with visual triggers), and Vanilla-T (vanilla backdoor with textual triggers).

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TrapVLA across simulation and real-world settings yielded several key findings regarding its effectiveness, stealthiness, and ability to inject configured failure modes.

### 9. Performance on Trap-LIBERO with OpenVLA-OFT (Table 1)

TrapVLA achieved the highest Attack Validity and Effectiveness (AVE) scores across all four Trap-LIBERO suites, ranging from 94.1% (Long) to 98.9% (Spatial). This indicates a strong balance between preserving clean-task performance (high SR) and reliably activating configured failure modes (high C-ASR). For instance, on the Object suite, TrapVLA achieved an SR of 96.8% and C-ASR values of 98.6% (Close), 99.0% (Grasp), 99.4% (Open), and 99.4% (Release).
In comparison, DropVLA showed some effectiveness for spatial deviations but was less consistent for temporal failures. Vanilla-I (visual triggers) had limitations, while Vanilla-T (vanilla textual backdoor) struggled particularly with inducing temporal failure modes like Early Close and Early Open. These results suggest that TrapVLA's explicit supervision of trigger-induced action residuals is effective for handling both spatial and temporal deviations.

### 10. Performance on Trap-LIBERO with π₀.₅ (Table 2)

When π₀.₅ was used as the victim model, Vanilla-T demonstrated improved performance compared to its results with OpenVLA-OFT. This was attributed potentially to π₀.₅'s dedicated action expert, which models continuous action flows, allowing for a stronger trigger-behavior association even with vanilla backdoor training. However, TrapVLA continued to achieve the best overall balance (highest AVE scores, e.g., 95.8% for Object), indicating its robustness across different VLA architectures and action generation paradigms (MLP-based vs. flow-matching-based). DropVLA, which inserts triggers only during short deviation intervals, was less effective for learning coherent target action flows in this context.

### 11. Performance on Trap-RoboTwin (Figure 5)

TrapVLA's effectiveness extended to more challenging bimanual manipulation tasks on the Trap-RoboTwin benchmark. It demonstrated high C-ASR for configured failure modes while successfully maintaining high task success rates on normal tasks for both Shoes and Fan tasks. This highlights the generalizability of TrapVLA across different robotic manipulation settings and complexities.

### 12. Real-world Evaluation (Table 3, Figures 6 & 9)

Experiments conducted on a physical ROKAE 6-DoF robot for tabletop pick-and-place tasks (eggplant and cup) confirmed TrapVLA's capabilities in real-world scenarios. TrapVLA achieved AVE scores of 97.0% (Eggplant) and 99.2% (Cup), significantly outperforming DropVLA. This demonstrated that TrapVLA could reliably activate diverse configured failure behaviors (e.g., premature gripper closure or off-target release) on a physical robot using only stealthy textual triggers, without degrading clean-task performance.

### 13. Trigger Stealthiness (Appendix A)

- **Linguistic Naturalness:** The selected textual triggers, generated by GPT-5.4 and ranked by perplexity, integrated naturally with original task instructions. Triggered instructions exhibited PPL/NLL scores very close to their clean counterparts, indicating minimal degradation in linguistic naturalness (Table 5).
- **Detection by ONION:** A standard backdoor detection method, ONION, failed to reliably identify TrapVLA's fluent textual triggers without extensively corrupting benign task text. At its default threshold, ONION detected only 2.7% of triggered prompts and failed to fully remove any trigger. Aggressive thresholds led to high false positives and collateral damage (Table 6, 7).
- **Detection by Codex:** A general-purpose language model (Codex, gpt-5.6-sol), when prompted to identify malicious modifications, classified all 185 test prompts (including 148 triggered ones) as CLEAN. This resulted in a 0.0% detection rate for triggers, suggesting high stealthiness against general language model scrutiny (Table 8).
- **Robustness to Synonym Substitution:** The attack remained effective even after substituting key words in the textual triggers with synonyms (e.g., "perspective" to "viewpoint"). The aggregated C-ASR decreased only marginally (0.8–1.6 percentage points), demonstrating that the attack does not depend on the exact lexical form but rather on the semantic content of the trigger (Table 9, 10).

### 14. Addressing Sparse Action Deviation (Appendix B)

Analysis of vanilla backdoor injection revealed that simply increasing the amount of backdoor data did not consistently resolve its learning difficulties. C-ASR varied non-monotonically with the backdoor-data ratio (Table 12), reinforcing the need for explicit supervision methods like Target Residual Steering to effectively associate triggers with sparse, localized action deviations.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that TrapVLA effectively injects configured failure modes into VLA models, maintaining high clean-task performance, and exhibits considerable stealthiness against detection mechanisms and lexical variations.

### 15. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces Configured Failure Trapping, a novel backdoor attack task that represents a shift in the understanding of security vulnerabilities in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Its significance and potential impact span both the research community and practical applications:

- **Novel Threat Model:** The most significant contribution is the formulation of a new class of backdoor attack. Moving beyond simply causing task failure, Configured Failure Trapping allows an adversary to dictate *how* a robot fails (e.g., specific spatial offsets, premature actions). This capability for fine-grained, deliberate sabotage is a more sophisticated and potentially insidious threat than unconstrained task disruption.
- **Enhanced Stealthiness:** The design of Configured Failure Trapping, leveraging stealthy and linguistically natural textual triggers, makes the attack difficult to detect. The induced failure modes are behaviorally plausible, resembling natural errors, which further obscures the malicious intent. This challenges existing detection mechanisms and necessitates the development of more robust countermeasures.
- **Precision and Control for Attackers:** This research demonstrates that an attacker can inject multiple distinct, configurable failure modes into a single VLA model, offering a high degree of control over the robot's behavior under specific trigger conditions. This expands the scope of potential malicious manipulation, allowing for targeted and predictable misbehavior.
- **New Benchmarking and Evaluation Paradigm:** The introduction of TrapEngine for synthesizing high-quality backdoor data and TrapEval for measuring Configured Attack Success Rate (C-ASR) and Attack Validity and Effectiveness (AVE) provides a framework for future research. These tools, along with the constructed Trap-LIBERO and Trap-RoboTwin benchmarks, enable systematic study and comparison of configured failure attacks and defenses.
- **Addressing Technical Challenges in Backdoor Injection:** The proposed TrapVLA method, particularly its Target Residual Steering (TRS) objective, effectively addresses the "sparse action deviation problem." By explicitly learning trigger-induced action residuals, it offers a solution for reliably associating localized malicious behaviors with global triggers, a challenge often faced in backdoor attacks on complex, sequential models.
- **Implications for VLA Security and Safety:** As VLA models are increasingly deployed in real-world, safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous systems, industrial robotics, healthcare), understanding and mitigating such sophisticated attacks becomes paramount. This work highlights a critical vulnerability that could lead to engineered accidents, espionage, or subversion of robotic systems. It underscores the urgent need for developers to consider not just task success, but also the *integrity* and *specificity* of task execution under various conditions.
- **Catalyst for Defense Research:** By demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of Configured Failure Trapping, this work is likely to stimulate new research into defense mechanisms. Future work may explore methods for detecting subtle behavioral deviations, identifying linguistically natural backdoor triggers, or developing robust training procedures that are resilient to such fine-grained manipulation.

In conclusion, this paper significantly advances the understanding of security vulnerabilities in VLA models by introducing a powerful and stealthy attack paradigm. It provides both the theoretical framework and practical methodology for injecting and evaluating configured failure modes, thereby setting a new benchmark for VLA security research and emphasizing the need for comprehensive defenses in the era of embodied AI.
