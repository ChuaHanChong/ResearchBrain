# Risk-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Oxford Robotics Institute developed the first framework that integrates Distributional Reinforcement Learning with distortion risk metrics to train risk-aware visuomotor policies for mobile manipulation. This framework enables runtime-adjustable risk sensitivity and successfully transfers these behaviors from privileged teacher policies to vision-based student policies via imitation learning, achieving comparable task performance to risk-neutral baselines while improving worst-case outcomes for risk-averse settings.

## Problem

- Mobile manipulators struggle to operate safely and reliably in uncertain, dynamic real-world environments due to compounded uncertainties.
- Standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) optimizes for expected return, neglecting the variance of outcomes and the critical tail-end risks of catastrophic failures.
- Existing risk-aware DRL methods are primarily applied to low-dimensional states, and transferring risk-aware behaviors to high-dimensional visual observations for mobile manipulation is an open challenge.

## Method

- A two-phase framework: first, a risk-aware 'teacher' policy is trained using Distributional PPO (DPPO) with privileged, low-dimensional observations and distortion risk metrics (Wang, CVaR).
- The teacher policy is explicitly conditioned on a scalar risk-sensitivity parameter, allowing dynamic adjustment of risk attitude during deployment.
- The learned risk-aware behaviors are then distilled into a 'student' policy, which operates on high-dimensional egocentric depth images, using the DAgger Imitation Learning algorithm.

## Results

- Risk-aware student policies achieve overall task performance comparable to risk-neutral baselines in both navigation and pick tasks.
- Risk-averse policies consistently demonstrate improved worst-case performance, as quantified by the **20%** Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) of the cumulative return, particularly in the challenging pick task.
- Risk-seeking policies achieve higher average cumulative returns but with increased variability, confirming the emergence of distinct risk-taking behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic, risk-aware DRL policies can be effectively learned for mobile manipulation and successfully transferred to vision-based student policies through imitation learning.
> - Training across a range of risk sensitivities (multi-beta training) can lead to more generalized and robust policies, even when evaluated in a risk-neutral context.
> - The teacher-student distillation approach is crucial for overcoming the sample inefficiency and high VRAM costs associated with direct vision-based RL on high-dimensional visual inputs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a crucial and active area of research at the intersection of mobile manipulation, reinforcement learning (RL), and safe autonomy. For robots to move beyond controlled laboratory settings and operate reliably in complex, dynamic, and human-inhabited environments, they must be capable of making decisions that explicitly account for uncertainty and risk.

**Mobile manipulation** combines the challenges of mobile navigation with the intricacies of robotic manipulation. Traditional approaches, such as model-based motion planning (e.g., CHOMP, Probabilistic Roadmaps), have excelled in static, well-defined environments. However, their reliance on accurate models, perfect perception, and up-to-date maps makes them computationally prohibitive and brittle in dynamic, unstructured spaces where real-time replanning is essential.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)** offers a compelling alternative by enabling robots to learn reactive control policies directly from sensor data. Recent advancements in deep RL have led to impressive successes in various robotic tasks. However, standard RL algorithms are typically formulated to maximize the *expected* return, inherently ignoring the variance of potential outcomes and the crucial tail-end risks of catastrophic failures. In safety-critical applications, such as robots interacting with humans or delicate objects, this risk-neutral approach is insufficient.

This limitation has led to the emergence of **Risk-Aware RL**. This subfield aims to optimize a risk-adjusted metric rather than just the expected return. **Distributional Reinforcement Learning (DRL)**, is a key enabler for risk-aware RL, as it models the full distribution of returns instead of just their expectation. This allows for the application of dynamic risk metrics, such as Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) or the Wang distortion risk metric, directly to the predicted outcome distributions. Previous applications of dynamic risk-aware DRL in robotics have primarily focused on low-dimensional proprioceptive states for tasks like quadrupedal, and humanoid locomotion, often dynamically adjusting risk sensitivity based on uncertainty estimations. Extending these methods to high-dimensional visual observations remains largely unexplored for complex mobile manipulation tasks.

Another significant challenge in applying RL to high-dimensional visual inputs is **sample inefficiency**. **Imitation Learning (IL)** and **policy distillation** frameworks, offer a common solution. These methods involve training an "expert" or "teacher" policy (often with privileged, low-dimensional state information or human teleoperation,) and then distilling its learned behaviors into a "student" policy that operates on more realistic, high-dimensional sensor data. While this teacher-student paradigm effectively bridges the gap between simulation and vision-based deployment for task expertise, existing frameworks have not explicitly focused on transferring *risk-aware* policy behaviors.

The authors explicitly identify and bridge this critical gap: developing a framework that integrates dynamic, risk-aware DRL with visuomotor policy distillation for mobile manipulation. This work is novel in its combination of learning risk-aware policies conditioned on egocentric depth observations with runtime-adjustable risk sensitivity, and demonstrating the successful transfer of these behaviors through Imitation Learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the pressing need for autonomous robots to operate safely and reliably in uncertain and dynamic real-world environments. For mobile manipulators, which combine navigation and interaction capabilities, the compounded uncertainties from perception, localization, and actuation introduce significant risks of undesirable outcomes. Standard reinforcement learning, by merely maximizing expected return, fails to account for these risks, particularly low-probability, high-cost catastrophic failures. This limitation severely restricts the safe deployment of robots in human-shared spaces.

The core objective of this paper is to enable mobile manipulators to make **risk-aware decisions** by reasoning about the full distribution of potential outcomes rather than just their averages. This capability allows robots to explicitly prioritize the avoidance of severe failures, thereby acting with an acceptable level of risk.

Specifically, the paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives, articulated as its main contributions:

1. **Develop the first framework to combine Distributional Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with distortion risk metrics** to train egocentric depth-based policies for mobile manipulation. A crucial aspect of this objective is to equip these policies with a **runtime-adjustable risk-sensitivity parameter**, allowing an operator or a higher-level system to modulate the robot's behavior from risk-averse to risk-seeking as needed.
2. **Demonstrate that these learned risk-aware behaviors can be successfully transferred** via Imitation Learning (IL) to a vision-based student policy. This student policy, conditioned on high-dimensional egocentric depth observations, should be capable of performing reactive whole-body control in unstructured and dynamic environments without prior maps, while preserving the risk-sensitive behaviors learned in simulation.

In essence, the authors aim to move beyond mere task competence to achieve **dependable autonomy**, where robots not only complete tasks but do so in a manner that accounts for and mitigates risks inherent in real-world operation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework is structured in two distinct phases:

**Phase 1: Training a Risk-aware Privileged Teacher Policy**

The first phase focuses on training an expert "teacher" policy ($\pi_\theta$) capable of exhibiting risk-aware behaviors. To overcome the computational challenges of direct training with high-dimensional visual inputs and to allow for efficient learning, this teacher policy is trained using **low-dimensional, privileged observations**.

- **Observation Space:** The privileged observations ($o_{teacher}$) include a ground-truth height scan ($\tilde{h}_t$), the robot's proprioceptive state ($s_{robot_t}$), task goals ($s_{goal_t}$), and other task-specific information ($s_{task_t}$). Importantly, the policy is explicitly **conditioned on a scalar risk-sensitivity parameter ($\beta$)**, which allows for continuous modulation of risk attitude during deployment.
- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** The authors employ **Distributional Proximal Policy Optimization (DPPO)**, a variant of PPO that incorporates distributional value estimation.
- **Critic Network:** A **Quantile Regression DQN (QR-DQN)** is used as the critic. Unlike standard RL critics that predict a single expected value, QR-DQN models the full value distribution $Z_\phi(s)$ as a mixture of Dirac delta functions at state-dependent quantiles. This provides a rich representation of the uncertainty in future returns.
- **Risk-aware Policy Updates:** To introduce risk sensitivity into the actor policy updates, **distortion risk metrics** are applied to the critic's predicted return distribution. Specifically, the Wang and CVaR metrics are investigated. These metrics generate a "distorted expectation" ($V_\beta(s)$) by re-weighting the quantile fractions based on the risk-sensitivity parameter $\beta$. For instance, a risk-averse policy (e.g., Wang $\beta > 0$) effectively shifts probability mass towards lower-value outcomes, making undesirable results appear more likely and thus penalizing risky states. This risk-sensitive value estimate $V_\beta(s)$ is then used in the Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) for updating the PPO policy.
- **Network Architecture:** The privileged height scan is processed by a small 2-layer CNN, then concatenated with other observations and the $\beta$ parameter. This combined input passes through an LSTM (for temporal dependencies) and a 3-layer MLP to output actions.
- **Training Details:** Policies are trained in the IsaacLab simulation environment using the RSL-RL library. A manually designed curriculum and domain randomization are employed to enhance training stability and transferability. During training, $\beta$ is uniformly sampled within defined ranges (e.g., $[-1.0, 1.0]$ for Wang, $[0.01, 1.0]$ for CVaR).

**Phase 2: Distilling into a Risk-aware Visuomotor Student Policy**

The second phase addresses the transition from privileged, low-dimensional observations to deployable, high-dimensional visual observations.

- **Imitation Learning (IL):** The risk-aware teacher policy is distilled into a "student" policy ($\pi_\psi$) that operates on egocentric depth images ($d_t$) along with robot proprioceptive and task information ($o_{student}$). The DAgger algorithm is used for this distillation process.
- **Student Architecture:** The student policy reuses the teacher's LSTM+MLP architecture, initialized with the teacher's weights. The teacher's height-scan CNN encoder is replaced with a **depth-image CNN encoder** ($E_{depth}(d_t)$) of matching output dimension.
- **Training Process:** The student policy is trained using supervised learning to minimize the L2 loss between its actions and the teacher's actions. The training proceeds in stages:
 1. Initially, the environment is stepped using teacher actions for a fixed number of episodes (600 in the pick task) to generate expert trajectories and mitigate distributional shift. During this stage, only the CNN depth encoder weights are updated.
 2. Subsequently, all student policy weights are unfrozen, and the environment is stepped using student actions, continuing the distillation process.
- **Robot and Tasks:** Experiments are conducted using a simulated Toyota HSR mobile manipulator, which has a holonomic base and a 5-DoF arm. Its head-mounted depth camera is fixed to observe the immediate frontal workspace. The policies output velocity commands for the base and joint position commands for the arm at 30Hz. Two tasks are evaluated:
 - **Navigation Task:** The robot must reach a 3D target while avoiding static and dynamically moving obstacles in a procedurally generated environment.
 - **Pick Task:** The robot must grasp and lift a cube to a target position above a table. This requires balancing quick execution with avoiding actions that could lead to dropping the cube or collisions.
- **Evaluation:** Extensive evaluations are performed on held-out test sets (32 environments for navigation, 32 environment-goal configurations for pick), with 25 rollouts per environment, across a range of risk-sensitivity parameters. Baseline comparisons are made against risk-neutral DPPO and PPO policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several important findings demonstrating the efficacy and characteristics of the proposed risk-aware mobile manipulation framework:

1. **Successful Transfer of Risk-Aware Behaviors:** The core hypothesis of the paper, regarding the transfer of risk-aware behaviors from teacher to student, is strongly supported. Analysis of the differences in individual reward terms between teacher and student policies in the navigation task (Fig. 7) shows that student policies maintain a stable performance gap relative to the teacher across varying risk sensitivities. This indicates that the risk-aware decision-making strategies learned by the privileged teacher are effectively distilled and replicated by the vision-based student. Furthermore, reward terms with higher weightings, which more strongly influence the teacher's policy, exhibit smaller relative differences, suggesting that the most impactful risk-aware behaviors are robustly transferred.

2. **Comparable Overall Task Performance:** The trained risk-aware student policies achieve overall task performance (e.g., task success rate, cumulative return) comparable to risk-neutral baselines (DPPO-Neutral, PPO) in both navigation and pick tasks (Fig. 5a, 5b). This is a crucial finding, as it demonstrates that incorporating risk-awareness does not necessitate a significant trade-off in general task competence. The framework enables safer decision-making without unduly sacrificing performance.

3. **Demonstrable Emergent Risk-Awareness:**
 - **Improved Worst-Case Performance for Risk-Averse Policies:** For the challenging "pick task" instance, risk-averse policies (e.g., Wang with $\beta > 0$ and CVaR with smaller $\beta$ values) consistently achieve better worst-case performance, as quantified by the 20% Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) of the cumulative return (Fig. 8). This means that even in the bottom 20% of outcomes, risk-averse policies perform better than their risk-neutral or risk-seeking counterparts, directly validating their ability to mitigate severe negative outcomes.
 - **Higher Average Return for Risk-Seeking Policies (with more variability):** Conversely, risk-seeking policies (Wang with $\beta < 0$) tend to achieve a higher *average* cumulative return (Fig. 8). Qualitatively, this is attributed to more aggressive strategies, such as attempting grasps sooner, which can lead to higher returns if successful, but also greater variability and increased risk of catastrophic failure (wider confidence bounds).
 - **Impact of Multi-Beta Training:** The observation that a DPPO-Wang policy evaluated at $\beta=0$ does not perform identically to a DPPO-Neutral policy (which is only trained for $\beta=0$) is noteworthy. This suggests that training across a mixture of $\beta$ values alters the state distribution encountered during learning, potentially leading to a more generalized or robust policy, even when evaluated in a risk-neutral context.

4. **Challenges with Specific Baselines and Extreme Risk Sensitivities:** The authors report difficulties in training PPO or DPPO-CVaR policies for the pick task, attributing this to the superior performance of DRL methods over non-distributional ones and known challenges in stabilizing CVaR policies. Furthermore, policies evaluated at very extreme risk sensitivities (e.g., CVaR with $\beta < 0.05$) showed performance degradation, likely due to unreliable risk-adjusted value estimates when emphasizing a very small, and potentially noisy, portion of the value distribution.

5. **Validation of Teacher-Student Approach:** The initial failure to train depth-based policies directly in simulation due to sample inefficiency and high VRAM costs (Fig. 6) underscores the necessity and effectiveness of the proposed two-phase teacher-student distillation approach. This validates the design choice of using a privileged teacher for initial learning, followed by distillation to a vision-based student.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the field of robotics by addressing a critical challenge for the widespread deployment of autonomous systems: safe and dependable operation in uncertain, dynamic, and human-inhabited environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging a Key Research Gap:** The paper is the first to successfully combine Distributional Reinforcement Learning with distortion risk metrics to train risk-aware visuomotor policies for mobile manipulation, explicitly demonstrating the transfer of these behaviors via Imitation Learning from privileged to vision-based policies. This bridges a crucial gap in current research, which has largely focused on either risk-awareness in low-dimensional state spaces or visuomotor control without explicit risk considerations.

2. **Enhanced Safety and Dependability for Mobile Manipulators:** By enabling mobile manipulators to reason explicitly about the full distribution of potential outcomes and prioritize the avoidance of catastrophic failures, this framework significantly enhances the safety and dependability of robotic systems. This is paramount for applications in sensitive domains like healthcare, elder care, logistics, and human-robot collaboration, where unexpected failures can have severe consequences.

3. **Runtime-Adjustable Autonomy:** The inclusion of a runtime-adjustable risk-sensitivity parameter ($\beta$) is highly impactful. It allows human operators or higher-level task planners to dynamically modulate the robot's behavior based on the current context, task requirements, or safety preferences. For instance, a robot might be more risk-seeking in a low-stakes task to achieve efficiency, but become highly risk-averse when operating near humans or fragile objects. This provides a flexible and adaptable control mechanism.

4. **Practical Pathway for Real-World Deployment:** The teacher-student distillation approach offers a practical and sample-efficient method to train complex visuomotor policies. By leveraging privileged information in simulation for an expert teacher and then distilling its knowledge into a vision-based student, the framework mitigates the notorious sample inefficiency of end-to-end vision-based RL and the challenges of direct sim-to-real transfer. This makes the development and deployment of risk-aware controllers for real mobile manipulators more feasible.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the need for hardware validation, incorporating epistemic uncertainty, direct risk-aware RL for student fine-tuning, improved reward shaping, and stabilizing extreme risk sensitivities, provide clear and exciting avenues for future research. This work lays a robust foundation upon which more advanced and robust risk-aware robotic systems can be built.

In conclusion, this paper presents a significant advancement towards creating more intelligent, safer, and dependable mobile manipulators. By equipping robots with the ability to learn and adapt their risk-taking behavior based on visual input and runtime parameters, it brings us closer to a future where robots can seamlessly and safely integrate into complex, dynamic human environments.
