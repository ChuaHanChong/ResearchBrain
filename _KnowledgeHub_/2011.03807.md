# Sim-to-Real Transfer for Vision-and-Language Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> A Vision-and-Language Navigation agent trained in the graph-based Matterport3D simulator on R2R is deployed on a TurtleBot2 with a 360-degree consumer camera and 2D laser. Color-jitter domain randomization handles visual gap; a U-Net subgoal model predicts nearby waypoints to replace the simulator's discrete graph actions, with ROS SLAM and path planning executing them. Real success reaches 46.8% with a prior map versus 55.9% in sim, dropping to 22.5% without any map.

## Problem

- Prior VLN progress is measured only in simulation, ignoring real sensing, actuation and localization errors, so component-level or simulated gains may misrepresent robotic progress.
- Existing instruction-following robots rely on artificial landmarks, test inside training environments, handle only a few commands or object types, or address manipulation rather than navigation.
- Panoramic graph simulators buy visual fidelity with low control fidelity: agents act by teleporting along graph edges averaging 2.1m, a space no physical robot possesses.

## Method

- A ==subgoal model==, a 4-stage **U-Net**, fuses a radial occupancy map from a 270-degree laser scan with panoramic **ResNet-152** features, outputting waypoint probability per range-heading bin, trained by Sinkhorn divergence against graph neighbors.
- ==Domain randomization== applies random color jitter (brightness/contrast/saturation 0.3, hue 0.01) to simulator panoramas during agent training, adapting to the Ricoh Theta V camera's limited dynamic range.
- A ROS harness wraps the standard VLN agent API with gmapping ==SLAM==, amcl localization and move_base planning, plus **Coda**: a digitized 325 square metre office with 111 crowdsourced instructions spanning 1,334m.

## Results

- The agent scores **55.9%** success in the Coda simulator versus **49.2%** on R2R test, showing the new environment and its instructions are in-domain and comparably difficult.
- Swapping to robot-camera panoramas costs **6.4%** success; domain randomization recovers part of it, cutting the drop to **3.9%** and the SPL drop from **6.2%** to **4.3%**.
- With a prior map the robot reaches **46.8%** success over 111 instructions with zero collisions or navigation failures; without any map it falls to **22.5%**, with only **29%** of predicted waypoints within 0.5m of ground truth.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language groundings learned in photorealistic simulation do transfer to unseen physical space; the dominant residual gap is action-space abstraction, not visual appearance or language.
> - Cheap color-jitter randomization recovers roughly 40% of the loss from a lower-quality camera, making expensive target-domain image collection unnecessary for modest visual shifts.
> - Abstractions that make simulation tractable become the transfer bottleneck: predicted waypoints miss narrow passages and hallucinate infeasible ones, arguing for continuous low-level action spaces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics aims to enable machines to perform tasks autonomously in diverse environments, often requiring interaction with humans. A significant challenge within this field is the development of robots that can interpret and execute unconstrained natural language instructions in novel, unstructured settings. Historically, evaluations of instruction-following robots have been conducted in limited contexts, such as small table-top environments or exclusively within simulations.

The task of Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN), introduced with datasets like R2R and environments like Matterport3D, has seen substantial progress in simulation over recent years. VLN agents learn to ground natural language instructions to visual observations and actions within photo-realistic virtual environments. This progress has been characterized by advancements in areas such as pragmatic speaker models, progress estimation, and backtracking mechanisms. However, a limitation of simulation-based evaluation is its inherent inability to capture real-world complexities such as sensing noise, actuation errors, localization inaccuracies, and the challenges associated with integrating disparate hardware and software components.

This paper addresses the "sim-to-real" gap, a critical challenge in robotics where models trained in simulated environments struggle to perform effectively when deployed on physical robots. It represents an effort to bridge this gap for VLN agents, moving beyond theoretical advancements in simulation to practical demonstrations on physical platforms. The work positions itself as one of the initial full-system evaluations of a robot performing unconstrained natural language instruction-following in a previously unseen physical building, directly assessing the real-world implications of simulation-based VLN research. It builds upon the cumulative advancements in VLN in simulation by attempting to transfer these learned capabilities to a robotic system.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate the feasibility and challenges of transferring a Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) agent, trained exclusively in simulation, to a physical robot for following unconstrained natural language navigation instructions in an environment previously unseen during training.

The motivation for pursuing this objective was multifaceted:

- **Assessing Real-World Implications of Simulation Progress:** Recent advancements in VLN within simulation environments have shown agents achieving increasingly high performance. However, the practical utility of these advancements for real-world robotics remained largely unverified. The researchers aimed to determine if the language groundings learned in simulation could effectively transfer to a physical robot operating in a tangible environment, thereby assessing the true implications of simulation-based progress.
- **Addressing Limitations of Prior Evaluations:** Most existing evaluations of instruction-following robots either focused on simplified, smaller-scale environments (e.g., table-top scenarios) or were conducted entirely in simulation. These approaches often neglect crucial real-world factors such as sensory noise, physical actuation constraints, localization errors, and the complexities of integrating various robotic components. This paper sought to conduct a comprehensive, full-system evaluation that accounts for these real-world challenges.
- **Bridging the Action Space Gap:** VLN agents in simulators typically operate with a high-level, discrete action space, often corresponding to traversing edges in a pre-defined navigation graph between panoramic viewpoints. Physical robots, in contrast, operate with low-level, continuous motor control actions. A significant motivation was to develop a mechanism to bridge this fundamental disparity in action spaces to allow a simulation-trained agent to command a physical robot.
- **Mitigating Visual Domain Differences:** The visual fidelity and characteristics of data collected in simulators (e.g., Matterport3D) can differ substantially from those captured by a physical robot's camera (e.g., consumer-grade 360° cameras). These visual domain shifts can degrade agent performance. A key motivation was to explore methods for adapting the agent to these visual differences without requiring extensive real-world data collection for training.
- **Enabling Reliable Sim-to-Real Comparison:** To accurately quantify the performance degradation or "sim-to-real gap," it was necessary to create parallel simulated and physical test environments. This involved digitizing a physical space and annotating it with navigation instructions, allowing for direct comparison under controlled conditions.
- **Investigating Cold-Start Performance:** A challenging yet realistic scenario in robotics is deploying a robot into a completely new environment without any prior mapping or exploration. The research was motivated to evaluate the VLN agent's performance in such a "cold-start" setting, where the robot must rely on its sensors and learned models to navigate from scratch.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a modular approach to transfer a simulation-trained VLN agent to a physical robot, integrating deep learning components with classical robotics systems.

**VLN Agent:** The foundation of the system was an existing state-of-the-art VLN agent, R2R-EnvDrop, trained on the R2R dataset. This agent leverages a 360° panoramic RGB sensor and operates within the Matterport3D simulator's discrete action space, defined by traversals between panoramic viewpoints.

**Test Environment and Data Collection:**
- **Coda Environment:** A 325m² commercial office space, named Coda, was selected as the previously unseen test environment. Its characteristics, including floor-to-ceiling glass walls and diverse room types, presented realistic challenges for robotic vision.
- **Simulator Construction:** Coda was digitized using a Matterport3D Pro 2 camera and web services, generating a point cloud, textured mesh, panoramic images, and a visibility graph. This data was then imported into the Matterport3D simulator to create a parallel Coda simulator environment.
- **Navigation Instructions:** Following R2R protocols, 111 natural language navigation instructions for 37 shortest-path trajectories were collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). These instructions were validated by human followers, ensuring quality and comparability to the R2R dataset.

**Robot Platform:**
- **Hardware:** A TurtleBot2 robot was used, equipped with a Kobuki mobile base, an Asus Xion Pro Live RGBD camera, and an Asus netbook. For 360° vision, a Ricoh Theta V 360° RGB camera was mounted at 1.35m (matching the Matterport camera height). A Hokuyo 2D laser scanner (270° range, 30m) was added for obstacle avoidance and mapping, mounted at 0.24m.
- **Software:** The robot ran ROS-kinetic. Standard ROS packages like `gmapping` (for SLAM), `amcl` (for localization), and `move_base` (for navigation) were utilized. PyTorch was used for the VLN agent and subgoal model, running remotely for computational efficiency.

**Evaluation Metrics:** Standard VLN metrics were employed in both simulation and physical robot experiments: Trajectory Length (TL), Navigation Error (NE), Success Rate (SR, defined as final position within 3m of goal), Oracle Success Rate (OS), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (NDTW), and Success-weighted Dynamic Time Warping (SDTW).

**Robot Pose Tracking:** An initial teleoperation phase was used to create a 0.05m resolution occupancy map of Coda using the laser scanner and ROS `gmapping`, which was then registered to the Matterport3D coordinate frame. During evaluations, robot pose was tracked using the ROS `amcl` particle filter.

**Adapting the VLN Agent for Sim-to-Real Transfer:**

- **Visual Domain Adaptation:**
 - **Challenge:** Discrepancies between the high-quality Matterport camera images (used for VLN agent training) and the lower-quality, consumer-grade Ricoh Theta V camera images.
 - **Approach:** Domain randomization was applied during VLN agent training. Random color jitter (varying brightness, contrast, saturation by 0.3 and hue by 0.01) was applied to each panoramic image in the Matterport simulator to simulate diverse lighting conditions and camera characteristics.
- **Action Space Adaptation (Subgoal Model):**
 - **Challenge:** Bridging the discrete, graph-based action space of the simulator with the continuous motor control of a physical robot.
 - **Approach:** A subgoal model was proposed to predict navigable nearby waypoints.
 - **Input:** The model took the most recent 2D laser scan (represented as a radial occupancy map) and ResNet-152 CNN features extracted from 12 headings and 3 elevations of the 360° RGB image.
 - **Architecture:** A 4-stage U-Net architecture processed the radial occupancy map and fused the visual features in the third downsampling stage.
 - **Training:** The subgoal model was trained on the Matterport3D dataset. Laser scans were simulated by measuring range to the reconstructed mesh. The model was trained to minimize Sinkhorn divergence between predicted waypoint distributions and the locations of valid neighboring viewpoints in the navigation graph. At test time, a confidence threshold was used to select up to 5 waypoints for the VLN agent.
- **Obstacle Avoidance and Path Planning:** The standard ROS `move_base` navigation module was integrated. It utilized observations from both the depth camera and laser scanner to maintain local and global cost maps, plan collision-free paths to selected subgoals, and execute robot motion.

**System Verification:** The VLN agent's codebase was refactored into ROS nodes. Mock ROS nodes for the panoramic camera, subgoal model, and `move_base` were created using simulator data and the navigation graph to verify that the robot's code replicated the original R2R-EnvDrop agent's performance in simulation within 1% error.

**Experimental Settings:**
- **'With Map' Setting:** The robot was provided with a pre-captured occupancy map of Coda and the simulator's navigation graph. The subgoal model was not utilized in this setting. This tests the assumption that existing robotics systems can navigate between pre-defined graph viewpoints.
- **'No Map' Setting:** The robot operated without prior access to an occupancy map or navigation graph. It performed Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) from scratch for each episode. This setting necessitated the use of the subgoal model for waypoint prediction and represented a "cold-start" scenario in an entirely unknown environment.

All experiments were conducted with previously unseen language instructions and visual appearance, under daylight conditions, with furniture restored to original positions to minimize environment drift between simulator and physical runs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the sim-to-real transfer of VLN agents:

**Coda Environment Characterization:**
- The Coda simulator environment was found to be slightly less challenging than the R2R test set for the R2R-EnvDrop agent. The agent achieved a 55.9% success rate (SR) in Coda (sim), compared to 49.2% on the R2R test set. This indicated that the Coda dataset was "in-domain" with respect to R2R, allowing for valid sim-to-real comparisons.

**Importance of Visual Domain Adaptation:**
- Switching from the Matterport camera to the robot's Ricoh Theta V camera *without* domain adaptation in the Coda simulator resulted in a 6.4% drop in success rate (from 55.9% to 49.5%).
- When domain adaptation (random color jitter) was applied during VLN agent training, the success rate with the Theta V camera in simulation improved to 52.0%. This represented a reduction of 3.9% compared to the Matterport camera in sim, indicating that domain randomization effectively mitigated a substantial portion of the visual domain differences.

**Sim-to-Real Performance 'With Map' Setting:**
- In the 'with map' setting, where the robot was provided with a pre-captured occupancy map and the simulator's navigation graph (subgoal model not used), the robot achieved a 46.8% success rate in the physical Coda environment.
- This performance represented a 5.2% reduction compared to using the robot camera in simulation *with* domain adaptation (46.8% vs. 52.0%). The total reduction from the Matterport-camera simulation baseline was 9.1% (55.9% to 46.8%).
- The robot completed 111 instructions over 1.3km of trajectories without any collisions or navigation failures.
- The performance reduction was attributed to slight viewpoint differences between the robot's path and the simulator's predefined viewpoints, as well as the presence of people in the environment during testing (a domain difference not captured in Matterport3D).
- This result suggested that, with prior mapping and navigation graph information, transferring a VLN agent to a physical robot is feasible with a measurable but manageable performance reduction.

**Sim-to-Real Performance 'No Map' Setting (Cold Start):**
- In the most challenging 'no map' setting, where the robot relied on SLAM from scratch per episode and the subgoal model for waypoint predictions, the instruction-following success rate significantly dropped to 22.5%.
- This constituted an additional 24.3% reduction in success rate compared to the 'with map' setting (22.5% vs. 46.8%).
- Collisions with objects (a table and a chair) were observed in this setting.
- The primary cause for this substantial drop was attributed to errors in the subgoal model's predictions. These included:
 - **False positives:** Predicting navigable paths where none existed (e.g., attempting to navigate down a spiral staircase).
 - **False negatives:** Failing to identify valid navigable paths (e.g., missing narrow "squeeze points" between furniture that were part of ground-truth trajectories).
- Trajectory similarity metrics (NDTW, SDTW) were lowest in this setting, indicating significant divergence from the ground-truth paths.
- This outcome indicated that VLN sim-to-real transfer in a completely unmapped, cold-start environment remains an open and challenging research problem.

**Overall Sim-to-Real Gap Quantification:** The study empirically quantified the contributions of different factors to the sim-to-real gap: approximately 3.9% due to remaining visual domain differences (after adaptation), 5.2% due to viewpoint differences/unaccounted real-world variability (in the 'with map' setting), and an additional 24.3% due to subgoal prediction errors and action space discrepancies (in the 'no map' setting).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a foundational empirical study on the sim-to-real transfer for Vision-and-Language Navigation, offering several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Pioneering Sim-to-Real Transfer for VLN:** The work represents one of the first successful attempts to transfer a VLN agent, trained on a large-scale dataset like R2R in simulation, to a physical robot. This demonstrates the potential for simulation-trained models to be deployed in real-world environments, validating years of research in VLN simulation.
- **Empirical Quantification of the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The study meticulously quantifies the performance degradation incurred during sim-to-real transfer, breaking it down into components such as visual domain differences, viewpoint discrepancies, and action space adaptation challenges (subgoal prediction errors). This detailed analysis provides critical insights for future research to target specific areas of improvement.
- **Validation of Language Grounding Transfer:** The relatively high success rate (46.8%) in the 'with map' setting suggests that the complex language groundings learned by VLN agents in simulation can effectively transfer to physical environments not seen during training. This indicates that the core vision-and-language understanding capabilities developed in simulation are robust enough to be useful in reality, given certain environmental priors.
- **Identification of Key Bottlenecks:** The stark performance drop in the 'no map' setting highlights that subgoal prediction and the abstraction of the simulator's navigation graph into real-world actions constitute a major challenge. This pinpoints a critical area for future research and development, emphasizing the need for more robust and accurate action space adaptation mechanisms.
- **Contribution of Resources and Framework:** The release of code for digitizing and annotating new VLN environments, along with the sim-to-real framework and subgoal model, provides valuable resources for the research community. This can accelerate future work by reducing the overhead associated with setting up sim-to-real experiments for VLN.
- **Implications for Embodied AI Development:** The findings have broader implications for embodied AI, suggesting that while simulation offers an efficient training ground, careful consideration of real-world deployment factors is crucial. It informs the design of future simulators and training methodologies, pushing towards more continuous action spaces or robust off-policy learning algorithms that can learn from fixed real-world data.
- **Guidance for Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines future directions, such as improving or eliminating the subgoal model, training VLN agents directly with low-level continuous action spaces (potentially using off-policy reinforcement learning or continuous motion simulators), and addressing exacerbated visual domain differences that might arise from such simulators. This serves as a roadmap for advancing VLN beyond its current simulation-centric paradigm.
