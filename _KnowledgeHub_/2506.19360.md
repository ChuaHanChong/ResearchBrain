# SoK: Can Synthetic Images Replace Real Data? A Survey of Utility and Privacy of Synthetic Image Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> Chung et al. present a Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) that empirically evaluates the utility and privacy of various synthetic image generation models and mitigation strategies. Their analysis establishes that synthetic data can offer a favorable utility-privacy tradeoff, often outperforming DP-SGD on real data for downstream tasks, with diffusion models generally demonstrating superior performance.

## Problem

- A lack of comprehensive, unified analysis and empirical benchmarking for synthetic image data regarding its utility and privacy. Existing literature focused more on tabular data or had limited scope.
- Inconsistencies and fragmentation in prior empirical studies on privacy-preserving image generation, making it difficult to compare different generative models and privacy attacks under unified conditions.
- Absence of a systematic framework to categorize and analyze methods, attacks, and mitigations across different stages of privacy-preserving data synthesis for image data.

## Method

- Introduced a novel =="generation-sampling-classification pipeline"== to systematically analyze information release stages and evaluate methods, attacks, and mitigations.
- Conducted a broad empirical evaluation across diverse generative models (==GANs==, ==VAEs==, ==Diffusion Models==, ==Data Condensation==) and privacy mitigation strategies (==DP==, ==adversarial training==).
- Employed three distinct, model-agnostic ==Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs)== to measure privacy and standardized classification performance metrics (F1-score, Accuracy, AUC) for utility across three varied datasets.

## Results

- Diffusion-based models (e.g., SMP-LoRA, LoRA, TI) consistently achieved more favorable utility-privacy tradeoffs compared to GANs and VAEs across both image-based and classifier-based attacks.
- Classifiers trained on high-quality synthetic data, especially from advanced diffusion models, showed superior utility for a given privacy level compared to classifiers trained directly on real data using DP-SGD.
- Several synthetic data generation methods (e.g., TI, SMP-LoRA) demonstrated a better capacity to maintain or improve performance on minority groups, potentially mitigating the disparate impact sometimes observed with direct DP applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal strategy for privacy-preserving synthetic image generation (e.g., sharing synthetic images vs. classifiers) is highly dependent on dataset characteristics, specific utility requirements, and privacy goals.
> - Synthetic data can provide superior utility-privacy tradeoffs compared to directly applying differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) to real data for classifier training.
> - Diffusion models generally offer more favorable utility-privacy tradeoffs, and implicit privacy gains can be achieved through careful hyperparameter tuning or specific generative process designs like multi-instance synthesis.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in generative models, particularly Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and more recently, diffusion models, have revolutionized the capability to synthesize realistic data. This technological leap has naturally spurred interest in Privacy-Preserving Data Synthesis (PPDS), where synthetic data can serve as a substitute for sensitive real data in applications like healthcare, finance, and social media. The authors meticulously contextualize their work by highlighting a critical gap in the existing literature: while PPDS for *tabular data* has seen several surveys and benchmarks, a comprehensive and unified analysis for *image data* remains largely unexplored.

Existing works on image generation for privacy, such as Hu et al., either focus exclusively on Differential Privacy (DP) without extensive Membership Inference Attack (MIA) comparisons or are limited in their scope of models or datasets (e.g., MNIST/FMNIST). Other surveys, like Troung et al., might discuss attacks and defenses for specific model types (e.g., diffusion models) but lack comparative experiments across diverse architectures. Crucially, the authors note inconsistencies in prior empirical studies (e.g., Stadler et al. vs. Sarmin et al. for tabular data), underscoring the need for a rigorous, unified evaluation framework for image data.

This SoK bridges these gaps by providing:
1. **A Unifying Framework:** Introducing a "generation-sampling-classification pipeline" to systematically categorize methods, attacks, and mitigations at different data release stages.
2. **Broad and Unified Empirical Evaluation:** Benchmarking a wide array of representative generative models (GANs, VAEs, Diffusion Models, Data Condensation) alongside various privacy mitigation strategies under consistent conditions and using standardized metrics for utility and privacy.
3. **Critical Evaluation and Contextualization:** Offering novel insights into the practical performance across diverse datasets, thereby grounding theoretical claims in real-world efficacy.

By addressing these deficiencies, the paper positions itself as a foundational resource for researchers and practitioners navigating the complex landscape of privacy-preserving synthetic image generation, offering a holistic view that was previously unavailable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the increasing demand for privacy-preserving techniques in data-intensive fields, juxtaposed with the advanced capabilities of modern generative models. The authors aim to provide clarity and actionable insights in a field where balancing data utility (fidelity, classification accuracy) and privacy (protection against MIAs) remains a significant challenge.

The paper sets out to answer four fundamental questions:
1. **Can synthetic data effectively replace real data?** This probes the fundamental utility of synthetic images for downstream tasks, particularly classifier training.
2. **Which is safer to share—synthetic images or classifiers trained on synthetic data?** This question investigates the privacy implications of different data release strategies along their proposed pipeline.
3. **Do mitigation strategies improve utility-privacy tradeoffs?** This assesses the effectiveness of various privacy-enhancing techniques, both explicit (e.g., DP, adversarial training) and implicit (e.g., hyperparameter tuning).
4. **Which generative models perform best across different data-sharing scenarios?** This seeks to identify optimal model architectures and training paradigms for different utility and privacy requirements.

By systematically addressing these questions through a structured survey and empirical evaluation, the authors intend to guide decision-making for real-world PPDS applications and chart future research directions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology is a novel **generation-classification pipeline** (Figure 1), which decomposes the PPDS process into three distinct stages of information release:
- **Stage A: Release Generative Model (g):** The generative model itself, trained on a private dataset $D_{tr}$, is released.
- **Stage B: Release Synthetic Image ($D_{syn}$):** A synthetic dataset, sampled from the trained generative model, is released.
- **Stage C: Release Classifier (f):** A classifier, trained on the synthetic dataset, is released.

Each stage presents unique attack surfaces and requires different mitigation strategies.

**Generative Models Benchmarked:**
The study conducts a broad empirical evaluation covering representative generative architectures and training paradigms:
- **GANs and VAEs:** Classic models like GAN (FreezeD) and VAE are included to establish a baseline.
- **Diffusion Models:** Advanced models such as DDPM, along with fine-tuned variants like LoRA and Textual Inversion (TI), are extensively evaluated.
- **Data Condensation (DC):** Methods like DCDM, which aim to synthesize compact datasets, are also included.
- **Privacy-Enhanced Models:** Variants integrating Differential Privacy (DP-Sinkhorn, DP-LDMs) and adversarial training (SMP-LoRA) are benchmarked.

**Privacy Attacks:**
To ensure model-agnostic and broadly applicable privacy assessment, the authors select three types of Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) as empirical privacy measures:
1. **Attack 1: Synthetic Image-based Attack:** Assesses privacy when synthetic images are released (Stage B). It compares similarities between real query images and the entire synthetic dataset in a latent space, assuming generators tend to produce synthetic images resembling the training set. This attack is generator-agnostic.
2. **Attack 2: Loss-based Classifier Attack:** Evaluates privacy when a classifier trained on synthetic data is released (Stage C). It infers membership by analyzing the classifier's loss values for auxiliary data, leveraging the assumption that classifiers exhibit lower loss for training samples. This attack is classifier-agnostic.
3. **Attack 3: Individual Risk via LiRA with Mislabeling:** A more fine-grained assessment of individual-level privacy risk (Stage C), inspired by LiRA. This involves training shadow models with intentionally mislabeled "canary" samples to compute likelihood ratios, offering a robust measure of individual training sample memorization.

The authors use the **strongest auxiliary data assumption** (Subset Auxiliary Data), where the adversary has access to both members and non-members from the original training distribution, representing a challenging privacy scenario.

**Utility Measurement:**
Utility is consistently measured by **classification performance** (F1-score for CelebA, Accuracy for Fitzpatrick17k, AUC for CheXpert) of a ResNet-18 classifier (pre-trained on ImageNet) trained either on real data (for DP-SGD baselines) or on the synthetic images. FID score is explicitly excluded due to the smaller dataset sizes, where it can be unstable.

**Datasets:**
Experiments are conducted on three diverse datasets:
- **CelebA:** A visually diverse dataset of celebrity faces, with a subset of 2000 images from 250 identities, used for attribute classification.
- **Fitzpatrick17k:** A medical dataset of skin disease images (3 classes), crucial for evaluating privacy in sensitive healthcare contexts.
- **CheXpert:** A large dataset of chest X-ray images (4 classes), included for generalizability across medical imaging domains with distinct visual characteristics.

**Hyperparameter Tuning:**
For each generative method, key hyperparameters (e.g., DP budget, LoRA rank, adversarial weight) are systematically explored using two-fold cross-validation to identify configurations that yield the best utility-privacy tradeoff.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive empirical evaluation yielded several critical insights:

- **RQ1: Dataset-Dependent Optimal Sharing Strategies:** The choice between releasing synthetic images (Attack 1) or classifiers trained on them (Attack 2) is highly dependent on dataset characteristics. For visually diverse datasets like CelebA and Fitzpatrick17k, sharing classifiers generally offers better privacy (lower MIA TPR). Conversely, for more visually homogeneous datasets like CheXpert, direct synthetic image sharing can sometimes be less risky for certain methods. Nonetheless, any synthetic data approach fundamentally improves privacy over directly sharing real images.

- **RQ2: Efficacy of Mitigations and Implicit Privacy:** Explicit mitigation techniques like adversarial training (SMP-LoRA) demonstrably achieve strong privacy while maintaining high utility, occasionally surpassing non-private baselines. DP-enhanced generative methods (DP-LDMs, DP-Sinkhorn) also boost privacy, though DP-Sinkhorn often incurs a significant utility cost. Intriguingly, non-mitigated models (LoRA, TI) can achieve considerable privacy gains through careful hyperparameter tuning, suggesting implicit regularization effects.

- **RQ3: Synthetic Data Outperforms DP-SGD on Real Data:** Classifiers trained on high-quality synthetic data, particularly from advanced diffusion models (e.g., SMP-LoRA, LoRA, TI), can achieve superior utility-privacy tradeoffs compared to classifiers trained directly on real data using DP-SGD. This highlights the potential of synthetic data as an effective intermediary for privacy-preserving downstream model training, sometimes offering better privacy for a given utility level.

- **RQ4: Diffusion Models Lead the Way, Non-Linear Tradeoffs:** Diffusion-based approaches generally deliver more favorable utility-privacy tradeoffs across both image-based and classifier-based attacks compared to GANs and VAEs. The study also reveals that the expected inverse relationship between utility and privacy is not always monotonic; some high-utility diffusion models (e.g., LoRA) surprisingly exhibit stronger privacy against specific attacks (e.g., Attack 2) than models with lower utility.

- **RQ5: Synthetic Image Quality vs. Utility:** A direct correlation was observed between the visual quality and feature representation in synthetic images and their utility for classification. Diffusion models and some mitigated methods (DP-LDMs, SMP-LoRA) effectively balance fidelity and privacy. In contrast, methods like DP-Sinkhorn often sacrifice visual coherence, leading to reduced utility, and DCDM can produce blended images.

- **RQ6: Disparate Impact and Fairness Considerations:** DP-SGD methods applied directly to real data showed a greater disparate impact on underrepresented subgroups (e.g., darker skin types in Fitzpatrick17k), leading to a larger reduction in accuracy compared to the non-private baseline. In contrast, several synthetic data generation methods (e.g., TI, SMP-LoRA) demonstrated a better capacity to maintain or even improve performance on minority groups relative to the non-private baseline, suggesting potential benefits for fairness.

- **RQ7: Individual-Level Privacy Risks and Multi-Instance Synthesis:** Using LiRA to assess individual-level memorization (Attack 3), non-mitigated fine-tuning methods like TI and LoRA, when configured to generate multiple synthetic images per real image, surprisingly resulted in lower individual privacy risk scores compared to a DP-SGD baseline. This implies that the design of the generative process, specifically multi-instance synthesis, can inherently offer some mitigation against individual-level privacy leakage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This SoK provides foundational empirical insights and actionable guidance that significantly impact the field of privacy-preserving synthetic image generation.

**Actionable Insights for Practitioners:**
- The findings emphasize that there is no "one-size-fits-all" solution for PPDS; the optimal strategy is deeply intertwined with **dataset characteristics**, specific **utility requirements**, and **privacy goals**.
- For visually rich and diverse datasets, sharing classifiers trained on synthetic data might be safer. For homogeneous data, direct synthetic image release might sometimes be feasible.
- **Diffusion models** are highlighted as generally superior for balancing utility and privacy, guiding practitioners toward these architectures.
- Beyond explicit privacy guarantees (like DP), **careful hyperparameter tuning** and certain **generative process designs** (e.g., multi-instance synthesis with TI/LoRA) can implicitly enhance privacy, offering alternative or complementary mitigation strategies.
- The work suggests that synthetic data can offer a more **equitable approach** to privacy preservation, potentially mitigating the disparate impact on minority subgroups sometimes observed with direct DP applications.

**Guidance for Future Research:**
- **Systematic Hyperparameter Optimization:** The study underscores the critical role of hyperparameters in the utility-privacy tradeoff. Future research should focus on advanced optimization techniques (e.g., Bayesian Optimization) to rigorously delineate the Pareto optimal frontier for these tradeoffs, providing clearer guidelines for model designers.
- **Synthetic Data for Augmentation:** Moving beyond data replacement, the paper opens avenues for exploring the optimal integration of synthetic data for augmentation within privacy-preserving frameworks, especially in data-scarce and sensitive domains.
- **Understanding Implicit Mitigation:** Further investigation into how generative process designs (like multi-instance synthesis) inherently reduce memorization and individual privacy risks is warranted.
- **Advanced Attack and Defense Co-evolution:** The continuous development of more sophisticated attacks and robust defenses for generative models will remain a crucial area of research.

**Ethical Considerations and Open Science:**
The authors demonstrate a strong commitment to ethical research by using publicly available, anonymized datasets and adhering to principles like the Menlo Report. Their dedication to open science, by promising to share source code, processed datasets, and evaluation protocols, ensures reproducibility and fosters collaborative advancement in this sensitive domain. This commitment is vital for building trust and accelerating progress in privacy-preserving AI technologies.

In conclusion, this SoK serves as an invaluable resource, systematically organizing existing knowledge, providing a robust empirical benchmark, and distilling actionable insights that will undoubtedly inform and accelerate both practical applications and future theoretical advancements in privacy-preserving synthetic image generation.
