# VLMs Can Describe, But Not Measure: Object-Centric Scene Understanding for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A modular perception pipeline turns one RGB-D frame into a task-independent object-centric scene representation: a promptable segmentation model proposes object masks, a VLM annotates each mask using both the crop and the full scene for context, and depth is taken as the mask-region median rather than predicted by the VLM. Across 151 tabletop scenes this cuts median depth error from 192.0 mm to 12.1 mm while keeping semantic recall near the VLM baseline.

## Problem

- Vision-language models give strong open-vocabulary semantics but unreliable metric estimates, so their depth and 3D positions do not support physical manipulation.
- Geometric perception and point-cloud segmentation methods recover 3D structure yet carry weak, closed-vocabulary semantics and need scene-specific training to generalize.
- Language-conditioned and open-vocabulary 3D mapping methods build task-conditioned or multi-view persistent maps, not a compact reusable representation from a single observation.

## Method

- ==Scene decomposition==: a class-agnostic or VLM-preconditioned ==segmentation model== proposes masks, then filters small, redundant, background and dominant planar regions into kept mask plus bounding-box pairs.
- ==Context-aware semantic annotation==: the annotation VLM receives both the full RGB scene and the masked object crop, so identity, attributes, affordances and relations are described without losing scene context.
- ==Geometric-semantic association==: per-mask valid depth pixels give a median representative range, combined with the mask centroid, fusing into per-object mask, semantics and geometry tuples for task planning.

## Results

- Direct VLM inference reached **95.9%** semantic recall at best but **96.7-413.9 mm** median depth error; the pipeline cut average median depth error from **192.0 mm** to **12.1 mm**, a **93.7%** reduction.
- The best pipeline configuration kept recall at **94.1%** versus **95.9%** for the VLM-only baseline, with median image-space position error of **15.2 px**.
- Mask-median beat bounding-box-center depth: **13.2 mm** versus **15.8 mm** with RGB-D and **69.9 mm** versus **100.1 mm** with monocular depth prediction, a **62.8%** gain over VLM depth.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decompose perception by competence: keep foundation models for open-vocabulary semantics, and delegate metric geometry to sensors or specialized depth models.
> - Aggregating depth over a whole segmentation mask is more robust than sampling one representative pixel, which can miss the object's visible surface entirely.
> - Semantic recall tracks VLM strength, whereas geometric accuracy barely varies across backends, so a weaker or local VLM costs little once geometry is delegated.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation in unfamiliar environments requires systems to combine several forms of perception. A robot must identify objects and their properties, estimate where those objects are located in three-dimensional space, determine how they relate to one another, and convert this information into actions. Existing approaches often perform well in only part of this process.

Vision-language models (VLMs) have expanded the ability of robots to recognize objects using open-ended natural-language concepts. Unlike conventional object detectors, which are usually trained for fixed categories, VLMs can describe unfamiliar objects, infer attributes, and connect visual content with language-based task instructions. However, VLMs generally estimate geometric quantities such as precise object position, depth, size, and pose less reliably than they identify semantic content.

A separate body of research addresses geometric perception. RGB-D sensors provide direct metric depth, while segmentation and three-dimensional reconstruction methods can identify spatial regions and recover object geometry. These techniques, however, commonly have limited semantic vocabularies or require training on task-specific data. Other robotic systems combine language reasoning and geometry, including language-conditioned manipulation policies, 3D value maps, scene graphs, and spatial reasoning frameworks. Many of these systems construct representations tailored to a particular instruction or task.

This paper contributes to the area by proposing a modular alternative. Rather than asking a VLM to both describe objects and measure them, the authors assign semantic interpretation to the VLM and geometric estimation to segmentation and depth-processing components. The goal is a reusable, task-independent object-centric representation constructed from a single RGB-D observation, without scene-specific training or fine-tuning.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to determine whether semantic and geometric scene understanding can be separated and then recombined to support robotic manipulation in previously unseen environments.

The paper is motivated by three related problems:

1. **VLMs provide semantic information more reliably than metric information.** A VLM may correctly identify a bottle, charger, tray, or block, but its estimate of the object’s distance from the camera may be inaccurate by many centimeters. Such errors are problematic for grasping, placement, collision avoidance, and motion planning.

2. **Geometric perception methods do not necessarily provide open-vocabulary understanding.** A depth map can indicate the location of a surface, but it does not by itself establish whether the surface belongs to a measuring tape, box, tool, or other object. Class-agnostic segmentation can isolate regions without assigning useful semantic labels.

3. **Task-specific representations limit reuse.** A perception system designed only to answer one instruction may not provide enough information for later tasks. The authors instead aim to build a scene representation independently of the task. A planner should be able to query the same representation for different instructions, objects, spatial relations, and metric data.

The intended output is an object-level knowledge base in which each scene entity has a segmentation mask, a natural-language description or semantic label, and geometric information derived from the image and depth data. This representation is intended to serve as an interface between open-vocabulary perception and symbolic or language-based planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall Pipeline

The system begins with one aligned RGB-D observation consisting of an RGB image and a metric depth image. It contains four main stages:

1. **Scene decomposition:** A foundation segmentation model produces candidate object masks.
2. **Semantic annotation:** A VLM assigns a description or label to each retained segmented region.
3. **Geometric grounding:** The segmentation masks are associated with the depth image to estimate object location and distance.
4. **Object-centric representation:** Semantic and geometric information are combined into a structured scene description that can be queried by a planner.

The architecture is modular. Segmentation, VLM annotation, and depth estimation can be replaced independently without changing the object-level representation.

#### 3.2. Scene Segmentation

The system uses class-agnostic or concept-conditioned segmentation models, including SAM, SAM 2, SAM 3, and FastSAM. The initial mask proposals are filtered to remove small regions, redundant masks, background regions, and dominant planar structures such as tables, floors, or walls.

For SAM 3, the authors first use a VLM to generate short noun phrases describing visible non-background objects. These phrases may include visual attributes such as color, size, and shape. SAM 3 then uses the phrases as segmentation prompts. For SAM 1, SAM 2, and FastSAM, the authors use automatic class-agnostic segmentation without semantic preconditioning.

Each retained mask is applied to the RGB image to produce an object-focused crop. The mask remains the common spatial reference connecting the semantic and geometric branches.

#### 3.3. Context-Aware VLM Annotation

Each segmented object is presented to a VLM using both the complete scene and the localized object crop. This design addresses two opposing problems. An isolated crop may remove contextual information needed to identify an object, while the full image alone does not clearly indicate which object should be described.

The VLM is instructed to assign one of the predefined semantic tags to the segmented object. Depending on the model, the output can include object identity, visual attributes, functional properties, affordances, and qualitative relationships. The authors explicitly avoid asking the VLM to provide the metric depth used in the proposed pipeline.

#### 3.4. Geometric Grounding

For each object mask, the method collects all pixels within the mask that have valid depth measurements. The representative object depth is calculated as the median of these values. The median is intended to reduce the influence of outliers, boundary errors, and invalid measurements.

The image-space centroid or bounding box of the mask provides two-dimensional localization. The geometric description therefore consists primarily of image position and representative metric depth. An object is represented as:

- its segmentation mask,
- its semantic description,
- its image-space position,
- its depth relative to the camera.

This is a compact representation rather than a complete six-degree-of-freedom pose or full three-dimensional shape model.

#### 3.5. Dataset and Evaluation

The authors collected a dataset of **151 tabletop scenes** using an Orbbec Femto Mega camera. The RGB images have a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, while the depth images have a resolution of 640 × 576 pixels. The scenes contain 26 distinct objects, including blocks, bottles, boxes, tools, measurement devices, a cup, a wallet, and ping-pong balls. Each scene contains between one and five task objects.

ArUco markers were added in separate images to establish reference object identities and positions. These marker-augmented images were used only to create ground-truth annotations and were not supplied to the perception models. The experiments were zero-shot: none of the models were trained or fine-tuned on the evaluation scenes.

Semantic performance was measured using recall of ground-truth object tags. Geometric performance was measured using image-space position error in pixels and depth error in millimeters. The authors compared direct VLM inference against the modular pipeline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Direct VLM Inference

The VLM-only baseline asked each model to identify objects, predict image-space bounding boxes, and estimate metric distance from the camera.

Semantic recall was generally high for several proprietary models. GPT 5.4 achieved the highest reported recall at **95.9%**, followed by Claude Sonnet 4.6 at **92.8%** and GPT 5.4-Mini at **90.0%**. Qwen 3.6 achieved **92.4%**, while Qwen 3-VL achieved **79.2%** and GPT 5.4-Nano achieved **60.3%**.

The models could also provide reasonable two-dimensional localization in some cases. GPT 5.4 had a median position error of **16.8 pixels**, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 had a median error of **29.3 pixels**. These results indicate that VLMs can often identify approximately where an object appears in an image.

Depth estimation was less reliable. GPT 5.4 had a median depth error of **151.6 mm**, Claude Sonnet 4.6 had **96.7 mm**, and Qwen 3-VL had **413.9 mm**. The results support the paper’s central distinction: semantic recognition and approximate image localization do not imply accurate metric measurement.

#### 4.2. Modular Pipeline

Adding segmentation and depth grounding preserved much of the semantic performance while substantially reducing geometric error. The SAM 3 and GPT 5.4 combination achieved **94.1% semantic recall**, compared with 95.9% for GPT 5.4 alone. Its median image-space position error was **15.2 pixels**, and its median depth error was **11.6 mm**, with a depth RMSE of **84.3 mm**.

Across the six VLM backends tested with SAM 3, the average median depth error decreased from **192.0 mm** for direct VLM inference to **12.1 mm** with measured RGB-D grounding. This corresponds to a reported reduction of approximately **93.7%**. Other segmentation models also produced useful results. SAM 2 with GPT 5.4 achieved a median position error of 13.6 pixels and a median depth error of 17.5 mm. SAM 1 produced comparable performance, while FastSAM showed higher geometric errors.

These results indicate that specialized perception components can improve geometric estimates without requiring the VLM to reason directly about metric distances.

#### 4.3. Depth Ablation

The authors compared median depth over the full segmentation mask with depth sampled at the center of the object’s bounding box. Using the complete mask was more reliable. With measured RGB-D data, average median depth error decreased from **15.8 mm** for bounding-box-center sampling to **13.2 mm** for mask-median extraction. With monocular depth predictions from Depth Anything 3, the error decreased from **100.1 mm** to **69.9 mm**.

The authors also evaluated the pipeline using predicted monocular depth instead of measured RGB-D. Across the six SAM 3 configurations, mask-based monocular depth reduced average median error from 192.0 mm for direct VLM inference to **71.3 mm**, a reported reduction of 62.8%. Although this was less accurate than measured depth, it remained an improvement over VLM-only estimation.

#### 4.4. Robotic Demonstration

The representation was integrated with a UR3 robot equipped with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper. The system used SAM 3, GPT 5.4, measured RGB-D data, and mask-median depth extraction. The resulting JSON scene description was passed to an existing planning framework, which generated behavior trees executed through ROS2.

Three sorting experiments were conducted with colored trays and blocks or household objects. The demonstrations showed that the object-centric representation could support natural-language task descriptions, object selection, tray-based placement, and multi-step execution. The paper presents these trials as proof-of-concept demonstrations rather than as a statistically evaluated manipulation benchmark.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper’s main contribution is architectural: it treats semantic description and metric measurement as different perception problems. This design can make VLM-based robotic systems more dependable by using VLMs for tasks at which they are comparatively effective and specialized sensing or geometric models for measurement.

The approach may support more reusable robotic systems because the scene representation is task-independent. The same object inventory and geometric data can potentially be queried for sorting, retrieval, rearrangement, or other instructions. Its modularity also allows the use of RGB-D sensors when available and monocular depth models when they are not.

Several limitations remain. The evaluation is restricted to 151 tabletop scenes with a relatively small object vocabulary and one dominant camera setting. The representation estimates image position and representative depth but does not recover full object pose, dimensions, or complete shape. Performance depends on segmentation quality, semantic tag matching, depth validity, and the ability of the VLM to identify partially occluded or visually similar objects. The robot experiments do not report large-scale success rates or detailed perception-to-action failure analysis. More complex environments, heavy clutter, severe occlusion, reflective surfaces, moving objects, and non-tabletop manipulation tasks could expose additional weaknesses.

Overall, the study provides quantitative evidence that VLMs can contribute useful semantic understanding to robotic perception, but that their geometric outputs should be grounded through explicit segmentation and depth processing before being used for physical manipulation.
