# Breaking Lock-In: Preserving Steerability under Low-Data VLA Post-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University introduced DeLock, a framework addressing "lock-in" in vision-language-action (VLA) models during low-data post-training, a phenomenon where policies over-specialize and lose their ability to generalize to new instructions. DeLock combines visual encoder weight-drift regularization with test-time Contrastive Prompt Guidance (CPG) to preserve pre-trained knowledge, demonstrating superior generalization across concept and spatial shifts in an 8-task benchmark compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Generalist VLA policies, when post-trained with limited data, suffer from "lock-in," losing their ability to respond to novel instructions that deviate from the training conditions.
- Existing solutions to enhance generalization in low-data post-training typically rely on complex external supervision signals, auxiliary objectives, or augmented datasets, increasing complexity and costs.
- Lack of systematic benchmarks to evaluate a policy's responsiveness to instruction-level shifts and disentangle them from visual or spatial distribution shifts.

## Method

- **DeLock** implements ==visual encoder weight-drift regularization== during low-data supervised fine-tuning to preserve the VLA's pre-trained visual grounding capabilities via an ==L2 penalty== on the visual encoder.
- ==Contrastive Prompt Guidance (CPG)== is applied at test time, modifying the policy's conditional denoising dynamics by contrasting novel (positive) and trained (negative) instructions to steer action generation.
- A dedicated **8-task benchmark** with paired ==lock-in probes== systematically evaluates concept and spatial reasoning generalization under instruction-level shifts.

## Results

- **DeLock** consistently outperformed strong baselines, showing high reliability under scene perturbations and superior performance on both concept- and spatial-lock-in probes across simulation and real-world tasks.
- Mechanistic analysis revealed standard SFT policies exhibited **collapsed cross-attention patterns** indicative of lock-in, while DeLock maintained prompt-conditioned attention shifts.
- Ablations confirmed both ==visual encoder regularization== and ==CPG== are essential and complementary; removing either causes significant degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - "Lock-in" is a definable post-training failure mode, manifesting as concept lock-in (language grounding collapse) and spatial lock-in (fixation on trained spatial targets).
> - Preserving pre-trained visual grounding through regularization is critical for robust downstream generalization, preventing the visual encoder from drifting too far from its original state.
> - Test-time prompt-contrast steering is essential for overcoming training-induced spatial and conceptual biases during rollout, effectively guiding the policy toward novel instruction targets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Generalist robot policies, often implemented as vision-language-action (VLA) models, have demonstrated capabilities in generalizing across tasks, environments, objects, and open-vocabulary instructions. Despite this, effective performance on specific downstream tasks or with particular robot embodiments typically necessitates adaptation through a process referred to as post-training. This adaptation commonly involves supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on tens to hundreds of hours of curated demonstrations relevant to the target domain.

However, the collection of extensive demonstration datasets is often resource-intensive and impractical for real-world deployments. Consequently, generalist policies are frequently post-trained with limited data and narrow instruction coverage, a scenario termed the low-data regime. In this setting, standard SFT presents a dilemma: while it can facilitate learning a target skill from limited demonstrations, it frequently leads to over-specialization to the specific distribution of the post-training data. This over-specialization can impair the policy's ability to respond to novel instructions that deviate from the training conditions.

Existing approaches to address this issue typically introduce additional supervision signals. These signals may be derived from external foundation models, auxiliary objectives (such as VQA-style losses or dynamics prediction losses), or augmented datasets (e.g., through human-robot co-training). While these methods can enhance generalization, they also increase system complexity, training requirements, and deployment costs. The present work investigates an alternative perspective: whether a VLA's inherent pre-trained knowledge can be preserved and leveraged to achieve post-training generalization in low-data settings without relying on supplementary external signals. The paper specifically identifies and formalizes a failure mode, termed "lock-in," which manifests as a policy's inability to generalize beyond its low-data post-training distribution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are threefold:
1. **Formalize and characterize "lock-in" as a common post-training failure mode in VLAs under low-data conditions.** The authors define lock-in as a phenomenon where a policy, after low-data supervised fine-tuning, becomes overly specialized to the post-training data, losing its ability to generalize to novel instructions. This failure mode is further categorized into:
 - **Concept lock-in:** The policy's language grounding collapses, leading it to fixate on specific objects or attributes observed during training, executing learned skills on them irrespective of the concept specified in a novel prompt.
 - **Spatial lock-in:** The policy remains anchored to specific spatial targets (e.g., left vs. right, upper vs. lower) from the training data, ignoring alternative spatial cues in new instructions.
2. **Propose and evaluate DeLock, a framework designed to mitigate lock-in by preserving and exploiting a VLA's internal pre-trained knowledge.** The framework aims to enable generalization to novel instructions without relying on additional supervision signals or augmented datasets. DeLock combines visual encoder weight-drift regularization during post-training with test-time contrastive prompt guidance (CPG).
3. **Develop a dedicated benchmark for systematically evaluating concept and spatial reasoning generalization under low-data post-training.** The motivation for this is the observation that existing benchmarks primarily assess performance on trained tasks, often using identical instructions, and may not adequately probe the policy's responsiveness to changes in instructions.

The overarching motivation for this work stems from the practical challenges of deploying generalist robot policies. The expense and logistical difficulties associated with collecting large-scale, diverse demonstration datasets often force deployments into low-data regimes. The "performance-generality dilemma" observed in standard SFT—where policies learn a specific skill but lose the ability to be steered by novel instructions—highlights a critical barrier to widespread robot autonomy. By formalizing lock-in and proposing a method that leverages internal pre-trained priors, the authors aim to enhance the robustness and steerability of VLA policies in realistic, resource-constrained environments, thereby reducing the dependency on complex external remedies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DeLock framework addresses the problem of lock-in in low-data VLA post-training by combining a regularization technique during training with a guidance mechanism at inference time.

**Problem Formulation:**
The research begins by outlining the standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) process. A pre-trained VLA policy, $\pi_{\theta_{pre}}$, which maps observations ($o$) and instructions ($\tau$) to action distributions ($a$), is adapted to a target skill. This adaptation involves minimizing a behavioral-cloning loss, $L_{BC}(\theta; D^\star)$, on a small, narrowly covered demonstration dataset $D^\star$. The resulting policy, $\pi_{\theta_{ft}}$, is prone to concept and spatial lock-in, as defined in the objectives.

**DeLock Framework Components:**

#### 3.1. Visual Encoder Weight-Drift Regularization for Knowledge Preservation

The authors identify visual grounding as a bottleneck for avoiding lock-in. Most modern VLA policies typically consist of a visual encoder ($v_{\theta_v}$), a language backbone ($l_{\theta_\ell}$), and an action expert ($a_{\theta_a}$). Standard low-data SFT often allows the visual encoder to update freely, which can cause its parameters to drift significantly from their pre-trained state ($\theta_{pre_v}$). This drift can impair the policy's ability to ground instructions to scene concepts and spatial relations.

To counter this, DeLock augments the SFT objective with an L2 penalty that discourages the visual encoder parameters ($\theta_v$) from deviating substantially from their pre-trained counterparts ($\theta_{pre_v}$). The total loss function, $L_{DeLock}$, is defined as:
$L_{DeLock}(\theta; D^\star) = L_{BC}(\theta; D^\star) + \lambda \| \theta_v - \theta_{pre_v} \|_2^2$
Here, $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter controlling the regularization strength. During post-training, the visual encoder parameters ($\theta_v$) are regularized, while the language backbone ($l_{\theta_\ell}$) and action expert ($a_{\theta_a}$) are adapted, typically using techniques like LoRA, to learn the task-specific skills. This strategy aims to preserve the VLA's pre-trained visual priors while still allowing for task-specific adaptation in the downstream modules.

#### 3.2. Contrastive Prompt Guidance (CPG) at Test Time

Building on the foundation of a visually preserved model, DeLock introduces Contrastive Prompt Guidance (CPG) during inference. CPG is a test-time mechanism that leverages the policy's retained grounding knowledge to steer its conditional denoising dynamics, thereby aligning execution with novel instructions.

The action generation process in many state-of-the-art VLA policies is parameterized as a conditional denoising process (flow-matching). An action chunk, $a_k$, at rollout step $k$ is generated by iteratively denoising a noisy initial state ($a^1_k \sim N(0, I)$) from time $t=1$ to $t=0$. The policy predicts a denoising vector field, $v_\theta(o_k, \tau, t)$, which is integrated via an Euler update rule: $a^{t+\delta}_k = a^t_k + \delta v_\theta(o_k, \tau, t)$, where $\delta < 0$.

CPG modifies this denoising process by contrasting a "positive" prompt ($\tau^+$), which corresponds to the novel instruction, with a "negative" prompt ($\tau^-$). The trained instruction is used as the negative prompt, as it represents the policy's post-training bias toward the training targets. The guided vector field, $v^{t}_{CPG,k}$, is defined as:
$v^{t}_{CPG,k} = v_\theta(o_k, \tau^-, t) + w (v_\theta(o_k, \tau^+, t) - v_\theta(o_k, \tau^-, t))$
where $w \ge 0$ is a tunable guidance scale. This guided field replaces the standard vector field in the Euler update ($a^{t+\delta}_k = a^t_k + \delta v^{t}_{CPG,k}$). This mechanism amplifies the contrastive signal ($v_{\tau^+} - v_{\tau^-}$), reinforcing instruction-relevant directions while suppressing shared biases, thus enabling effective steering even if the policy's prediction for $\tau^+$ alone might still overfit to training targets.

#### 3.3. Benchmark Development

To systematically evaluate lock-in failures, the authors developed an 8-task evaluation suite, encompassing four LIBERO-based simulation tasks and four real-world tasks on the DROID setup. Crucially, all tasks, except one focused purely on OOD location shift (MokaPot-on-Stove), are designed as "paired lock-in probes." In these probes, post-training demonstrations cover a restricted set of concept and/or spatial variants, while evaluation involves keeping the scene fixed and altering only the concept or spatial token in the instruction. This design allows for targeted assessment of a policy's ability to respond to novel instructions, distinguishing it from merely adapting to visual or spatial distribution shifts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DeLock involved both qualitative mechanistic analysis and quantitative performance comparisons against baselines and ablations across simulation and real-world tasks.

#### 4.1. Mechanistic Analysis: Signatures of Lock-In

- **Concept Lock-In:** The BLOCK-STACKING [C] task, where the post-training prompt was "stack blue block on green block" and the novel prompt was "stack green block on blue block," was used to probe concept lock-in.
 - Standard SFT policies exhibited collapsed vision-language cross-attention patterns, consistently focusing on the blue block regardless of the instruction. This suggests a failure to shift conceptual grounding.
 - DeLock, in contrast, demonstrated a clear prompt-conditioned shift in attention between the blue and green blocks when their instructed roles were swapped, indicating preserved visual grounding.
- **Spatial Lock-In:** The CUP-TO-BOX [S] task, trained on "put right cup to box" and evaluated on "put left cup to box," was used for spatial lock-in analysis.
 - A counterfactual rollout analysis showed that without CPG, the policy remained biased toward the trained "right cup" behavior, even when presented with the novel instruction.
 - With CPG enabled, the prompt-contrast term actively steered the denoising trajectory towards the new "left cup" spatial target, effectively overcoming the training-induced spatial bias.

#### 4.2. Generalizing Beyond Post-Training: OOD Configurations and Novel Instructions

Quantitative evaluations were performed across all eight tasks, assessing generalization under Out-of-Distribution (OOD) locations and novel instructions.
- **Overall Performance:** DeLock consistently outperformed strong baselines, demonstrating high reliability under scene perturbations and superior performance on both concept- and spatial-lock-in probes. Notably, DeLock achieved performance comparable to or exceeding that of $\pi_{0.5}$-DROID, a state-of-the-art generalist policy post-trained with substantially more curated demonstrations.
- **Baseline Performance:**
 - **RETAIN:** A low-data post-training method employing weight-space interpolation, showed moderate robustness to OOD location shifts. However, it failed to generalize across all novel-prompt tasks, indicating that parameter merging alone was insufficient to address behavioral lock-in under instruction changes.
 - **$\pi_{0.5}$-DROID:** While performing well on OOD locations and some simpler concept shifts, its performance declined significantly on spatial reasoning tasks like CUP-TO-BOX [S] (where it exhibited stochastic switching between targets) and failed entirely on the fine-grained OPEN-LABELED-DOOR [C+S] task. This suggests that even large-scale generalists can struggle with fine-grained instruction following when adaptation demands going beyond dominant post-training patterns.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **DeLock w/o Vis-Reg (without visual encoder regularization):** Performance degraded substantially across both OOD location shifts and novel-prompt settings. This finding underscored the criticality of preserving pre-trained visual grounding for robust downstream generalization.
 - **DeLock w/o CPG (without contrastive prompt guidance):** While showing some success on concept probes, it failed on all spatial lock-in tasks. This demonstrated that test-time prompt-contrast steering is essential for correcting training-induced spatial biases during rollout.
 - **DeLock w/ Frozen-Vis (with a frozen visual encoder):** Achieved non-trivial performance but consistently underperformed the full DeLock framework. This highlights the benefit of controlled, regularized adaptation of the visual encoder over rigid freezing.
- **Conclusion from Ablations:** The effectiveness of DeLock arises from the complementary roles of preserving visual grounding during training and applying test-time prompt-contrast steering during inference.

#### 4.3. Additional Findings (from Appendix)

- **In-Distribution Performance:** All methods, including baselines and DeLock variants, exhibited high success rates in-distribution using trained prompts. This confirmed that the primary challenge was generalization beyond the post-training instruction coverage rather than initial skill acquisition.
- **CPG with Invalid Prompt:** When CPG was used with a semantically invalid positive prompt (e.g., "put mug on write plate" instead of "right plate"), the policy reverted to its post-training bias. This indicated that CPG's efficacy relies on the positive prompt providing valid, task-relevant semantic content.
- **Comparison with Spatial Forcing:** DeLock outperformed Spatial Forcing, a baseline that uses external 3D foundation model supervision, across simulation tasks. This suggests that DeLock's approach of leveraging internal pre-trained priors can be more effective than introducing additional external supervision for lock-in mitigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a formalization of "lock-in" as a critical failure mode in low-data post-training of generalist VLA policies, differentiating between concept and spatial manifestations. This clear definition is significant for guiding future research efforts aimed at improving the robustness and steerability of robotic systems.

The proposed DeLock framework offers a method for mitigating lock-in without the increased complexity, training requirements, or deployment costs associated with introducing external supervision signals (e.g., from foundation models or auxiliary objectives) or augmented datasets. By demonstrating that a VLA's internal pre-trained knowledge can be sufficient for generalization when appropriately preserved and leveraged, this work presents an alternative paradigm to existing approaches that often rely on external models for assistance.

The development of a novel 8-task benchmark suite, encompassing both simulated and real-world environments, is also a notable contribution. This benchmark is specifically designed to probe lock-in failures under instruction-level shifts, which addresses a gap in existing evaluation methodologies that often focus on in-distribution performance or limited types of generalization. This suite provides a standardized tool for future research to systematically assess how well policies maintain responsiveness to varied instructions under low-data adaptation.

The potential impact of DeLock extends to the practical deployment of robot foundation models. By enabling generalist policies to adapt to specific tasks and embodiments with limited demonstrations while retaining their ability to follow novel instructions, DeLock could significantly reduce the cost and effort required for real-world robot skill acquisition. This could facilitate more flexible and robust robotic systems in diverse applications where exhaustive data collection is unfeasible, making advanced robot capabilities more accessible and adaptable to specialized domains. The identified limitations also outline clear directions for future work, including scaling to broader instruction distributions and developing more adaptive regularization and guidance strategies.
