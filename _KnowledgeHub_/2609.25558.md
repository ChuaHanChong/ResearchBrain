# HABILIS Brain 0: Geometry-Change Supervision for Vision-Language-Action and Residual Flow Recovery

> [!summary] Summary
> GC-VLA (HABILIS Brain 0) is a vision-language-action policy trained to predict multiview future-minus-current geometry-change tokens from current observations, derived offline from monocular depth and DINO features over a 0.5-second horizon. A four-stage pipeline adds a flow-matching ActionExpert, then a frozen-base residual velocity policy with a binary router. It reaches 95.20% success on LIBERO, 99.55% with residual recovery.

## Problem

- Current-frame geometric supervision such as static depth describes what is visible but not the geometric transition an action produces over a manipulation horizon.
- Future-prediction world models reconstruct whole future observations or latent states, leaving unresolved which future information is worth representing and spending capacity on unchanged scene content.
- Action supervision is absent in human video and inconsistent across embodiments, so policies tied to a common action space cannot pretrain on broad action-free data.

## Method

- ==Geometry-change== targets: ==Depth Anything 3== pseudo-depth feeds ==DINOv2==, and the future-minus-current patch-feature difference is quantized into 100 tokens per view across head and two wrist slots, 300 total.
- **GC-VLM** backbone plus **GeometryReader**: 12 blocks copied onto a 36-layer ==Molmo2-ER== stack predict change tokens; zero-initialized ==cross-attention== residual readers expose pooled GC states to the ==flow-matching== ActionExpert.
- Four-stage curriculum ending in **GCRF**: geometry pretraining, detached action alignment on unified 14-dimensional bimanual end-effector deltas, coupled adaptation, then a binary router plus one bounded ==tanh residual velocity== added across ten flow steps.

## Results

- LIBERO: **95.20%** (1,904/2,000) for Stage 1-3 GC-VLA, rising to **99.55%** (1,991/2,000) with GCRF; suite scores **99.4** Spatial, **100.0** Object, **99.4** Goal, **99.4** Long.
- Curriculum-free ablation swapping current-depth VQ for head+wrist change tokens lifts success **82.1%** to **86.1%**; frozen linear probes gain **+3.7 pp** manipulation-phase accuracy and **+0.020** action-chunk R-squared.
- Applying the residual across all ten flow steps scores **97.5%** on a four-task panel versus **86.5%** for either impulse variant; removing the GeometryReader changes little (**82.5%** versus **80.0%**, not significant).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervising the difference between representations, not the future state itself, focuses capacity on what an action changes and needs no action labels, enabling pretraining on human video.
> - Where a correction enters matters: a bounded residual distributed through flow integration far outperforms an equal-magnitude impulse at the first or final step.
> - Near-ceiling benchmark success can hide brittleness: the same policy drops to 51.09% macro-average under LIBERO-PRO perturbations, collapsing on position and task changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot acting on a scene has to anticipate transitions such as an approach, contact, or object movement. GC-VLA trains a robot policy to predict how the scene's geometry will change over a nominal half-second horizon, rather than to describe only its current geometry. It then uses that representation to condition continuous action generation. The future frame is used to make a training label and is not supplied to the deployed policy. In a matched direct-training comparison, change targets outperform current-depth targets.

The project page describes this hero video as demonstrating "sustained operation, precise manipulation, and adaptive planning." It is an illustrative company demo. It is not the paper's reported LIBERO result or direct evidence for geometry-change supervision.

### 2. A target made from change

The target is the difference between spatial geometry representations of current and future observations. During training, pseudo-depth and DINOv2 features are used offline to construct and quantize that target. The frame offset is rounded according to the source frame rate.

The target uses three fixed camera slots: head/global, left wrist, and right wrist. Each view has 100 spatial token positions, and missing views are masked. The head view captures scene-level change, while the wrist views provide local gripper–object observations.

At inference, the policy receives the current instruction, RGB views, and robot state. It does not use a future image, a future-derived target, or the offline DA3/DINO target encoder.

Training proceeds in stages:

- Stage 1 learns geometry-change tokens without action labels.
- Stage 2 adds the continuous ActionExpert and stops action-flow gradients at the VLM interface.
- Stage 3 enables those gradients into trainable VLM components.

### 3. The controlled target comparison

The strongest test of the idea is a direct-training LIBERO ablation. Two policies start from the same VLA checkpoint and follow the same direct LIBERO training path, without Stage 1 geometry pretraining or Stage 2 detached alignment.

The two policies differ in their targets:

- The current-depth VQ control uses 100 tokens and succeeds in 1,642 of 2,000 episodes (82.1%).
- The head-and-wrist future–current depth-delta variant uses 200 tokens and succeeds in 1,722 of 2,000 episodes (86.1%).

That is 80 more successes, a 4.0 percentage-point gain. Success means the share of episodes completed successfully.

This comparison avoids crediting the staged pretraining recipe for the entire difference. However, it also changes the token count and view coverage along with the target type. It therefore does not isolate target content from every representational change. The ablation's depth-delta targets are also distinct from the paper's principal DA3–DINO feature-difference target.

Table 5, stage-free closed-loop comparison: 100 versus 200 tokens, and 1,642/2,000 (82.1%) versus 1,722/2,000 (86.1%), a gain of 80 episodes and 4.0 percentage points.

### 4. A residual on top

The final stage, Stage 4, freezes GC-VLA and trains GCRF from closed-loop feedback. In GCRF, a binary router decides whether to apply a single bounded residual velocity correction. If the router intervenes, that correction is held constant through ten flow-integration steps within one replan, while the base velocity is reevaluated at each updated flow state. The correction acts on the action-generation flow and does not change the final action after generation.

On the full canonical LIBERO evaluation, Stage 1–3 GC-VLA succeeds in 1,904/2,000 episodes (95.20%). The frozen base plus residual succeeds in 1,991/2,000 (99.55%), which is 87 more episodes or 4.35 percentage points under the same evaluation contract. Because GCRF adds closed-loop training on the benchmark distribution, these numbers describe the full training procedures. They are not evidence that geometry pretraining alone reaches 99.55%.

### 5. Change, then act

GC-VLA shows that a policy can learn a spatial account of what changed, use that representation to form actions, and add bounded corrections during execution. The authors aim for an embodiment-agnostic visual interface that can learn from video before robot-specific action alignment. Their results support geometry-change supervision, but do not establish general embodiment transfer.
