# More Than a Feeling: Learning to Grasp and Regrasp using Vision and Touch

> [!summary] Summary
> An end-to-end action-conditional deep model predicts whether a candidate grasp adjustment will succeed, using raw RGB camera images plus two high-resolution GelSight tactile images processed by ResNet stacks with late fusion. Iteratively selecting the highest-scoring adjustment yields regrasping without sensor calibration or contact-force modeling, reaching 94% grasp success on unseen objects and halving applied force.

## Problem

- Vision-only grasping picks a grasp configuration before contact, so it cannot measure or react to ongoing contact forces during interaction.
- Model-based visuo-tactile grasping needs accurate object and robot models plus calibrated tactile sensors, and breaks under model misspecification.
- Prior tactile work only classifies stability of an ongoing grasp, or optimizes a policy tied to on-policy data with hand-engineered PCA tactile features.

## Method

- ==Action-conditional grasp-outcome network==: three ==ResNet-50== stacks over RGB and two weight-tied GelSight images, an action MLP, ==late-fusion== concatenation, ==cross-entropy== on binary outcome.
- Regrasp optimization by ==stochastic search==: sample about 5,000 candidate actions (3D motion, in-plane rotation, force), execute the ==argmax-probability== action, lift above a 0.9 threshold.
- ==Self-supervised data collection== with randomized pose and gripping force, GelSight-based ==automatic success labeling==, plus augmentation with zero-motion and pre-move state-action pairs.

## Results

- Cross-validation accuracy: tactile+vision **80.28%**, tactile-only **79.34%**, vision-only **73.03%**, action-free variant **76.43%**, chance **62.80%**, on **18,070** examples.
- On robot: **94.0%** success over the easy unseen-object set versus **63.2%** vision-only and **75.9%** cylinder fitting; **73.6%** versus **50%** and **66.8%** on hard objects.
- Minimum-force objective cut mean applied force from **20 N** to **10 N** with **94/100** versus **95/100** success; vision-only fell from **18 N** to **6 N** at **76%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditioning a grasp-outcome predictor on the candidate action, rather than classifying current stability, lets one trained model serve new objectives without retraining or new data.
> - Tactile images on a regular 2D grid let ImageNet-pretrained CNNs transfer directly, avoiding sensor calibration, analytic contact models, and hand-designed features.
> - Learned models discover interpretable physical strategies, such as preferring downward motion and lowering force when contact is unstable, that hand-coded heuristics rarely encode.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Robotic grasping is a fundamental problem in robotics, enabling robots to interact with and manipulate objects in their environment. Traditionally, robotic grasping research has been dominated by **analytical or model-based approaches**. These methods rely on precise geometric models of objects, grippers, and environments, alongside manually defined grasping metrics (e.g., force closure, form closure). While providing valuable theoretical insights, their practical application often falters due to model inaccuracies, environmental uncertainties, and the computational complexity of real-time pose estimation and force control.

More recently, the field has seen a significant shift towards **data-driven and learning-based approaches**, largely propelled by advances in deep learning. These methods, often trained through human supervision, simulation, or large-scale autonomous robotic data collection, predict grasp outcomes directly from sensor observations. A notable trend in this area, particularly from groups like Sergey Levine’s, has been the use of **vision and depth sensors** (e.g., RGB-D cameras) as the primary input modality. These vision-centric methods have achieved impressive results in selecting initial grasp configurations before contact. Examples include large-scale self-supervised learning systems that collect hundreds of thousands of grasp attempts to train deep neural networks for grasp success prediction.

However, a critical limitation of vision-only approaches is their inability to effectively gather and react to **post-contact feedback**. Humans heavily rely on the sense of touch to initiate contact, adjust finger placement, and balance forces during manipulation. Incorporating rich tactile feedback into robotic grasping has remained a significant challenge. This difficulty stems from several factors: hardware limitations (cost, sensitivity, durability of tactile sensors), the complexity of processing high-dimensional tactile data, and the intricate problem of integrating tactile inputs into real-time control schemes for iterative adjustments. While some prior works have explored tactile sensing for slip detection, grasp stability assessment, or adapting grasping force, they often rely on hand-engineered features, analytical models, or simpler tactile sensor modalities. The integration of high-resolution, raw tactile data with vision in an end-to-end learning framework for active regrasping has been largely underexplored.

This paper directly addresses this gap by investigating how a robot can learn to leverage **high-resolution tactile information** (specifically from GelSight sensors) to iteratively and efficiently adjust its grasp. It moves beyond selecting a single grasp configuration pre-contact, focusing instead on continuous, reactive adjustment (regrasping) based on dynamic visuo-tactile feedback. This aligns with a broader trend in robotics towards more interactive and dexterous manipulation, where robots can adapt to unforeseen contacts and manipulate objects with greater compliance and precision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the observation that human grasping is a deeply interactive process, heavily relying on tactile feedback for continuous adjustment, whereas most robotic grasping research has historically neglected this crucial modality, favoring vision. The paper seeks to bridge this gap by enabling robots to mimic this human-like iterative adjustment process.

The **key objectives** of this paper are:

1. **Develop an end-to-end learning model for regrasping:** The primary objective is to create a deep, multimodal convolutional network that learns to adjust grasps from raw visuo-tactile data, predicting the outcome of candidate grasp adjustments. This model should be capable of selecting promising actions iteratively to achieve successful grasps.
2. **Overcome limitations of traditional approaches:** The authors aim to reduce the significant engineering effort typically required for integrating tactile sensors. This means the model should ideally require neither explicit calibration of tactile sensors nor analytical modeling of contact forces, relying instead on data-driven learning from raw inputs.
3. **Demonstrate the utility of combined vision and touch for iterative grasping:** Prove that integrating rich tactile feedback (GelSight) with visual input significantly enhances a robot's ability to iteratively adjust and improve its grasps, leading to higher success rates compared to vision-only or traditional methods.
4. **Achieve efficient and robust grasping policies:** The learned policies should be able to quickly achieve stable grasps on a wide variety of objects, including novel ones, and minimize the number of regrasp attempts.
5. **Enable fine-grained control, such as minimum force grasping:** Show that the action-conditional nature of the learned model allows for optimization of secondary objectives, such as grasping with minimal force, without requiring re-training, which is crucial for handling fragile objects.
6. **Provide interpretability of learned behaviors:** Analyze the acquired grasping behaviors to understand what strategies the model has learned from raw data, providing insights into the importance of different action parameters like force and height.

In essence, the authors are motivated to develop a more robust, adaptive, and human-like robotic grasping system by incorporating the rich, dynamic information provided by high-resolution tactile sensors into a deep learning framework for continuous, interactive manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel **action-conditional model** that learns regrasp policies from raw visuo-tactile data in an end-to-end fashion. The core idea is to train a deep neural network to predict the probability of future grasp success given the robot's current visuo-tactile observations and a candidate grasp adjustment action.

#### 3.1. Hardware Setup

The experimental setup consists of:
- **Robot Arm:** A 7-DoF Sawyer arm.
- **Gripper:** A Weiss WSG-50 parallel gripper.
- **Tactile Sensors:** Two **GelSight sensors**, one mounted on each finger of the gripper. GelSight sensors are optical tactile sensors that provide high-resolution (1280x960 pixels, 30 Hz) images of the contact surface topography by imaging a deforming elastomer membrane. This provides rich information about shape, texture, and even shear/slip.
- **Vision Sensor:** A Microsoft Kinect2 sensor positioned in front of the robot, providing RGB and depth data of the scene.

#### 3.2. Deep Visuo-Tactile Models

The central component is a **deep neural network** (Fig. 3) designed for multimodal inputs and action-conditional outcome prediction.
- **Input Modalities:**
 - **Vision:** Raw RGB images from the frontal Kinect camera.
 - **Tactile:** Raw RGB images from the two GelSight sensors (one for each finger). For tactile inputs, background subtraction (difference between images before and after contact) is used to emphasize deformations. Images are downscaled to 256x256 for network processing.
 - **Action:** A 5-dimensional vector representing a candidate grasp adjustment, comprising 3D motion (x, y, z translation), in-plane rotation, and change in gripping force. The end-effector's current pose (position and angle) is also provided as input.
- **Network Architecture:**
 - **Feature Extraction:** Each image modality (RGB camera, GelSight Left, GelSight Right) is processed by a separate deep convolutional network. Specifically, the penultimate layer of a 50-layer **ResNet** architecture, pre-trained on ImageNet, is used. The weights for the two tactile networks are shared.
 - **Action Processing:** The action vector and end-effector pose are processed by a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
 - **Fusion:** A **late fusion approach** is employed. The feature vectors from all four input branches (RGB camera, two GelSights, and action/pose MLP) are concatenated.
 - **Output Layer:** The concatenated features are fed into a two-layer fully-connected network, which outputs a single value representing the **predicted grasp success probability** `f(s, a)`.
- **Training:**
 - The network is trained end-to-end to minimize the cross-entropy loss between the predicted success probability and the binary grasp outcome labels.
 - **Pre-training:** ImageNet pre-trained weights are used for the convolutional layers to accelerate training.
 - **Data Augmentation:** Random cropping of GelSight images (224x224 from 256x256) is used.

#### 3.3. Regrasp Optimization

Once the action-conditional model `f` is trained, it is used to select the optimal grasp adjustment action `a*` at time `t` that maximizes the predicted success probability: `a* = argmax_a f(s_t, a)`.
- **Search Strategy:** A **stochastic search** approach is used. The robot randomly samples a large number of potential actions (4900 random actions plus 100 fixed-translation/rotation actions with varying force) and evaluates their success probability using the learned model. The action with the highest predicted success probability is then executed.
- **Action Space:** The actions considered include translations in `[-2, +2] cm`, gripper rotations in `[-17°, +17°]`, and gripping forces in ` N`.
- **Grasp Execution:** If the predicted success probability for the chosen action exceeds a threshold (set to 0.9), the re-grasp includes lifting the object; otherwise, it only adjusts the gripper. Platt scaling is applied to probabilities for calibration.

#### 3.4. Data Collection

A crucial aspect is the **self-supervised automated data collection process**:
- **Trials:** Over 6,450 grasping trials were initially collected from 65 diverse training objects. Later, an additional 25,404 data points were collected through an "on-policy" approach, using the learned model to guide data collection.
- **Trial Procedure:**
 1. Kinect depth data is used to approximate the object's position.
 2. The end-effector's (x, y) coordinates are set to the object's center plus a random perturbation, height randomized, and orientation randomized.
 3. The gripper closes with a randomized gripping force (`F`).
 4. The gripper attempts to lift the object and holds for 4 seconds.
 5. If the object remains in the gripper, it's a success; otherwise, it's a failure. The object is then randomized for the next trial.
- **Automatic Labeling:** Grasp outcomes (success/failure) are automatically generated using deep neural network classifiers (trained to detect contact from raw GelSight images). A small subset of ambiguous samples underwent manual labeling and fine-tuning.
- **Data Augmentation (Intermediate States):** Additional data points are generated by considering intermediate states: when the robot is gripping (zero translation/rotation action) and when it has released the gripper but not yet moved (same action as when in contact). This augmentation brings the initial dataset to 18,070 examples.
- **GelSight Robustness:** The study notes that while replacing GelSight gels (due to wear and tear) initially degraded performance, training with data from several different gels led to a model robust to specific gel variations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed visuo-tactile action-conditional model for robotic grasping and regrasping.

#### 4.1. Model Prediction Performance

- **Action-Conditional Advantage:** The model's ability to predict future grasp outcomes given a candidate action (`f(s,a)`) is critical. Table I demonstrates that withholding action information ("Tactile + Vision (no action)") significantly drops prediction accuracy (76.43% vs. 80.28%). This confirms that the model successfully learns to evaluate the importance of different actions.
- **Multimodal Superiority:** The full "Tactile + Vision (+ action)" model consistently outperforms ablated versions. It achieves the highest cross-validation accuracy (80.28%), surpassing "Vision only (+ action)" (73.03%) and "Tactile only (+ action)" (79.34%). This highlights the synergistic benefit of combining both high-resolution vision and touch.
- **Higher Resolution vs. Data Volume:** While the authors acknowledge that their vision-only model's performance might be lower due to a smaller dataset compared to prior work (40x less data than), the tactile+vision model still significantly outperforms it, suggesting the inherent value of tactile information even with less visual data.

#### 4.2. Robot Grasping Performance

- **Superior Grasp Success:** On the physical robot, the "Tactile + Vision" model significantly outperforms both the "Vision only" model and a manually engineered "Cylinder fitting" baseline.
 - On the "Easy" set of 11 previously unseen objects, the "Tactile + Vision" model achieved an impressive **94.0% average grasp success rate**, compared to "Vision only" at 63.2% and "Cylinder fitting" at 75.9%.
 - On the more challenging "Hard" set of 11 unseen objects (after retraining with more on-policy data), the "Tactile + Vision" model still achieved **73.6% success**, outperforming "Vision only" (50.0%) and "Cylinder fitting" (66.8%).
- **Effectiveness on Challenging Objects:** The largest improvements with the visuo-tactile model are observed for compliant, small, or irregular objects where visual cues alone might be insufficient to ascertain a good grasp. This demonstrates the model's ability to generalize to novel and difficult cases.

#### 4.3. Interpretability of Learned Behaviors

Qualitative analysis of the learned model reveals interpretable and useful grasping strategies:
- **Grasping Force Modulation:** The model learns to modulate the applied force based on the current contact state (Fig. 4). While stronger grasps are generally preferred for stable contacts (Fig. 4a), the model learns to *reduce* force in unstable situations (e.g., grasping a corner of a cube), predicting that high forces could lead to object displacement and slippage (Fig. 4b). This demonstrates a nuanced understanding beyond simple force application.
- **Downward Motion Preference:** The model strongly prefers downward motion (z-translation) for successful grasps (Fig. 6 and 7). Analysis (Fig. 5) shows that downward movement is beneficial when the fingers are gripping the top of an object, aiming to lower the grasp point, which intuitively increases stability by lowering the center of gravity relative to the contact points. This suggests the model implicitly learned to grasp objects lower to improve stability.

#### 4.4. Minimum Force Grasping

One of the most significant practical findings is the model's ability to optimize for secondary objectives like minimizing applied force while maintaining a high success rate.
- When optimizing for "minimum force" with a constraint on success rate (>90%), the "Tactile + Vision" model achieved a **94% success rate** with a mean applied force of **10 N**. In contrast, optimizing for "maximum success" yielded a 95% success rate but with a higher mean force of **20 N** (Fig. 8a).
- Similarly, for the "Vision only" model, "minimum force" optimization resulted in 76% success with a mean of **6 N**, compared to 76% success with 18 N for "maximum success" (Fig. 8b).
- This demonstrates that the action-conditional model can effectively reduce the force exerted during grasping without compromising performance. This capability is crucial for safely handling **fragile or delicate objects**, a major limitation for many current robotic manipulators.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in robotic manipulation, particularly in the realm of dexterous grasping. Its key contributions and potential impacts are multi-faceted:

1. **Advancement in Visuo-Tactile Integration:** The paper presents the first action-conditioned model for learning to grasp directly from raw, high-resolution visuo-tactile inputs. This is a pioneering approach that moves beyond traditional reliance on single modalities or hand-engineered features for tactile data. By demonstrating the efficacy of end-to-end learning from raw sensor streams, it paves the way for more robust and adaptable robotic perception systems.

2. **Reduced Engineering Effort:** A major practical benefit is the elimination of the need for explicit sensor calibration and analytical modeling of contact forces. This data-driven, self-supervised learning paradigm significantly reduces the human engineering effort typically required to integrate complex sensors like GelSight into robotic systems, making advanced tactile sensing more accessible for robotic applications.

3. **Enhanced Grasping Robustness and Efficiency:** The demonstrated superior performance on novel and challenging objects, especially compliant or irregular ones, highlights the model's ability to generalize and adapt. The iterative regrasping capability, informed by tactile feedback, makes the grasping process more robust to initial uncertainties and environmental variations, leading to higher success rates and fewer attempts.

4. **Enabling Advanced Manipulation Capabilities:** The ability to optimize for secondary objectives, such as "minimum force" grasping, is a crucial advancement. This feature is invaluable for applications involving fragile objects (e.g., in manufacturing, packaging, or agriculture), where excessive force can lead to damage. This opens up new possibilities for robots to handle delicate items with human-like precision and care.

5. **Foundation for Active Sensing and Control:** The work underscores the value of active, interactive sensing in robotics. Rather than passively observing, the robot actively probes the environment, uses the feedback to refine its actions, and iteratively improves its grasp. This paradigm aligns with how humans interact with objects and lays a foundation for more sophisticated closed-loop control strategies in robotics.

While the approach has limitations (e.g., single-step predictions, coarse actions, cluttered environments), the paper identifies clear avenues for future work, such as moving towards more continuous feedback control and finer-grained manipulation. Ultimately, "More Than a Feeling" demonstrates the profound impact of combining cutting-edge tactile sensing with deep learning, pushing robotic grasping closer to the dexterity and adaptability observed in biological systems.
