# Beyond Failure Recovery: An Engagement-Aware Human-in-the-loop Framework for Robotic Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> An Engagement-aware Model Predictive Control (E-MPC) framework is presented for human-in-the-loop robotic systems, proactively managing user engagement alongside task success and workload constraints. Tested on a robot-assisted bite acquisition system, E-MPC significantly improved subjective user experience metrics, including engagement (4.00 vs 2.40), interaction satisfaction (4.00 vs 2.80), and agency satisfaction (4.30 vs 2.60) compared to a strong baseline, while maintaining 100% task success and workload compliance.

## Problem

- Traditional human-in-the-loop (HIL) robotics are robot-centric, primarily involving humans for failure recovery or uncertainty resolution, treating them as problem-solving tools.
- This failure-driven interaction paradigm leads to reduced user engagement and a diminished sense of agency, particularly in human-centered applications like assistive robotics.
- A trade-off exists between interaction frequency (which can boost engagement) and user workload, where excessive queries lead to fatigue, necessitating a balanced approach.

## Method

- Developed an ==Engagement-aware Model Predictive Control== (**E-MPC**) framework that explicitly optimizes user engagement as a primary objective, alongside task success and workload constraints.
- Proposes a predictive interaction dynamics model that describes how user engagement evolves based on the frequency and difficulty of robot queries.
- Leverages both task-assistive queries (for functional aid) and "engagement-only" queries (to modulate engagement without direct task assistance) within a receding-horizon MPC scheme.

## Results

- E-MPC demonstrated higher Engagement Tracking Accuracy (**4.00** vs **2.40**), Interaction Satisfaction (**4.00** vs **2.80**), and Agency Satisfaction (**4.30** vs **2.60**) in a real-robot study, with 9 out of 10 participants preferring E-MPC over a WorkloadAware baseline.
- Achieved comparable Task Success and Workload Compliance to baselines, showing that enhanced user experience does not necessitate compromising functional performance.
- Simulation studies confirmed E-MPC's superior performance in jointly optimizing engagement, workload, and task success across diverse user personas and query types, outperforming traditional approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling and proactively optimizing user engagement within an MPC framework can significantly improve the subjective user experience in human-robot interaction.
> - Strategic inclusion of "engagement-only" queries allows robots to maintain desired user engagement levels with minimal workload, addressing the engagement-workload trade-off.
> - Balancing task success, user workload, and a desired engagement level in a unified optimization problem leads to more human-centric and acceptable robotic systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional human-in-the-loop (HIL) robotic systems primarily leverage human assistance to enhance robot performance, robustness, and reliability. In this paradigm, human intervention is typically solicited only when the robot encounters uncertainty, requires additional information, or faces an imminent task failure. This approach treats humans predominantly as tools for problem-solving or error correction, with the interaction being robot-centric.

However, the increasing deployment of robots in human-centered applications, such such as caregiving, assistive technologies, and household tasks, introduces new requirements that extend beyond mere task success and failure recovery. In these contexts, the quality of robot assistance is significantly influenced by the human user's experience of the interaction. Factors like user engagement, sense of agency, and overall satisfaction become critical for long-term acceptance and psychological well-being. Prior research in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) has underscored the importance of sustaining user engagement over extended interactions and its link to a user's sense of agency. Studies have indicated that engagement can wane over time, even with successful robot execution, and that users may prefer meaningful involvement rather than purely passive observation, even if it impacts efficiency.

Concurrently, another line of HIL research has begun to address the trade-off between task success and human workload, recognizing that excessive queries can lead to user fatigue. Shared autonomy approaches blend continuous human input with autonomous control, operating at a lower, control-level interaction. Furthermore, some work focuses on recognizing and detecting engagement, but these approaches typically do not model engagement as an objective that can be actively shaped by the robot.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by addressing a gap in existing HIL frameworks. It moves beyond solely failure-driven or workload-aware interaction strategies to explicitly integrate user engagement as a primary objective in the robot's decision-making process. By proactively planning interactions not just to resolve uncertainty but also to maintain a desired user engagement level, the work seeks to develop robotic systems that are more attuned to human needs and preferences in long-horizon, human-centered applications. It distinguishes itself from prior work by modeling engagement as a continuous, controllable state within a predictive control framework, aiming to provide a principled method for balancing autonomy and interaction for improved user experience.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of conventional human-in-the-loop (HIL) robotic approaches when applied to human-centered contexts. In many assistive or household robotics settings, current HIL strategies are predominantly "robot-centric," meaning they involve users almost exclusively when the robot requires assistance to complete an action successfully, typically due to uncertainty or failure.

The authors identify several problems with this failure-driven interaction paradigm:
- **Reduced User Engagement and Agency:** By confining user involvement to corrective interventions, users can be relegated to passive observers for extended periods of a task. This passivity can lead to reduced engagement and a diminished sense of agency, which is the perceived ability to influence ongoing outcomes. In critical applications like physical caregiving, where users might already have mobility limitations that reduce their ability to intervene, such interaction policies can further exacerbate disengagement. For example, a user being continuously and passively fed by a robot may experience a loss of control and involvement.
- **Mismatched Interaction Preferences:** Many users, particularly in assistive contexts, may prefer to be meaningfully involved in the robot's decision-making process, rather than merely receiving continuous autonomous assistance. This preference for control can be vital for psychological well-being and long-term acceptance of robotic aid.
- **Workload-Engagement Trade-off:** While sparse, failure-driven interaction can lead to disengagement, excessively frequent interaction can equally be detrimental by increasing user workload and leading to fatigue. This creates a trade-off that requires careful management, especially in long-horizon tasks where interactions are repeated over time and individual preferences for engagement can vary.

To address these motivations, the research establishes the following key objectives:
1. **Develop an Engagement-aware HIL Framework:** The central objective is to propose a novel framework, termed Engagement-aware Model Predictive Control (E-MPC), that explicitly treats user engagement as an objective to be optimized during task execution. This moves beyond the conventional view of interaction solely as a mechanism for resolving uncertainty.
2. **Proactive Interaction Planning:** The framework aims to enable robots to proactively decide *when* and *how* to involve the user, not just when assistance is functionally needed, but also to maintain a user-specified desired level of engagement throughout the task.
3. **Model Interaction Dynamics:** A specific objective is to develop a predictive model that captures how user engagement evolves over time as a function of both the frequency and type of interaction. This model should allow the robot to anticipate the impact of its interaction choices on user engagement.
4. **Balance Multiple Objectives and Constraints:** The framework is designed to balance potentially conflicting objectives: maintaining user engagement, respecting user workload constraints, and ensuring task success.
5. **Empirical Validation:** The final objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed E-MPC framework through comprehensive simulation experiments across diverse user personas and to validate its performance in a real-world human-robot interaction scenario, specifically a robot-assisted bite acquisition system with participants emulating mobility limitations. This includes assessing both objective performance metrics and subjective user experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on **Engagement-aware Model Predictive Control (E-MPC)**, a human-in-the-loop framework designed to proactively manage user engagement, workload, and task success in robotic systems.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as achieving successful task completion while bounding user workload and optimizing user engagement towards a desired level. The robot can either query the user for input or proceed autonomously. The system operates with a skill library $S = \{\phi_1, \dots, \phi_N\}$ and a planner that sequences skills. Each skill $\phi_i$ at time $t$ has an associated confidence $c_t = C(\phi_i_t, o_t) \in$, representing the likelihood of success.

Interaction actions $q_t \in Q$ are parameterized by query difficulty ($q_{d,t}$) and query response difficulty ($q_{rd,t}$). Task-assistive queries $Q_{assist,\phi_i}$ provide functional input for skill execution under uncertainty. Feasible queries $Q_{feas,\phi_i}$ include task-assistive queries and "engagement-only" queries that do not directly aid task completion but can boost engagement (e.g., asking about food shape).

The framework maintains three state variables: current user engagement $g_t \in$, desired user engagement $g_{des} \in$, and current user workload $w_t \in$.

#### 3.2. B. Interaction Dynamics Modeling

The core of E-MPC is an interaction dynamics model that describes the evolution of user engagement $g_t$. Engagement is treated as a latent, internal state, estimated online through interactions and calibrated query impacts.
The model hypothesizes that engagement is influenced by:
- **Query Response Difficulty ($q_{rd,t}$):** Different query response difficulties (e.g., multiple-choice vs. drawing-based) lead to different engagement outcomes. This is captured by a mapping $k_{q_{rd}}$ from difficulty to an "immediate engagement boost" value in $$.
- **Query Frequency ($\delta_t$):** The time gap between the current time step $t$ and the last query time step $t_k$ influences how much prior engagement decays.

The engagement dynamics are modeled as:
$g_t = e^{-\lambda_g \delta_t} g_{t_k} + (1 - e^{-\lambda_g \delta_t}) k_{q_{rd}} q_{rd,t}$
where $\lambda_g > 0$ is the decay rate. This model implies that engagement evolves as a combination of a discounted prior engagement level (reflecting decay over time) and an instantaneous engagement impulse from the current query. When no query is issued, the engagement boost is zero, and engagement purely decays. The parameter $\lambda_g$ controls the relative weighting between past and current engagement, with larger values indicating less persistent memory. The mapping $k_{q_{rd}}$ is obtained through a user-specific calibration process.

#### 3.3. C. User Workload Modeling

A linear discrete-time user workload model is adopted from prior work:
$w_t = \gamma w_{init} + \sum_{j=0}^{H-1} w_j^T q_{t-j} + w_0$
This model considers a weighted initial workload ($\gamma w_{init}$), the impact of past interaction actions ($q_{t-j}$) on current workload (coefficients $w_j$), a history length $H$, and a bias term $w_0$.

#### 3.4. D. Engagement-aware Model Predictive Control (MPC)

E-MPC uses an MPC framework to optimize interaction actions $q_t$ over a finite prediction horizon $L$. The MPC objective is formulated to minimize a cost function that includes engagement tracking and workload constraint violation penalties, subject to task success constraints.

1. **User Desired Engagement Level ($g_{des}$):** This is a self-reported preference, specified by the user before and potentially updated during a task, serving as the target for engagement.
2. **Engagement Tracking Cost:** $J_g = (g_{des} - g_{t+l})^2$. This term encourages the predicted engagement level $g_{t+l}$ to track the desired level $g_{des}$.
3. **Task Success Constraint:**
 $q_{t+l} \in \begin{cases} Q_{assist,\phi_i_{t+l}}, & \text{if } C(\phi_i_{t+l}, o_{t+l}) \leq \tau_c \text{ and } r_{\phi_i_{t+l}}^t = \tau_r \\ Q_{feas,\phi_i_{t+l}} \cup \{0\}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
 If the skill confidence falls below a threshold $\tau_c$ and the maximum retries $\tau_r$ have been exhausted, the robot *must* ask a task-assistive query. Otherwise, it can choose any feasible query or proceed autonomously.
4. **User Workload Constraint:** $J_w = \lambda_w \max(0, w_{t+l} - \tau_w)^2$. This term penalizes workload $w_{t+l}$ exceeding a safe threshold $\tau_w$ with a large constant $\lambda_w$, allowing violations only if heavily penalized.

The finite-horizon MPC optimization problem at time $t$ is:
$\min_{\{q_{t+l}\}} \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \left( (g_{des} - g_{t+l})^2 + \lambda_w \max(0, w_{t+l} - \tau_w)^2 \right)$
subject to the task success constraint for $l = 0, \dots, L-1$. Only the first control input $q_t$ is applied, and the process repeats in a receding-horizon manner.

#### 3.5. E. Evaluation Scenario: Robot-assisted Bite Acquisition

The E-MPC framework is instantiated on a robot-assisted bite acquisition system using a Kinova Gen3 7-DoF arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper.
- **Skill Library:** Skewering ($\phi_1$), Scooping ($\phi_2$), and Twirling ($\phi_3$).
- **Skill Confidences:** Modeled as binary values derived from the uncertainty of a transformer-based skill parameter estimation model (using Monte Carlo Dropout) and a failure identification module (e.g., checking if the interaction point is within the food item's segmentation mask).
- **Query Types:**
 - **Task-assistive queries:** Drawing-based queries (e.g., asking the user to draw the skewering point and angle on an image) for each skill type, used when the robot is uncertain. These are classified as "Hard" ($q_{d,t}=2, q_{rd,t}=2$).
 - **Engagement-only ("fake") query:** A multiple-choice question about the food item's shape, intended to promote engagement without directly aiding task execution. This is classified as "Easy" ($q_{d,t}=1, q_{rd,t}=1$).

This setup allows for a clear distinction between queries that are functionally necessary and those primarily designed to modulate user engagement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The E-MPC framework was evaluated through extensive simulation experiments and a real-robot user study.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments

Simulation experiments compared E-MPC against four baselines: AlwaysQuery (always asks task-assistive queries), NeverQuery (always autonomous), TaskConfidenceAware (queries only when skill confidence is low), and WorkloadAware (optimizes for task success and user workload, based on). Three user personas (low, medium, high desired engagement $g_{des} \in \{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$) were tested under varying pre-query skill success rates and workload thresholds.

- **Engagement Tracking Accuracy:** E-MPC consistently achieved high Engagement Tracking Accuracy across all three personas. Its advantage became more pronounced as the desired engagement level ($g_{des}$) increased, outperforming TaskConfidenceAware and WorkloadAware, which struggled to maintain higher engagement levels. AlwaysQuery could track high engagement well but disregarded workload.
- **Skill Success:** E-MPC achieved higher Skill Success than all baselines except AlwaysQuery. This was attributed to E-MPC's strategy of proactive querying, not only when confidence was low but also when interaction was beneficial for engagement, which helped mitigate issues from noisy confidence estimates.
- **Satisfaction:** E-MPC demonstrated superior performance on the Satisfaction metric (which jointly considers engagement tracking, workload compliance, and task success) compared to all baselines. WorkloadAware, while strong, showed reduced satisfaction for medium- and high-engagement personas due to its lack of explicit engagement optimization. AlwaysQuery's high workload led to reduced satisfaction.
- **Workload Thresholds:** When allowable workload was low, E-MPC balanced engagement tracking with workload compliance, resulting in higher overall satisfaction compared to AlwaysQuery, which prioritized interaction frequency.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **NoWorkloadCst:** Without the workload constraint, E-MPC occasionally violated the workload threshold, especially for high $g_{des}$, leading to decreased satisfaction.
 - **NoEngagementTrack:** This ablation consistently underperformed E-MPC across all settings, highlighting the necessity of the engagement tracking cost.
- **Effect of "Fake" Queries:** The inclusion of "engagement-only" (fake) queries, such as easy multiple-choice questions about food shape, resulted in a statistically significant improvement in the Satisfaction metric across all personas. These queries provided engagement boosts with low workload cost, allowing for better alignment with user engagement preferences.
- **Heuristic Baselines:** E-MPC consistently achieved higher satisfaction compared to simple heuristic baselines (Random and Periodic querying), demonstrating the value of its explicit engagement modeling and adaptive interaction planning.

#### 4.2. B. Real-Robot User Study

A real-robot user study was conducted with 10 participants emulating mobility limitations (using resistance bands) on the robot-assisted bite acquisition system. The study compared E-MPC against the WorkloadAware baseline, identified as the strongest competitor from simulations. User-reported metrics on a 5-point Likert scale (Engagement Tracking Accuracy, Workload Compliance, Task Success, Interaction Satisfaction, Agency Satisfaction) were collected, along with qualitative feedback and eye-gaze analysis.

- **Subjective User Experience:**
 - E-MPC achieved significantly better performance on **Engagement Tracking Accuracy** (E-MPC: 4.00 ± 0.67 vs. WorkloadAware: 2.40 ± 1.07, p=0.003), **Interaction Satisfaction** (E-MPC: 4.00 ± 1.05 vs. WorkloadAware: 2.80 ± 1.14, p=0.032), and **Agency Satisfaction** (E-MPC: 4.30 ± 0.95 vs. WorkloadAware: 2.60 ± 1.26, p=0.007).
 - Both E-MPC and WorkloadAware performed comparably on **Task Success** (E-MPC: 4.50 ± 0.97 vs. WorkloadAware: 4.70 ± 0.48, p=0.925) and achieved 100% **Workload Compliance** (all participants found workload acceptable for both methods).
- **User Preference:** 9 out of 10 participants preferred E-MPC over the WorkloadAware baseline, citing improved engagement and a sense of balance.
- **Role of "Fake" Queries:** During the user study, 51.65% of the queries issued by E-MPC were engagement-only ("fake") queries, demonstrating their effective strategic use in maintaining desired engagement levels.
- **Eye-gaze Analysis:** An additional analysis of study videos revealed that when user engagement deviated substantially from the desired level, participants' gaze behavior became less task-focused (e.g., 123% higher rate of missing target objects, 37% lower gaze at the food plate), and head motion variability increased. This provided a behavioral indicator consistent with engagement misalignment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that broadens the scope of human-in-the-loop robotic systems beyond mere failure recovery to include the proactive management of user engagement. This shift is particularly significant for human-centered applications like assistive robotics, where the user's experience, psychological well-being, and sense of agency are critical for long-term acceptance and effective deployment.

The introduction of **Engagement-aware Model Predictive Control (E-MPC)** demonstrates a principled approach to integrating user engagement as a directly optimized objective alongside task success and workload constraints. By modeling how engagement evolves based on interaction frequency and type, E-MPC enables robots to make informed decisions about *when* and *how* to involve users, even when explicit task assistance is not strictly required. The empirical validation, both in simulation across diverse user preferences and in a real-robot study with subjective user reports and behavioral analysis, indicates that E-MPC can substantially improve subjective user experience metrics such as Engagement Tracking Accuracy, Interaction Satisfaction, and perceived Agency Satisfaction, without compromising task success or exceeding acceptable workload levels. The strategic use of "fake" or engagement-only queries highlights a novel mechanism for modulating user experience in a resource-efficient manner.

The potential impact of this work includes:
- **Enhanced User Experience in Assistive Robotics:** By prioritizing user engagement and agency, E-MPC can contribute to more human-centric and acceptable assistive technologies, fostering greater independence and psychological well-being for individuals with mobility limitations.
- **Broader Applicability:** The core E-MPC framework is designed to be extensible to various long-horizon human-robot interaction scenarios (e.g., robot-assisted grooming, meal preparation, collaborative assembly), requiring only domain-specific adaptations of skill libraries, query sets, and confidence models.
- **New Directions for HRI Research:** This work motivates further investigation into the complex interplay between engagement, workload, task success, and agency, and encourages the development of more sophisticated, human-aware interaction planning algorithms. It also opens avenues for exploring alternative interaction modalities beyond explicit queries and for developing more transparent and adaptive engagement regulation strategies.

Despite its contributions, the research identifies several limitations that suggest important future work. These include addressing the transparency and potential for perceived deception regarding "fake" queries, developing more robust online estimation methods for user engagement and preferences to reduce reliance on explicit calibration, and expanding the framework to incorporate a wider range of interaction modalities beyond query actions. Crucially, future studies involving actual target populations with mobility limitations are needed to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the framework's real-world efficacy and impact.
