# IDD: A Dataset for Exploring Problems of Autonomous Navigation in Unconstrained Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> The IDD (Indian Driving Dataset) introduces a large-scale collection of images captured from Indian road scenes, providing pixel-level semantic and instance annotations across 10,004 frames for autonomous navigation. This new benchmark highlights a performance drop for state-of-the-art models compared to structured datasets, underscoring the challenges of real-world generalization to unstructured environments.

## Problem

- Autonomous navigation systems rely on datasets primarily representing 'structured driving environments,' leading to algorithms that struggle to generalize to diverse real-world conditions.
- A significant gap exists in datasets that capture 'unstructured environments,' characterized by ambiguous road boundaries, highly diverse traffic participants, and less adherence to traffic rules, prevalent in many global regions.
- Current computer vision algorithms for scene understanding perform poorly when deployed in these complex, highly variable unstructured road settings.

## Method

- Developed **IDD** by collecting 182 distinct drive sequences from urban, rural, and highway areas in Indian cities, capturing the intricacies of unstructured driving conditions.
- Annotated 10,004 frames with fine-grained, ==pixel-level semantic and instance labels==, incorporating a novel ==four-level label hierarchy== for 34 categories to manage scene complexity and ambiguity.
- Introduced new, context-specific labels such as 'auto-rickshaw,' 'billboard,' 'curb,' and distinct 'drivable fall-back' and 'non-drivable fall-back' areas, crucial for understanding Indian road scenes.

## Results

- State-of-the-art semantic segmentation models achieved mean IoU scores of approximately **66.6%** at the most granular Level 3 on IDD, significantly lower than their performance on structured datasets.
- Cross-dataset evaluation showed a model trained on Cityscapes achieved only **34% mIoU** when tested on IDD, while an IDD-trained model reached **49% mIoU** on Cityscapes, quantifying the domain shift.
- IDD contains nearly **double** the instance count of traffic participants compared to Cityscapes, and its unique classes like 'auto-rickshaw' and 'billboard' have substantial pixel counts, confirming its distinct characteristics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unstructured driving environments, as represented by IDD, present significantly different label distributions and pose a greater challenge to state-of-the-art semantic segmentation and instance segmentation models compared to structured datasets.
> - A substantial domain discrepancy exists between IDD and structured datasets, evidenced by significant performance drops when models trained on one domain are applied to the other.
> - Models trained on the diverse IDD dataset demonstrated a capability to generalize somewhat better to structured environments than models trained solely on structured data could generalize to unstructured ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous navigation systems relies heavily on comprehensive datasets for training and evaluating computer vision algorithms, particularly for scene understanding. In recent years, several large-scale datasets, such as KITTI, Cityscapes, Berkeley Deep Driving (BDD100K), and Mapillary Vistas, have significantly contributed to the advancements in this field. These datasets typically focus on "structured driving environments," characterized by clearly defined infrastructure like lanes, a limited number of well-categorized traffic participants, minimal variation in object or background appearance, and a general adherence to traffic regulations.

This work introduces the IDD (Indian Driving Dataset) to address a recognized gap in the existing research landscape: the limited availability of datasets representing "unstructured environments." These environments, prevalent in large parts of the world, including Asia, South America, and Africa, often do not conform to the assumptions made by datasets focused on structured settings. The paper argues that current algorithms are largely untested in their ability to generalize to such diverse and complex road conditions. By providing a dataset reflecting these challenges, IDD aims to expand the scope of autonomous navigation research beyond well-delineated European or North American road scenarios. It positions itself as a critical resource for developing more robust and globally applicable self-driving technologies, suggesting that existing datasets are reaching saturation in terms of the challenges they pose, and the next frontier lies in understanding and navigating these more complex, unstructured settings. The dataset is specifically designed to enable the exploration of new problems such as domain adaptation, few-shot learning for rare or highly variable classes, and more complex behavior prediction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce the IDD, a novel dataset meticulously designed for road scene understanding in unstructured driving environments. This objective is driven by several detailed motivations arising from the limitations of existing datasets and the global requirements for autonomous navigation:

**Context of Existing Datasets:**
Current datasets predominantly capture structured driving environments, such as those found in Europe or North America. These environments typically feature well-maintained roads, clear lane markings, consistent traffic signage, predictable traffic flow, and a relatively homogeneous set of traffic participants. Algorithms trained on such data often struggle when deployed in regions where these conditions are not met.

**Limitations of Structured Environments:**
The assumptions inherent in structured environment datasets include:
- Well-delineated infrastructure, such as clear lanes and sidewalks.
- A small and well-defined set of categories for traffic participants.
- Low variation in object or background appearance.
- Strict adherence to traffic rules by drivers and pedestrians.
The authors contend that these assumptions are largely unfulfilled in many parts of the world, highlighting a critical gap in the generalizability of current autonomous navigation systems.

**Specific Challenges in Unstructured Environments:**
The motivation for IDD is further underpinned by specific characteristics of unstructured environments that pose unique challenges for autonomous systems:
- **Ambiguous Road Boundaries:** Unlike the clear-cut roadsides found in many structured environments, Indian roads often have undefined boundaries. Roadsides can consist of muddy terrain that might be partially drivable, or roads themselves can be obscured by dirt, leading to highly ambiguous drivable areas. This contrasts with models trained on datasets like Cityscapes, which may misclassify flat areas adjacent to roads as safe driving zones even if they are not.
- **Diversity of Vehicles and Pedestrians:** Unstructured environments exhibit a much broader range of traffic participants. This includes unique vehicle types, such as auto-rickshaws, which behave differently from standard cars. Even common vehicle categories like cars display higher within-class appearance variations due to greater wear and tear and a wider range of manufacturing years. There is also a higher frequency of motorbikes, often with multiple riders, and pedestrians frequently cross roads at arbitrary locations rather than designated crosswalks. Furthermore, traffic participants may adhere less strictly to traffic rules and signals.
- **Extensive Use of Information Boards:** Billboards and other large information displays are common in these environments. These can be crucial for localization and mapping by serving as landmarks or indicating specific businesses or services. They can also be attached to vehicles, providing additional contextual information.
- **Diversity of Ambient Conditions:** The dataset captures a wide range of ambient conditions. Images are collected at various times of day (mid-day, dawn, dusk), including scenes with heavy shadows common in long summer seasons and heavily clouded skies. Variations in particulate matter (fog, dust, smog) also lead to significant appearance changes that challenge models trained on more consistent visual data.

**Need for IDD:**
The combined effect of these factors is a significantly greater complexity in road scenes. This complexity necessitates:
- **Larger-scale and More Diverse Data:** To train algorithms capable of handling the increased variability.
- **Expanded Label Sets:** To account for new vehicle types, distinct drivable surfaces, and prevalent background elements.
- **New Problem Formulations:** The inherent challenges in IDD are intended to drive research in areas such as domain adaptation (transferring knowledge from structured to unstructured environments), few-shot learning (addressing rare but critical classes like animals), and more sophisticated behavior prediction algorithms that account for less constrained traffic dynamics.
- **Addressing Dataset Saturation:** The authors observe that state-of-the-art semantic segmentation algorithms achieve lower accuracies on IDD compared to Cityscapes, even with larger training data. This highlights that conventional datasets may be becoming "saturated," indicating that the next set of challenges and advancements will arise from more complex and diverse datasets like IDD. The dataset aims to provide a platform for developing models that can perform safely and reliably across diverse global geographies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of the IDD dataset involved a systematic approach to data acquisition, annotation, and statistical validation to ensure its relevance and quality for autonomous navigation research in unstructured environments.

**Data Acquisition:**
The data was collected from Bangalore and Hyderabad, two rapidly growing cities in India, including their urban, rural, and peripheral highway areas. The collection spanned various road types—single-lane, double-lane, and highways—with diverse traffic compositions. The authors emphasize that these locations represent highly unstructured driving conditions due to ongoing construction, poorly defined road boundaries, the pervasive presence of pedestrians and jaywalkers, and a high density of two-wheeled vehicles and trucks. A total of 182 distinct drive sequences were recorded.

**Frame Selection and Annotation:**
From the collected video sequences, 10,004 individual frames were selected for fine-grained annotation. These frames were chosen from one of the forward-facing cameras of a stereo pair. To capture varied scenarios, images were sampled at different rates, with denser sampling in crowded areas and locations of particular interest, such as traffic junctions. The annotation process involved creating layered polygon masks, similar to the methodology employed for the Cityscapes dataset, to delineate objects and regions with pixel-level precision.

**Label Hierarchy and Annotation:**
A total of 34 labels were defined for fine annotation. Recognizing the increased complexity and potential for ambiguity in unstructured scenes, the authors introduced a novel four-level label hierarchy. This hierarchy was designed to allow for varying degrees of semantic granularity and to manage ambiguity among certain classes. The levels are structured as follows:
- **Level 1:** 7 broad labels.
- **Level 2:** 16 labels.
- **Level 3:** 26 labels.
- **Level 4:** 30 labels (the most granular, excluding labels combined due to extreme rarity).
Each higher-level category is defined as the union of ambiguous labels from the level below it, reducing ambiguity in more generalized representations.
The label set was expanded to include classes not present in popular benchmarks like Cityscapes, such as "auto-rickshaw," "billboard," "animal," and "curb," which are common in Indian road scenes. Critically, the dataset includes distinct labels for "road," "drivable fall-back" (areas safely drivable besides the main road), and "non-drivable fall-back" (unsafe or non-drivable flat surfaces), to address the prevalent ambiguity of drivable areas. "Fall-back" labels were also assigned to highly ambiguous objects to ensure comprehensive coverage.
To ensure high annotation quality and consistency, the annotation team underwent a rigorous training process. They were initially tasked with re-annotating images from the Cityscapes dataset, and their annotations were compared against the ground truth. This iterative process continued until the annotators consistently achieved over 95% accuracy relative to Cityscapes ground truth labels.

**Statistical Analysis and Dataset Splits:**
A detailed statistical analysis of pixel counts per label was conducted, revealing significant class imbalance, particularly at Level 4 (e.g., "parking," "animal," "caravan," "traffic light" have very few pixels). The lower levels of the hierarchy were designed to mitigate this imbalance.
The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets with an approximate distribution of 70%, 10%, and 20%, respectively. To maintain data integrity and avoid data leakage, splitting was performed at the drive sequence level, meaning all images from a single sequence were assigned to the same split. Multiple splitting iterations were performed to ensure that all 30 labels in Level 4 were proportionally represented across the train, validation, and test sets.

**Comparison with Other Datasets:**
The authors provide a metadata comparison with other prominent semantic segmentation datasets for autonomous navigation, including Cityscapes, KITTI, Camvid, Leuven, Daimler, ADE20K, Mapillary Vistas, and BDD100K. IDD is characterized by its use of calibrated cameras without distortions and the availability of nearby frames/video data, making it most similar to Cityscapes in terms of technical acquisition. This differentiates it from BDD100K (dashboard camera, reflections, night images, rain distortions) and Mapillary Vistas (diverse cameras, no video data). IDD boasts 10,004 labeled images from 182 sequences, featuring 30 (or 34) labels, with an average resolution of 1678x968 pixels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of IDD reveals several distinct characteristics compared to existing datasets and benchmarks current state-of-the-art models on its complex, unstructured data.

**Label Statistics:**
The dataset exhibits label distributions significantly different from Cityscapes. IDD contains a higher pixel count for categories such as "truck," "bus," "motorcycle," "guard rail," "bridge," and "rider," reflecting the nature of traffic in India. Crucially, the new labels introduced, including "autorickshaw," "billboard," "curb," "drivable-fallback," and "non-drivable-fallback," also possess substantial pixel counts, indicating their prevalence and importance in these environments. In terms of instances of traffic participants, IDD features almost double the count compared to Cityscapes, while maintaining a similar proportional distribution across categories.

**Control Experiments:**
Control experiments were conducted to establish upper bounds for Intersection-over-Union (IoU) scores, demonstrating the impact of processing resolution on segmentation accuracy. By downsampling ground truth masks and then upsampling them for evaluation, the study showed that lower-resolution processing significantly degrades segmentation results. For instance, subsampling ground truth by a factor of 128 (e.g., 128x downsampling and then upsampling) resulted in mean IoU scores dropping from approximately 99% to 48% at hierarchy Level 3, highlighting the challenge of achieving high accuracy with reduced input resolution.

**Domain Discrepancy:**
To quantify the shift in data distributions, a DRN-D-38 (Dilated Residual Networks) model was trained and tested across IDD, Cityscapes, Mapillary Vistas, and BDD100K datasets using a common set of 16 labels. The findings indicate that IDD presents a greater challenge than Cityscapes, exhibiting a similar level of "hardness" to Mapillary Vistas when models are trained and tested on their respective datasets (e.g., 70% mIoU for IDD vs. 76% for Cityscapes on common labels).
Cross-dataset evaluations demonstrated a significant performance drop when models were applied to different domains:
- A Cityscapes-trained model achieved only 34% mIoU when tested on IDD.
- Conversely, an IDD-trained model achieved 49% mIoU when tested on Cityscapes.
Notably, the IDD-trained model performed better when tested on Cityscapes and BDD100K compared to the reverse scenario, suggesting that IDD's broader diversity enables models to generalize somewhat better to structured environments than models trained solely on structured data can generalize to unstructured environments.

**Semantic Segmentation Benchmark:**
State-of-the-art semantic segmentation models consistently achieved lower accuracies on IDD compared to their performance on more structured datasets. For instance, the DRN-D-38 model achieved mean IoU scores of 85.9% (Level 1), 72.6% (Level 2), and 66.6% (Level 3). Submissions to an associated challenge using advanced models like DeeplabV3+, PSPNet, and an ensemble of Wider ResNet-38 with DeeplabV3 Decoder achieved slightly higher mIoU scores, peaking around 74.3% at Level 3.
Detailed analysis of individual class IoUs revealed scores below 25% for categories such as "bicycle," "traffic light," "vehicle fall-back," and "fence," which is attributed to their low pixel counts within the dataset. The confusion matrix highlighted significant misclassifications between semantically similar or visually ambiguous classes, including:
- "Motorcycle" and "bicycle."
- "Billboard" and "traffic sign."
- "Obs-str-bar-fallback," "vegetation," and "traffic light."
- "Building" and "billboard."
- "Vegetation" and "wall," "pole," "fence."
- "Drivable," "non-drivable," and "vegetation."
Qualitative examples demonstrated that models trained on IDD produced significantly better predictions in unstructured settings than those trained on Cityscapes. This included correctly identifying muddy areas as drivable, and accurately segmenting novel classes like auto-rickshaws, curbs, and billboards.

**Instance Segmentation Benchmark:**
An instance segmentation benchmark was also established for identifying and segmenting individual instances of traffic participants. Performance was assessed using Average Precision (AP) and AP@50 metrics. Challenge submissions using prominent instance segmentation architectures achieved the following:
- MaskRCNN with ResNet101 backbone: AP of 0.268 and AP@50 of 0.499.
- PANet: AP of 0.376 and AP@50 of 0.661.
These scores indicate that instance segmentation remains a significant challenge in the highly varied and often occluded scenes of IDD.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The IDD dataset holds significant implications for the advancement of autonomous navigation research and the deployment of self-driving technologies globally. Its introduction addresses several critical gaps and opens new avenues for scientific inquiry.

**Addressing a Critical Gap:**
IDD is presented as the first dedicated large-scale dataset to specifically target autonomous driving challenges in "unconstrained" or "unstructured" environments. By focusing on data collected from Indian roads, it provides a unique perspective on road scene understanding in geographies outside the commonly studied North American and European contexts, where road infrastructure and traffic behaviors differ markedly. This directly contributes to developing self-driving systems that are not limited to idealized conditions but can operate robustly in diverse global settings.

**Advancing Research Challenges:**
The empirical results, particularly the lower performance of state-of-the-art algorithms on IDD compared to Cityscapes, underscore the increased complexity and diversity of the dataset. This highlights the limitations of current methodologies and indicates that existing benchmarks based on structured environments may be nearing saturation. IDD therefore serves as a vital new benchmark, pushing the boundaries of what robust computer vision and machine learning algorithms must achieve for practical autonomous navigation. The fine-grained annotations and the novel four-level label hierarchy offer varied levels of complexity, allowing researchers to evaluate models for real-time applications (using coarser labels) or for highly detailed scene understanding (using finer labels).

**Enabling New Research Directions:**
The inherent characteristics of IDD actively promote research into several advanced computer vision and machine learning problems:
- **Domain Adaptation:** The observed domain discrepancy between IDD and structured datasets strongly motivates research into domain adaptation techniques. This is crucial for developing models that can transfer learned knowledge from abundantly available structured data to unstructured environments, potentially even with non-overlapping label spaces, reducing the need for extensive re-annotation.
- **Few-Shot Learning:** The presence of rare but critical classes (e.g., animals) and classes with high within-class appearance variance (e.g., auto-rickshaws) makes IDD an ideal testbed for few-shot learning. Algorithms that can learn effectively from limited examples are essential for deploying robust systems in environments where every possible object category cannot be exhaustively sampled.
- **Behavior Prediction:** The less constrained traffic participant behavior, lower adherence to traffic rules, ambiguous drivable areas, and potentially absent visual cues (like clear lane markings) in IDD present a uniquely challenging setting for behavior prediction. This will drive the development of more sophisticated, context-aware prediction models crucial for safe navigation.
- **Higher-Level Reasoning and Scene Understanding:** Beyond basic semantic segmentation, the unstructured nature of IDD offers opportunities for research in higher-level reasoning problems, such as comprehensive scene understanding, collision avoidance, and path planning. The dataset encourages algorithms to consider more complex interactions and infer intent in ambiguous situations, leading to more intelligent navigation strategies.

**Promoting Real-World Deployment:**
By providing a dataset that reflects real-world complexities in underserved geographies, IDD contributes directly to the development of self-driving car technology that is reliable, safe, and globally applicable. The insights gained from tackling the challenges presented by IDD will inform the design of more resilient autonomous systems, accelerating their responsible deployment in diverse markets worldwide. The open availability of the dataset and associated benchmarks further fosters collaborative research in this critical area.
