# Evolutionary Hyperparameter Optimization to Find Lightweight CNN Models for Autonomous Steering

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Lawrence Technological University applied an Evolution Strategy with the 1/5th success rule to automatically optimize lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models for autonomous steering, achieving up to an 85% reduction in model VRAM usage (Quarter-Size model at 146 MB vs. Baseline at 936 MB) while enabling real-time operation in simulated environments with minor performance trade-offs.

## Problem

- Autonomous driving systems face computational constraints, memory limitations, and power efficiency requirements on onboard processors, hindering the deployment of complex deep learning models.
- Training high-performing deep learning models typically requires extensive, diverse datasets, which are impractical and costly to acquire for all autonomous driving scenarios, leading to overfitting risks with smaller datasets.
- Manual tuning of deep learning model architectures and hyperparameters is a labor-intensive and iterative process.

## Method

- The study developed an ==(N+M) Evolution Strategy (ES)== framework, integrated with the ==1/5th success rule== for dynamic ==mutation step size== adjustment, to automate hyperparameter tuning for ==CNN== and ==Dense Neural Network (DNN)== architectures (a ==PilotNET== baseline).
- Models were trained using a relatively small dataset (2,957 images) collected from the LTU ACTor platform, focusing on optimizing CNN filter counts and DNN neuron counts.
- Four distinct model sizes (Baseline, Optimized, Half-Size, Quarter-Size) were tested in a 2D GazelleSim simulation environment to assess real-time applicability and resource utilization.

## Results

- The ES framework consistently optimized hyperparameters, producing the 'Optimized' model with the lowest MAE of **0.41 degrees** (vs. Baseline's **0.63 degrees**), though with a slight size increase (**250M parameters**, **1050 MB VRAM**).
- The 'Quarter-Size' model achieved an **85%** reduction in VRAM usage (**146 MB** vs. Baseline's **936 MB**) and significantly fewer parameters (**4.97M**), completing **4 m/s** simulations with an MAE of **0.88 degrees**.
- All models completed 2 m/s simulated trials, but only the Baseline and Quarter-Size models successfully completed the 4 m/s trials, indicating that the 'Optimized' and 'Half-Size' models encountered a pipeline inference rate bottleneck at higher speeds despite similar average inference times.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evolutionary Strategies with dynamic step size adjustment (1/5th success rule) effectively and reliably optimize deep neural network architectures for specific performance and resource constraints in autonomous steering.
> - Significant reductions in model size and VRAM usage are achievable with marginal compromises in predictive accuracy, making lightweight models suitable for deployment on hardware-constrained autonomous platforms.
> - Real-time performance bottlenecks in autonomous driving systems can be attributed to the entire end-to-end image-to-steering pipeline within the simulator, not solely model size or VRAM, especially at higher speeds.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous driving systems require robust and efficient models capable of real-time decision-making, particularly for tasks such as steering angle prediction based on visual input. The broader research landscape for autonomous driving often confronts a trade-off between model accuracy and computational efficiency. Larger, more complex deep learning models, while potentially achieving higher accuracy, demand significant computational resources, memory, and energy. These requirements pose challenges for deployment on resource-constrained onboard processors in autonomous vehicles, where real-time inference and energy efficiency are crucial.

Previous research has explored various deep learning models for image-to-steering angle prediction, demonstrating continuous improvements in performance,–. However, the development of lightweight, well-generalized, and adaptable models specifically designed for real-time steering angle prediction in autonomous driving remains an active area of investigation. This challenge is compounded by the practical difficulties and high costs associated with collecting and annotating large, diverse datasets for every possible driving scenario. While small datasets can lead to overfitting, their utility can be enhanced within regulated operational design domains (e.g., specific city routes), allowing for incremental expansion and techniques like transfer learning or few-shot learning.

This work addresses these challenges by exploring the application of Evolutionary Strategies (ES), a type of Evolutionary Algorithm (EA), for the automated optimization of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Dense Neural Networks (DNNs). Unlike gradient-based optimization methods, ES algorithms are particularly suited for complex, non-differentiable, or noisy search spaces, making them relevant for hyperparameter optimization in deep learning. The research positions itself as an alternative to knowledge distillation techniques, which involve training smaller models to mimic larger pre-trained ones. Instead, this study directly utilizes ES to discover inherently efficient and compact model architectures, thereby circumventing the need for large datasets and the two-stage model design process typically associated with distillation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop lightweight CNN-based models capable of real-time steering angle prediction, specifically designed to mimic human driving behavior along predefined paths. This objective is motivated by several critical considerations pertinent to the deployment of autonomous driving systems:

**Computational Constraints and Efficiency:** Autonomous vehicles operate with onboard processors that have limited computational power and memory. Larger deep learning models, despite their potential for higher accuracy, incur increased computational costs, memory requirements, and energy consumption. These factors lead to slower inference times, which are detrimental for real-time applications like autonomous driving where decisions must be made in milliseconds. Minimizing model size is therefore a core design challenge for practical deployment.

**Data Scarcity and Generalization:** Training high-performing deep learning models typically requires extensive, diverse, and well-labeled datasets. For autonomous driving, acquiring such comprehensive datasets for every conceivable scenario is often impractical and cost-prohibitive. While small datasets can present a risk of overfitting, the authors argue that this is less problematic in scenarios with regulated operational design domains (ODDs), such as a vehicle operating within a defined city or on specific routes. In such contexts, small datasets can serve as a starting point, with capabilities for future expansion using techniques like transfer learning and few-shot learning. This research aims to develop models that perform effectively even with limited data.

**Automated Model Optimization:** The performance of deep learning models is highly dependent on their architecture and hyperparameters. Manual tuning of these parameters is a labor-intensive and iterative process. The research is motivated by the potential of Evolutionary Strategies (ES) to automate this optimization. ES algorithms are population-based and robust in navigating complex, non-differentiable search spaces, making them suitable for discovering optimal architectural hyperparameters. The goal is to identify models that offer a balance between predictive accuracy and reduced computational footprint.

Specifically, the research established three detailed goals:
1. To develop a framework for applying the (N+M) Evolution Strategy (ES) with the 1/5th success rule for automated hyperparameter tuning of CNN and DNN architectures.
2. To minimize the size of a baseline CNN model while ensuring satisfactory performance in steering angle prediction.
3. To validate the real-world applicability of these ES-optimized models within autonomous vehicle systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involved data acquisition, pre-processing, establishing a baseline model, implementing an Evolution Strategy for hyperparameter optimization, and comprehensive model testing in a simulated environment.

**Data Acquisition and Pre-processing:**
Data was collected using the LTU ACTor autonomous driving platform, which integrates with the Robot Operating System (ROS). A forward-facing camera captured images, synchronized with corresponding steering angles, and stored in rosbag files. The data collection environment was a predefined circular red brick path at Lawrence Technological University. To introduce variability and enhance robustness, driving sessions included clockwise and counterclockwise directions, smooth and zigzag maneuvers, and driving along inner and outer path edges. A custom script extracted and pre-processed the data, saving images at 200 ms intervals with filenames encoding timestamps and steering angles. Images were cropped to remove irrelevant regions. The resulting dataset was relatively small, consisting of 2,957 images, split into 70% for training (2,069 images), 20% for validation (592 images), and 10% for testing (296 images). No image augmentation was applied.

**Model Training and Baseline Establishment:**
Models were trained using the Keras API with a PyTorch backend, leveraging GPU compute capacity. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function was used to quantify the difference between predicted and actual steering angles, while the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in degrees served as the primary performance metric for accuracy.
Initial experiments began with a single-layer CNN, which was deemed insufficient. The NVIDIA PilotNET architecture, a combination of CNN and DNN layers, was adopted as the baseline model due to its established presence in autonomous steering research. The study focused on benchmarking performance improvements relative to this recognized architecture rather than achieving absolute state-of-the-art performance. Early stopping was implemented, terminating training if no improvement in MSE was observed over four epochs, to enhance training efficiency and prevent overfitting.

**Hyperparameter Management:**
The initial hyperparameter search space encompassed convolutional filter units, dense layer neuron units, batch sizes, learning rates, activation functions, and optimizers. Preliminary experiments indicated that focusing optimization efforts primarily on layer units (filters and neurons) yielded better performance and reduced search time. Consequently, batch size (32), learning rate (0.001), activation function (ReLU), and optimizer (Adam) were fixed to match the baseline PilotNET configuration. The allowed range for CNN filter and DNN neuron units was set from 20% to 300% of the PilotNET baseline values.

**Optimization Framework and ES Algorithm:**
CNN filter counts and DNN neuron counts were encoded as real-valued genes within the Evolution Strategy (ES) framework. The (N+M)-ES approach was employed, iteratively optimizing hyperparameters by combining the N best-performing parent models with M offspring models generated through mutation. This strategy facilitates both exploitation of promising hyperparameter regions (via top parent selection) and exploration of new possibilities (via offspring mutation).

The ES algorithm integrated the 1/5th success rule to dynamically adjust the mutation step size (σ). If the success rate (fraction of offspring outperforming parents) over a generational window exceeded 1/5, the step size was increased by a factor α (e.g., 1.22) to encourage broader exploration. If the success rate was less than or equal to 1/5, the step size was decreased by a factor β (e.g., 0.82) to promote focused refinement. A window size of 5 generations was used for success rate calculation. The optimization process iterated through initialization (N random parent models), child generation (M offspring via Gaussian mutation on parent hyperparameters normalized to their search space), training and evaluation of offspring, and selection of the top N models for the next generation. The process continued for a maximum of 100 generations or until an MAE of 0.1 degrees was achieved.

Training was performed on Lawrence Technological University’s NVIDIA A100 GPU server, utilizing its 80GB VRAM to support parallel training of multiple parent and offspring models, enabling larger N and M values (as detailed in Table III) for enhanced population diversity and hyperparameter space exploration.

**Model Selection:**
Four distinct model architectures were designed to evaluate the effect of ES on performance and size:
1. **Baseline:** The original PilotNET architecture.
2. **Optimized:** A PilotNET-based architecture with CNN and DNN layers optimized by ES within the full search space.
3. **Half-Size:** A PilotNET CNN architecture with half the baseline number of filters per layer, and DNN layers optimized by ES within a search space that was also halved.
4. **Quarter-Size:** A PilotNET CNN architecture with a quarter of the baseline number of filters per layer, and DNN layers optimized by ES within a search space that was also quartered.
It is noted that pretrained weights of PilotNET were not used in this study.

**Model Testing:**
To assess real-time applicability, models were tested in GazelleSim, a 2D simulation environment, on the same circular path used for data collection. The testing pipeline involved capturing simulated camera images, feeding them to the trained model for steering angle prediction, translating the predicted angle into a control command, and using this command to adjust the vehicle's trajectory in a closed-loop system. The inference tests were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX A2000 Laptop GPU (8GB VRAM, 35W power limit) to simulate a low-power onboard compute environment. Key metrics included distance traveled in specific durations and speeds (2 m/s and 4 m/s), number of parameters, VRAM usage during inference, MSE, MAE, and average inference time over 1000 predictions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrated the viability of using Evolutionary Strategy with the 1/5th success rule for hyperparameter optimization in autonomous steering applications, yielding models with varied performance and resource footprints.

**Effectiveness of Evolution Strategy:**
Across multiple ES optimization runs (5-10 for each model size), the 1/5th success rule consistently and reliably sorted hyperparameters, resulting in positively optimized models without observed instability or failed optimization runs. This supports the method's statistical significance for optimizing convolutional and fully connected dense neural network layers for steering angle prediction.

**Model Architectures and Parameters:**
Table III detailed the specific layer configurations after ES optimization. For instance, the "Optimized" model showed significant changes in filter counts (e.g., 6, 9, 43, 28, 61 for CNN layers) and DNN neuron counts compared to the fixed PilotNET baseline. The "Half-Size" and "Quarter-Size" models demonstrated proportionate reductions in their initial CNN layer filter counts, with their DNN layers also undergoing ES optimization within their respective reduced search spaces.

**Computational Utilization and Accuracy (Table V):**
- **Baseline Model:** Exhibited reasonable accuracy (MSE: 1.01, MAE: 0.63 degrees) with a relatively large size (245M parameters, 936 MB VRAM) and an average inference time of 4.8 ms. This model served as a capable reference, completing simulations at both 2 m/s and 4 m/s.
- **Optimized Model:** Achieved the lowest error rates (MSE: 0.49, MAE: 0.41 degrees), indicating improved predictive accuracy over the baseline. However, its size slightly increased to 250M parameters and 1050 MB VRAM, with an inference time of 4.5 ms.
- **Half-Size Model:** Showed a substantial reduction in size (61.4M parameters, 420 MB VRAM) and a slightly reduced accuracy compared to the baseline (MSE: 1.33, MAE: 0.78 degrees). Its inference time was 4.2 ms.
- **Quarter-Size Model:** Represented the most significant reduction in model size (4.97M parameters, 146 MB VRAM – approximately 15% of the baseline's VRAM) with a 4.1 ms inference time. Its accuracy was lower than other models (MSE: 1.88, MAE: 0.88 degrees).

**Simulation Performance (Table IV):**
- **Low Speed (2 m/s):** All four models completed the 10-minute and 60-minute trials at 2 m/s, traveling approximately 1200 meters and 7200 meters, respectively. Performance variations across models were minor (<0.18% deviation), suggesting architectural equivalence under conditions where inference capacity was not saturated.
- **High Speed (4 m/s):** The Baseline and Quarter-Sized models successfully completed the 60-minute trial at 4 m/s, covering over 14400 meters. However, the Optimized and Half-Sized models failed at this speed. This outcome suggests a hard inference rate threshold rather than a bottleneck related to model size or VRAM, given that all models exhibited similar inference times (Table V). The bottleneck was attributed to the end-to-end image-to-steering pipeline within the simulator, which was not fast enough to handle the 4 m/s simulated speed in real-time for certain configurations.

**Trade-offs and Balance:**
The study highlighted a critical balance between model performance and resource usage. While the "Optimized" model achieved the best predictive accuracy, its slight size increase meant it failed to operate at higher simulated speeds. Conversely, the "Quarter-Size" model, despite a minor increase in error (MAE difference of <1.0 degree compared to baseline), demonstrated functionality at 4 m/s while consuming significantly fewer resources (15% of baseline VRAM). This trade-off is considered valid for low-speed self-driving applications where substantial size reduction with marginal performance decrease is acceptable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous driving by demonstrating a practical application of Evolutionary Strategies for optimizing deep neural network architectures. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

**Validation of Evolutionary Optimization:** The study successfully developed and validated a framework for applying the (N+M) Evolution Strategy with the 1/5th success rule for automated hyperparameter tuning of CNN and DNN layers. The observed reliability and consistent positive optimization results across various model sizes establish the method as a viable technique for improving application efficiency in autonomous driving systems.

**Enabling Lightweight, Real-time Autonomous Systems:** The findings highlight the feasibility of creating lightweight neural network architectures that maintain satisfactory steering angle prediction performance while significantly reducing computational resource demands. This balance between efficiency and performance is crucial for deploying autonomous systems on hardware-constrained platforms, leading to more practical and accessible autonomous vehicle technology.

**Foundation for Cost-Effective and Scalable Solutions:** By demonstrating that substantial model size reduction (e.g., the Quarter-Size model requiring only 15% of baseline VRAM) can be achieved with only minor performance compromises, the research lays a foundation for developing cost-effective and scalable self-driving solutions. Reduced model sizes translate to lower hardware costs, lower energy consumption, and wider applicability in various autonomous vehicle scenarios.

**Advancement in Autonomous Driving Research:** This work contributes to the ongoing research efforts in leveraging evolutionary algorithms for autonomous driving applications. It provides empirical evidence of how such algorithms can address critical challenges related to model efficiency and deployment constraints, particularly for real-time steering control.

**Future Development Directions:** The research identifies several avenues for future work, which could further amplify its impact:
- **Real-World Deployment:** Deploying and evaluating the ES-optimized CNN models on the LTU ACTor autonomous driving platform for real-world performance validation. Initial preliminary tests suggest simulation-equivalent real-world performance.
- **Embedded Platform Evaluation:** Testing these optimized models on low-power, low-compute embedded platforms, such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano, to directly benchmark real-time performance (frames per second and latency) and assess scalability for cost-effective systems.
- **Dataset Expansion and Advanced Learning Techniques:** Expanding the dataset to include diverse roads, surfaces, geometries, and lighting conditions, and exploring techniques like few-shot learning and meta-continual learning to enhance model generalization and adaptability.
- **Model Stability and Smoothness:** Integrating Recurrent Neural Network layers to incorporate past steering inputs, which could potentially improve model stability and the smoothness of steering control.

Overall, this research moves toward more deployable and efficient autonomous driving solutions by systematically addressing the challenge of model complexity through intelligent architectural optimization.
