# Courteous Anticipation: Improving Long-Lived Task Planning in Persistent Shared Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A learning-augmented planning framework introduces "courteous anticipation" to minimize cumulative planning costs for multiple robots operating in persistent shared environments. This approach, which uses independent GNNs to estimate future costs for each robot, reduced overall costs by 17.41% compared to myopic planning in a multi-robot restaurant simulation.

## Problem

- Traditional task planners exhibit myopic or "selfish" behavior, optimizing only for immediate goals or a single robot's future tasks, even in shared persistent environments.
- These short-sighted actions create negative cumulative side effects, such as obstructing other robots or placing objects in locations inaccessible to them, leading to increased overall system costs.
- The issue is intensified when robots have heterogeneous capabilities and responsibilities, often necessitating costly "help" actions or significant detours.

## Method

- A **"courteous anticipatory planning"** objective function is proposed, which balances the acting robot's immediate task cost with the aggregated expected future costs for all robots in the shared environment.
- The aggregated future cost is made tractable by factoring it into independent per-robot estimates, each computed by a dedicated ==Graph Neural Network (GNN)==.
- Candidate plans for the current task are generated through ==focused sampling==, and their terminal states are evaluated using the GNN estimators to select the most courteous option.

## Results

- Courteous anticipatory planning lowered cumulative costs by **10.43%** (myopic) and **4.03%** (selfish) in a two-robot home environment, and by **17.41%** (myopic) and **13.24%** (selfish) in a three-robot heterogeneous restaurant scenario.
- The modularity of independent, learned per-robot cost estimators was confirmed, allowing for flexible addition or removal of robots without retraining existing models.
- The framework also facilitated effective proactive actions by an idle robot, which reduced the overall unit cost by **31.4%** by making beneficial changes to the environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Traditional myopic or selfish planning approaches in persistent shared environments lead to accumulated negative side effects and suboptimal collective performance over time.
> - Integrating considerations for the distinct capabilities and responsibilities of all co-located robots into a single robot's planning significantly improves long-term collective efficiency.
> - Factoring the multi-robot future cost into independent, learned per-robot estimators offers a tractable and modular solution for courteous anticipatory planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a challenge in autonomous robotics where multiple agents operate within a shared, persistent environment over extended periods. The broader research landscape encompasses several related areas:

- **Conventional Task Planning:** Traditional task planners, as described in references such as, typically focus on achieving immediate goals as quickly as possible. These methods primarily consider single-task or single-robot scenarios, lacking foresight into future tasks or the impact of current actions on other agents sharing the environment. Their solutions often optimize for local, immediate costs.
- **Learning-Augmented Planning:** Advances in learning-augmented planning integrate learned heuristics or value functions to accelerate the planning process or guide search. However, these methods generally aim to improve the efficiency of solving the current task rather than reasoning about the long-term, multi-agent consequences of a robot's actions on the environment state for future tasks or other robots.
- **Single-Robot Anticipatory Planning:** A more recent development is anticipatory planning, where a single robot considers not only its immediate task objective but also the potential impact of its current actions on its *own* future tasks. This involves estimating future costs using learned models to avoid computationally expensive full rollouts. While an improvement over myopic planning, these approaches remain "selfish" in shared multi-robot contexts, as they do not account for other robots.
- **Proactive Robot Assistance and Environment Design:** Other related research explores proactive behaviors, such as anticipating object usage for assistance, designing environments to align human-robot expectations, or minimizing divergence from a goal state. Online task planning for temporal task arrival has also been considered. However, these typically do not address the specific challenge of a robot's actions impacting the distinct capabilities and responsibilities of *multiple* other robots in a persistent shared space.
- **Side Effect Minimization in Symbolic Planning:** Some work, like Klassen et al., addresses negative side effects in symbolic planning, but typically within a single-task context, without explicitly modeling sequences of future tasks or the diverse capabilities of multiple robots.

The present paper identifies a critical gap: existing methods, including single-robot anticipatory planning, do not adequately address the negative cumulative side effects that arise when multiple robots with varying capabilities and responsibilities operate in persistent shared environments. Their individual, even "selfish anticipatory," optimizations can lead to suboptimal collective performance over long task sequences. This work introduces "courteous anticipatory planning" as a solution, specifically targeting the problem of inter-robot dependencies and friction arising from physical state changes in a shared environment, framing it as a symbolic planning behavior problem rather than a multi-agent coordination or communication problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to reduce the cumulative planning cost for multiple robots sharing a persistent environment over long sequences of tasks. The authors identify a significant limitation in standard task planning approaches when deployed in such scenarios:

- **Myopic and Selfish Behavior:** Traditional task planners typically focus on solving each task in isolation, optimizing only for the immediate goal. Even more advanced "anticipatory" planners, while considering a robot's *own* future tasks, often neglect the impact of their actions on *other* robots sharing the environment. This behavior is termed "myopic" and "selfish" respectively.
- **Compounding Negative Side Effects:** In persistent environments, where the terminal state of one task becomes the initial state of the next, myopic or selfish actions can create negative side effects. For example, a robot might leave an object in a location that obstructs another robot, is inaccessible to it, or requires a third robot with specific capabilities to resolve a suboptimal state. These side effects can compound over lengthy task sequences, increasing the overall cost for all robots involved.
- **Impact of Heterogeneity:** The problem is exacerbated when robots have distinct capabilities (e.g., height, tools) and responsibilities (e.g., tasks assigned). A robot's actions might create states that another robot is physically incapable of resolving, necessitating costly "help" actions or significant detours.

To illustrate this motivation, the paper provides an example in a shared kitchen with a cook, server, and cleaner robot. If the cook completes a "MakePasta" task by moving a dirty bowl to a high counter to clear the stove (a myopic plan), it inadvertently blocks the server for a later "ServePasta" task and forces the short cleaner to seek help to retrieve the bowl for a "CleanBowl" task. This increases the total cost for the sequence of tasks.

The **key objective** is to enable robots to exhibit "courteous anticipation." This involves:
1. **Completing their own immediate task.**
2. **Anticipating how their current actions will affect the future tasks of *all* robots sharing the environment.**
3. **Considering the *distinct capabilities* and *responsibilities* of all other robots.**
4. **Selecting a plan that jointly minimizes immediate cost and the aggregated expected future cost across all robots.**

The **motivation** is to mitigate these negative side effects and improve the collective performance and efficiency of multi-robot systems in shared, long-lived deployments. The authors specifically aim to develop a tractable method for this objective, recognizing that directly enumerating all possible future task assignments across all robots for exact cost computation would be combinatorially intractable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce a framework for "courteous anticipatory planning" to address the identified limitations. Their methodology is structured around a novel planning objective and a learning-augmented, model-based planner.

**Problem Statement in PDDL:**
The problem is formalized using PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language). An environment consists of objects, predicates defining relationships, an initial state, and parameterized actions. Tasks are goal predicates. In a shared environment with a set of robots *R*, each robot *r* has responsibilities: a set of tasks *τ* it may be assigned and their probabilities *P<sub>r</sub>(τ)*. Tasks arrive one at a time from a sequence, and the environment persists, meaning the terminal state of one task becomes the initial state of the next. The overall aim is to minimize the total cost over the entire sequence of tasks, even though the full sequence is unknown in advance.

**Courteous Anticipatory Planning Objective:**
The core of the methodology is the proposed objective function, which extends single-robot anticipatory planning (Eq. 2) to a multi-robot context. Given a current task *τ<sub>c</sub>* and starting state *s<sub>0</sub>*, the acting robot selects a terminal state *s'<sub>g</sub>* for its plan that minimizes:
`s*<sub>g</sub> = argmin<sub>s'<sub>g</sub> ∈ G<sub>τ</sub></sub> [V*<sub>s'<sub>g</sub></sub>(s<sub>0</sub>) + Σ<sub>r∈R</sub> V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)]` (Eq. 3)

This objective jointly minimizes:
- `V*<sub>s'<sub>g</sub></sub>(s<sub>0</sub>)`: The immediate cost for the acting robot to complete its current task *τ<sub>c</sub>* and reach a candidate terminal state *s'<sub>g</sub>*.
- `Σ<sub>r∈R</sub> V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)`: The aggregated expected future cost for *all* robots *r* in the environment. `V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)` represents the anticipatory cost for robot *r* to complete a single subsequent task (drawn from its responsibilities) starting from the state *s'<sub>g</sub>*.

This formulation differs from selfish anticipatory planning by aggregating costs across *all* robots, not just the acting one, thereby influencing the choice of terminal state.

**Tractability and Factored Formulation:**
Directly computing the aggregated expected future cost across all robots (which involves combinatorial consideration of future task sequences) is intractable. The authors address this by:
- **Factoring the Sum:** The sum `Σ<sub>r∈R</sub> V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)` is computed by independently estimating each robot's anticipatory cost `V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)`.
- **Per-Robot Learned Estimators:** Each `V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)` is estimated using a separate, learned model specifically trained for robot *r* and its responsibilities. These models implicitly capture how the environment state *s'<sub>g</sub>* interacts with robot *r*'s capabilities and constraints (e.g., objects placed in inaccessible locations).

**Courteous Anticipatory Planning Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
The overall planning process for an acting robot given an initial state *s<sub>0</sub>* and current task *τ* is as follows:
1. Initialize the best plan `π*` and total cost `V*<sub>total</sub>` to an infinite value.
2. Iterate `N` times to generate candidate plans:
 a. **Generate Candidate Plans (`SAMPLEPLAN`):** A focused sampling procedure (based on Talukder et al.) is used. Instead of exhaustively searching all possible plans, this method augments tasks with "placement predicates" and "object state predicates" (e.g., specifying where an object should be placed or its final state like clean/dirty). This generates a diverse set of satisficing plans that differ in ways critical for downstream costs. FastDownward with ff-astar is used to solve these "proxy tasks," ensuring generated plans accomplish the original task.
 b. For each candidate plan `π`, extract its terminal state `s<sub>g</sub>` and immediate cost `V<sub>sg</sub>`.
 c. **Estimate Total Cost:** Calculate `V<sub>total</sub> = V<sub>sg</sub> + Σ<sub>r∈R</sub> APCOSTESTIMATOR<sub>r</sub>(s<sub>g</sub>)`. Each `APCOSTESTIMATOR<sub>r</sub>(s<sub>g</sub>)` is a query to the learned model for robot `r`.
 d. If `V<sub>total</sub>` is less than or equal to `V*<sub>total</sub>`, update `V*<sub>total</sub>` and `π*`.
3. Return the best plan `π*`.

**Per-Robot Anticipatory Cost Estimation:**
- **Data Generation:** Training data is generated offline. For each robot *r*, its responsibilities (tasks and likelihoods) are used to simulate tasks in training environments. FastDownward with ff-astar is used to solve these tasks, and the resulting plan costs are used to compute the actual anticipatory cost `V<sup>r</sup><sub>A.P.</sub>(s'<sub>g</sub>)` for a given terminal state. The world state is represented as a sparse scene graph, which serves as input to the GNN.
- **Model Architecture:** Each `APCOSTESTIMATOR<sub>r</sub>` is an independent Graph Neural Network (GNN). It uses four GINConv layers, followed by batch normalization and Leaky ReLU activations. Node features are aggregated into a graph-level representation via mean and sum pooling, and a final linear layer outputs the anticipatory cost estimate.
- **Modularity:** A key feature is that each GNN is trained independently for a single robot's responsibilities. This design allows for modular deployment: adding or removing a robot from the shared environment only requires training or removing that specific robot's estimator, without needing to retrain existing ones.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluated their courteous anticipatory planning approach against two baselines: Myopic Planning (classical planning minimizing immediate cost) and Selfish Anticipatory Planning (minimizing immediate cost plus the acting robot's own expected future cost). Experiments were conducted in two simulated PDDL environments of increasing complexity.

**Cost Structure:** Planning cost represented physical execution time, with movement actions incurring dynamic costs proportional to distance, and other actions having fixed unit costs.

**A. Courteous Anticipation from Responsibilities Alone (Home Environment)**
- **Environment:** Two robots with similar physical capabilities but distinct responsibilities (kitchen-centric, living-room-centric) in home environments curated from PROCTHOR. Tasks were rearrangement-style, derived from ALFRED. Improvements here arise solely from reasoning about distinct responsibilities and shared surfaces.
- **Qualitative Example (Fig. 3):** When the living-room organizer was tasked to "ClearSofa," a myopic planner placed trash on a nearby armchair, and a selfish planner placed it on a counter outside the living room. The **courteous planner** placed the trash directly in a bin, anticipating the kitchen organizer's future responsibility for trash disposal. This led to lower costs for the kitchen organizer's subsequent tasks.
- **Quantitative Results:** Over 100 sequences of 20 tasks, courteous anticipatory planning reduced cumulative planning cost by **10.43%** compared to the myopic planner and **4.03%** compared to the selfish anticipatory planner.
- **Trend (Fig. 3, bottom):** While all planners generally reduce costs over time by organizing the environment, courteous robots consistently performed better, indicating their ability to leave the environment in states more favorable for all robots.

**B. Distinct Capabilities Amplify Courteous Anticipation (Restaurant Environment)**
- **Environment:** A more complex restaurant-inspired benchmark with three *heterogeneous* robots differing in capabilities and responsibilities:
 - **Cook:** Tall, heat-resistant, stove access. Responsibilities: "MakingFood" tasks.
 - **Server:** Taller, dining table access. Responsibilities: "ServeFood," "RestockFood."
 - **Cleaner:** Scrubbing end-effector, washes cookware. Responsibilities: "CleanItem," "ClearContainers."
 - An explicit "help" action was introduced, incurring a fixed cost (100 sec) when one robot needed another to resolve an inaccessible object state, incentivizing courteous planning.
- **Qualitative Example (Fig. 4):** When the Cook was tasked to "MakePasta," the **courteous anticipatory planner** included an action to place the dirty pan directly on the bussing cart on its return path, making it easily accessible for the Cleaner. Myopic and selfish planners often left objects on the stove or shared surfaces, obstructing others and incurring higher downstream costs.
- **Quantitative Results (Table II):** Performance was evaluated by progressively adding robots (single, two-robot subsets, full three-robot team) over 200 sequences of 10*N* tasks (where *N* is the number of robots).
 - **Single-Robot Setting:** Courteous and selfish planning yielded identical results, as expected, because there were no other agents to be courteous to.
 - **Two-Robot Settings (averaged):** Courteous planning showed improvements of **6.7%** over myopic and **5.5%** over selfish anticipatory planning.
 - **Three-Robot Setting:** Courteous anticipatory planning outperformed myopic by **17.41%** and selfish anticipatory planning by **13.24%**.
- **Modularity Validation (Table II):** The results directly validated the modularity claim. Estimators trained for two-robot configurations were reused without modification or retraining in the three-robot experiments, confirming the plug-and-play property of the independent per-robot estimators.
- **Distinct Capabilities Drive Courteous Behavior (Fig. 5):** The experiments showed how courteous behavior adapted to the presence of different robots:
 - Cook alone: Courteous behavior was identical to selfish.
 - Cook + Server: The courteous Cook moved finished items from the stove to the counter, knowing the Server could not access the hot stove.
 - Cook + Cleaner: The courteous Cook placed dirty pans on the bussing cart, where the Cleaner could easily reach them.
 - Cook + Server + Cleaner: The courteous Cook simultaneously exhibited both behaviors, demonstrating reasoning about the distinct capabilities and responsibilities of all present robots.
- **Proactivity via Courteous Anticipation (Fig. 6):** An additional experiment introduced an "idle" Cleaner robot with a limited time budget (100 sec) to act proactively after every two tasks by the Cook and Server, aiming to reduce their aggregated expected future cost.
 - Result: The free courteous Cleaning robot reduced the overall unit cost from 976 to 670, a **31.4% improvement**.
 - Example: The Cleaner proactively cleaned dirty dishes in the sink, benefiting the Cook later. This behavior was absent in selfish or myopic approaches, highlighting that courteous anticipation enables effective proactive actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-robot task planning in persistent shared environments, particularly relevant for long-lived autonomous systems.

- **Addressing a Critical Oversight:** The work highlights and effectively addresses a fundamental limitation in existing task planning approaches: their myopic or "selfish" nature leads to accumulated negative side effects and increased costs in multi-robot, shared settings. This gap is crucial for robust, long-term robot deployments.
- **Novel Planning Objective and Framework:** The introduction of "courteous anticipation" as a planning objective is a key conceptual advance. By explicitly factoring in the aggregated expected future costs for *all* robots, accounting for their distinct capabilities and responsibilities, the framework promotes cooperative behavior without direct inter-robot communication or complex joint policy learning.
- **Tractable and Modular Solution:** The methodology's use of a factored formulation with independent, learned per-robot anticipatory cost estimators is a practical engineering solution. It elegantly bypasses the combinatorial intractability of joint rollouts and supports modular deployment. This "plug-and-play" capability, demonstrated empirically, simplifies system design and scalability by allowing robots to be added or removed without requiring extensive retraining of the entire multi-agent system.
- **Empirical Validation of Hypotheses:** The extensive experimental results in two distinct environments (home and restaurant) robustly validate the core hypotheses. The increasing gains observed when distinct capabilities are present and as more robots are added underscore the practical value of courteous anticipation. The qualitative examples clearly illustrate how specific planning decisions are altered to benefit other robots.
- **Enabling Proactive Behavior:** The demonstration of proactive actions by an idle robot, guided by courteous anticipation, showcases the potential for more intelligent and self-optimizing multi-robot systems. This ability to make foresightful, beneficial changes to the environment during downtime can significantly enhance overall system efficiency and resilience.
- **Broad Applicability:** The findings have broad implications for real-world robotics applications where multiple autonomous agents share physical spaces and resources. This includes scenarios in smart homes, automated warehouses, hospitals, manufacturing floors, and service robotics. By reducing friction, minimizing the need for costly "help" actions, and promoting a more organized environment, courteous anticipatory planning can lead to more efficient, reliable, and collaborative multi-robot operations over extended periods.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** While focusing on sequential task arrival, the framework lays a strong foundation for future extensions to more complex multi-agent challenges, such as concurrent task execution, simultaneous robot actions, and dynamic environment changes, further expanding its real-world utility.
