# Robust Navigation with Cross-Modal Fusion and Knowledge Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> A method for robust robot navigation was developed, utilizing cross-modal fusion and a teacher-student knowledge transfer framework to enhance generalization and sim-to-real transfer, especially for noisy sensor inputs. The approach achieved an 80.2% success rate in novel scenes and maintained 81.2% success under hard noise, demonstrating successful deployment on a physical robot.

## Problem

- Learning-based robot navigation policies exhibit limited generalization to novel environments and suffer from the "sim-to-real gap".
- Real-world sensor noise, particularly pose estimation errors, significantly degrades robot navigation performance.
- Training Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents directly in the real world is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.

## Method

- A teacher-student distillation architecture is proposed, where an expert teacher learns an optimal policy in an ideal, noise-free simulated environment.
- The student agent learns to navigate robustly from noisy multi-modal inputs (egocentric map and laser) by imitating the teacher's perception and decision-making.
- Cross-modal fusion and knowledge transfer are achieved via feature distillation to align noisy student features with clean teacher features, and policy distillation to mimic teacher actions.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved an **81.2%** success rate under "hard" sensor noise, surpassing the best baseline by **16.5%** (Map+Laser at **64.7%**) and demonstrating substantially lower collision time.
- It exhibited superior generalization across varying speed limits (**98.1%** and **88.1%** success rates) and in an entirely novel simulated environment (**80.2%** success rate).
- Successful sim-to-real transfer was validated on a physical DJI RoboMaster EP robot, which robustly navigated sequentially through five real-world goals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a contrastive objective during teacher training is crucial for learning discriminative, noise-sensitive feature representations that facilitate effective knowledge transfer.
> - Cross-modal fusion with specific transform layers enables the student to effectively compensate for sensor noise by aligning noisy input features with the teacher's clean representations.
> - The dual mechanism of feature and policy distillation allows for comprehensive knowledge transfer, enhancing the student's generalization to diverse operational conditions and new environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot navigation is a fundamental challenge in robotics, with various approaches developed over time. Traditionally, Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)-based methods have been widely used. These approaches, while effective, often rely on intricate, manually-designed modules that can become fragile in complex real-world environments characterized by dynamic objects, pose estimation errors, or low-texture surfaces.

In recent years, learning-based methods, particularly those leveraging deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and deep neural networks, have garnered significant attention due to their potential for end-to-end training and robust decision-making. These methods have been applied to various navigation tasks, including point-to-point, object-goal, and visual-language navigation. However, DRL-based approaches face notable challenges. A primary concern is their limited generalization capability, meaning policies learned in one specific environment may not perform well in different, even slightly varied, conditions. Furthermore, training DRL agents often requires extensive interaction with the environment, leading to high sampling costs and low data efficiency. To circumvent the impracticality of real-world training, policies are frequently learned in high-fidelity simulators. This introduces the "sim-to-real gap," where a policy optimized in simulation may exhibit degraded or even unsafe performance when transferred to a physical robot. Bridging this gap is crucial for wider real-world deployment of learning-based navigation systems.

The robustness of navigation policies in real-world settings is further complicated by pervasive sensor noise. Mobile robots typically integrate multiple sensors such as lasers, cameras, and Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs). Multi-modal observations are recognized for providing complementary information that can be leveraged to detect and mitigate data misalignment and errors. Researchers have explored multi-sensor data fusion techniques to enhance perception. Another strategy to improve policy generalization involves explicit denoising procedures or, more commonly, techniques like domain randomization. Domain randomization systematically varies parameters in the simulation environment during training, aiming to expose the agent to a diverse range of conditions and thus learn a more robust policy. However, excessively randomizing domains can sometimes distort the natural data distribution in the state space, potentially making it harder for DRL algorithms to identify task-relevant patterns and leading to sub-optimal policies.

This paper positions itself within this context by addressing the critical issues of generalization and sim-to-real transfer for learning-based navigation. It specifically focuses on compensating for sensor noise, particularly pose estimation errors, and learning a generic navigation policy that performs robustly across varying working conditions and in novel environments. The authors aim to combine the benefits of multi-modal fusion with knowledge transfer techniques to overcome the limitations of existing approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the generalization capability of learning-based mobile robot navigation systems and to facilitate the transfer of these navigation skills from simulated environments to the real world. The authors seek to develop a method that allows robots to navigate robustly despite the presence of sensor noise and variations in operational conditions.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several limitations observed in current learning-based navigation approaches:
- **Poor Generalization and Sim-to-Real Gap**: While learning-based methods show promise, their practical deployment is hindered by their difficulty in generalizing to unseen environments or even slight variations in known environments. Policies trained exclusively in simulation often fail in reality due to discrepancies between the two domains. This necessitates robust mechanisms for sim-to-real transfer.
- **Cost of Real-World Training**: Deep reinforcement learning algorithms typically require millions of interaction steps to converge to an effective policy. Training such agents directly in the real world is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and potentially hazardous. Simulators offer a safe and scalable training ground, but the resulting policies must effectively transfer to physical robots.
- **Ubiquitous Sensor Noise**: Real-world robot sensors are inherently noisy. Pose estimation errors, in particular, can significantly distort the robot's perception of its surroundings (e.g., an egocentric map), leading to inaccurate decision-making. Robust navigation requires methods capable of handling these perceptual uncertainties.
- **Limitations of Existing Generalization Methods**: While domain randomization is an effective technique for improving generalization, it can sometimes introduce data distributions that are far from natural, potentially making it more challenging for DRL algorithms to identify critical patterns for the navigation task and leading to sub-optimal policies.
- **Leveraging Multi-Modal Data**: Robots are typically equipped with multiple sensors providing complementary information. The motivation includes developing a method that can effectively fuse these multi-modal observations to enhance perception and compensate for noise, rather than simply processing them in isolation or through rudimentary concatenation.
- **Need for Robust Representations**: The authors recognize that learning a discriminative and noise-sensitive feature representation is critical for a navigation policy to be robust. They aim to incorporate mechanisms that encourage the learning of such representations, specifically those that are invariant to certain types of noise while being sensitive to task-relevant information like pose.

To address these motivations, the research proposes a framework that leverages a teacher-student distillation architecture, allowing an expert policy learned in an ideal, noise-free simulated environment to guide a student policy that learns to operate robustly with noisy multi-modal inputs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a robust navigation framework based on a cross-modal fusion method and a knowledge transfer paradigm, implemented through a teacher-student distillation architecture. The overall goal is to train a student agent that can operate effectively with noisy multi-modal inputs by imitating an expert teacher trained in an ideal environment.

**Problem Formulation:**
The robot navigation problem is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The agent receives two types of observations: a laser signal ($o_l \in \mathbb{R}^n$) and an egocentric occupancy map ($o_m \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times h \times w}$). The egocentric map is derived from a global map, cropped around the robot's estimated pose ($\hat{p}_t = (\hat{x}_t, \hat{y}_t, \hat{r}_t)$), making it susceptible to pose estimation errors. The navigation task involves guiding the robot to a sequence of five goal coordinates. The action space consists of 3-dimensional continuous vectors ($a_t = (v_x, v_y, \omega)$) representing linear and angular speeds.

**Teacher Module:**
The teacher module is responsible for learning an expert navigation policy ($\pi_{\theta}^t$) in an ideal simulated environment that lacks sensor noise.
- **Inputs**: The teacher receives a label egocentric map ($o_m^t$) without pose estimation noise and a noisy laser signal ($\hat{o}_l^t$).
- **Architecture**:
 - The egocentric map is processed by a 3-layer Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to extract a feature vector ($z_t$).
 - The laser signal is processed by a 2-layer Fully-Connected (FC) network to extract a feature vector ($q_t$).
 - These two features are concatenated to form the state representation ($s_t = (z_t, q_t)$) for the actor-critic networks.
- **Training**: The teacher policy is trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.
- **Reward Function**: A dense reward function is used, comprising:
 - A constant step penalty ($r_{\text{step}}^t = -0.01$).
 - A collision penalty ($r_{\text{col}}^t = -0.05$).
 - A goal achievement reward ($r_{\text{goal}}^t = 4.0$).
 - A reward based on the change in Euclidean distance to the goal ($\Delta d_p^t$).
- **Auxiliary Objective (Contrastive Learning)**: To enhance the generalization ability and learn a discriminative feature representation sensitive to pose estimation error, a contrastive objective (InfoNCE loss) is integrated.
 - Positive pairs are constructed by applying data augmentation (ColorJitter and RandomScale) to the same egocentric map ($o_m^t$). This encourages the network to focus on relative pose information rather than variations in color or scale.
 - Negative pairs consist of other samples within the mini-batch.
 - A momentum encoder, similar to MoCo, is used to prevent mode collapse.
 - The InfoNCE loss is defined as: $L_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\mathbb{E}[\log \frac{\exp(q \cdot k^+ / \tau)}{\sum_{j=0}^B \exp(q \cdot k_j^- / \tau)}]$.
- **Total Teacher Loss**: The final loss for the teacher module is a weighted sum of the PPO objective and the InfoNCE loss: $L_{\text{teacher}} = L_{\text{PPO}} + \beta_t \cdot L_{\text{InfoNCE}}$, where $\beta_t=0.2$.

**Student Module:**
The student module is designed to imitate the teacher's perception and decision-making in the presence of sensor noise. It incorporates a multi-modal fusion network and a policy network, both trained via supervised learning.
- **Inputs**: The student receives noisy egocentric maps ($\hat{o}_m^t$) and noisy laser signals ($\hat{o}_l^t$).
- **Architecture**:
 - The noisy map is processed by a 3-layer CNN to obtain feature $\hat{z}_t$.
 - The noisy laser signal is processed by a 2-layer FC network to obtain feature $\hat{q}_t$.
- **Cross-Modal Fusion and Knowledge Transfer**:
 - **Feature Distillation ($L_{\text{FD}}$)**: A "transform module" (composed of 2 FC layers) takes the concatenated noisy features $(\hat{z}_t, \hat{q}_t)$ as input and learns a function $T_{\theta}(\hat{z}_t, \hat{q}_t)$ to align these noisy features with the teacher's clean feature $z_t$. This effectively aims to "restore" the original feature representation from noisy inputs. The objective is to maximize cosine similarity: $L_{\text{FD}} = -\mathbb{E}[\frac{z_t \cdot T_{\theta}(\hat{z}_t, \hat{q}_t)}{||z_t|| \cdot ||T_{\theta}(\hat{z}_t, \hat{q}_t)|}]$.
 - **Policy Distillation ($L_{\text{PD}}$)**: The student's policy ($\pi_s^{\theta}$) is trained to mimic the teacher's policy ($\pi_t^{\theta}$) using a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence objective: $L_{\text{PD}} = \mathbb{E}[\text{KL}(\pi_t^{\theta}(s_t)||\pi_s^{\theta}(s_t))]$. This ensures the student learns similar decision-making behaviors.
- **Total Student Loss**: The student module is optimized with a weighted combination of the feature distillation and policy distillation objectives: $L_{\text{student}} = \alpha_s \cdot L_{\text{FD}} + \beta_s \cdot L_{\text{PD}}$, where $\alpha_s=0.25$ and $\beta_s=0.75$.

The two-phase training procedure enables the student to learn to perceive and react in noisy environments by leveraging the expert knowledge and discriminative feature representations acquired by the teacher in an ideal setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed method was evaluated in both simulated and real-world environments, with a focus on generalization across varying working conditions.

**Experiment Setup:**
- **Simulation Environment**: An 8.08m × 4.48m simulated space was used.
- **Observations**: Laser signal with 60 rays covering -135° to 135°, and an egocentric occupancy map of 2.56m × 2.56m.
- **Actions**: 3-dimensional continuous control commands: $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$.
- **Noise Model**:
 - During training: Episodic shift $\delta_{\text{shift}} \sim U(-0.5, 0.5)$ and uniform pose estimation noise $\delta_p^t \sim U(-0.1, 0.1)$. Laser signal also received uniform noise $\delta_l^t \sim U(-0.1, 0.1)$.
 - $\delta_{\text{shift}}$ remained fixed per episode but varied across episodes; $\delta_p, \delta_l$ varied at each time step.
- **Navigation Task**: Reach five randomized goals (A, B, C, D, E) sequentially within a time limit (500 steps/20 seconds for training, 60 seconds for testing).
- **Control Modes**: Training involved an MDP setting, while testing involved real-time interaction, including control frequency variations, leading to slight differences in state transition functions.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Success Rate**: Percentage of episodes where all 5 goals are reached.
- **Activation Count**: Average number of goals reached per episode.
- **Navigation Time**: Average time taken to complete the task.
- **Collision**: Average time spent in collision per episode.

**Baselines:**
- **Pose+Laser**: An RL agent using concatenated features from pose estimation and noisy laser signals.
- **Map+Laser**: An RL agent using features from a CNN-processed egocentric map and noisy laser signals.
- **Teacher**: The expert RL agent trained in an ideal, noise-free environment, tested zero-shot in noisy environments.

**Generalization Across Speed Limit:**
The method was tested under two different speed limits: (1m/s, 1m/s, $\pi/4$ rad/s) and (0.5m/s, 0.5m/s, $\pi/6$ rad/s).
- In the higher speed limit scenario, the proposed method achieved a 98.1% success rate and 4.96 activation count, with only 2.01s collision time, significantly outperforming all baselines. Map+Laser achieved 90.6% success but with higher collision time (8.37s).
- In the lower speed limit scenario, the proposed method again demonstrated superior performance with an 88.1% success rate, 4.53 activation count, and 2.34s collision time. Baselines showed substantial degradation, with Pose+Laser achieving only 28.2% success.
These results indicate that the cross-modal fusion and policy distillation contribute to better generalization to unseen dynamics, beyond just noise compensation.

**Generalization Across Noise:**
The system was evaluated under two noise scales: "easy" ($\delta_{\text{shift}} \sim U(-0.25, 0.25)$, $\delta_p^t, \delta_l^t \sim U(-0.05, 0.05)$) and "hard" ($\delta_{\text{shift}} \sim U(-0.75, 0.75)$, $\delta_p^t, \delta_l^t \sim U(-0.15, 0.15)$).
- In "easy" noise, most methods performed well (e.g., Map+Laser 95.7%, Teacher 94.3%, Ours 97.3%).
- In "hard" noise, baseline performance degraded considerably. The Teacher module's success rate dropped to 34.1%, highlighting its inability to handle unseen noisy states without specific training. The proposed method maintained an 81.2% success rate, outperforming the best baseline (Map+Laser at 64.7%) by 16.5% and achieving significantly lower collision time (3.45s vs. 7.26s).
- **T-sne Visualization**: A T-sne plot illustrated that the feature embedding from the student's multi-modal fusion transform layer (green dots) clustered much closer to the teacher's clean label map features (blue dots) than the student's raw noisy map features (orange dots). This visually supports the claim that the cross-modal fusion successfully aligns and restores features from noisy inputs.

**Generalization Across Scene:**
The methods were tested in an entirely new environment with a different layout, maintaining the speed and noise settings from the training environment.
- Pose+Laser failed completely (0% success rate), indicating overfitting to the training scene.
- Map+Laser achieved 72.2% success, and Teacher 61.5% success, demonstrating some inherent generalization of egocentric map-based representations.
- The proposed method achieved an 80.2% success rate, indicating superior generalization to novel scenes. This suggests that the egocentric map is an appropriate state space and the distillation framework enhances scene generalization.

**Sim-to-Real Generalization:**
Real-world experiments were conducted on a DJI RoboMaster EP robot equipped with an RPLiDAR S2 and an Nvidia Xavier NX. The real-world layout matched the training environment. Robot pose information was obtained via point-cloud matching, which introduces real-world pose estimation noise. The robot successfully navigated to all five goals (A,B,C,D,E) sequentially, demonstrating the method's applicability to physical systems.

**Ablation Study:**
- **Without transform layers**: Removing the transform layers in the student module and directly using CNN features for feature distillation resulted in a lower negative cosine similarity (converging around 0.4) compared to the full method (converging above 0.6). This indicates that the laser signal, when properly fused through the transform layers, provides crucial additional information for feature restoration and alignment.
- **Without contrastive objective (teacher)**: The teacher module still performed perfectly in the ideal environment, but the student trained without its contrastive objective achieved only 56.1% success in hard noise (compared to 81.2% for the full method). This highlights the necessity of the contrastive objective in training a teacher that learns a sufficiently discriminative and noise-sensitive representation for effective knowledge transfer.
- **Without feature distillation (student)**: Removing the feature distillation objective from the student module led to a reduced success rate of 72.5% in hard noise (compared to 81.2% for the full method). This underscores the importance of explicit feature alignment in enabling the student to robustly perceive its environment.

The ablation studies collectively demonstrate that the multi-modal fusion, the contrastive learning objective for the teacher, and the feature distillation for the student are all crucial components contributing to the proposed method's robust navigation performance and generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a significant contribution to the field of learning-based robot navigation by addressing key limitations related to generalization and sim-to-real transfer. The proposed method, leveraging cross-modal fusion and a teacher-student distillation framework, offers a robust approach to autonomous navigation in challenging, noisy environments.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Robustness to Sensor Noise**: The integration of a cross-modal fusion network with a dedicated transform function allows the student module to effectively compensate for sensor noise, particularly pose estimation errors. This is crucial for real-world deployment where perfect sensor readings are unattainable. The empirical evidence, especially in high-noise scenarios and the T-sne visualization, strongly supports this capability.
- **Enhanced Generalization**: The method demonstrates superior generalization across diverse working conditions, including variations in speed limits, different scales of sensor noise, and entirely novel scene layouts. This marks an advancement over traditional DRL methods which often overfit to training environments or struggle with dynamics mismatch. The ability to generalize to unseen environments is a critical step towards practical, deployable autonomous systems.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The teacher-student distillation framework provides a robust mechanism for transferring knowledge from an ideal simulated environment to a real robot operating with noisy sensors. By learning from an expert teacher and adapting to noisy observations through feature and policy distillation, the student avoids the high costs and risks of direct real-world DRL training. The successful real-world experiments on a DJI RoboMaster EP robot validate this sim-to-real transfer capability.
- **Discriminative Representation Learning**: The inclusion of a contrastive objective in the teacher's training is shown to be essential for learning a discriminative feature representation that is sensitive to pose information while being invariant to other augmentations. This representation learning is fundamental for the student's ability to effectively align features and achieve robust navigation.
- **Principled Knowledge Transfer**: The combination of feature distillation and policy distillation in the student module offers a comprehensive approach to knowledge transfer. Feature distillation ensures that the student's perception aligns with the teacher's, while policy distillation guides its decision-making, leading to a more consistent and robust overall behavior.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Deployment of Learning-Based Robotics**: By effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap and improving generalization, this research facilitates the faster and safer deployment of deep reinforcement learning-based navigation solutions in various real-world applications, from industrial automation to service robotics.
- **Reduced Training Costs and Complexity**: The ability to train expert policies in ideal simulations and then distill knowledge to a student capable of handling real-world noise reduces the need for extensive and costly real-world data collection and iterative policy refinement.
- **Foundation for Adaptive Navigation**: The principles of learning robust representations and compensating for noise could be extended to more complex scenarios, potentially forming a foundation for adaptive navigation systems that can continuously learn and adapt to changing environmental conditions or sensor degradation.
- **Integration with Existing Systems**: The authors suggest that the proposed method could serve as a motion planning module embeddable directly into traditional SLAM frameworks, enhancing their robustness in dynamic or perceptually challenging environments. This hybrid approach could combine the strengths of both traditional and learning-based methods.

**Limitations and Future Directions**:
The paper acknowledges a current limitation: the method does not explicitly consider dynamic obstacles. Future work is envisioned to incorporate locomotion information about dynamic obstacles and integrate a real-time mapping module for enhanced obstacle avoidance and navigation capabilities. This would extend the method's applicability to more complex, human-populated or rapidly changing environments.
