# Reliability-Asymmetric Spacecraft Autonomy: Co-Designing a Capable Learned GNC Stack with a Verified, Adaptation-Aware Runtime Shield

> [!summary] Summary
> The AMPLE-GNC autonomy system for deep-space missions co-designs a learned guidance, navigation, and control stack with a formally verified runtime shield to achieve both high capability and certifiable safety. It demonstrated over 90% reduction in Deep Space Network contacts and enabled 97.8% recovery from actuator sign faults in simulations, robustly handling unmodeled dynamics within a real-time budget.

## Problem

- Deep-space missions require high onboard autonomy due to communication latency, but also demand certifiable safety for high-consequence failures.
- Learned autonomy offers capability but lacks robust verifiability for flight, while traditional rule-based autonomy is certifiable but brittle and lacks generalization.
- Reconciling the transient 'unsafe' behavior of online-adapting controllers with worst-case runtime safety shields often leads to premature safe-holds, preventing recovery.

## Method

- Introduces **AMPLE-GNC**, a three-tier GNC stack based on a 'reliability asymmetry' principle, integrating a formally verified runtime shield (Lustre, Kind 2 model checker) to bound less-verifiable learned components, coupled with a split-conformal recovery-deadline certificate permitting bounded transient excursions during adaptation.
- Employs a deployed edge commander using fine-tuned language models with grammar-constrained decoding to provide hard output validity for natural language commands.
- Develops a Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA)-based fault-adaptive controller that learns system identification to feed an analytic control law, enabling recovery from unmodeled actuator faults.

## Results

- The RMA controller achieved **97.8%** recovery for actuator sign faults and **94.4%** for continuous-gain faults, outperforming fault-unaware PD (**0%**) and end-to-end deep RL (**0%**) baselines on the settled gate.
- The adaptation-aware runtime assurance allowed the recovering RMA controller to remain **94.5%** autonomous, leading to a **90.3%** reduction in DSN contacts under nominal conditions and **98.3%** under stress, with the verified shield incurring **0.02%** control loop overhead.
- The commander achieved **84%** planner-executable rate for PDDL+ actions and **48.0%** exact match generalization on a de-leaked test set, meeting flight memory constraints (277 MB int5) on a Raspberry Pi 5.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The 'reliability asymmetry' principle effectively bridges the capability-certifiability gap by strategically bounding capable, opaque learned components with simpler, formally verifiable ones.
> - A hybrid architecture, combining learned system identification (via GRU) with an analytic control law, can significantly outperform end-to-end learned policies for robust fault adaptation.
> - A statistical, distribution-free conformal recovery-deadline certificate provides a method for licensing transient 'unsafe' behavior during adaptation, resolving the tension between adaptive control and worst-case runtime safety monitors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in the development of autonomous systems for deep-space missions: the tension between achieving high capability and ensuring certifiable safety. Traditional rule-based autonomy, while certifiable and flight-proven, lacks the generality to handle novel commands or unmodeled faults. Conversely, learned autonomy, utilizing methods such as foundation models for planning and deep reinforcement learning for control, offers enhanced capability and adaptability but typically lacks robust verifiability, making its deployment in safety-critical space missions problematic.

The paper positions its work at the intersection of four distinct research domains that have largely developed in isolation:
- **Runtime Assurance (RTA) for Control:** RTA architectures, such as Simplex, allow complex, high-performance controllers to operate within monitored safety conditions, reverting to simpler, verified safety controllers upon violation. The current work extends RTA by certifying recovery time bounds for online-adapting controllers, rather than solely focusing on immediate state safety or parameter bounds.
- **Edge-Deployable Language Models (LMs):** This area focuses on compressing and optimizing large language models for execution on resource-constrained hardware, like spacecraft processors. The paper contributes to the application of grammar-constrained decoding to ensure valid outputs from small, fine-tuned LMs, distinguishing between syntactic validity and semantic correctness in evaluation.
- **Fault-Adaptive Control:** This field aims to enable control systems to adapt to unknown or changing plant dynamics. The research draws from meta-reinforcement learning and Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) techniques, specifically applying RMA to spacecraft attitude control. It differentiates itself by using a hybrid approach of learned system identification feeding an analytic control law, in contrast to end-to-end learned policies.
- **Formal Verification of Monitors:** This involves using mathematical and logical methods to formally prove properties of system components. The paper utilizes an SMT-based model checker (Kind 2) to verify the soundness of linear temporal logic (LTL) invariants, ensuring the trustworthiness of the runtime safety monitor.

The central contribution of this work within this landscape is not the advancement of any single one of these areas in isolation, but rather their integrated composition under a "reliability asymmetry" principle. This principle acknowledges the differing reliability and verifiability of different autonomy tiers and strategically assigns authority and assurance to mitigate the limitations of less verifiable components. This approach aims to achieve both capability and certifiability concurrently, which has been a persistent challenge for deploying advanced AI in spacecraft.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the unique operational demands and criticality of deep-space missions. Spacecraft operating beyond cislunar space face two fundamental pressures:
- **Distance and Communication Latency:** One-way light time can range from seconds to many minutes, rendering real-time human teleoperation impractical. Deep Space Network (DSN) contact is also a scarce and oversubscribed resource. Consequently, deep-space vehicles require a high degree of onboard autonomy to interpret goals, sequence maneuvers, and maintain control authority through disturbances without constant ground intervention.
- **High Consequence of Failure:** A deep-space asset represents a multi-billion-dollar investment with no possibility of human repair or recovery. This necessitates an extremely high level of assurance for any onboard decision-making system, requiring a robust safety argument that can withstand rigorous flight-readiness reviews.

These two pressures define the core objective of the paper: to design an autonomy system that is simultaneously **capable** and **certifiable**.
- **Capability** implies the system can interpret novel goals, fly through unmodeled faults (e.g., actuator failures, sensor degradations), and operate effectively without continuous ground contact. This often points towards learned, data-driven approaches.
- **Certifiability** demands that the system's safety can be rigorously assured and formally justified for flight, traditionally favoring rule-based, enumerable, and provably stable control systems.

The significant challenge, and thus a key motivation, is that capability and certifiability are typically in tension. Learned autonomy offers capability but lacks verifiable guarantees, while classical autonomy is certifiable but brittle and lacks generalization.

The key objectives of the AMPLE-GNC system, guided by the principle of reliability asymmetry, are detailed as follows:
- **Develop a Deployable Edge Commander:** To translate open-ended natural language operator commands into structured, machine-executable plans (PDDL+ actions) onboard, within strict flight compute and memory constraints, while providing a hard guarantee of output validity.
- **Engineer a Fault-Adaptive Controller:** To robustly recover from unmodeled actuator faults (e.g., sign reversals, continuous gain changes) that defeat classical control methods and end-to-end learned policies, maintaining mission objectives (e.g., settled pointing).
- **Integrate a Formally Verified Runtime Shield:** To monitor the entire stack, enforcing safety invariants with machine-checked soundness, thereby bounding the behavior of the less verifiable learned components.
- **Design Adaptation-Aware Runtime Assurance:** To reconcile the operation of a capable, online-adapting controller with a worst-case safety shield. This involves allowing for bounded, transient "unsafe" states during controller adaptation, while still guaranteeing safety by catching non-recovery or critical excursions using a certified recovery deadline.
- **Provide Honest and Reproducible System Evidence:** To demonstrate the end-to-end functionality, performance, and certifiability of the integrated stack on a representative testbed (Basilisk) with rigorous, independently verifiable measurements against skeptical review standards.

The underlying motivation is to bridge the gap between advanced AI capabilities and flight-readiness requirements, demonstrating that these seemingly opposing goals can be achieved through a co-designed, layered architecture.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces AMPLE-GNC, a three-tier guidance-navigation-control stack designed to achieve both capability and certifiability for deep-space missions. The architecture is founded on the principle of reliability asymmetry, where verifiable components bound the operation of more capable but opaque learned components.

**System and Testbed:**
The entire stack operates as a closed loop on the Basilisk 6-DOF attitude simulator, a flight-software-compatible framework. Attitude is represented using Modified Rodrigues Parameters (MRPs). The system is instrumented with ablation switches to isolate contributions of individual tiers and utilizes a versioned "stress battery" for consistent evaluation, incorporating inertia and sensor randomization, an actuator-fault library (sign reversals, continuous effectiveness gains, additive bias, total loss), and single-event-upset rates.

**A. The Verified Runtime Shield:**
The shield is the safety-critical component. It monitors nine Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) safety invariants covering pointing, body rate, power, wheel momentum, thermal, propellant, link, eclipse, and attitude-rate bounds. These invariants are formulated as "bounded-horizon predictor-soundness" properties: if the monitor predicts a safe trajectory, then it is indeed safe within the prediction horizon. This allows for conservative operation without compromising safety.
- **Formal Verification:** Each invariant is encoded in the Lustre synchronous dataflow language. Its predictor-soundness property is formally proved using the Kind 2 SMT-based model checker (v2.3.0 with Z3 backend). This process confirmed soundness for 9 invariants and 16 total properties at `k=1`.
- **Empirical Validation:** The formal proof is complemented by a runtime study over 30,000 adversarially generated states, demonstrating a detection completeness of 1.000, meaning all genuinely unsafe states were flagged. The combination of formal proof and empirical validation revealed and corrected an unsound first-order predictor, leading to the adoption of a second-order predictor.
- **Low Overhead:** The shield evaluation is designed for flight compute, measured at 0.02% of the 10 Hz control budget (microsecond-scale).

**B. Commander: NL→PDDL at the Edge:**
This tier translates natural language operator commands into PDDL+ actions.
- **Model Selection and Fine-tuning:** A pretrained SmolLM2-360M model is fine-tuned using low-rank adaptation. This leverages existing general language competence and specializes it to the action schema of a 20-template NASA-operations corpus. The deployed model is an int5-quantized GGUF, with memory dominated by vocabulary embedding, indicating vocabulary footprint as a key edge constraint.
- **Grammar-Constrained Decoding:** A context-free grammar is synthesized directly from the action schema (GBNF). Decoding is constrained by this grammar, guaranteeing that the emitted PDDL+ actions are always well-formed and in-vocabulary. This provides a hard output-validity guarantee, separate from semantic correctness.
- **De-leaked Evaluation:** A novel, de-leaked test set with out-of-vocabulary fillers is constructed to honestly measure semantic generalization, distinguishing it from memorization. Evaluation is performed on a "settled gate" with a dwell window.

**C. Controller: Fault-Adaptive Control via RMA:**
This component is responsible for reactive 6-DOF control, particularly recovery from unmodeled actuator effectiveness faults (sign, continuous gain, bias, total loss).
- **RMA Mechanism:** The Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) approach is employed. A "privileged teacher" policy, with knowledge of the true fault (z), implements an analytic inertia-scaled PD law. A learned GRU "student" policy infers an online estimate (ˆz) from the history of observations, previous actions, and observation deltas, crucial for identifying fault signs and magnitudes. The architecture is a hybrid: learned recurrent network for system identification, fixed analytic law for control.
- **Training:** The student is trained by direct z-regression on teacher rollouts, followed by on-policy DAgger to correct covariate shift.
- **Latching Mechanism:** To prevent steady-state limit cycling caused by estimation drift in low-excitation regimes, the fault estimate is latched once pointing converges and re-adaptation only occurs if the system drifts out of tolerance.
- **Evaluation:** Controllers are compared on a "settled gate" criterion (attitude held within 0.2° over a 10s dwell window) over a held-out fault taxonomy. Performance is compared against fault-unaware PD, classical adaptive laws (including Nussbaum-gain), and end-to-end recurrent RL (GRU+PPO, TD3+HER).
- **Capability Envelope:** The controller's performance is characterized within its training randomization envelope. Separate experiments measure extrapolation performance beyond this envelope and investigate the impact of data volume vs. randomization breadth.
- **Sensor-Noise Robustness:** Evaluation includes star-tracker measurement noise and mounting bias to assess robustness.

**D. Adaptation-Aware Runtime Assurance:**
This combines the verified shield with the fault-adaptive controller.
- **Problem:** A traditional latching safe-hold shield (Simplex-style) would trigger on the initial transient "unsafe" behavior of the adapting controller, preventing recovery.
- **Solution:** The verified monitor remains unchanged, but the engagement logic is made recovery-aware. The controller is permitted to operate within a defined "recovery zone" until either a critical excursion past a mission attitude-safety limit (Φc = 90°) or a missed recovery deadline occurs.
- **Conformal Recovery-Deadline Certificate:** A split-conformal prediction method is used to derive a distribution-free upper bound on the controller's recovery time (dα). This guarantees P(recovery ≤ dα) ≥ 1−α for exchangeable future faults, without distributional assumptions or a controller model.
- **Integration:** The Kind-2 verified backstop at Φc, combined with the conformal recovery deadline, ensures that genuinely failing controllers are caught, while genuinely recovering ones retain authority for the certified duration.

**E. Robustness, Verified Thresholds, and Fault Envelope:**
- **Adversarial and Monte-Carlo Stress:** The stack is subjected to cross-entropy search for worst-case faults and a large-scale rare-event campaign (1500 trials) to characterize tail behavior.
- **Shield-Soundness Falsification:** An empirical campaign tests predictor soundness, identifying regimes where the initial predictor was unsound and validating the 2nd-order predictor fix.
- **Controller Capability Envelope:** Operational stabilization (≤5° held) is evaluated for faults beyond the strict 0.2° science gate, particularly for combined gain-plus-bias faults and total actuator loss, mapping where the controller's capability ends and the shield's responsibility begins.

**F. Deployment and Reproducibility:**
- **Hardware Benchmarking:** The controller-plus-shield hot loop is exported to a C artifact and benchmarked on a Raspberry Pi 5. The int5 commander runs under llama.cpp.
- **Air-Bearing Testbed:** The control law and live shield are also demonstrated on a 3-DOF air-bearing testbed.
- **Reproducibility:** An executable manifest (`python -m tools.reproduce`) allows for independent regeneration and verification of all 19 headline numbers from a clean checkout.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings, quantified by rigorous evaluation on the Basilisk testbed and, where applicable, on hardware.

**A. Commander: NL→PDDL at the Edge**
- **Hard Validity Guarantee:** Grammar-constrained decoding, synthesized from the action schema, provides a hard guarantee that the deployed decoder emits only well-formed, in-vocabulary PDDL+ actions. This is distinct from semantic accuracy.
- **Planner Executability:** The full predicted action validated against a real PDDL+ domain achieved an 84% planner-executable rate on the shipped test set.
- **Semantic Generalization:** On a de-leaked test set designed to measure generalization, the commander achieved 37.8% exact match and 51.3% action match for novel phrasings with in-vocabulary entities. A re-finetune with a phrasing-diverse corpus improved exact-match generalization to 48.0% (from 31.1%).
- **Resource Constraint:** The memory footprint (277 MB int5) meets the flight target, with the vocabulary (tied-embedding) identified as the dominant cost, rather than transformer depth. This suggests phrasing diversity, not model scale, is the primary lever for improved generalization in this domain.

**B. Controller: Fault-Adaptive Control via RMA**
- **Superior Fault Recovery:** The latched RMA student controller recovered 97.8% of actuator-sign faults and 94.4% of continuous-gain faults on the settled gate (attitude held within 0.2° over a dwell window) for held-out faults within its training randomization envelope.
- **Baseline Performance:** This performance contrasts with:
 - Fault-unaware PD: 0% recovery for all fault classes.
 - End-to-end deep reinforcement learning (GRU+PPO): 0% recovery for all fault classes.
 - Classical-adaptive law (sign-ID): 100% on sign faults, but 55.2% on continuous-gain faults.
 - TRAIN-tuned Nussbaum-gain baseline: 45.2% on sign faults, 3.2% on continuous-gain faults.
- **Role of Structure:** The success is attributed to the hybrid structure of learned system identification (GRU) feeding an analytic control law, outperforming end-to-end learning for this fault regime.
- **Architectural Limits:** For faults like constant additive bias and total actuator loss, all controllers, including the privileged oracle, scored 0% on the strict science gate, indicating architectural limits of the integral-free law and inherent uncontrollability, respectively.
- **Capability Envelope Quantification:** A split-conformant retrain, trained strictly within the declared regime, showed a 30-point drop in performance (66.6% for sign, 57.0% for continuous-gain) when tested on extrapolative faults beyond its envelope. Further, 4x more in-regime data worsened extrapolation (53.2%/43.0%), indicating that randomization breadth, not data volume, is critical for held-out performance.
- **Sensor Noise Robustness:** The controller's performance remained flat even with star-tracker noise up to 0.05° per-step and a 0.1° mounting bias, demonstrating robustness to typical sensor disturbances.

**C. Adaptation-Aware Runtime Assurance**
- **Tension Resolution:** A standard binary latching safe-hold shield suppresses adapting controllers. The proposed adaptation-aware engagement, coupled with a split-conformal recovery-deadline certificate, resolves this tension.
- **Certified Autonomy:** At an alpha (α) of 0.1 (corresponding to 90.2% empirical coverage), the certificate allowed the recovering RMA controller to remain 94.5% autonomous, retaining authority. In contrast, the diverging fault-unaware PD controller was caught and safe-held (0% autonomous).
- **Live Performance:** In the integrated closed loop, the RMA controller achieved 100% autonomy, while the diverging PD controller was escalated 100% of the time, with the shield imposing a measured overhead of 0.02%.
- **Verification:** The Kind-2-verified monitor remained unchanged, ensuring its soundness, and the critical angle Φc (90°) acted as a definitive safety backstop.

**D. Robustness, Verified Thresholds, and the Fault Envelope**
- **Adversarial Resilience:** Cross-entropy search for worst-case faults revealed transients up to 132.8°, which were consistently shield-caught, with the controller still recovering. A rare-event campaign showed 93.6% gate success and only 0.20% of faults exceeding the mission limit Φc.
- **Improved Shield Soundness:** Empirical falsification of the initial monitor revealed 316 unsound decisions (0.019% of total) due to unmodeled angular acceleration. A Kind-2-verified second-order predictor, incorporating worst-case angular acceleration, eliminated these soundness violations (316 → 82 → 0).
- **Controller Operational Stabilization:** Beyond the strict 0.2° science gate, the RMA controller operationally stabilized 68% of combined gain-plus-bias faults at ≤5° (median best pointing 2.55°), a 6x improvement over PD (11% stabilized) and preventing tumbling, despite failing the strict science gate for these faults due to its integral-free design. Total actuator loss remained primarily a shield-only regime, with 27% operational stabilization.

**E. Integrated Evaluation and Mission Benefit**
- **End-to-End Demonstration:** The full stack (commander, verifier, RMA controller, adaptation-aware shield) demonstrated autonomous recovery from a multi-axis fault within the 100 ms control loop budget.
- **Significant DSN Contact Reduction:** The autonomous stack reduced DSN contacts by 90.3% under nominal conditions and 98.3% under solar-particle-event stress. This reduction is critical, as a fault-unaware baseline would saturate the scarce DSN contact budget (1.8x nominal, 17x under stress).
- **Tier-Attributable Benefit:** The DSN reduction was decomposed across the commander (maneuver autonomy), the shield (anomaly handling), and the fault-adaptive controller (fault recovery), with the latter's share growing under stress.
- **Safety vs. Skill Transfer:** The verified shield's safety guarantee is transferable across different dynamics regimes, while the learned controller's skill is regime-specific, a property leveraged by the reliability-asymmetric design.

**F. Deployment and Reproducibility**
- **Flight Compute Feasibility:** Benchmarking on a Raspberry Pi 5 showed the controller-plus-shield loop executed in 0.025 ms/cycle (4,000x under 100 ms budget), and the commander emitted grammar-valid PDDL+ actions at 28 tok/s (1.7s per action) with a 437 MB resident set, meeting flight hardware constraints.
- **Hardware Prototyping:** The control law and live shield were also successfully deployed on a 3-DOF air-bearing testbed.
- **Full Reproducibility:** All 19 headline numbers were successfully re-derived from a clean checkout using an executable manifest, confirming the reproducibility of the results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of spacecraft autonomy, with implications for future deep-space missions and safety-critical autonomous systems more broadly.

**A. Bridging the Capability-Certifiability Gap:**
The primary significance lies in demonstrating a viable approach to simultaneously achieve both high capability (interpreting novel goals, adapting to unmodeled faults) and certifiable safety in onboard spacecraft autonomy. This directly addresses a long-standing challenge where advanced learned autonomy has been deemed unsuitable for flight due to verification difficulties. By strategically bounding learned components with formally verifiable ones, the work provides a pathway for deploying AI that previously could not meet flight-readiness standards.

**B. Validation of the Reliability-Asymmetry Principle:**
The paper establishes "reliability asymmetry" as a practical and effective architectural principle. It shifts the paradigm from attempting to verify complex learned models directly to designing systems where less verifiable, capable components operate under the strict supervision of highly verifiable, simpler components. This approach offers a more tractable path to assurance than direct verification of large, opaque models. Its effectiveness is demonstrated through:
- Grammar-constrained decoding for language models.
- A formally verified runtime shield.
- A conformal, adaptation-aware runtime assurance scheme that resolves the inherent tension between an adapting controller and a worst-case safety monitor.

**C. Advancement in Specific Autonomy Components:**
- **Edge Commander:** The work demonstrates a deployable natural language commander that provides a hard output-validity guarantee at flight compute, moving beyond simple lookup tables or brittle rule sets. This could enhance operator interaction and mission flexibility.
- **Fault-Adaptive Control:** The RMA-based fault-adaptive controller significantly outperforms classical and end-to-end learned baselines on challenging unmodeled actuator faults (especially continuous gain faults) on a stringent "settled gate," which is a critical capability for mission resilience.
- **Runtime Assurance:** The introduction of a split-conformal recovery-deadline certificate provides a distribution-free, statistically guaranteed method for licensing transient "unsafe" behavior during adaptation, a crucial innovation for combining adaptive control with runtime safety.

**D. Methodological Rigor and Reproducibility:**
The emphasis on "honest gates," de-leaked test sets, seed-swept confidence intervals, and a fully executable reproduction manifest sets a high bar for evaluation. This methodological rigor enhances the credibility of the findings for flight-readiness reviews, demonstrating that learned-plus-verified systems can be characterized to an auditable standard.

**E. Operational Feasibility and Mission Impact:**
The end-to-end demonstration on a 6-DOF simulator, coupled with hardware benchmarking on a Raspberry Pi 5, confirms the operational feasibility of the entire stack within flight compute and power budgets. The measured reduction in DSN contacts (over 90%) highlights a substantial mission benefit, potentially making currently infeasible deep-space missions viable by significantly reducing reliance on scarce ground communication. This suggests the technology is not merely an efficiency improvement but a precondition for certain classes of missions.

**F. Generalizable Design Patterns:**
While the specific policies are for attitude control, key design patterns like the conditional-authority mechanism, the estimate-then-control decomposition, and the rigorous evaluation methodology are generalizable. These could inform the design and assurance of autonomous systems in other safety-critical domains beyond spacecraft.

In summary, this research provides a concrete, tested framework for co-designing and deploying capable, learned autonomy in safety-critical, resource-constrained environments. By resolving the capability-certifiability tension through architectural design and rigorous verification, it offers a pragmatic and demonstrable path toward more resilient and autonomous deep-space exploration.
