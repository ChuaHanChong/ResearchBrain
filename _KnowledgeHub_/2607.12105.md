# Robust In-Hand Manipulation via Priors in Reinforcement Learning and Mechanical Design

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northwestern University integrated physics priors into reinforcement learning reward shaping and mechanical fingertip design to enhance robotic in-hand manipulation. This combined approach led to a 3.3x increase in average success rate for continuous in-hand rotation under varying gravity conditions, utilizing only proprioceptive feedback and demonstrating improved robustness and efficiency.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning methods for in-hand manipulation often overlook task-relevant physics, leading to issues with robustness and efficiency.
- Achieving robust in-hand manipulation in challenging scenarios, such as varying hand orientations and gravity disturbances, typically requires auxiliary sensing modalities or privileged simulation information.
- Integrating the benefits of mechanical design (e.g., passive adaptation) with advanced, learning-based control policies remains a challenge in robot manipulation.

## Method

- A global ==grasp-quality prior==, based on classical ==grasp analysis==, was integrated into the ==reinforcement learning reward function== to encourage stable contact configurations.
- A local ==contact-geometry prior== was implemented through the mechanical design of ==anisotropic (cylinder-aligned) fingertips== to passively facilitate task-aligned rolling and suppress undesired off-axis motion.
- A two-stage ==Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA)== framework, combined with an initial ==grasp cache==, a ==hand-orientation curriculum==, and extensive ==domain randomization==, was used to train policies with only proprioceptive feedback for ==zero-shot sim-to-real transfer==.

## Results

- The grasp-quality reward term alone increased the average success rate for in-hand rotation from **24%** to **56%** with default fingertips and further boosted it to **83%** when combined with the aligned cylindrical fingertip.
- The aligned cylindrical fingertip consistently outperformed other morphologies, achieving the largest cumulative rotation (**2.29 turns**) and longest holding time (**31.9 seconds**) on the cylinder object, and the highest overall success rate across various objects.
- Even when evaluated with a policy not specifically trained for its geometry, the aligned cylindrical fingertip demonstrated superior zero-shot transfer, achieving **2.57 cumulative turns** robustly, confirming its inherent mechanical advantage in facilitating task-aligned rolling and rejecting disturbances.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Embedding task-relevant physics priors, both algorithmically (via reward shaping) and mechanically (via hardware design), provides effective inductive biases that significantly improve the robustness and efficiency of in-hand manipulation.
> - A dense reward signal derived from the minimum eigenvalue of the grasp Gramian matrix can guide RL policies towards maintaining well-conditioned grasps, crucial for sustained manipulation and disturbance rejection.
> - Anisotropic fingertip curvature can passively shape finger-object interactions, enabling desired task-aligned rolling while mechanically reducing off-axis motion, thereby alleviating control complexity and enhancing real-world performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

In-hand manipulation, encompassing skills such as regrasping, tool use, and general object handling, is a fundamental capability for autonomous robots. This process necessitates the coordination of multiple fingers to guide object motion, maintain grasp stability, and regulate finger-object contacts. Each of these sub-skills is governed by specific physical principles. However, a significant portion of current research, particularly in reinforcement learning (RL), approaches in-hand manipulation as a generic policy-learning problem. These methods often do not explicitly integrate the underlying physics relevant to low-level manipulation skills, leading to challenges in robustness and efficiency.

The existing landscape of in-hand manipulation research can be broadly categorized into computational algorithmic development and mechanical system design. Algorithmic approaches, including analytical methods (kinematic planning, grasp mechanics, trajectory optimization) and learning-based methods (reinforcement learning), aim to generate and optimize finger motions. While analytical methods offer theoretical foundations and interpretable solutions, they are often sensitive to real-world uncertainties and struggle to scale to high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) hands and diverse objects. Learning-based methods, particularly deep reinforcement learning, have shown promise in acquiring complex manipulation policies and adapting to uncertainties, even achieving impressive feats like rotating a Rubik's Cube. Nevertheless, many of these learning approaches frequently depend on auxiliary sensing modalities (e.g., motion capture, tactile sensors) or rely on privileged information during simulation for successful sim-to-real transfer. Furthermore, robustness to varied environmental conditions, such as arbitrary hand orientations and gravity disturbances, remains an area requiring further investigation.

Concurrently, mechanical design approaches explore leveraging the inherent physical properties of robotic hands to manage contact uncertainties. This involves incorporating features like compliance, underactuation, soft materials, and task-specific morphology to enable passive adaptation, distribute contact forces, and simplify control. Designs such as rotary fingertips, spherical hand mechanisms, or wheeled fingers are examples of embedding manipulation structures directly into the hardware to bias object motion and mitigate undesired slip. While these mechanical priors can enhance passive stability and reduce computational demands, their integration with advanced, learning-based control policies is often limited, with many evaluations relying on predefined motions or simplified controllers.

This paper addresses a perceived gap by integrating task-relevant physics priors into both the reinforcement learning framework (via reward shaping) and the mechanical design of robotic fingertips. The authors seek to combine the strengths of both algorithmic and mechanical approaches to enhance robustness, efficiency, and sim-to-real transfer for in-hand manipulation, particularly in challenging scenarios involving varying gravity and relying solely on proprioceptive feedback. The work highlights that maintaining well-conditioned grasps for sustained manipulation and exploiting fingertip morphology for task-aligned rolling are often overlooked aspects in generic policy learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate the extent to which robots can effectively leverage task-relevant physics to guide and accelerate the learning of in-hand manipulation skills. This overarching question motivates the development and evaluation of specific mechanisms for integrating such physics priors.

The specific task chosen for this investigation is continuous in-hand rolling manipulation, referred to as "in-hand rotation." The goal is for a multifingered robotic hand to reorient an object about a fixed, hand-local axis through rolling and finger gaiting. A key challenge introduced to rigorously assess the impact of physics priors is the requirement for the robot to perform manipulation under varying palm orientations. This variation introduces significant disturbances from gravity, which can substantially alter the contact conditions and manipulation dynamics. To further increase the challenge and emphasize the role of the proposed priors, the robotic system is restricted to using only proprioceptive feedback during execution, without access to tactile or visual information.

The researchers are motivated to explore two fundamental and complementary physics priors relevant to in-hand rotation with a multifingered hand:
1. **Global Grasp Stability Prior:** This computationally imposed prior focuses on ensuring stable contact configurations through coordinated finger placements. Existing reinforcement learning approaches often do not explicitly prioritize maintaining well-conditioned grasps, which is crucial for sustained manipulation and resistance to disturbances. The motivation here is to provide an inductive bias that guides the learning process towards inherently more stable grasp layouts.
2. **Local Contact Geometry Prior:** This mechanically imposed prior is based on the contact interface at the fingertip. It aims to leverage fingertip curvature to anisotropically alter rolling mobility. The motivation is to explore how hardware embodiment can passively shape the finger-object interaction, favoring desired task-aligned rolling while mechanically reducing undesired off-axis motion, thereby alleviating the control burden.

The overarching hypothesis motivating this work is that embedding physics priors into both the learning pipeline (algorithmic prior) and the fingertip morphology (mechanical prior) will lead to improved task robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer. Specifically, the authors aim to demonstrate that these integrated priors can enhance rotation efficiency, grasp stability, and disturbance rejection across various objects and challenging palm orientations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology integrates two physics priors: a global grasp-quality prior into the reinforcement learning framework as an algorithmic bias, and a local contact-geometry prior implemented through anisotropic fingertip design as a hardware bias.

#### 3.1. Physics-Prior Modeling

- **Task Definition:** The objective is continuous object rotation about a fixed axis $ \hat{a} $ defined in the palm-fixed hand frame ($F_h$). This hand-local definition makes the task objective independent of the global palm orientation, though gravity acts differently in the hand frame with varying palm poses.
- **Global Grasp-Quality Prior:** This prior is derived from classical grasp analysis, viewing in-hand rotation as a sequence of quasistatic finger-object contacts. The grasp quality metric, $q_{grasp}$, is defined as the minimum eigenvalue of the grasp Gramian matrix ($M = GG^T$), where $G$ is the grasp map that transforms contact forces into object wrenches. This metric quantifies the strength of wrench generation in the weakest direction, serving as a proxy for resistance to disturbances. This metric depends solely on contact positions, making it suitable as a dense reward-shaping term during policy training without requiring force measurements during deployment.
- **Local Contact-Geometry Prior:** This prior focuses on individual finger-object contacts using Montana’s contact formulation for pure rolling. It models how fingertip geometry affects relative rolling mobility. The relative curvature matrix ($K = K_o + K_f$, where $K_o$ and $K_f$ are object and fingertip curvature matrices) determines contact-point velocity for a given rolling angular velocity. The analysis predicts that an anisotropic fingertip, specifically a cylinder-aligned design, can permit rolling along the task direction (high mobility) while suppressing off-axis motion (zero mobility), thereby passively rejecting tangential disturbances.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning Formulation and Policy Training

- **MDP Formulation:** In-hand rotation is framed as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process. The policy outputs 16-DoF target joint positions for an Allegro Hand, which are tracked by a low-level PD controller. The policy operates at 20 Hz, with episodes lasting 400 policy steps (approximately 20 seconds).
- **Observation Space:** The policy receives only proprioceptive feedback: 16 normalized joint positions and 16 joint targets. No tactile or visual feedback is used during execution.
- **Training Framework:** The two-stage Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) framework is employed:
 - **Stage I (Teacher Policy):** A teacher policy is trained in simulation with access to proprioception and privileged information (object pose, scale, mass, friction coefficient, center-of-mass offset). The teacher policy and a privileged encoder (mapping privileged information to a latent variable) are trained jointly using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
 - **Stage II (Student Policy):** A student policy is trained using an adaptation module. This module estimates the privileged latent variable from a short history (30 frames) of proprioceptive observations via a 1D convolution and MLP. The adaptation module is trained to regress to the frozen teacher latent, and the student policy is fine-tuned with PPO using the estimated latent. This allows zero-shot transfer to the real robot using only proprioception.
- **Training Enhancements:**
 - **Initial Grasp Cache:** An offline cache of validated initial hand-object configurations is used to expedite training by focusing on manipulation rather than grasp acquisition. Grasps are filtered for stability across varying 90° palm rotations.
 - **Hand-Orientation Curriculum:** A four-stage curriculum (maximum perturbations of 0°, 45°, 90°, 180°) is implemented. This progressively exposes the policy to increasing gravity-induced disturbances by rotating the hand-object system at reset.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Randomization of object scale, mass, friction, CoM offset, PD gains, and application of external random forces in simulation prevents overfitting and aids generalization and sim-to-real transfer.

#### 3.3. Reward Design

The composite reward function includes three main components:
- **Task Term ($r_{task}$):** Rewards angular velocity aligned with the desired task axis while penalizing off-axis rotation.
- **Grasp-Quality Shaping Term ($r_{gq}$):** Uses the $q_{grasp}$ metric (minimum eigenvalue of the hand-frame grasp Gramian). It is incorporated as a dense shaping term, mapping the raw grasp-quality value to a bounded, monotonically increasing reward via an adaptive scale and clipping. This encourages well-conditioned grasps and penalizes near-degenerate ones.
- **Regularization Terms ($r_{reg}$):** Penalizes object linear velocity, deviation from the initial cached hand joint configuration, excessive joint torque, and instantaneous mechanical work to promote stable and efficient execution.

#### 3.4. Fingertip Design and Fabrication

- **Design:** Four fingertip morphologies are designed: flat, spherical, cylinder-aligned, and cylinder-orthogonal. These are parameterized by their principal curvature pairs and share the same overall envelope, length, and mounting interface to ensure that any observed differences in manipulation behavior are attributable to local surface curvature. The curved variants use a nominal radius of 40 mm.
- **Fabrication:** Fingertips are fabricated by single-piece silicone casting using reusable 3D-printed molds. Smooth-On Dragon Skin 30 (Shore 30A hardness) platinum-cure silicone rubber is used to maintain a localized contact region while providing sufficient surface tack for stable rolling.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup and Protocol

- **Hardware:** A 16-DoF Allegro V4 four-fingered hand mounted on a Franka Research 3 arm. The arm fixes palm orientation before each rollout.
- **Sensing:** Only proprioceptive feedback for the policy. A RealSense D435 camera tracks AprilTags on the object for post-processing evaluation, not for policy input.
- **Objects:** Evaluation uses a cylinder (aluminum can), a cuboid (3D-printed PLA), and a sphere (foam-rubber ball) to test generalization across various contact geometries. Objects are wrapped with paper to increase friction.
- **Palm Orientations:** Four fixed palm orientations (0°, 45°, 90°, 180°) are tested to evaluate robustness against varying gravity directions.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Mean cumulative rotation about the task axis (Rot), mean episode duration capped at 35 seconds (TTT), and a gated success rate (SR), which requires maintaining grasp for at least 10 seconds and achieving at least 0.15 turns of rotation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation assessed the impact of the global grasp-quality prior and the local contact-geometry prior in both simulation and real-world scenarios across different objects and palm orientations.

#### 4.1. Evaluation on the Global Grasp-Quality Prior

The inclusion of the grasp-quality (GQ) reward-shaping term consistently improved performance metrics:
- **Overall Improvement:** With the default Allegro hand fingertip, the GQ term increased the average success rate (SR) from 24% to 56%, mean episode duration (TTT) from 16.6 to 22.7 seconds, and mean cumulative rotation (Rot) from 0.78 to 1.25 turns. When combined with the aligned cylindrical fingertip, the GQ term further boosted SR from 64% to 83%, TTT from 26.1 to 31.1 seconds, and Rot from 1.06 to 1.27 turns.
- **Robustness to Palm Tilts:** The GQ term enhanced robustness across various palm orientations. With the default fingertip, significant gains were observed at 0° and 45° tilts. With the aligned fingertip, the largest improvements were noted at more challenging 90° and 180° tilts, indicating its effectiveness in gravity-disturbed conditions.
- **Synergistic Effect:** The combination of the grasp-quality prior in the reward function and the aligned cylindrical fingertip yielded the best overall SR and TTT, suggesting a synergistic relationship between the algorithmic and mechanical priors.
- **Simulation Consistency:** Similar performance trends were observed in simulation, reinforcing the findings.

#### 4.2. Evaluation on the Local Contact-Geometry Prior

This evaluation focused on the impact of fingertip morphology, with all policies trained using the grasp-quality term.
- **Performance on Cylinder:** For the cylinder object (used in modeling), the aligned cylindrical fingertip demonstrated superior performance, achieving the largest rotation (2.29 turns) and longest holding time (31.9 seconds). In contrast, the spherical fingertip maintained grasp for nearly as long but achieved substantially less rotation (1.39 turns), implying less constrained task-aligned motion. The orthogonal and flat fingertips performed comparatively worse across metrics.
- **Generalization Across Objects:** The aligned cylindrical design achieved the highest TTT and SR across all three evaluated objects (cylinder, cuboid, and ball). Its rotation performance was slightly lower than other designs on the ball, which may be attributed to the ball's geometry being less aligned with the trained rolling motion dynamics.
- **Robustness to Palm Tilts:** The aligned fingertip improved SR at every palm tilt angle. The most substantial gain was observed at a 90° tilt (from 48% to 81% SR with vanilla vs. GQ-trained aligned policies), which aligns with the geometric hypothesis. At this tilt, gravity imposes significant lateral loads, making off-axis disturbances a primary failure mode. The anisotropic curvature of the aligned fingertip, hypothesized to suppress such motion, effectively mitigated this issue.
- **Simulation Limitations:** Simulation results did not clearly differentiate the effects of fingertip designs, which the authors attribute to limitations in contact rendering fidelity within the simulator.

#### 4.3. Isolated Contribution of Fingertip Morphology (Fixed Policy Evaluation)

To isolate the direct contribution of fingertip geometry, a single policy (trained with default fingertips) was evaluated on all four physical fingertip morphologies.
- **Zero-Shot Transfer:** The aligned cylindrical fingertip achieved 2.57 cumulative turns with low variance (Std = 0.10) and performed robustly for the full 35-second episode duration. In contrast, the flat, orthogonal, and spherical fingertips plateaued below 1.7 turns and terminated significantly earlier (40-48% of the available time).
- **Support for Hypothesis:** This experiment demonstrates that the aligned cylindrical fingertip passively facilitates task-aligned rolling and rejects out-of-plane disturbances, even when the controller was not explicitly trained with this specific geometry. This highlights the intrinsic mechanical advantage provided by the anisotropic design.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by demonstrating a principled approach to integrating physics knowledge into both reinforcement learning and mechanical design for enhanced robustness and learning efficiency.

- **Improved Manipulation Robustness and Efficiency:** The combination of a global grasp-quality prior as a reward-shaping term and a local contact-geometry prior embedded in fingertip morphology significantly improves rotation efficiency, grasp stability, and disturbance rejection for in-hand manipulation. This is particularly notable given the policy's reliance solely on proprioceptive feedback and its robustness against varying gravity directions.
- **Guidance for Skill Learning:** The findings suggest that explicit modeling of task-relevant physics priors provides useful inductive biases for reinforcement learning. This can guide the policy towards more physically sound and stable manipulation strategies, potentially reducing the sample complexity required for learning complex dexterous skills.
- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** By incorporating physical principles directly into the hardware design, the approach leverages passive mechanical properties to bridge the reality gap. The anisotropic fingertip, in particular, enables robust performance in the real world even with policies not explicitly trained for it, indicating a powerful mechanism for improving sim-to-real transfer.
- **Reduced Sensing and Control Demands:** Demonstrating robust in-hand manipulation with only proprioceptive feedback implies that such integrated physics priors can potentially reduce the reliance on high-bandwidth exteroceptive sensing (e.g., tactile or vision) or complex real-time contact force regulation, simplifying the overall system architecture.
- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated for in-hand rotation, the general concept of embedding complementary physics priors into both algorithmic and mechanical aspects of robot design could be applicable to other contact-rich manipulation tasks, offering a path towards more capable and resilient robotic systems.

#### 5.1. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- The generalization of these physics priors to other manipulation categories (e.g., in-hand translation, regrasping, tool use) or tasks involving more diverse contact modes (sliding, sticking) and whole-hand interactions remains unevaluated.
- The robustness to disturbances was primarily assessed through changes in gravity. A more controlled and quantitative evaluation under direct external perturbations is needed to substantiate claims of general disturbance rejection.
- The effectiveness of the proposed method for sim-to-real transfer has not been quantitatively analyzed in terms of how much of the real-world performance gain is attributable solely to the priors versus other factors in the training and adaptation pipeline. Addressing this could provide clearer insights into the specific benefits of the priors for bridging the reality gap.
