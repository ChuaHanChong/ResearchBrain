# Cross-modality Force and Language Embeddings for Natural Human-Robot Communication

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT and Harvard Medical School developed a framework that creates shared embeddings for human-applied force profiles and natural language descriptions, enabling robots to bidirectionally translate between these modalities. This approach helps robots understand and respond to human intent more accurately in physical interaction tasks, showing up to 30% improvement over baselines.

## Problem

- Communicating nuanced physical interactions, such as the "gentleness" of a touch, to robots effectively using language alone is challenging.
- Physical demonstrations convey direct force but often lack the broader context and reasoning that natural language provides.
- Prior research in multimodal robotics had not addressed creating shared embeddings specifically between force trajectories and natural language descriptions.

## Method

- A **dual autoencoder** architecture was developed to learn a ==shared latent space== for force profiles and natural language phrases, facilitating bidirectional translation.
- A dataset was collected from human participants performing both phrase-to-force demonstrations and force-to-phrase descriptions using a robot manipulator.
- The model was trained with ==multitask learning objectives==, including ==reconstruction, contrastive, and translation losses==, to ensure robust cross-modality representation.

## Results

- The dual autoencoder architecture consistently outperformed baseline methods by **20-30%** in force-language translation accuracy.
- The framework demonstrated effective generalization to out-of-distribution (unseen) modifiers and directions, indicating robustness for novel inputs.
- SBERT embeddings led to superior force profile reconstruction and better generalization for unseen inputs, while binary phrase vectors provided higher linguistic precision in translated textual outputs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Force profiles and linguistic descriptions can be effectively embedded into a unified latent space, enabling seamless integration and bidirectional translation between physical and verbal cues.
> - Natural human-human collaboration, particularly in physical tasks like therapy, involves a synergistic combination of verbal and haptic communication, which robots can emulate.
> - Different language representations (e.g., binary phrase vectors vs. SBERT embeddings) offer distinct advantages, presenting a trade-off between physical fidelity in force reconstruction and linguistic precision in textual output.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in human-robot interaction (HRI): the effective communication of human intentions, instructions, and desired behaviors to robots, particularly in tasks requiring physical contact and force control. Historically, early roboticists developed specific robot languages (e.g., VAL) which proved insufficient for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks that often involve subconscious human knowledge difficult to articulate verbally. This led to alternative approaches such as teaching by demonstration, programming by demonstration, skill acquisition, and imitation learning, which bypass the need for explicit linguistic translation of physical skills.

More recently, advances in natural language processing have spurred interest in language-grounded robot control. However, a fundamental limitation persists: language alone may not effectively convey nuanced physical interactions, such as the "gentleness" of a touch. Physical demonstrations, while conveying direct force, often lack the broader context and reasoning that language can provide. This paper positions itself at the intersection of these two communication paradigms, arguing for the necessity of integrating verbal and haptic communication modalities to achieve synergistic human-robot coordination, similar to how humans collaborate on physical tasks.

In the landscape of multimodal embeddings in robotics, previous work has explored shared spaces between vision and force data for manipulation or language and vision for navigation. However, the existing literature had not specifically addressed the creation of shared embeddings between force trajectories and natural language descriptions. This research aims to fill this gap by developing a framework for bidirectional translation between physical forces and their linguistic descriptions, drawing inspiration from natural human-human interactions, such as those observed in physical therapy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a unified method for representing language and force that facilitates their seamless integration and coordination in human-robot communication. The motivation stems from the observation that natural human-human collaboration often involves a synergistic combination of verbal and physical cues. For instance, when two individuals carry a heavy object, they coordinate both through verbal instructions and the physical forces applied. Similarly, in contexts like physical therapy, a therapist verbally explains a procedure (e.g., "lift your leg gently") while simultaneously providing an assistive force, demonstrating the intended action. This integrated approach clarifies ambiguity and builds trust, which is difficult to achieve with either modality in isolation.

The paper argues that language alone is insufficient for communicating complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks, as certain motions and behaviors, particularly those involving subtle forces or compliance control, are challenging to describe verbally. Conversely, physical demonstration lacks the contextual information, nuance, and reasoning that language can provide. Therefore, the core motivation is to enable robots to achieve a similar level of coordination by integrating verbal and haptic communication modalities.

Specifically, the paper outlines the following contributions as key objectives:
1. **Framework Development:** To present a framework for cross-modality embedding of force profiles and words, enabling translation between physical force curves and natural language descriptions. The goal is to show that despite their apparent differences, language and force profiles can be embedded in a unified latent space where their proximity can be quantified, allowing them to supplement, integrate, or substitute each other.
2. **Data Collection Methodology:** To develop a paired data collection methodology involving human participants performing both language-to-force and force-to-language translations, capturing human intuition regarding force-language relationships.
3. **Evaluation and Generalization:** To establish evaluation metrics and present results validating the framework's effectiveness and its ability to generalize to unseen data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a framework based on a dual autoencoder architecture to learn a shared latent space for force profiles and natural language phrases. This unified representation aims to enable robots to translate human-applied force profiles into linguistic descriptions and generate appropriate force outputs from language instructions.

**A. Data Collection:**
A dataset was collected from 10 participants (6 males, 4 females, aged 21-32) interacting with a UR10 manipulator arm. The data collection focused on simple hand motions to capture human intention and fundamental primitives while minimizing confounding variables. Two main procedures were employed:
1. **Phrase-to-Force:** Participants were presented with 42 distinct phrases (combinations of direction and optional modifier words, e.g., "smoothly down forward"). They then gripped the robot's end-effector and physically demonstrated the motion corresponding to the phrase, while force measurements were recorded.
2. **Force-to-Phrase:** The robot executed randomly generated force trajectories (combining primary direction, magnitude, and duration). Participants experienced these motions and then constructed descriptive phrases using a predefined vocabulary to characterize the felt force profile.
Force profiles were normalized across participants to account for individual variations in strength and movement patterns. The study adhered to IRB ethical guidelines.

**B. Force Profile Representation:**
Raw force data, measured by a force-torque sensor on the robot's end-effector, consists of time-varying 3D force vectors (Fx, Fy, Fz). To create a uniform representation for the neural network, each force profile was:
1. **Resampled:** To 256 evenly spaced time steps over a fixed duration of 4 seconds, resulting in a 3 × 256 tensor.
2. **Integrated:** Each axis of the resampled force profile was integrated over time to obtain an impulse profile.
3. **Flattened:** The impulse profile was flattened into a 768-dimensional vector, serving as input to the force encoder.

**C. Language Representation:**
Two distinct approaches were used for encoding phrases into numerical representations:
1. **Minimal Viable Vocabulary (MVV) Binary Phrase Vectors:** This vocabulary comprised 18 direction words and 12 modifier words. Each phrase was composed of exactly one modifier and one direction word. Each word was one-hot encoded (31 dimensions for modifier, 31 for direction, including an "empty" option for each). These binary vectors were concatenated to form a 62-dimensional phrase representation.
2. **Extended SBERT Vocabulary (SBERT Embeddings):** To handle open-vocabulary inputs, the Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model was leveraged. SBERT produces 768-dimensional semantically meaningful representations of complete text. For any input text, the system identifies the most semantically similar phrase within the MVV using cosine similarity of SBERT embeddings.

**D. Dual Autoencoder Architecture:**
The core of the framework is a dual autoencoder architecture (Figure 6).
- **Encoders:** `E_force` maps the 768-dimensional impulse vector to a 16-dimensional shared latent space `z`. `E_phrase` maps either the 62-dimensional binary phrase vector or the 768-dimensional SBERT embedding to the same 16-dimensional latent space `z`.
- **Decoders:** `D_force` reconstructs a force profile from `z`. `D_phrase` reconstructs a phrase representation from `z`.
The network uses linear transformations and Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions.

**E. Multitask Learning Objectives:**
The model was trained by jointly optimizing three loss functions to encourage a robust cross-modality latent representation:
1. **Reconstruction Loss (Lr):** Measures how accurately each autoencoder reconstructs its own input from the latent vector. Mean Squared Error (MSE) was used for force profiles and SBERT embeddings, while cross-entropy was used for binary phrase vectors.
2. **Contrastive Loss (Lc):** Aligns paired force and phrase embeddings in the shared latent space by minimizing the distance between corresponding latent vectors and pushing apart non-corresponding vectors, using a margin parameter.
3. **Translation Loss (Lt):** Measures the model's ability to translate between modalities. It involves encoding an input from one modality (e.g., force) and decoding it into the other modality (e.g., phrase), then comparing the result to the ground truth. This loss encourages modality-agnostic features in the shared embedding.
The overall loss function was a weighted sum of these three losses, with all weights (kr, kz, kt) set to 1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation aimed to assess force-language translation performance, generalization to unseen examples, and the impact of phrase representation (binary vs. SBERT). Two variants of the dual autoencoder (DAE) framework were tested: DAE_B (binary phrase vectors) and DAE_S (SBERT embeddings). These were compared against three baseline models: SVM-KNN, DMLP_B (Multi-Layer Perceptron with binary phrase vectors, no shared latent space), and DMLP_S (MLP with SBERT embeddings, no shared latent space).

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Force Profile Accuracy (FPAcc):** Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth force profiles (lower is better).
- **Force Direction Accuracy (FDAcc):** Cosine similarity between predicted and ground-truth total impulse vectors (closer to +1 is better).
- **Modifier Similarity (ModSim):** Cosine similarity of SBERT embeddings for predicted and ground-truth modifier words (closer to +1 is better).
- **Direction Similarity (DirSim):** Cosine similarity of SBERT embeddings for predicted and ground-truth direction words (closer to +1 is better).
- **Full Phrase Similarity (PhraseSim):** Average of ModSim and DirSim (closer to +1 is better).

**Experiments:**
1. **In-Distribution Evaluation:** 90/10 train/test split, repeated 30 times.
2. **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Modifiers:** A single modifier was held out from the training set and tested.
3. **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Directions:** A single direction was held out from the training set and tested.

**Results and Analysis:**

- **In-Distribution Results:**
 - All four neural approaches (DMLP_B, DMLP_S, DAE_B, DAE_S) significantly outperformed the SVM-KNN baseline in reproducing force profiles (FPAcc) and capturing aggregate direction (FDAcc).
 - The DAE variants consistently outperformed their DMLP counterparts across all metrics, indicating the effectiveness of the shared latent space approach for robust force-language mapping.
 - DAE_B achieved the highest ModSim (0.58) and PhraseSim (0.78) scores, suggesting that binary phrase vectors can be advantageous for precise linguistic mapping when the training distribution covers the same words.
 - This confirms that the dual autoencoder effectively translates force-language pairs under in-distribution conditions.

- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Modifiers:**
 - DAE_S achieved the best force profile reconstruction (FPAcc: 5.41), demonstrating the benefit of SBERT embeddings for generalizing to unseen adverbial cues by producing more accurate physical force trajectories.
 - DAE_B maintained higher phrase similarity scores (PhraseSim: 0.68), indicating stronger preservation of adverb semantics in the textual output, even for unseen modifiers.
 - The DAE models generalized more effectively to unseen modifiers than MLPs, indicating the latent space's ability to retain semantic representations of adverbs.

- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Directions:**
 - Both DAE_S and DMLP_S showed superior force profile reconstruction (FPAcc) and directional accuracy (FDAcc) for unseen directions. This suggests that SBERT's continuous semantics facilitate better extrapolation of physical orientation and overall impulse direction for novel directional words.
 - Conversely, the binary-vector based models (DAE_B, DMLP_B) achieved higher ModSim, DirSim, and PhraseSim scores. This implies that while SBERT-based models produced more faithful physical force reconstructions, their textual outputs were less aligned with the precise wording of the missing direction.
 - This indicates a trade-off: SBERT excels in zero-shot direction encoding on the force side but incurs a penalty in accurately reproducing specific textual labels.

**Summary of Findings:**
The dual autoencoder architecture (DAE) demonstrated 20-30% improvement over baseline methods. The models exhibited a capacity to generalize to unseen inputs. A clear trade-off was identified: SBERT embeddings led to superior force reconstruction accuracy and generalization capabilities, while binary phrase vector encoding yielded better linguistic precision, especially for in-distribution performance. The choice of representation should be guided by specific application requirements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for cross-modality embedding of force profiles and verbal descriptions, representing a step towards more natural and intuitive human-robot communication. By enabling bidirectional translation between how humans describe forces in language and how robots can produce them, the work provides a foundation for robots to understand and respond to human intent in a more nuanced manner, particularly in tasks requiring physical interaction.

The ability of the dual autoencoder architecture to outperform baseline approaches, coupled with its robust generalization to unseen force profiles and phrases, indicates its potential for real-world human-robot interaction tasks. The findings highlight that different language representations (binary vs. SBERT) offer distinct advantages, allowing for flexibility in system design based on whether physical fidelity or linguistic precision is prioritized.

The immediate significance lies in applications like rehabilitation therapy, where coordinated force control and clear verbal communication are essential for patient safety and efficacy. By integrating these modalities, robots could provide assistance that is both physically appropriate and semantically understood by patients, potentially leading to more effective and comfortable therapeutic interventions. Beyond rehabilitation, the framework could be applied to various collaborative manipulation tasks in manufacturing, logistics, or personal assistance, where robots need to interpret subtle human cues and respond with appropriate physical actions.

The architectural simplicity of the chosen encoder-decoder design establishes clear baselines and allows for interpretability and data efficiency. The framework's architecture-agnostic nature suggests that future work could integrate more advanced neural network architectures (e.g., transformers, graph networks) to further enhance performance, building upon the fundamental force-language mappings established here. This work addresses a recognized gap in multimodal robotic learning by specifically focusing on the integration of haptic and linguistic information, paving the way for robots that can understand "gentle" or "forceful" not just as abstract concepts but as actionable physical commands grounded in human perception.
