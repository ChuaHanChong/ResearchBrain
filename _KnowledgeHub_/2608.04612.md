# GASP: GPU-Accelerated Safe Planner for Real-Time Collision-Aware Motion Generation with Latent Trajectory Sampling

> [!summary] Summary
> GASP is a GPU-accelerated joint-space motion planner that predicts residual interior control points of a clamped degree-7 B-spline while inserting boundary control points analytically to guarantee endpoint derivative constraints. A conditional VAE samples multiple candidates, decoded and collision-checked in parallel on GPU. It reaches 71.9% success at 1.08 ms inference, versus 16.1% for GPU trajectory optimization, and halves training-time collisions in robotic table tennis.

## Problem

- Optimization-based GPU planners incur high, variable runtime cost, limiting their use inside online control loops and reinforcement-learning pipelines with rapidly changing boundary conditions.
- Analytical online trajectory generators deliver deterministic low-latency jerk-limited motion but ignore workspace collisions, self-collisions, and coupled geometric feasibility across joints.
- Diffusion and flow-based generative planners cover multimodal trajectory distributions but their iterative sampling procedures remain far too costly for sub-millisecond inference.

## Method

- Boundary-conditioned clamped degree-7 ==B-spline==: the network predicts residual offsets on 8 free interior control points, while first three and last two points are computed in closed form.
- ==Conditional VAE== latent branch supplies control-point offsets and concatenated latent codes, letting one start-goal query be replicated into a GPU batch of decoded, validated candidates with a speed-heuristic selector.
- Feasibility-driven training without expert trajectories: differentiable position, velocity, acceleration, jerk bound penalties plus world/self-collision costs, jerk smoothness, settling-time term, and annealed KL regularizer.

## Results

- On the shared evaluation set **GASP** with batch 16 reaches **71.94%** success at **1.08 ms**, versus 16.06% at 56.78 ms for cuRobo optimization and 79.12% at 4.24 ms for the analytic generator.
- Ablations show the CVAE branch and batching matter: removing CVAE drops success from **67.70% to 59.88%**, and batch 1 drops to 58.20%; grouped convolutions at g=8 fall to 52.72%.
- As a reinforcement-learning reset planner in table tennis it matches baseline return rate (**0.858 vs 0.873**) while cutting colliding trajectory segments roughly **50%** over 8,000 training steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Enforcing boundary derivative constraints analytically and learning only interior residuals shrinks the prediction problem and pushes residual failures into the trajectory interior.
> - Sampling many candidates and validating them in parallel converts spare GPU throughput into success rate at nearly constant per-batch latency, favoring stochastic planners over deterministic regressors.
> - Feasibility penalties alone can replace expert trajectory supervision, but the learned planner stays tied to one robot, fixed loss weights, and static primitive collision geometry.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous robotic systems, particularly for manipulation tasks, necessitates real-time motion planners capable of generating collision-aware and dynamically feasible trajectories. This requirement is amplified in closed-loop control systems where perception updates, moving targets, model uncertainties, and tracking errors introduce non-stationary boundary conditions.

Traditional optimization-based planners, such as CHOMP and GPU-accelerated variants like cuRobo, offer flexibility in handling diverse constraints. However, their iterative nature can lead to variable computational costs and runtimes, which can be prohibitive for high-frequency online deployment, especially within GPU-based reinforcement learning (RL) pipelines where latency and hardware integration are critical. Conversely, analytical online trajectory generators, exemplified by Ruckig, provide low latency and deterministic runtimes by computing jerk-limited trajectories but typically do not incorporate collision avoidance or consider coupled geometric feasibility.

The field has seen the emergence of learning-based motion planners, which aim to amortize the computational cost of trajectory generation through neural inference. Early approaches focused on accelerating existing planning methods by predicting initializations or feasible motion priors. More recent work has moved towards direct kinodynamic trajectory generation in joint space, with some methods enforcing endpoint constraints analytically while learning other trajectory parameters to reduce the learning burden. Generative models, including diffusion-based and flow-matching formulations, have also been explored to generate diverse trajectory distributions, but their sampling procedures can be computationally intensive for sub-millisecond inference requirements.

GASP positions itself at the intersection of these approaches. It aims to combine the speed of analytical planners with the collision-awareness and constraint handling capabilities typically found in optimization-based methods, while leveraging the parallelization benefits of GPU hardware. Unlike optimization-heavy pipelines, GASP performs trajectory prediction in a single forward pass. It distinguishes itself from purely analytical generators by incorporating explicit collision checking. Relative to recent generative planners, GASP employs a lightweight residual B-spline parameterization and analytically enforced boundary constraints to achieve near-millisecond batched inference, while retaining the ability to sample multiple trajectory candidates through a conditional latent variable. The work addresses the need for fast, reliable, and safe motion generation, particularly for repetitive tasks in known environments and as a component within RL training loops.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a real-time, collision-aware joint-space motion planner, named GASP (GPU-Accelerated Safe Planner), suitable for online deployment in known static environments. This planner aims to repeatedly generate dynamically feasible trajectories under changing boundary conditions, a common requirement in closed-loop robotic manipulation.

The motivation for GASP stems from several limitations observed in existing motion planning paradigms:
- **Computational Cost and Runtime Variability of Optimization-Based Planners:** While effective for constraint handling, iterative optimization methods often incur significant and variable computational costs, hindering their integration into real-time online learning and control systems that demand high-frequency, deterministic inference.
- **Lack of Collision Awareness in Analytical Planners:** Analytical trajectory generators offer speed and deterministic runtime but typically disregard workspace and self-collisions, limiting their applicability in safety-critical environments.
- **Integration Challenges in GPU-based RL Pipelines:** Modern reinforcement learning often utilizes GPU hardware for training. Running motion planning on the same hardware can simplify system integration and reduce latency, highlighting a need for GPU-native planning solutions.
- **Need for Robustness to Non-Stationary Conditions:** Robotic tasks frequently involve non-zero initial velocities and accelerations due to tracking errors or dynamic environments. Planners need to maintain high performance under such non-stationary boundary conditions.
- **Balancing Speed and Safety:** There is a persistent challenge in achieving a balance between rapid trajectory generation and ensuring safety through comprehensive feasibility checks, including collision avoidance.

To address these motivations, the specific contributions of this work are articulated as:
1. **Development of a boundary-conditioned clamped B-spline planner:** This approach analytically enforces endpoint derivative constraints, reducing the learning complexity by focusing the neural network on predicting only the unconstrained interior control-point residuals. This design guarantees prescribed boundary conditions and promotes trajectory smoothness by construction.
2. **Creation of a GPU-batched, sampleable neural planning pipeline:** The entire pipeline, encompassing trajectory sampling, dense spline evaluation, feasibility checking, and candidate selection, is designed for parallel execution on the GPU. This architecture enables the generation and validation of multiple trajectory candidates with near-millisecond inference times.
3. **Feasibility-driven training and evaluation:** The planner is trained without requiring expert trajectory supervision, instead utilizing differentiable feasibility penalties for joint limits, dynamic limits, self-collision, world-collision, and smoothness. This training methodology prioritizes generating safe and feasible trajectories. The evaluation includes validation against analytical, optimization-based, and neural baselines, and deployment in a robotic table tennis scenario.

In essence, GASP aims to bridge the gap between fast but collision-agnostic analytical planners and safe but computationally intensive optimization-based planners, by leveraging a learned, GPU-accelerated, and sampleable generative model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GASP's methodology integrates a learned generator with a boundary-conditioned clamped B-spline representation, designed for online, real-time operation. The pipeline is structured around predicting unconstrained interior control points while enforcing boundary conditions analytically, and parallelizing trajectory validation on the GPU.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation

The planning problem involves generating a smooth, collision-free, and kinodynamically feasible joint-space trajectory ζ(t) = (q(t), ˙q(t), ¨q(t)) from an initial state ζ(0) = (q₀, ˙q₀, ¨q₀) to a final state ζ(T) = (qₜ, ˙qₜ), where T is the trajectory horizon. The initial state is constrained up to acceleration, and the final state up to velocity.

**1) Joint Limits:** Trajectories must adhere to lower and upper bounds for joint position (L_q), velocity (L_˙q), acceleration (L_¨q), and jerk (L_⃛q) at every sampled time t_k.
**2) Collision Avoidance:** Explicit world and self-collision checks are performed along the decoded trajectory. Robot links and workspace obstacles are approximated using primitive collision geometries (e.g., spheres for robot, cuboids for obstacles) to facilitate efficient collision checking. Differentiable collision losses are integrated during training.

#### 3.2. B. Trajectory Representation

GASP employs a clamped B-spline of degree d=7 to parameterize the joint position trajectory over a normalized time τ ∈, where physical time t = Tτ.

**1) B-spline Trajectory Parameterization:** The trajectory q(τ) is defined by a weighted sum of B-spline basis functions N_{i,d}(τ) and a set of n_c control points C = {c₀..., c_{n_c-1}}. A key aspect is that the network predicts only the unconstrained interior control points (C_{free}). The first three and last two control points (c₀, c₁, c₂, c_{n_c-2}, c_{n_c-1}) are computed analytically based on the initial and final boundary conditions (q₀, ˙q₀, ¨q₀, qₜ, ˙qₜ), using a derivation from prior work. This ensures that the analytically constrained boundary points are fixed. The model predicts residual offsets (∆C_θ) relative to a baseline of linearly interpolated free control points (C_{baseline}), forming C_{free} = C_{baseline} + ∆C_θ. For fast inference, B-spline basis matrices for position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk are precomputed and cached.

#### 3.3. C. Model Architecture

The planner utilizes a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) operating on the temporal sequence of B-spline control points. Its architecture (summarized in Figure 2) is designed to map boundary conditions to residual corrections for the baseline trajectory.

**1) Input Encoding and Normalization:** The network input comprises a concatenation of the normalized initial and final boundary states. Normalization scales these quantities to approximate values within [-1, 1], improving numerical conditioning.
**2) Baseline Trajectory Construction:** A baseline trajectory is first constructed by computing boundary control points analytically and linearly interpolating the remaining internal control points. This baseline inherently satisfies boundary constraints, B-spline smoothness, and consistency with the horizon T.
**3) Latent Sampling Layer:** To enable the generation of multiple trajectory candidates per query, GASP incorporates a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) branch with a latent variable z ∈ R^{d_z}. During training, an encoder maps normalized boundary inputs (optionally with baseline free control points) to the parameters of a Gaussian posterior (mean μ_q, diagonal covariance Σ_q). Samples for z are drawn from this posterior and regularized towards a standard normal prior using a KL divergence term. During inference, z is sampled directly from the standard normal prior, allowing the generation of diverse trajectory proposals in parallel.
**4) Convolutional Refinement Network:** The core of the planner is a 1D CNN that processes a sequence of kinematic descriptors (positions, velocities, accelerations, and jerks) sampled from the current B-spline trajectory, concatenated with baseline control points. This CNN, consisting of 1D convolutions, non-linearities, and skip connections, predicts residual corrections specifically for the unconstrained midpoint region of the trajectory, leaving the analytically fixed boundary control points unaltered. The output is a refined set of control points, which are then used to decode the dense B-spline trajectory.

#### 3.4. D. Model Training

GASP is trained using differentiable penalties corresponding to feasibility and regularization objectives. The total training objective L_{train} combines several loss components:
- **L_{kin}:** Penalizes violations of joint position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk limits. It uses a worst-case interior violation penalty for each derivative order, applying a squared penalty for exceedances beyond a specified margin.
- **L_{col}:** Penalizes world and self-collisions using differentiable collision violation signals (from cuRobo). This term is non-negative and increases with penetration depth, with the maximum violation over the interior trajectory samples dominating the penalty.
- **L_{jerk}:** A smoothness regularizer that penalizes normalized jerk over the interior trajectory.
- **L_{time}:** Encourages time-efficient motion. It distinguishes between feasible and infeasible trajectories; feasible trajectories are evaluated based on their terminal settling behavior, while infeasible trajectories are ranked by their largest collision or kinematic violation.
- **L_{KL}:** A KL divergence term that regularizes the latent variable z towards a standard normal prior, as part of the CVAE formulation. The weight for L_{KL} is annealed during training.

Training is performed using PyTorch Lightning with AdamW optimizer, cosine annealing learning rate schedule, and gradient clipping. A minibatch size of 64 is used, and each epoch consists of 256 optimizer steps.

#### 3.5. E. Data Generation

Training samples consist of boundary-condition pairs (initial and final states, and a horizon T). These are generated online on the GPU. Initial positions are sampled collision-free, and initial velocities and accelerations are within limits or set to zero. Two on-GPU rejection filters are used:
- **Reachability-aware filter:** Restricts initial positions to regions around the goal that are reachable within T, considering kinematic limits.
- **Inevitable-violation filter:** Rejects samples that would unavoidably violate position limits or collide within a short duration (0.1s).
Rejected samples are resampled on the GPU.

Offline validation datasets use a similar sampler, followed by an analytic-planner filter that rejects samples whose minimum feasible analytic duration exceeds T. The remaining samples are partitioned into in-collision and collision-free groups based on whether the analytic trajectory collides, allowing for evaluation of GASP's ability to find feasible alternatives.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GASP focused on its training-time feasibility, offline comparisons against established baselines, and its deployment as an online reset planner in a reinforcement learning (RL) setup for robotic table tennis.

#### 4.1. A. Training Performance

Figure 4 illustrates the training evolution, showing the aggregate feasibility percentage on validation datasets over epochs. The full CONV model consistently achieved the highest aggregate feasibility compared to ablated variants (e.g., w/o CVAE, w/o horizon curriculum, w/o z-offset). This suggests that the combined architectural components contribute to a broader distribution of feasible candidates per query. Figure 6 provides visual examples of normalized joint-space trajectories, distinguishing between successful and failed samples, indicating the B-spline's smoothness and the planner's attempt to respect constraint margins.

#### 4.2. B. Comparison with State of the Art

GASP (specifically, the CONV variant with a deployment batch size of 16, denoted CONV@16) was compared against:
- **Ruckig:** An analytical, real-time, jerk-limited online trajectory generator that does not perform collision checking.
- **cuRobo:** A GPU-accelerated optimization-based motion planner that includes collision checking.
- **MLP architecture:** A neural planner based on a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) as described in.

Table I provides a detailed failure breakdown across planner types on a shared evaluation set.
- **Success Rate and Inference Time:** CONV@16 achieved a success rate of 71.94% ± 1.54% with an inference time of 1.08 ms ± 0.01 ms. In comparison, the Analytic planner achieved 79.12% ± 0.91% success with 4.24 ms ± 0.03 ms inference time (though without collision checking), and cuRobo Opt achieved 16.06% ± 1.29% success with 56.78 ms ± 0.73 ms inference time. The MLP baseline showed 67.00% ± 1.49% success at 0.77 ms ± 0.03 ms.
- **Collision Rate:** CONV@16 had a collision rate of 4.28% ± 0.83%, significantly lower than cuRobo Opt (83.94% ± 1.29%) and Analytic (20.88% ± 0.91%). This indicates GASP's effectiveness in collision avoidance compared to these baselines, particularly cuRobo in this specific evaluation setup, possibly due to cuRobo’s dataset filtering.
- **Robustness to Non-Stationary Conditions:** Figure 7 demonstrates GASP’s robustness to non-zero initial velocities and accelerations. As the scaling factor for initial velocity/acceleration increased, CONV@16 maintained performance comparable to the Analytic baseline for moderate nonstationarity and substantially outperformed cuRobo Opt at higher scaling factors. This suggests improved robustness to non-stationary initial states.
- **Planner Compatibility (Figure 8):** When evaluated on datasets filtered by different planners, CONV@16 maintained a strong success rate across all filtered datasets (cuRobo Opt, Analytic, CONV@16 filters), indicating its ability to find feasible trajectories even in scenarios where other planners struggle. In contrast, cuRobo Opt performed well primarily on data filtered by itself.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

Table II presents an ablation study on key components of the convolutional planner.
- **CVAE Module:** Removing the CVAE module (w/o CVAE) or disabling its full integration (w/o z Concat, w/o z Offset) generally resulted in lower success rates, suggesting that the conditional latent sampling contributes to widening the distribution of feasible candidates. The full CVAE integration, with latent code concatenation and offset, yielded the highest success.
- **Horizon-Curriculum Schedule:** Training without the curriculum schedule (w/o sched.) resulted in a slightly lower success rate (65.38% vs. 67.70% for baseline CONV@16), indicating the benefit of annealing the horizon during training.
- **Convolutional Capacity:** Increasing convolutional groups (g=2, 4, 8) or varying kernel size (k=3, 7) impacted performance. The default setting (g=1, k=5) generally performed better or comparably. Notably, a smaller kernel size (k=3) resulted in a substantially lower success rate (40.22% ± 20.19%).
Table III compared different deployment back-ends and the impact of collision checking on inference time. GPU deployment was consistently faster than CPU-ONNX, and disabling collision checking reduced inference time but also decreased the reported "success" (as collision-free is part of success definition).

#### 4.4. D. GASP in Real-World Table Tennis

GASP was deployed as a reset planner within a multi-step reinforcement learning agent for a robotic table tennis system.
- **Collision Reduction and Task Performance:** As shown in Figure 9, GASP significantly reduced the fraction of colliding trajectory segments by approximately 50% during RL training, compared to a baseline planner. This collision reduction was achieved without degrading the agent's task performance; GASP-trained policies reached a return rate (0.858) comparable to the baseline (0.873).
- **Real-Time Deployment:** GASP operated at the robot control frequency on the same GPU as the perception and policy stack, generating collision-free joint-space reset trajectories for new tactical targets in real-time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GASP represents a notable advancement in real-time motion planning for robotic manipulation, particularly for applications requiring high-frequency, collision-aware trajectory generation in known environments. Its significance stems from the successful integration of several methodological choices that collectively address existing challenges in the field.

The core design principle of combining a boundary-conditioned clamped B-spline trajectory representation with a lightweight convolutional residual neural network offers several advantages. By analytically enforcing initial and final derivative constraints, GASP inherently guarantees smooth trajectories that meet specified boundary conditions. This approach also simplifies the learning task for the neural network, allowing it to focus on predicting only the unconstrained interior control points, where most feasibility issues arise. This focus contributes to the model's efficiency and accuracy in refining the trajectory for safety and dynamic feasibility. The ablation studies validate that this comprehensive architectural design, including convolutional feature extraction, latent sampling, and curriculum-based training, contributes to the highest aggregate feasibility.

The GPU-accelerated and batched nature of GASP is a key impact area. By performing latent sampling, trajectory decoding, joint limit checking, and collision checking in parallel on the GPU, GASP achieves near-millisecond inference times. This parallelization allows the generation and validation of multiple trajectory candidates per query, significantly increasing the probability of finding a feasible solution without a proportional increase in latency. This speed and parallelism make GASP highly suitable for online deployment, especially within GPU-based reinforcement learning pipelines where low latency and deterministic runtimes are critical for efficient training and inference. The experimental results, showing success rates comparable to or exceeding analytical solutions with collision awareness, and substantially faster inference than GPU-based trajectory optimization, underscore this advantage.

Furthermore, GASP’s feasibility-driven training approach, which uses differentiable penalties for various constraints (joint limits, collisions, smoothness), removes the need for expert trajectory supervision. This simplifies data generation and makes the planner more adaptable to new environments or robot configurations by focusing on constraint satisfaction rather than mimicking pre-recorded motions. The robust performance under non-stationary initial conditions (non-zero velocity and acceleration), a common challenge for many optimization-based planners, demonstrates GASP’s practical applicability in dynamic scenarios.

The successful real-world deployment of GASP as an online reset planner in a competitive robotic table tennis system highlights its practical utility. Its ability to reduce collisions during RL training by approximately 50% while maintaining comparable task performance demonstrates its potential to enhance safety and efficiency in complex robotic learning tasks. This outcome suggests that GASP can serve as a valuable component in higher-level learning and control loops, facilitating safer and more stable robot operations.

Potential future impacts include the extension of GASP to multi-robot generalization, the incorporation of richer and more dynamic obstacle representations, and the development of learned objective-weight scheduling to adapt the planning behavior to different task priorities. These developments could broaden GASP's applicability to more complex and unstructured environments, further advancing the state of real-time, collision-aware motion planning.
