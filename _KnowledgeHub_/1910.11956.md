# Relay Policy Learning: Solving Long-Horizon Tasks via Imitation and Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Relay Policy Learning (RPL) combines imitation and reinforcement learning within a hierarchical framework to solve multi-stage, long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. It leverages unstructured 'play' demonstrations to initialize policies, then refines them with reinforcement learning, achieving higher success rates on complex kitchen tasks compared to non-hierarchical or from-scratch methods.

## Problem

- Applying reinforcement learning to multi-stage, long-horizon robotic tasks is challenging due to issues like sparse rewards and difficult exploration in large state-action spaces.
- Traditional imitation learning often requires carefully segmented or labeled demonstrations, which are expensive to collect and can suffer from compounding errors when executed in novel states.
- Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) methods have struggled with practical hurdles such as defining meaningful subgoals, effective exploration, and segmenting skills in complex scenarios.

## Method

- A bi-level hierarchical policy architecture is used, where a high-level policy outputs subgoals every H steps, and a low-level policy executes primitive actions towards the subgoal at every time step.
- Relay Imitation Learning (RIL) pre-trains these policies using 'relay data relabeling' on unstructured demonstrations to automatically generate goal-conditioned supervision for both high-level subgoals and low-level actions.
- Relay Reinforcement Fine-tuning (RRF) refines RIL-initialized policies through goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, incorporating off-policy data relabeling and regularization to maintain consistency with demonstrations.

## Results

- Relay Imitation Learning (RIL) achieved an average step completion score of 2.4 out of 4 and a 21.7% full success rate from unstructured demonstrations, outperforming flat behavior cloning baselines.
- Reinforcement learning fine-tuning (RRF) substantially improved policy performance, yielding significantly higher success rates on 17 different compound goals compared to RIL-only, from-scratch HRL, and flat policy fine-tuning baselines.
- The method successfully distilled individually fine-tuned policies for multiple tasks into a single multi-task goal-conditioned policy with only a slight performance drop, demonstrating generalization across complex sequences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unstructured 'play' demonstrations can be effectively leveraged for hierarchical policy pre-training through novel data relabeling schemes, avoiding the need for meticulously segmented data.
> - Initializing hierarchical policies with imitation learning from demonstrations significantly improves exploration and sample efficiency for subsequent reinforcement learning in complex, long-horizon tasks.
> - A fixed-horizon low-level policy combined with goal-conditioned learning simplifies hierarchical policy design and makes it more robust to fine-tuning compared to methods requiring variable-length segmentation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements with reinforcement learning (RL), yet its application has largely been confined to short-horizon, relatively simple tasks. Addressing multi-stage, long-horizon problems remains a substantial challenge. This paper positions itself at the intersection of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), Imitation Learning (IL), and Goal-Conditioned RL, aiming to overcome the limitations that have historically hindered their scalability to complex robotic scenarios.

Traditional HRL methods, while conceptually appealing for temporal abstraction, have faced practical hurdles such as effective exploration in large state-action spaces, the difficulty of defining meaningful subgoals or segmenting skills, and sparse reward signals. Pure imitation learning, on the other hand, can effectively learn from expert demonstrations but often suffers from compounding errors when executed in novel states not encountered in the training data (the "distribution shift" problem). Moreover, collecting high-quality, segmented, and task-specific demonstrations is often expensive and burdensome.

This work directly addresses these challenges by proposing a novel synthesis. It acknowledges the strengths of both IL (for bootstrapping complex behaviors) and RL (for continuous improvement and robust adaptation). Instead of requiring perfectly structured or segmented demonstrations, it leverages *unstructured and unsegmented "play" demonstrations*. This is a significant practical advantage, as such data is far easier to collect. The core innovation lies in making the imitation learning phase highly "amenable" to subsequent RL fine-tuning, a point where many hierarchical imitation learning (HIL) approaches have traditionally fallen short, as they primarily focus on extracting skill primitives without considering their future learnability via interaction.

Relay Policy Learning (RPL) builds upon prior work in goal-conditioned RL, which has shown promise in improving sample efficiency and generalization by allowing agents to learn to reach diverse states as goals. By applying goal-conditioning in a hierarchical manner and introducing a unique "relay data relabeling" scheme, RPL simplifies the credit assignment problem inherent in long-horizon tasks. It contrasts with purely "from scratch" HRL methods, which struggle with exploration, and with flat (non-hierarchical) IL+RL methods, which might not scale as effectively to long-horizon problems due to the complexity of a single, monolithic policy. Essentially, RPL seeks to find a sweet spot, using the structure of hierarchy and the guidance of demonstrations to bootstrap learning, then refining it with interaction in a way that minimizes common pain points.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and scalable method for solving multi-stage, long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

- **Bridging the Gap for Long-Horizon Tasks:** While RL has excelled in short-horizon problems, applying it to complex, sequential robotic tasks (e.g., opening multiple cabinets, moving objects, and turning switches in a specific order) remains challenging due to issues like sparse rewards and exploration. RPL aims to provide a practical solution for these "temporally extended" problems.
- **Leveraging Unstructured Demonstrations:** A significant practical bottleneck in robot learning is the collection of expert data. Traditional imitation learning often requires carefully segmented or labeled demonstrations, which is arduous. The authors motivate their work by aiming to leverage more accessible, "unstructured and unsegmented" human demonstrations (e.g., general "play" in an environment) to provide an initial behavioral foundation.
- **Improving Amenability to RL Fine-tuning:** Pure imitation learning often struggles with compounding errors and distribution shifts, limiting its direct applicability to complex tasks where perfect demonstrations are rare. The motivation is to design an imitation learning approach that specifically facilitates further improvement through reinforcement learning, allowing policies to adapt and overcome initial imperfections.
- **Simplifying Hierarchical Learning:** HRL has its own set of complexities, particularly regarding skill segmentation and reward definition for different levels of hierarchy. The authors aim to simplify this problem by introducing a novel "relay data relabeling" algorithm that implicitly handles these issues, allowing the low-level policy to act for a fixed number of steps regardless of the specific goal achieved. This fixed-horizon approach simplifies the design and training.
- **Generalizability and Scalability:** The objective is to create a general-purpose algorithm applicable across various tasks, capable of learning diverse, goal-conditioned behaviors in challenging environments, ultimately leading to a single, multi-task policy that can achieve multiple complex goals.

In essence, the core motivation is to make the learning of complex, long-horizon robot tasks more practical and robust by intelligently combining the data-efficiency of imitation learning with the adaptability of reinforcement learning, all while minimizing the burden on human demonstrators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Relay Policy Learning (RPL) is a two-phase approach combining imitation and reinforcement learning within a hierarchical framework.

#### 3.1. Relay Policy Architecture

RPL employs a bi-level hierarchical policy:
- **High-level policy ($\pi_h$):** Takes the current state ($s_t$) and a long-term high-level goal ($s_h^g$) and outputs a *subgoal* ($s_l^g$) for the low-level policy. This decision is made every $H$ time steps (e.g., 30 steps).
- **Low-level policy ($\pi_l$):** Takes the current state ($s_t$) and the subgoal ($s_l^g$) commanded by the high-level policy, and outputs a primitive *action* ($a_t$) to be executed in the environment. This policy operates at every single time step.
This structure introduces temporal abstraction, allowing the high-level to operate at a coarser granularity.

#### 3.2. Relay Imitation Learning (RIL)

This initial phase pre-trains the hierarchical policies using a pool of unstructured, unlabeled "play" demonstrations (D). The key innovation here is "relay data relabeling," which automatically generates goal-conditioned supervision from these demonstrations without requiring explicit human labeling or segmentation.

- **Data Preparation:** From the raw demonstration trajectories $\tau = \{s_0, a_0, \dots, s_T, a_T\}$, two datasets are constructed: $D_l$ for the low-level and $D_h$ for the high-level.
 - **Low-level Relabeling (Algorithm 2):** For every state $s_t$ in a demonstration, and for every action $a_t$, the low-level goal $s_l^g$ is relabeled as any state $s_{t+w}$ that is reached within a fixed window $W_l$ steps (e.g., $W_l=30$). This generates tuples $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+w})$ for $D_l$. This allows the low-level policy to learn to reach any state *near* its current state within a short horizon.
 - **High-level Relabeling (Algorithm 3):** Similarly, for the high-level, a window $W_h$ (e.g., $W_h=260$) is used. A high-level goal $s_h^g$ is chosen as a state $s_{t+w}$ within this window. The "action" for the high-level policy (which is a subgoal for the low-level) is selected as $s_{t+\min(W_l, j)}$, where $j$ is the step difference to the high-level goal. This forces the high-level to select an intermediate subgoal that the low-level can reasonably achieve within its horizon, moving towards the distant high-level goal.
- **Policy Training:** Both $\pi_l$ and $\pi_h$ are trained via supervised learning (Behavior Cloning) by maximizing the likelihood of actions (or subgoals for $\pi_h$) given the relabeled states and goals from $D_l$ and $D_h$. This pre-training initializes the policies, making them proficient at reaching nearby states and setting appropriate intermediate subgoals.

#### 3.3. Relay Reinforcement Fine-tuning (RRF)

This phase improves the RIL-initialized policies through environment interaction and reinforcement learning.
- **Goal-Conditioned HRL:** A variant of Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) with adaptive step size is used for optimization.
 - **Low-level optimization:** The low-level policy $\pi_l$ is trained to maximize a reward function $r_l(s_t, a_t, s_l^g)$ for reaching its commanded subgoal $s_l^g$.
 - **High-level optimization:** The high-level policy $\pi_h$ is trained to maximize a reward function $r_h(s_t, s_l^g, s_h^g)$ for selecting subgoals $s_l^g$ that lead to reaching the high-level goal $s_h^g$.
- **Incorporating Demonstrations:** RRF leverages demonstrations in two ways:
 1. **Initialization:** The policies are initialized with the effective RIL-trained policies.
 2. **Regularization:** The policy gradient objective for both levels is augmented with a maximum likelihood term that encourages the policies to stay close to the behaviors shown in the original relabeled demonstration pools ($D_l, D_h$). This combines the strengths of RL (exploration and adaptation) with the efficiency of IL (guidance).
- **Off-policy Data Leveraging (IRIL-RPL variant):** Trajectories collected during RL fine-tuning, even if they don't achieve their original commanded goals, can be relabeled using the same "relay data relabeling" scheme. These relabeled "off-policy" experiences are then added to the demonstration buffers ($D_l, D_h$), further increasing data efficiency and allowing the policies to learn from states they naturally explore during RL.
- **Multi-task Policy Learning:** To handle multiple complex tasks, policies are fine-tuned individually for each high-level goal. Subsequently, all these fine-tuned policies are distilled into a single, multi-task goal-conditioned policy, avoiding the challenges of multi-task optimization with policy gradients.

The overall approach intelligently combines the benefits of hierarchical structure, goal-conditioning, demonstration bootstrapping, and reinforcement learning, with a particular focus on simplifying data collection and improving learnability for long-horizon tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in a challenging MuJoCo-based kitchen simulation environment featuring a 9 DoF Franka robot interacting with various objects (microwave, oven burners, kettle, cabinets). The tasks involved multi-stage "compound goals," each requiring the manipulation of four distinct elements in sequence (e.g., open microwave, move kettle, turn on light, slide cabinet). The authors collected 400 sequences of unstructured and unsegmented human "play" demonstrations. Policy performance was evaluated using a "step-completion score" (out of 4) and overall success rate for fully achieved tasks.

#### 4.1. Relay Imitation Learning from Unstructured Demonstrations

- Even in the pure imitation learning phase, RIL demonstrated superior performance. It achieved an average step completion score of 2.4 (out of 4) and a 21.7% full success rate, significantly outperforming flat behavior cloning (BC) variants (with or without data relabeling), which scored lower in both metrics (e.g., flat GCBC with relabeling: 2.2 steps, 8.8% success). This indicates that the hierarchical structure and relay data relabeling provide benefits for learning complex behaviors even before RL fine-tuning.

#### 4.2. Relay Reinforcement Fine-tuning of Imitation Learning Policies

- **Significant Improvement with RRF:** All variants of RPL (NPG-RPL, DAPG-RPL, IRIL-RPL) showed substantial improvements in average success rate and step-completion scores across 17 different compound goals after RL fine-tuning. This highlights the critical role of environment interaction in refining the initially imperfect imitation policies.
- **Superior to From-Scratch HRL:** When compared against an on-policy variant of HIRO (a hierarchical RL algorithm learning from scratch), RPL learned much faster and achieved considerably higher success rates. This underscores the significant advantage of using demonstrations for effective policy initialization in complex environments.
- **Outperformance over Flat Policy Fine-tuning:** RPL consistently outperformed flat policy fine-tuning baselines (e.g., DAPG-BC, DAPG-GCBC). The authors attribute this to the hierarchical structure's ability to simplify credit assignment and reward specification, making the RL problem more tractable.
- **Advantage over Pre-trained Low-Level Baselines:** RPL also surpassed methods that only pre-train low-level primitives from demonstrations and learn the high-level from scratch. This suggests that effective high-level guidance, also initialized from demonstrations, is crucial for navigating the goal space.
- **Robustness against Oracle Segmentation:** RPL performed better than a hierarchical imitation learning baseline that used hand-specified, variable-length oracle segmentation. This indicates that RPL's fixed-length window and non-segmentation approach is more robust and amenable to fine-tuning, as variable-length segments can make exploration more challenging.
- **Closed-Loop vs. Open-Loop:** RPL significantly outperformed a "Nearest Neighbor" baseline that simply executes open-loop actions from the closest demonstration, emphasizing the necessity of learning a closed-loop, adaptive policy.
- **Multi-task Policy Distillation:** While fine-tuning individual policies for each task achieved the highest success, these could be distilled into a single, multi-task goal-conditioned policy with only a slight drop in performance, demonstrating the method's capability for generalization across multiple long-horizon tasks.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Analysis

- **Role of Window Size:** Experiments showed that smaller low-level window sizes ($W_l$) led to better performance for both imitation learning and RL fine-tuning. As the window size increased, the learning problem became more challenging, highlighting the trade-off between temporal abstraction and subtask complexity.
- **Reward Function for Fine-tuning:** Comparing sparse, Euclidean distance, and element-wise sparse rewards, the sparse reward function proved most effective during fine-tuning. This suggests that once exploration is sufficiently guided (by the RIL initialization), sparse rewards are less prone to local optima than denser alternatives.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Relay Policy Learning (RPL) presents a significant advancement in robot learning, particularly for complex, long-horizon tasks, by effectively integrating and enhancing both imitation and reinforcement learning within a hierarchical framework. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Complex Robotic Behaviors:** RPL directly tackles the notorious challenge of learning multi-stage, temporally extended tasks in robotics. By demonstrating high success rates on challenging manipulation sequences in a realistic kitchen environment, it pushes the frontier of what autonomous robots can achieve. This opens doors for robots to perform more intricate chores, assembly, or interactive tasks in unstructured environments.
- **Reducing Data Collection Burden:** One of the most impactful contributions is its ability to leverage *unstructured and unsegmented* demonstrations. This "play data" is far easier and less costly to collect than meticulously labeled, task-specific, or segmented demonstrations. This accessibility of data can significantly accelerate research and development in robot learning, moving towards systems that can learn from natural human interaction.
- **Robust and Amenable Learning:** The method's design ensures that the initial imitation learning phase produces policies that are highly "amenable" to further improvement via reinforcement learning. This robustness addresses the long-standing issue of compounding errors in pure imitation learning and makes the learned policies more adaptive to novel situations and environmental variations. The fixed-horizon low-level policies simplify reward design and exploration for the RL phase.
- **Advancing Hierarchical RL and IL:** RPL contributes to the theoretical and practical understanding of combining hierarchical structures with learning from demonstrations. It shows how intelligent data relabeling can unlock the potential of unstructured data for hierarchical skill acquisition, surpassing previous methods that either struggled with exploration (from-scratch HRL) or lacked fine-tuning capabilities (traditional HIL).
- **Practical Applicability for Real-World Robotics:** The design choices (e.g., multi-task policy distillation, the use of sparse rewards in fine-tuning) indicate a strong focus on practical deployment. The success in simulation suggests a promising path towards applying this method to physical robots, where data efficiency and robust performance are paramount. The ability to distil multiple fine-tuned policies into a single, compact multi-task policy is also crucial for memory and inference efficiency on robot hardware.

In future work, the authors plan to explore generalization to even longer sequences and extrapolation beyond the demonstration data, as well as integrating off-policy RL algorithms for further data efficiency on physical robots. These directions underscore RPL's potential to evolve into an even more powerful and versatile framework for autonomous robot learning, bridging the gap between current laboratory capabilities and real-world complex applications.
