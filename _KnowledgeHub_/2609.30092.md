# Self-Adaptive VLA for Robust Robot Deployment

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Adaptive VLA is a post-training recipe that lets a flow-matching VLA compensate deployment-time hardware shifts by conditioning on its own failed rollouts. Expert demonstrations are relabelled with pre-compensated actions, a lightweight plug-in context encoder compresses each rollout into one token that modulates the policy through adaptive layer normalization, and tokens from several trials are summed. It recovers over 80% of nominal performance.

## Problem

- VLA policies are memoryless or see only a short context window, so they repeat identical failures and cannot notice deployment-time hardware shifts from wear or imperfect calibration.
- Prior context-extension and memory methods, such as gated cross-attention memory or in-context imitation, target short-term recall rather than identifying and compensating physical hardware offsets.
- Rapid-adaptation modules for locomotion and in-hand rotation rely on simulation-trained proprioceptive histories, and no prior method reuses a policy's own real failure rollouts across trials.

## Method

- ==Contextualized expert data== construction: roll out the frozen base policy under injected actuation-bias or encoder-offset shifts, then pair each sub-optimal rollout with expert demonstrations whose actions and proprioception are pre-compensated for that shift.
- **Plug-in context encoder**: ==DINOv3== ViT tokens from three cameras plus MLP-projected state and action tokens, fused by two ==RoPE self-attention== blocks and attention pooling into one token added to the DiT's ==AdaLN== timestep conditioning.
- Context-token ensembling: tokens from successive trials are summed linearly before modulation, trained with the base policy's ==conditional flow-matching== loss while the VLM backbone stays frozen, giving zero inference overhead.

## Results

- Across four bi-manual and dexterous tasks, shifts cut base success from **88.8%** to **7.5%** and **5.0%**; one trial of context restores **45.0%** and **46.3%**, six-trial ensembling reaches **72.5%** and **75.0%**.
- Ensembled recovery ratios of **80%** and **84%** beat Gated Memory Policy's **28%** and **43%**, which plateaus after two trials, and shift-data fine-tuning alone, which only reaches **10.0%** and **15.0%**.
- Context tokens span a subspace matching the six shift axes: six components carry **99.8%** of variance, opposite signs give cosine near **-0.99**; dropping proprioception cuts actuation-bias success from **70%** to **45%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditioning on a policy's own failures turns a memoryless controller into an iterative estimator: repeated trials disambiguate shifts that look identical from a single rollout.
> - Training a context encoder on a low-dimensional, physically parameterized shift family yields a near-linear latent space, so plain vector summation suffices for aggregating evidence.
> - Static context tokens buy zero-overhead adaptation but forbid in-trial correction, so the recipe needs environments where an initial failing rollout is safe to collect.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot that works on a calibrated bench can fail when it is moved to another workstation or when its hardware drifts. The same command may produce a different movement, or an encoder may report the wrong joint position. Matching deployment hardware to training hardware takes calibration work that is difficult to repeat across many robots. The authors take a different approach: a VLA policy uses its own failed attempts as evidence about the shift, rather than requiring on-site recalibration.

The project page describes an Assemble Ring task with a 20-DoF dexterous hand under an unknown joint encoder offset. Trial 0 fails at grasping, and Trial 1 fails at assembly. In Trial 2, conditioned on Trials 0 and 1, the robot succeeds.

### 2. How a failed attempt becomes training context

The method, Self-Adaptive VLA, is applied after training and works in two parts. First, the authors inject known hardware shifts and collect rollouts from the frozen base policy. Second, they take existing human expert demonstrations and compensate the expert actions for the sampled shift. For a shift that also affects sensing, they transform the expert proprioception too. Each adjusted expert target is paired with a shifted rollout as context. The rollout provides evidence about the shift. The desired behavior comes from expert data, never from the sub-optimal rollout itself.

A context encoder compresses a history of camera views, robot state (proprioception), and actions into one context token. That token is added to the policy's diffusion-timestep conditioning before adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN), which modulates the action-generating network. At deployment, the encoder computes a token after each trial, and tokens from multiple trials are summed to condition the next attempt. Once a token is computed, the method adds zero additional computation in the high-frequency closed-loop control cycle.

Method overview. (a) Base expert data, shift-injected rollouts, and the pairing of rollout context with shift-compensated expert data. (b) Multimodal context becomes a token that modulates DiT blocks through AdaLN. (c) Tokens from successive trials are summed before conditioning the next policy run.

### 3. What the comparisons show

The evaluation uses two metrics:

- **Success rate** is the fraction of evaluation episodes in which the task is completed in up to six trials. An episode counts as successful if any one trial completes it.
- **Performance recovery** is the gain over the shifted base policy divided by the gap between that policy's shifted and nominal success rates.

Each method is tested on 20 evaluation episodes. Each episode has an unseen hardware shift, and the scene is reset between trials.

On Assemble Ring under joint encoder offset, the nominal base success is 95%. The ensembled Self-Adaptive VLA reaches 90% success and 93% recovery, versus 20% success for the memoryless base policy. Across four precision-critical tasks (Transport Corn, Insert Tube, Cap Marker, and Assemble Ring), the ensemble's average recovery ratio is 80% for actuation bias and 84% for joint encoder offset. These are task-average recovery ratios, not success rates.

A second comparison asks whether shifted data alone is enough. A "Base + Shift Data" baseline is fine-tuned on shifted rollouts but lacks the context encoder. It performs poorly on average, with 3% recovery for actuation bias and 12% for joint encoder offset.

### 4. From one station to another

The authors also tested Assemble Ring on two uncalibrated workstations. The base policy's success is 95% on the training workstation, 70% on New Workstation #1, and 10% on New Workstation #2. Self-Adaptive VLA, with up to two context trials, scores 95%, 90%, and 50%, respectively. These results show more robust performance on new stations, not full recovery on both.

### 5. Scope

In this approach, a failed attempt becomes information the policy carries into its next attempt, while an expert demonstration defines the behavior it is trying to recover. The authors frame this as a route to iterative adaptation without extra computation in the high-frequency control loop. It does not eliminate all adaptation costs or the need for a safe first attempt. Several limits apply:

- The context token is static during a trial.
- The evaluated shifts are actuation bias and joint encoder offsets.
- The authors say the current framework is limited to shifts modeled in robot configuration space.
