# Fleet-DAgger: Interactive Robot Fleet Learning with Scalable Human Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley introduced Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL), a framework where multiple robots learn from multiple human supervisors to continuously improve their policies. They developed Fleet-DAgger algorithms, including the C.U.R. method, which prioritize robots for human intervention, achieving up to 8.8x higher Return on Human Effort (ROHE) and increased task successes in large-scale simulations and physical experiments.

## Problem

- Efficiently scaling human supervision for continually improving robot policies in large commercial and industrial robot fleets (N robots, M humans, N > M).
- Existing interactive learning methods primarily focus on single-robot, single-human scenarios and do not address the complex supervisor allocation problem for multi-robot fleets.
- Lack of a formal framework and standardized benchmark for multi-robot, multi-human interactive learning.

## Method

- Formalized Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL), defining the supervisor allocation problem and introducing Return on Human Effort (ROHE) as a metric for human supervision efficiency.
- Developed **Fleet-DAgger**, a family of algorithms for IFL that utilize interactive imitation learning with various priority functions for human-to-robot allocation, including the novel C.U.R. (Constraint-Uncertainty-Risk) algorithm.
- Created the open-source Interactive Fleet Learning Benchmark (IFLB) on NVIDIA Isaac Gym, featuring environments for complex robotic tasks and a modular architecture for algorithm evaluation.

## Results

- C.U.R. consistently outperformed baselines in large-scale simulations (100 robots, 10 humans), achieving higher cumulative successes and up to **8.8x** greater ROHE across Humanoid, Anymal, and AllegroHand tasks.
- In real-world experiments with 4 ABB YuMi robots and 2 human supervisors, C.U.R. demonstrated higher ROHE, more successful task completions, and fewer hard resets compared to baseline methods.
- Sensitivity analyses revealed that both very short and very long human intervention times negatively impact ROHE, and that an optimal number of human supervisors exists beyond which returns diminish due to superfluous interventions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Intelligent supervisor allocation is critical for maximizing both immediate fleet task throughput and long-term policy improvement, significantly impacting overall system performance and human efficiency.
> - The Return on Human Effort (ROHE) metric effectively quantifies the trade-off between fleet performance gains and human intervention costs, serving as a robust objective for IFL algorithm optimization.
> - Hierarchical prioritization of robots based on constraint violations, uncertainty, and risk (C.U.R.), combined with an initial learning period, allows for more effective collection of valuable teleoperation data and reduces unnecessary hard resets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid growth of commercial and industrial robot fleet deployments (e.g., Amazon, Waymo, Zoox) has necessitated robust mechanisms for handling situations where robots encounter difficulties or risks. A common solution is to integrate remote human teleoperators who can intervene and guide robots when autonomous policies fail. This paper addresses a critical, yet previously underexplored, aspect of such deployments: how to effectively use these human interventions not just for immediate problem-solving, but for *continual learning* to improve the robot fleet's policies over time.

The existing research landscape can be broadly categorized:

- **Supervisory Control and Sliding Autonomy (Execution-Time Allocation):** A significant body of work focuses on how humans manage robot fleets during *execution*, where the robot policies are typically fixed. This includes "sliding autonomy," "adjustable autonomy," or "adaptive automation," which study dynamic control transfer between humans and robots. Key challenges here involve managing human workload ("fan-out"), optimizing human-robot team performance, and efficient task allocation in multi-robot systems. However, these works largely assume fixed robot control policies and do not integrate human interventions for *policy learning*.
- **Single-Robot, Single-Human Interactive Learning:** This area, often associated with imitation learning (IL) and interactive reinforcement learning (RL), explores how a single robot can learn from a single human supervisor. Algorithms like DAgger have been developed to mitigate distribution shift in IL by actively querying a human for demonstrations. To reduce human burden, "robot-gated" approaches (e.g., SafeDAgger, EnsembleDAgger, ThriftyDAgger, LazyDAgger) enable the robot to decide when to request help based on metrics like uncertainty, novelty, or risk. While effective for individual robots, these approaches do not scale to large fleets or consider the allocation of *multiple* humans across *multiple* robots.
- **Multi-Robot Learning without Human Supervision:** Recent advancements, particularly with platforms like NVIDIA Isaac Gym, have enabled massively parallel simulation of multiple robots for accelerated reinforcement learning. These works leverage the computational power of GPUs to train policies for large robot groups. However, they generally do not involve human supervision or address how human intervention could be integrated for policy improvement.
- **Limited Multi-Robot, Single-Human Interactive Learning:** A few works, such as Swamy et al. (2020), have explored the multi-robot, *single-human* problem, focusing on learning human preferences to assist a single operator in supervising a small fleet. While a step towards fleet learning, it still does not address the multi-human, large-fleet scenario.

**The Gap Addressed:**
This paper identifies and addresses a critical gap: the formalization and algorithmic solution for **Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL)**, where *multiple robots* interactively query and learn from *multiple human supervisors*. This setting introduces a novel "supervisor allocation problem" where the goal is to maximize fleet throughput not just during execution, but also by improving the underlying robot policy through efficient allocation of limited human attention. The work aims to bridge the gap between large-scale robot learning and the practical need for human oversight and continuous policy improvement in real-world deployments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of robot fleets in commercial and industrial settings, where human teleoperators are indispensable for handling exceptions and ensuring task progress. The authors recognize that these human interventions represent a valuable source of data that can be harnessed for continual policy improvement, transforming reactive assistance into proactive learning.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Formalize Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL):** To provide the first comprehensive mathematical framework for multi-robot, multi-human interactive learning, defining the problem setting, the roles of robots and humans, and the dynamics of policy updates.
2. **Define a Scalable Evaluation Metric:** To introduce a new metric, **Return on Human Effort (ROHE)**, that effectively quantifies the efficiency of IFL algorithms. ROHE measures the cumulative reward of the robot fleet normalized by the total human effort expended, thus directly reflecting the productivity gains relative to human cost.
3. **Develop a Family of Scalable IFL Algorithms:** To propose **Fleet-DAgger**, a family of algorithms designed for supervisor allocation in the IFL setting. These algorithms build upon interactive imitation learning principles and prioritize robots for human intervention based on various criteria.
4. **Create an Open-Source IFL Benchmark (IFLB):** To provide a standardized, open-source software toolkit and benchmark environments built on NVIDIA Isaac Gym. This benchmark is crucial for reproducible research, efficient development, and comparative evaluation of new IFL algorithms for complex robotic tasks.
5. **Validate Algorithms through Extensive Experiments:** To rigorously evaluate the proposed Fleet-DAgger algorithms, including a novel one named C.U.R., through large-scale simulation experiments (100 robots) and real-world physical experiments (4 robot arms, 2 human supervisors), demonstrating their effectiveness in maximizing ROHE and fleet performance.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to make human supervision of robot fleets not only reactive and reliable but also *scalable, efficient, and proactive* in driving continuous robot learning and performance improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors tackle the Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL) problem through a multi-pronged approach involving formalization, algorithm development, a standardized benchmark, and empirical validation.

#### 3.1. Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL) Formalization

The paper formally defines the IFL setting involving N robots and M human supervisors (N ≥ M).
- **Robot Environment:** Each robot operates as an independent Markov Decision Process (MDP) but shares a common, non-stationary policy (π_θ_t). Robots can enter "fault states" (constraint violations) from which they cannot progress without human intervention.
- **Human Supervisors:** Humans possess a shared policy (π_H) and can either teleoperate a robot (if c(s)=0) or perform a "hard reset" (if c(s)=1), which resets the robot to an initial state and incurs a fixed time cost (t_R). Humans can only assist one robot at a time.
- **Supervisor Allocation Problem:** The core of IFL is the allocation policy (ω) that assigns M humans to N robots at each timestep based on robot states, current robot policy, and ongoing interventions. This allocation directly influences both immediate task progress and the data collected for policy updates.
- **Return on Human Effort (ROHE):** A novel metric is introduced: ROHE = (Cumulative Fleet Reward) / (Total Human Time Spent). This metric directly quantifies the efficiency of human supervision in improving fleet performance, acting as the primary objective for optimizing allocation policies.
- **Policy Update:** Human intervention data (teleoperation, but not hard resets) is collected into a dataset (D_t_H) and used to continually update the robot policy (π_θ_t) via a policy update function (f), typically supervised learning.

#### 3.2. Fleet-DAgger Framework

Fleet-DAgger is presented as a family of IFL algorithms that utilize interactive imitation learning with dataset aggregation (DAgger). Its core mechanism is an allocation policy `ω` based on a unique **priority function `ˆp`** and predefined minimum intervention times (`t_T` for teleoperation, `t_R` for hard resets).
- **Allocation Logic:** At each timestep, robots are scored by `ˆp`. Humans prioritize ongoing interventions until minimum times `t_T` or `t_R` expire. Available humans are then assigned to the highest-priority unassisted robots. Robots with `ˆp(·) = 0` do not receive assistance.

#### 3.3. Fleet-DAgger Algorithms (Baselines and Novel)

The paper proposes and evaluates several algorithms within the Fleet-DAgger framework, each defined by its priority function `ˆp`:

- **Constraint (C):** A baseline where robots only learn from initial offline data. Human supervision is limited to hard resets for constraint-violating robots (`ˆp(·)=1` if c(s)=1, else `0`).
- **Random:** A baseline that assigns random priorities to robots, with a threshold to control total human supervision.
- **Fleet-EnsembleDAgger (U.C.):** Adapts EnsembleDAgger. Prioritizes robots based on epistemic uncertainty (U-prioritization, using ensemble variance or Shannon entropy) combined with C-prioritization.
- **Fleet-ThriftyDAgger (U.G.C.):** Adapts ThriftyDAgger. Prioritizes based on a linear combination of uncertainty (U), estimated probability of task failure (G-prioritization, using a goal critic Q-function), and C-prioritization. Requires pretraining a goal critic.
- **Constraint-Uncertainty-Risk (C.U.R.):** The novel proposed algorithm. Prioritizes based on a hierarchical scheme: **C**onstraint violations first, then **U**ncertainty, then **R**isk (R-prioritization, defined as probability of constraint violation estimated by a safety critic Q-function). C.U.R. also includes an initial period (`t_I`) where constraint violations are *not* prioritized, allowing human effort to focus on learning valuable teleoperation data instead of immediate resets. Requires pretraining a safety critic.

#### 3.4. Interactive Fleet Learning Benchmark (IFLB)

To standardize research, the authors developed the IFLB, an open-source Python toolkit.
- **Environments:** Built on NVIDIA Isaac Gym, allowing GPU-accelerated simulation of hundreds/thousands of robots. It includes three high-dimensional, continuous state/action environments: Humanoid (bipedal locomotion), Anymal (quadruped locomotion), and AllegroHand (dexterous manipulation). Constraint violations are task-specific (e.g., falling down, dropping cube).
- **Software Architecture:** Modular design with interfaces for `agents` (robot policy), `supervisors` (human policy, e.g., trained RL agent or teleoperation), and `allocations` (priority functions). This allows flexible development and evaluation of new IFL algorithms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides compelling evidence for the efficacy of the IFL framework and the proposed C.U.R. algorithm through extensive simulation and physical experiments.

#### 4.1. IFLB Simulation Experiments (N=100 robots, M=10 humans)

Experiments were conducted across Humanoid, Anymal, and AllegroHand environments, using a trained PPO agent as the algorithmic supervisor (π_H) and initializing robot policies with behavior cloning.

- **Impact of Allocation:** The choice of IFL algorithm significantly affects all measured metrics (cumulative successes, hard resets, idle time, ROHE), underscoring that efficient human-to-robot allocation is critical in this setting.
- **C.U.R. Performance:** The novel C.U.R. algorithm consistently outperformed all baselines across all environments in terms of:
 - **Cumulative Successes:** Achieved higher total task completions.
 - **Return on Human Effort (ROHE):** Demonstrated up to 8.8× higher ROHE than baselines, indicating much more efficient use of human supervision. An increasing ROHE over time suggests effective policy learning that reduces the need for interventions.
 - **Hard Resets and Idle Time:** Generally incurred fewer hard resets and less idle time compared to baselines (especially the Constraint baseline, which struggles with a fixed low-performing policy).
- **AllegroHand Nuances:** For the challenging AllegroHand task, C.U.R. still showed higher ROHE and successes, but surprisingly incurred *more* hard resets and a smaller ROHE margin compared to some baselines. The authors hypothesize this is because prioritizing hard resets for an initially low-performing policy in a very hard task inadvertently creates more opportunities for constraint violations, rather than focusing on learning. The initial C.U.R. period (ignoring constraint violations) helps mitigate this.

#### 4.2. Physical Block-Pushing Experiment (N=4 ABB YuMi robots, M=2 humans)

A real-world experiment with human supervisors provided further validation. Robots learned to push a block to a goal, with humans performing teleoperation or physical hard resets.

- **C.U.R. Outperformance:** C.U.R. achieved higher ROHE, more cumulative successes, and lower hard resets and idle time compared to baselines.
- **Reduced Margin in Real-World:** The performance margins were smaller than in simulation. Hypothesized reasons include:
 - **Safety Critic Training Difficulty:** Training an accurate safety critic in high-dimensional image space is more challenging for real-world data, reducing the effectiveness of the risk component.
 - **Human Supervisor Behavior:** U-prioritization (uncertainty) may be less suitable for real-world human supervisors. Humans might arbitrarily choose one of several equally good actions, leading to high robot uncertainty that doesn't necessarily indicate a need for supervision, thus potentially generating less "useful" data for policy improvement compared to algorithmic supervisors.

#### 4.3. Insights from Ablation and Sensitivity Studies (Appendix)

The appendix provides detailed analysis:

- **Ablations of C.U.R.:** Showed that all components (Constraint, Uncertainty, Risk, and the initial period of not prioritizing constraints) contribute to C.U.R.'s overall performance, with C.U.R. generally outperforming its ablated versions. The initial period (C.U.R.(-i)) was particularly important for difficult tasks like AllegroHand.
- **Sensitivity to Number of Humans (M):** Increasing M generally improved cumulative successes, but there was a point of diminishing returns (e.g., M=25 vs. M=50 showing similar performance). M=10 often yielded the highest ROHE, suggesting an optimal allocation that avoids "superfluous interventions." A very low M (e.g., 1 human) led to high idle times and hard resets due to bottlenecked resets.
- **Sensitivity to Minimum Intervention Time (t_T):** Both very short (t_T=1) and very long (t_T=20+) intervention times negatively impacted ROHE. Too short interventions lead to premature control ceding, while too long interventions can be superfluous and provide less useful learning data (resembling offline data collection).
- **Sensitivity to Hard Reset Time (t_R):** As expected, increasing t_R consistently decreased ROHE, cumulative successes, and increased idle time, as more human effort was locked into unrewarding reset tasks.

In summary, the results highlight the critical role of intelligent supervisor allocation in interactive fleet learning, demonstrating that algorithms like C.U.R. can significantly boost fleet performance and human efficiency, even though real-world complexities can modulate these gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the fields of robotics, machine learning, and human-robot interaction, with substantial potential for impact on real-world robot deployments.

1. **First Formalization of Multi-Robot, Multi-Human Interactive Learning:** This paper introduces the novel and highly relevant problem setting of Interactive Fleet Learning (IFL). By formally defining the components, objectives, and dynamics of multi-robot, multi-human interactive learning, it provides a foundational framework for future research in this critical area.
2. **Novel and Effective Metric (ROHE):** The introduction of Return on Human Effort (ROHE) offers a practical and intuitive metric for evaluating the efficiency of interactive learning systems. This metric moves beyond mere task success to consider the cost of human supervision, which is crucial for commercial and industrial applications where human time is a valuable and limited resource.
3. **Scalable Algorithmic Framework (Fleet-DAgger) and Novel Algorithm (C.U.R.):** Fleet-DAgger provides a general framework for developing supervisor allocation strategies, and the proposed C.U.R. algorithm demonstrates superior performance in balancing task progress, learning, and human effort. Its hierarchical prioritization (Constraint, Uncertainty, Risk, with an initial learning phase) is a practical and well-motivated approach.
4. **Open-Source Benchmark (IFLB):** The release of the IFL Benchmark (IFLB), built on NVIDIA Isaac Gym, is a major contribution to the research community. It enables rapid prototyping, reproducible experiments, and standardized evaluation of IFL algorithms across complex, high-dimensional robotic tasks. This accelerates progress by lowering the barrier for entry and allowing researchers to build upon established foundations. The use of Isaac Gym highlights the capability to scale to thousands of robots in simulation, a critical factor for fleet learning.
5. **Empirical Validation with Scale and Real-World Experiments:** The extensive experiments, including large-scale simulations (100 robots) and physical robot experiments (4 ABB YuMi arms with human supervisors), provide strong empirical validation of the proposed methods. The physical experiments, in particular, demonstrate the practical applicability of the framework and algorithms, while also revealing nuances and challenges unique to human-in-the-loop systems in the real world.
6. **Practical Relevance for Industry:** The problem of efficiently supervising and continually improving robot fleets is directly relevant to companies deploying autonomous systems (e.g., Amazon, Waymo, Zoox). This work offers concrete strategies and a benchmark for optimizing human-robot team performance, reducing operational costs, and accelerating the deployment of more capable and reliable robot fleets.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards exciting avenues for future research:
- **Modeling Assumptions:** Homogeneous human supervisors, identical robot state/action spaces, independent robots, perfect human situational awareness, constant hard reset times, and synchronous timesteps simplify the problem. Relaxing these assumptions (e.g., learning from non-homogeneous supervisors, managing communication issues in large fleets) is crucial for even broader applicability.
- **Algorithmic Supervisors in Simulation:** The use of algorithmic supervisors in most simulations provides a controlled environment but might not fully capture the complexities of human behavior. Future work will involve more large-scale physical experiments with human supervisors.
- **Focus on Imitation Learning:** The current framework primarily uses imitation learning (DAgger). Extending IFL to other learning paradigms, such as reinforcement learning, is an important next step.

Overall, "Fleet-DAgger" lays critical groundwork for scalable, efficient, and interactive learning in multi-robot systems. By providing both a theoretical framework and practical tools, it is poised to significantly influence the design and deployment of future robot fleets.
