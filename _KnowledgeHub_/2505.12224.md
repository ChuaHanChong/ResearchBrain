# RoboFAC: A Comprehensive Framework for Robotic Failure Analysis and Correction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Harbin Institute of Technology developed RoboFAC, a framework combining a large-scale, failure-centric dataset and a lightweight multimodal model to diagnose errors and suggest corrections for robotic Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The framework achieved an average failure analysis score of 79.10%, surpassing GPT-4o (57.42%), and improved real-world task success rates by 29.1% while reducing inference latency by approximately 3x.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, trained on successful demonstrations, lack structured supervision for diagnosing and recovering from failures in complex or unstructured environments.
- General Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) exhibit reduced performance in specialized robotic manipulation due to a lack of fine-grained failure data and suffer from high computational requirements and latency for real-time deployment.
- Existing specialized datasets for robotic failure analysis offer limited scope, often focusing on simpler tasks, coarse-grained diagnostics, and lacking multi-level corrective strategies.

## Method

- Constructed the **RoboFAC** dataset, a large-scale, diverse, ==failure-centric== collection of **9,440** erroneous manipulation trajectories and **78,623** QA pairs, featuring a ==three-level hierarchical failure taxonomy== and ==multi-dimensional corrective annotations==.
- Developed the lightweight **RoboFAC** multimodal model, fine-tuned on the custom dataset to provide unified ==task understanding==, ==failure detection==, ==identification==, ==explanation==, and both high-level and low-level corrective suggestions.
- Integrated the **RoboFAC** model as an ==external critic== within real-world VLA control pipelines to enable ==real-time failure detection and recovery==, enhancing task success rates without retraining the base policy.

## Results

- RoboFAC-7B achieved an average failure analysis score of **79.10%**, outperforming GPT-4o (**57.42%**) and Gemini-2.0 (**51.11%**) across both simulated and real-world test sets.
- In real-world robotic manipulation tasks, RoboFAC-7B (Low-level correction) increased the final task success rate to **61.25%** (from **47.5%** without correction) and reduced inference latency by approximately **3x** (**6.7 +/- 0.5** seconds) compared to GPT-4o.
- RoboFAC models demonstrated strong multi-dimensional capacity, achieving high scores in task planning, low-level correction, and all failure-related abilities (detection, identification, locating, explanation), showcasing robustness in sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Domain-specific fine-tuning on a comprehensive failure dataset enables lightweight multimodal models to achieve superior performance in robotic failure analysis and correction compared to general-purpose proprietary MLLMs.
> - Low-level, action-oriented corrective instructions are more effective than high-level task adjustments for resolving real-world manipulation failures when guiding fine-tuned VLA policies.
> - A modular framework for failure analysis and correction can significantly enhance the robustness and recovery capabilities of VLA systems in dynamic environments, with practical deployment advantages due to reduced inference latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated advancements in robotic manipulation by interpreting natural language instructions and visual observations to generate control actions. These models, exemplified by approaches like RT-2 and π0, integrate multimodal perception with action generation, often leveraging large-scale pre-trained vision-language models for robotic control. Despite these developments, VLA models encounter limitations in complex, long-horizon, and unstructured environments.

A primary constraint is their reliance on datasets primarily composed of successful expert demonstrations. This training paradigm typically omits structured supervision for diagnosing failures and implementing recovery strategies, which limits the robustness of VLA models in scenarios where errors are inevitable. When a VLA model encounters an error, its inability to self-diagnose and re-plan can lead to cascading failures.

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), while possessing strong general reasoning capabilities, often exhibit reduced performance in specialized robotic manipulation domains. This limitation arises because general MLLMs are not typically trained on fine-grained robotic failure data. Furthermore, their substantial computational requirements and high API latency can impede real-time, on-device deployment critical for practical robotics.

Existing specialized datasets for robotic failure analysis, such as RoboFail, AHA, RACER, and Guardian datasets, have partially addressed these issues. However, these datasets are frequently characterized by simpler tasks, coarse-grained diagnostics, and a lack of multi-level corrective strategies, thus offering limited scope for comprehensive failure reasoning and recovery.

The RoboFAC framework addresses these limitations by providing a structured approach to robotic failure analysis and correction. It introduces a large-scale, failure-centric dataset with systematically categorized failure types and multi-level corrective strategies. Concurrently, it develops a lightweight multimodal model specifically tailored for robotic task understanding, failure analysis, and correction. This approach aims to bridge the gap between general VLA capabilities and the practical requirements for robust, real-time robotic deployment in environments prone to failures. By decoupling failure reasoning from primary execution, RoboFAC proposes a modular enhancement to VLA systems, without necessitating retraining of the base policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and recovery capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in dynamic and unstructured robotic manipulation environments. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified limitations within the current state of robotic control and multimodal AI.

One key motivation is the inherent fragility of existing VLA models when confronted with failures. These models are predominantly trained on datasets comprising successful task executions, which precludes them from learning how to diagnose or recover from errors. Consequently, in complex or open-world scenarios, even minor deviations can lead to task failure, with no inherent mechanism for self-correction. The framework aims to provide structured supervision for failure diagnosis and recovery, enabling VLA models to re-plan or adjust their actions once an error occurs, thereby preventing cascading breakdowns.

A second motivation stems from the inadequacies of general-purpose Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) in the specialized domain of robotics. While MLLMs demonstrate broad reasoning abilities, they often lack the fine-grained understanding required for intricate robotic manipulation failures. This domain-specific knowledge gap means they may misinterpret or fail to identify the root causes of errors in robotic tasks. Furthermore, the substantial size and computational demands of proprietary MLLMs result in high API latency, rendering them unsuitable for real-time, on-device deployment in practical robotic systems where quick responses are critical. The researchers are motivated to develop a specialized, lightweight model that can operate efficiently with low latency while maintaining competitive performance.

A third driving factor is the perceived limitations of existing datasets specifically designed for robotic failure analysis. Previous datasets tend to be restricted to simplistic tasks, offer only coarse-grained diagnostics, and often lack the comprehensive, multi-level corrective strategies necessary for nuanced robotic recovery. This limitation hinders the development of models capable of detailed failure explanation and targeted intervention. The objective is to construct a more comprehensive and diverse failure-centric dataset that captures a wider array of failure types, task complexities, and multi-dimensional corrective annotations.

Therefore, the key objectives of the RoboFAC framework are:
1. To construct a large-scale and diverse dataset (RoboFAC dataset) specifically designed for robotic failure analysis and correction, encompassing various task complexities, simulated and real-world environments, and a hierarchical taxonomy of failures, along with multi-dimensional question-answer annotations for comprehensive failure understanding and correction.
2. To develop a lightweight and deployable multimodal model (RoboFAC model) specialized for robotic failure video reasoning, capable of unified task understanding, failure diagnosis, and corrective suggestion.
3. To integrate this specialized model as an external critic within real-world VLA control pipelines to enable real-time failure detection and recovery, thereby enhancing task success rates and overall system robustness with reduced inference latency.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to provide a systematic and practical solution for enhancing the resilience of robotic manipulation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RoboFAC framework is developed through a two-pronged approach: the construction of a specialized dataset for robotic failure analysis and correction, and the development of a multimodal language model fine-tuned on this dataset for diagnostic and corrective reasoning.

#### 3.1. RoboFAC Dataset Construction

The RoboFAC dataset is designed to be large-scale, diverse, and failure-centric.
- **Scale and Diversity:** The dataset comprises 9,440 erroneous manipulation trajectories and 78,623 Question-Answer (QA) pairs across 53 distinct scenes. It includes data from both simulated and real-world environments. Tasks range from short-horizon to long-horizon and dynamic scenarios, encompassing 14 simulated and 6 real-world tasks (two of which are exclusive to the real-world environment). To enhance robustness to domain shifts, the dataset intentionally incorporates variations in backgrounds, object configurations, and camera viewpoints. In addition to failure trajectories, 1,160 successful simulated trajectories and 122 successful real-world trajectories were collected to prevent overfitting to failure patterns.
- **Failure Taxonomy:** A three-level hierarchical taxonomy, inspired by classic robotics literature and prior analyses, is established to categorize failures:
 - **Task Planning Error:** Errors at the highest level, related to incorrect task decomposition or language grounding. This includes "Step Omission" (a required substage is skipped) and "Wrong Object" (incorrect object manipulation).
 - **Motion Planning Error:** Failures arising from limited spatial reasoning or inaccurate mapping of instructions to poses. This includes "Position Deviation" (end-effector fails to reach the correct position) and "Orientation Deviation" (end-effector fails to reach the correct orientation).
 - **Execution Control Error:** Failures caused by physical imprecision, latency, or dynamic misalignment during interaction. This includes "Grasping Error" (improper or insufficient gripper closure) and "Timing Error" (subtask executed at an incorrect time).
- **Data Collection Pipeline:**
 - **Simulation Data:** Collected for 14 robotic tasks within the ManiSkill environment, augmented with objects from the YCB Object Dataset and scenes from ReplicaCAD/AI2-THOR. Expert policies were defined by specifying target end-effector poses for each substage. To generate failure data, the expert policy was deliberately modified with code snippets that introduce errors at selected substages. Each robotic failure video was recorded with a descriptive text detailing the substage of failure, failure taxonomy, and an explanation of the error, including perturbed end-effector poses when applicable.
 - **Real-World Data:** Collected for 6 tasks using teleoperation with a SO-100 robotic arm, alongside corresponding textual descriptions.
- **Data Annotation Pipeline:** Raw data was annotated to create video-based QA samples for eight distinct question types: task identification, task planning, failure detection, failure identification, failure locating, failure explanation, high-level correction, and low-level correction. Five unique question templates were designed for each type to ensure diversity. Reference answers for five types (task identification, task planning, failure detection, failure identification, and failure locating) were directly extracted from the textual descriptions. For the remaining three types (failure explanation, high-level correction, and low-level correction), GPT-4o was employed to generate reference answers using the video and textual description as inputs.
- **Quality Control Process:** A three-stage process was implemented:
 - **Simulation Validation:** Physical validity constraints (e.g., collision detection, trajectory discontinuity) were enforced to filter out spurious failures.
 - **LLM-Based Annotation Validation:** Annotations, structured as JSON outputs, were automatically parsed and validated against a predefined schema.
 - **Human Verification and Consistency:** A random 10% sample of the dataset was reviewed by two independent human annotators, who assigned quality scores (0-3). Krippendorff’s α, an ordinal distance metric, yielded a consistency score of 0.86, indicating high inter-annotator reliability. Samples with quality ratings of 0 or 1 were removed.

#### 3.2. RoboFAC Model Development

- **Architecture:** The RoboFAC model is built upon Qwen2.5-VL, an open-source multimodal model comprising an LLM backbone, a vision encoder, and an MLP-based vision-language merger.
- **Fine-tuning Strategy:** To bridge the domain gap between general VLMs and robotic manipulation, the visual encoder was frozen to preserve general visual representations, while the merger and LLM backbone were fine-tuned. This allowed the merger to learn robot-specific semantic alignment and the LLM backbone to integrate embodied visual signals into language reasoning.
- **Training Details:** RoboFAC-3B and RoboFAC-7B models were fine-tuned on 60K simulated QA pairs from the RoboFAC dataset for one epoch. The learning rate was set to 1 × 10⁻⁵. DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 offload strategy was used for memory optimization, with a batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation steps of 2 (for 3B) or 4 (for 7B). Training was conducted on 4 Nvidia GeForce RTX 4090 GPUs.
- **Capabilities:** The fine-tuned RoboFAC model is designed to perform:
 - **Task Understanding:** Identifying the robotic task and decomposing it into a sequence of substages.
 - **Failure Analysis:** Detecting task failure, identifying the failure type, locating the error substage, and providing detailed explanations.
 - **Failure Correction:** Generating both high-level (sequence of sub-tasks) and low-level (fine-grained end-effector movement direction) corrective suggestions.

#### 3.3. Real-world Integration and Evaluation

- **System Setup:** A physical evaluation system using a SO-100 robotic arm and the lerobot framework was employed. A GR00T-N1 VLA policy was fine-tuned on 100 teleoperated demonstrations per task.
- **Deployment Architecture:** A server-client architecture was used, with the RoboFAC correction model running on a dedicated server (or external API for GPT-4o) and the GR00T-N1 policy on a client device.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** During execution, the robotic arm pauses at a predefined timestamp, and a video segment is sent to the correction model. The generated textual instruction is then appended to the original prompt, and execution resumes. Each trial allowed for up to four correction rounds (five execution attempts total).
- **Metrics:** Success rates after the first and fourth correction rounds were measured. Latency, defined as the time from sending observations to receiving the corrective instruction, was also recorded.
- **Comparison Settings:** Five conditions were evaluated: No Correction, GPT-4o, untuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B, RoboFAC-7B (Low-level correction), and RoboFAC-7B (High-level correction).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated RoboFAC’s performance in failure analysis and real-world failure correction, comparing it against open-source baselines and proprietary models.

#### 4.1. Results on Failure Analysis Task

The RoboFAC models (RoboFAC-3B and RoboFAC-7B) were evaluated against Qwen2.5-VL-3B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Gemini-2.0, and GPT-4o on a test set comprising 10K simulated and 8K real-world QA pairs. The real-world test set included two tasks unseen during training.

- **Overall Performance:** RoboFAC-7B achieved an average failure analysis score of 79.10%, which represented a 34.1% improvement over GPT-4o (57.42%) and surpassed Gemini-2.0 (51.11%). RoboFAC-3B also demonstrated strong performance, achieving an average score of 76.80%. This indicates that the domain-specific training and model design contribute to superior performance.
- **Multi-Dimensional Capacity:** RoboFAC models exhibited strong capabilities across all eight diagnostic dimensions. They achieved high scores in task planning, low-level correction, and all failure-related abilities (detection, identification, locating, and explanation). For instance, in simulated data, RoboFAC-7B scored 99.91% for task identification, 66.21% for task planning, 99.78% for failure explanation, and 67.25% for low-level correction. In contrast, generalist models like GPT-4o, while competitive in aspects like failure detection, showed comparatively limited performance in task planning and hierarchical correction, suggesting a gap in their ability to perform complex, multi-step reasoning under physical constraints.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** RoboFAC models maintained performance on real-world robotic videos comparable to that observed in simulation across most task dimensions, consistently outperforming all baseline models in the majority of evaluation categories. Despite not being trained on real-world data, both RoboFAC-3B and RoboFAC-7B demonstrated robustness under domain shift. Generalist models occasionally achieved slightly higher scores in perception-oriented dimensions (e.g., failure detection, task identification) in real-world scenarios, attributed to their extensive pretraining on broad visual-language data. RoboFAC, optimized through domain-specific fine-tuning, prioritized action-centric reasoning over generic visual recognition, leading to superior performance in task planning and both high-level and low-level correction tasks.

#### 4.2. Results on Real-world Manipulation

Real-world experiments addressed the effectiveness of RoboFAC in correcting robotic errors and the impact of instruction granularity.

- **Effect of Fine-Tuned RoboFAC (Q1):** RoboFAC-7B (Low-level correction) achieved the highest final success rate of 61.25% after four correction rounds across four manipulation tasks (PlaceCube, PushCube, PullCubeTool, StackCube). This outperformed GPT-4o (56.25%), the untuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B (50.0%), and the "No Correction" baseline (47.5%). This validates the efficacy of fine-tuning on the RoboFAC dataset. Furthermore, RoboFAC-7B reduced inference latency by approximately 3× (6.7 ± 0.5 seconds) compared to GPT-4o (24.3 ± 3.4 seconds), indicating suitability for real-time robotic deployment.
- **Effect of Instruction Granularity (Q2):** Low-level (action-level) corrections consistently outperformed high-level (task-level) corrections. RoboFAC-7B (Low-level) achieved 61.25% success, while RoboFAC-7B (High-level) achieved 50.0% success after five attempts. This suggests that, for the fine-tuned VLA policy, most real-world failures originated from execution-level inaccuracies (e.g., imprecise grasp poses) rather than high-level task misunderstanding, making action-level guidance more effective.
- **Observation on Policy Behavior:** The VLA policy exhibited directional sensitivity to corrective language (e.g., adjusting towards a specific region). However, it showed limited sensitivity to precise quantitative constraints, indicating that fine-grained magnitude control via language remains a challenge for current language-conditioned VLA systems.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Examples

Qualitative examples (Figures 5, 6, 7 in the paper) further illustrated RoboFAC-7B's diagnostic capabilities. The model provided accurate and concise failure explanations and generated effective low-level corrective feedback that guided the GR00T N1 robot to successfully recover from grasping failures in real-world scenarios. For instance, in an instruction to "Put the blue cube in the box," where the robot initially failed to grasp, RoboFAC-7B suggested, "Move the robot arm slightly backward to create space, then adjust its position to the left and downward to align with the center of the cube before attempting to grasp it again," leading to successful task completion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoboFAC framework introduces a systematic methodology for addressing robotic failures, presenting implications for the robustness and practical deployment of robotic manipulation systems.

One significant contribution is the **RoboFAC Dataset**. This large-scale, diverse, and hierarchically structured dataset provides a valuable resource for training and evaluating models in robotic failure analysis and correction. By encompassing a wide range of tasks, environments, and failure types—both in simulation and the real world—and annotating these with multi-dimensional question-answer pairs, the dataset facilitates the development of models that can reason about `what`, `why`, and `how to correct` robotic errors. Its public availability supports future research in this domain.

The **RoboFAC model**, a lightweight, specialized multimodal model, demonstrates that domain-specific fine-tuning on comprehensive failure data can yield performance competitive with, and in some aspects superior to, general-purpose large proprietary models such as GPT-4o. The model’s ability to perform unified task understanding, failure diagnosis, and corrective suggestion contributes to enhancing the cognitive capabilities of robotic systems.

A key impact lies in the **improved robustness and recovery capabilities of VLA systems**. When integrated as an external critic, RoboFAC significantly enhances task success rates in real-world robotic manipulation by providing timely and relevant corrective instructions. This addresses a critical limitation of existing VLA models that lack inherent mechanisms for self-diagnosis and recovery from unexpected failures. The demonstrated 29.1% relative improvement in task success rates underscores its practical value.

Furthermore, the **efficiency and deployability** of the RoboFAC model are notable. Its lightweight architecture and approximately 3x reduction in inference latency compared to GPT-4o make it suitable for real-time, on-device deployment in robotic systems. This addresses a practical bottleneck associated with large, cloud-dependent MLLMs, thereby advancing the feasibility of autonomous robots in environments requiring rapid decision-making.

The research also offers insights into **instruction granularity**, indicating that low-level, action-oriented corrections are often more effective for VLA policies that have already undergone task-specific fine-tuning. This finding can inform the design of future robotic feedback and learning systems.

In summary, RoboFAC contributes to the field of embodied AI by providing a comprehensive framework that systematically tackles robotic failures. It moves beyond passive execution by enabling proactive diagnosis and recovery, thereby making robotic manipulation systems more reliable and adaptable in complex, unstructured environments. The framework's potential impact extends to the development of more autonomous and resilient robotic agents, reducing the need for constant human intervention in failure scenarios and paving the way for advanced applications in various domains.
