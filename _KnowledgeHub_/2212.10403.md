# Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive survey systematically reviews the current state of reasoning in Large Language Models, clarifying definitions, categorizing techniques to elicit reasoning, and detailing evaluation methods. It synthesizes findings on LLMs' emergent reasoning capabilities and persistent limitations, concluding that while models show impressive performance and human-like patterns, definitive evidence for "true reasoning" remains elusive.

## Problem

- The rapid and fragmented growth of research on Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to an ambiguous understanding of their 'reasoning' capabilities.
- There are conflicting claims and lack of clarity regarding whether LLMs genuinely 'reason' or merely perform sophisticated pattern matching.
- A need exists to systematize diverse techniques and evaluation methods used to assess and improve LLM reasoning.

## Method

- The survey provides a structured definition and categorization of different reasoning types, with a focus on informal deductive reasoning in LLMs.
- It systematically categorizes techniques into ==fully supervised finetuning==, various prompting strategies (including ==Chain-of-Thought== and its variants), and hybrid methods.
- The paper reviews common benchmarks and analytical methods used to evaluate LLM reasoning, encompassing end-task performance and formal analysis of reasoning steps.

## Results

- Despite advancements and the effectiveness of CoT, LLMs still struggle with genuinely complex reasoning tasks requiring deep compositional generalization or long-term planning.
- High performance or generated rationales do not definitively confirm 'true reasoning,' as LLMs can rely on heuristics, produce inconsistent rationales, or be sensitive to training data term frequencies.
- The survey concludes that it is premature to confirm if LLMs are capable of 'true reasoning' in a human-like cognitive sense, highlighting the ambiguity and areas for future research.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reasoning abilities in LLMs appear as 'emergent properties' at scales typically exceeding 100 billion parameters.
> - Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting significantly improves LLM performance on diverse reasoning tasks by eliciting intermediate steps and enhancing out-of-distribution robustness.
> - LLMs exhibit reasoning patterns that include 'human-like content effects,' where prior knowledge and conclusion believability influence their predictions.

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-3, PaLM, and ChatGPT have brought about an unprecedented surge in interest in their capabilities, particularly regarding "reasoning." This paper emerges at a critical juncture where the observed performance of LLMs on various complex tasks has led to claims of emergent reasoning abilities, while simultaneously sparking intense debate and skepticism about whether these models truly "reason" or merely exhibit sophisticated pattern matching.

This survey directly addresses this central debate by attempting to provide a structured overview of the current state of knowledge. It fits into the broader research landscape by:

- **Synthesizing a Fast-Evolving Field:** The field of LLM reasoning is characterized by rapid advancements, new techniques, and a constant influx of research papers. This survey provides a much-needed consolidation and organization of this diverse literature, helping researchers understand the landscape, identify key trends, and avoid redundancy. It acknowledges its own limitations in capturing every concurrent development, recommending readers to check citing papers for updates, which is a testament to the field's pace.
- **Clarifying Ambiguous Terminology:** The term "reasoning" itself is multifaceted and often loosely applied in the context of LLMs. The paper provides a dedicated section to define and categorize different types of reasoning (deductive, inductive, abductive, formal vs. informal) and discusses how these apply to LLMs. This definitional clarity is crucial for fostering more precise scientific discourse.
- **Addressing the "True Reasoning" Debate:** The core of the paper grapples with the philosophical and empirical question of whether LLMs genuinely reason. It highlights conflicting views (e.g., Kojima et al. vs. Valmeekam et al.) and meticulously compiles evidence for and against true reasoning, reflecting the ongoing academic discussion. This positions the survey as a critical meta-analysis rather than just a descriptive review.
- **Mapping Techniques and Evaluation Paradigms:** It systematically categorizes techniques designed to improve or elicit reasoning (finetuning, prompting, hybrid methods) and evaluation benchmarks. This mapping provides a valuable resource for researchers looking to apply or develop new methods and understand the limitations of current evaluation practices.
- **Bridging AI and Cognitive Science:** By discussing "human-like content effects" on reasoning (Dasgupta et al., 2022), the survey touches upon the intersection of AI and cognitive science, exploring whether LLM reasoning patterns align with human cognition, thereby contributing to the interdisciplinary understanding of intelligence.

In essence, this survey serves as a vital compass in the dynamic landscape of LLM research, guiding researchers through the complex questions surrounding reasoning, synthesizing disparate findings, and pointing towards the critical challenges and opportunities ahead. It is a foundational work for anyone seeking to understand the current state and future directions of reasoning in LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of "Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models: A Survey" is to provide a comprehensive, detailed, and up-to-date overview of the current state of knowledge regarding reasoning in Large Language Models. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific aims:

1. **Clarify the Concept of Reasoning in LLMs:** Given the abstract and often ambiguous nature of "reasoning," especially when applied to artificial intelligence, a key objective is to provide a structured definition and categorization of reasoning types (deductive, inductive, abductive, formal, informal) relevant to LLM research. The authors specifically state their focus on "informal deductive reasoning" due to its prevalence in the literature.
2. **Systematize Techniques for Improving/Eliciting Reasoning:** The paper aims to categorize and explain the various methodological advancements that have been proposed to enhance or bring forth reasoning capabilities in LLMs. This includes fully supervised finetuning, various prompting strategies (Chain-of-Thought and its variants, rationale engineering, problem decomposition), and hybrid approaches that combine training with prompting.
3. **Review Methods and Benchmarks for Evaluation:** A crucial objective is to survey the tools and metrics used to assess reasoning abilities in LLMs. This includes standard end-task performance benchmarks (e.g., for arithmetic, commonsense, symbolic reasoning) and emerging methods for deeper, more formal analysis of the reasoning process itself.
4. **Summarize Key Findings and Implications:** The authors intend to distill the most significant discoveries and their consequences from previous research in the field. This includes observations about emergent abilities, the impact of Chain-of-Thought prompting, human-like reasoning patterns, and the persistent limitations of LLMs in complex reasoning tasks.
5. **Stimulate Discussion and Suggest Future Directions:** Beyond reviewing the existing literature, a core motivation is to reflect on the current state of the field, engage in insightful discussion about fundamental questions (e.g., "Are language models really able to reason?"), and offer concrete suggestions for future research avenues to advance the field.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the significant progress LLMs have shown in natural language processing and the observed "emergent behaviors" related to reasoning. However, this progress has led to conflicting claims and a lack of clarity regarding the true extent and nature of LLM reasoning. The authors highlight the ambiguity by quoting contradictory statements about LLM reasoning capabilities. Therefore, the survey is motivated by a critical need to provide clarity, consolidate knowledge, and offer a structured framework for understanding this rapidly evolving and contentious area, thereby fostering more focused and productive research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a survey paper, the methodology of "Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models" is centered on a systematic literature review and organization. The authors' approach involves defining the scope, categorizing the literature into logical segments, and synthesizing findings from a vast array of research papers.

1. **Defining Reasoning:** The paper begins by clarifying the fundamental concept of reasoning. It draws on definitions from psychology, philosophy, and computer science, and then distinguishes between various types of reasoning:
 - **Deductive Reasoning:** Drawing conclusions necessarily following from premises.
 - **Inductive Reasoning:** Drawing probable conclusions based on observations.
 - **Abductive Reasoning:** Inferring the best explanation for a set of observations.
 - **Analogical, Causal, and Probabilistic Reasoning:** Briefly mentioned as other forms.
 - **Formal vs. Informal Reasoning:** Distinguishing between structured, rule-based reasoning and intuitive, experience-based reasoning. The authors explicitly state a particular focus on "informal deductive reasoning" in LLMs, reflecting its common usage in the literature.

2. **Categorization of Techniques for Eliciting/Improving Reasoning:** The core of the survey's "methodology" in organizing the literature lies in its three-pronged categorization of techniques:
 - **Fully Supervised Finetuning:** This approach involves training smaller language models on specific datasets containing explicit reasoning steps. The authors note its limitations in data requirements and domain specificity.
 - **Prompting & In-Context Learning:** This is a major focus, reflecting the paradigm shift brought by large LLMs. It is further subdivided into:
 - **Chain of Thought (CoT) and its Variants:** A detailed discussion of providing intermediate reasoning steps in prompts, including Zero-shot-CoT, code-based reasoning, iterative CoT, and knowledge-retrieval CoT.
 - **Rationale Engineering:** Methods to refine, explore, and verify rationales generated by LLMs (e.g., complexity-based prompting, self-consistency, external verifiers, self-verification).
 - **Problem Decomposition:** Strategies to break down complex problems into subproblems for LLMs (e.g., least-to-most prompting, decomposed prompting, successive prompting).
 - **Other Techniques:** Including selection-inference, backward chaining, abductive prompting for binary questions, numerical reasoning simplification, and distilling reasoning to smaller models.
 - **Hybrid Methods:** This category combines improving LLM reasoning capabilities through training with effective prompting techniques. It includes:
 - **Reasoning-Enhanced Training & Prompting:** Pretraining or finetuning LLMs on datasets that explicitly include "reasoning" (e.g., scientific/mathematical data, CoT data).
 - **Bootstrapping & Self-Improving:** Iteratively refining LLMs on their own generated rationales, such as Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR).

3. **Review of Evaluation Methods and Benchmarks:** The survey systematically lists common benchmarks for evaluating LLM reasoning, categorized by the type of reasoning task:
 - **End-Task Performance:** Covering Arithmetic Reasoning (e.g., GSM8K, Math), Commonsense Reasoning (e.g., CSQA, StrategyQA), Symbolic Reasoning (e.g., Last Letter Concatenation, Coin Flip), and other diverse tasks (e.g., BIG-bench, SCAN, Table Reasoning).
 - **Analysis on Reasoning:** This section goes beyond mere accuracy metrics to discuss efforts aimed at formally analyzing the quality and consistency of reasoning steps, including metrics like ROSCOE, synthetic datasets like PrOntoQA, and ablation studies on CoT.

4. **Synthesis of Findings and Discussion:** The authors synthesize the overarching findings and implications from the surveyed literature, focusing on key takeaways such as emergent abilities, CoT effectiveness, human-like reasoning patterns, and persistent limitations. They then use these findings as a basis for a reflective discussion on the core question of LLM reasoning capabilities and propose future research directions.

The authors maintain a neutral and critical stance, consistently highlighting both the impressive capabilities and the significant limitations or unclarities in LLM reasoning. They cite a broad range of contemporary papers (up to late 2022/early 2023), demonstrating an up-to-date understanding of the field. The paper structure itself (as shown in Figure 1) serves as a clear roadmap for their systematic review process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey distills several crucial findings and implications from the vast body of research on reasoning in Large Language Models:

1. **Reasoning as an Emergent Ability of LLMs:** A significant observation is that reasoning capabilities appear to "emerge" in LLMs only when they reach a certain scale, typically models with over 100 billion parameters (e.g., GPT-3). Smaller models like GPT-2 and BERT rarely exhibit these abilities. This finding suggests that large-scale models might be inherently better suited for general reasoning problems, although the underlying reasons for this emergence are not yet fully understood.

2. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Elicits "Reasoning" in LLMs:** CoT prompting, which involves providing LLMs with intermediate natural language reasoning steps or a simple "Let's think step by step" instruction, dramatically improves their performance on various reasoning tasks (arithmetic, symbolic, commonsense). This technique helps LLMs generate explicit rationales before providing a final answer. Furthermore, CoT prompting has been shown to enhance the out-of-distribution robustness of LLMs, a benefit not typically seen with standard prompting or finetuning. While CoT leads to valid individual proof steps even with fictional premises, LLMs may still select incorrect steps when multiple options are available.

3. **LLMs Show Human-Like Content Effects on Reasoning:** Research indicates that LLMs exhibit reasoning patterns similar to humans. Their predictions can be influenced by prior knowledge and the believability of conclusions, mirroring cognitive biases observed in human reasoning. This suggests that while LLMs may not always succeed, their failures often occur in scenarios challenging for humans, providing some evidence for a resemblance in reasoning mechanisms.

4. **LLMs Are Still Unskilled at Complex Reasoning:** Despite impressive advancements and the benefits of techniques like CoT, LLMs continue to struggle with genuinely complex reasoning tasks, especially those requiring deep compositional generalization, long-term planning, or nuanced understanding of implicature. Studies show that even in relatively simple commonsense planning domains, LLMs underperform significantly compared to human capabilities. This finding implies that many existing benchmarks might be too simplistic to truly gauge the advanced reasoning abilities of LLMs, necessitating the development of more challenging evaluations.

5. **Ambiguity of "True Reasoning":** The survey emphasizes that high performance on reasoning tasks, step-by-step rationale generation, or human-like content effects do not definitively confirm "true reasoning" in LLMs. The authors highlight several counter-arguments:
 - Performance might be based on heuristics rather than genuine reasoning.
 - Generated rationales can be incorrect or inconsistent, indicating "reasoning-like responses" rather than actual reasoning.
 - LLM performance can be sensitive to term frequencies in training data (e.g., numbers), which would not be expected if they were truly solving problems through reasoning.
 - LLMs struggle with associating memorized relevant information.
 This leads to the overarching conclusion that it is premature to definitively state whether LLMs are truly capable of reasoning in a human-like cognitive sense.

In summary, the field observes strong emergent capabilities and effective elicitation techniques like CoT, hinting at reasoning processes. However, these are coupled with persistent limitations in complex tasks and a fundamental ambiguity about the cognitive nature of these "reasoning" demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The survey "Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models" holds significant importance for the field of artificial intelligence and specifically for researchers working on Large Language Models. Its potential impact spans several dimensions:

1. **Clarifying the Research Landscape:** By systematically organizing the diverse and rapidly growing literature on LLM reasoning, the survey provides a critical reference point. It helps new researchers understand the foundational concepts and existing techniques, preventing redundant efforts and fostering more informed research directions. For experienced researchers, it offers a consolidated view of progress and identifies areas needing further attention.

2. **Driving Definitional Rigor:** The explicit effort to define and categorize "reasoning" within the context of LLMs is invaluable. It encourages greater precision in terminology and discussion, which is essential for scientific progress. Moving beyond vague claims of "reasoning" towards specific types of reasoning helps in designing more targeted experiments and evaluations.

3. **Informing Model Development and Evaluation:** The detailed review of techniques (finetuning, prompting, hybrid methods) provides a practical guide for developers seeking to enhance LLMs' reasoning capabilities. Similarly, the comprehensive list of benchmarks and the critique of their limitations will push for the creation of more sophisticated and challenging evaluation methods that can truly differentiate between genuine reasoning and superficial pattern matching. This could lead to a new generation of benchmarks designed to test specific reasoning faculties more rigorously.

4. **Guiding Future Research:** The paper's "Reflection, Discussion, and Future Directions" section is perhaps its most impactful contribution. By posing fundamental questions like "Are language models really able to reason?" and highlighting unresolved issues (e.g., understanding emergent abilities, improving robustness, addressing complex reasoning failures), the authors lay out a clear agenda for future work. This includes:
 - Developing better benchmarks that reflect real-world, complex reasoning tasks.
 - Conducting deeper analysis into the mechanisms of LLM reasoning beyond surface-level performance.
 - Focusing on improving LLM reasoning capabilities through architectural changes, training data design, and optimization objectives, rather than solely relying on prompting.
 - Exploring more meaningful applications of LLM reasoning in areas like decision-making, legal reasoning, and scientific discovery.

5. **Fostering Interdisciplinary Dialogue:** By touching on human-like reasoning patterns and the philosophical implications of AI reasoning, the survey can stimulate greater collaboration and dialogue between AI researchers, cognitive scientists, and philosophers. Understanding how AI models "reason" in comparison to humans can yield insights into both artificial and natural intelligence.

In conclusion, this survey is more than just a summary; it is a critical analysis that acts as a signpost for a nascent but profoundly important field. It helps frame the fundamental questions surrounding LLM reasoning, provides a structured overview of current approaches, and outlines a compelling research agenda. Its timely publication during a period of intense public and academic interest in LLMs ensures its relevance and potential to shape the trajectory of AI research in the coming years.
