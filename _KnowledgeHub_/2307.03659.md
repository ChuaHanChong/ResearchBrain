# Decomposing the Generalization Gap in Imitation Learning for Visual Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research quantifies the individual contributions of various environmental factors to the generalization gap in visual robotic manipulation, revealing that changes in camera position and table texture pose the greatest challenges. The study also introduces 'Factor World,' a new simulated benchmark, and demonstrates that simple data augmentations and visually diverse out-of-domain robotic data effectively improve generalization, unlike existing large-scale pre-trained visual representations which often struggle.

## Problem

- Robotic policies trained in one environment often fail to generalize effectively to new, unseen conditions, exhibiting a 'generalization gap.'
- There is a lack of quantitative understanding regarding which specific environmental factors contribute most to this generalization difficulty in vision-based robotic manipulation.
- Prior methods like data augmentation and pre-trained representations have not fully closed this gap, and their specific impacts on different environmental factors are not well-understood in robotics.

## Method

- The study systematically decomposes environmental variations into distinct factors (e.g., lighting, textures, camera/table positions, distractors) and quantifies their individual and combined impacts on ==generalization gap== performance.
- Researchers developed **Factor World**, a new simulated benchmark with 11 customizable environmental factors, enabling controlled and fine-grained evaluation of generalization challenges.
- The effectiveness of common generalization improvement techniques (==data augmentation== like random crops and photometric distortions, and pre-trained visual representations like ==CLIP== and ==R3M==) and different data collection strategies was investigated.

## Results

- On real robots, new camera positions resulted in a **45.8%** success rate, and new table textures achieved **52.8%**, significantly lower than the **91.7%** in the original environment, highlighting them as the most challenging factors.
- The Factor World simulated benchmark, offering **19 tasks** and **11 customizable factors**, provided consistent findings with real-world experiments, confirming that new backgrounds, distractors, and lighting were easier to generalize to.
- Random crop augmentation consistently improved generalization across multiple factors on both real and simulated platforms, and photometric distortions specifically enhanced performance on texture-based factors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Changes in camera position and table texture consistently present the most significant generalization challenges for robotic policies across both real and simulated environments.
> - Most environmental factors do not exhibit strong compounding effects; generalizing to a combination of two factors is typically no harder than generalizing to the more challenging individual factor.
> - Simple data augmentations (random crops and photometric distortions) effectively improve generalization, even for non-spatial factors, while current large-scale pre-trained vision-language models (CLIP, R3M) do not consistently bridge the generalization gap in robotics.
> - Leveraging visual diversity from out-of-domain robotic tasks significantly improves in-domain generalization, often being more beneficial than focusing solely on increasing the quantity of narrow in-domain data or pre-training on non-robotics datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation often encounters significant challenges when policies trained in one environment are applied to new, unseen conditions. While deep learning has enabled considerable progress, robotic policies frequently exhibit a "generalization gap" where performance degrades in environments that differ from the training setup.

Prior research efforts to address this generalization issue have explored several avenues. These include the development and application of various data augmentation techniques to increase the diversity of training data, as well as the utilization of representations pre-trained on large-scale datasets from other domains, such as static images (ImageNet), human videos (Ego4D), or natural language. Additionally, there has been a focus on creating and curating large, reusable robotic datasets to provide more varied training experiences.

Despite these advancements, a substantial generalization gap persists, and there is a lack of clear understanding regarding the precise nature of the challenges. Specifically, prior work has not extensively quantified the individual contributions of different environmental factors to this gap. While computer vision models have shown robustness to many visual variations, robotic policies tend to be less resilient due to the typically smaller and less diverse datasets available for training. Furthermore, robotics data collection is often an active, controlled process, which can inadvertently limit the natural variations (e.g., backgrounds) present in datasets. Robotic tasks also necessitate dynamic, multi-step decision-making, distinguishing them from many static computer vision problems like image classification.

This paper addresses these gaps by systematically decomposing environmental variations into distinct factors and quantifying their respective contributions to the generalization gap in imitation learning for visual robotic manipulation. By analyzing these factors, the work aims to provide a more principled understanding of where existing models struggle, thereby informing future strategies for data collection, model design, and algorithm development. It also introduces a new simulated benchmark to facilitate more controlled and fine-grained evaluations of generalization performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to understand and quantify the factors that contribute most to the difficulty of generalization in vision-based robotic manipulation tasks, particularly within the imitation learning paradigm. The overarching question driving this study is: "What are the factors that contribute most to the difficulty of generalization to new environments in vision-based robotic manipulation?"

The motivation for pursuing this question stems from several observations within the field:
- **Persistent Generalization Gap:** Despite ongoing research into data augmentation, pre-trained representations, and diverse dataset collection, robotic policies frequently fail to generalize effectively to novel environments. This indicates a fundamental challenge that current methods have not fully overcome.
- **Lack of Specificity in Existing Knowledge:** While existing literature acknowledges the problem of generalization, it often lacks precise, quantitative insights into *which specific environmental dimensions* are most problematic for robotic policies. For instance, observations about robustness to mild lighting changes or the impact of new backgrounds have often been qualitative or lack the specificity needed to guide targeted improvements.
- **Optimizing Resource Allocation:** Collecting high-quality and diverse robotic data is resource-intensive. Without a clear understanding of the most challenging generalization factors, efforts to collect more data or design new models may not be optimally directed. A quantitative decomposition of the generalization gap could inform researchers on where to invest resources for maximum impact, for example, by prioritizing data diversity along particularly difficult dimensions.
- **Differences from Computer Vision:** Robotic manipulation tasks differ significantly from traditional computer vision tasks. They involve dynamic, sequential decision-making in physical environments, often with smaller, less varied datasets. These differences necessitate a formal study of environmental factors specifically in the context of robot learning, rather than relying solely on insights from broader computer vision research. Robot datasets often lack natural variations because researchers control the environment during data collection, leading to less inherent diversity compared to passive image collection.
- **Development of Targeted Solutions:** By identifying and quantifying the most significant contributors to the generalization gap, the research aims to encourage the development of algorithms and techniques specifically designed to address these identified challenges, potentially leading to more robust and generalizable robotic systems.

To achieve this, the paper sets out to:
1. **Decompose Environmental Variations:** Characterize environmental changes as a combination of independent factors, such as lighting, background, distractor objects, table texture, object texture, table position, and camera position.
2. **Quantify Factor Contributions:** Systematically measure how much each of these individual and combined factors contributes to the generalization gap in imitation learning policies.
3. **Evaluate Across Modalities:** Conduct evaluations on both real robot language-conditioned manipulation tasks and a newly designed simulated benchmark to ensure consistency and provide a broader context for the findings.
4. **Develop a Controlled Benchmark:** Introduce "Factor World," a new simulated benchmark comprising 19 tasks with 11 customizable environment factors, to enable controlled, fine-grained, and reproducible evaluations of generalization performance.
5. **Assess Existing Solutions:** Investigate the effectiveness of common generalization improvement techniques, such as data augmentation (random crops, photometric distortions) and pre-trained visual representations, in mitigating the impact of these environmental factors.
6. **Inform Data Collection Strategies:** Study how different data collection approaches, particularly those emphasizing visual diversity from out-of-domain data, influence generalization capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic approach to decompose and quantify the generalization gap in visual robotic manipulation across both real-world and simulated environments.

#### 3.1. Environment Factor Decomposition

The study identifies and categorizes environmental variations into specific factors. For real robot evaluations, five factors are considered: lighting condition, distractor objects, background, table texture, and camera pose. In the simulated environment, "Factor World," a more comprehensive set of 11 factors is implemented, including object position, initial arm position, camera position, table position, object size, object texture, distractor objects and their positions, floor texture, table texture, and lighting. The study specifically focuses on seven of these factors: lighting, texture of objects, texture of the table, texture of the background, distractor objects, camera position, and table position, excluding object size, shape, and initial arm pose from the primary analysis.

#### 3.2. Real Robot Manipulation Setup

- **Platform:** Experiments are conducted using mobile manipulators equipped with a 7-DoF right-side arm, a two-finger gripper, a mobile base, and a head-mounted camera system.
- **Task:** The focus is on a language-conditioned "pick" skill, where the robot must pick up a specified object (e.g., "pick pepsi can") from a countertop, potentially amidst distractor objects. Six specific objects are used for evaluation.
- **Observations:** The policy receives 300x300 RGB image observations from the last six time-steps and a natural language instruction.
- **Actions:** Actions are discretized along 10 dimensions, covering arm movements (xyz-position, roll, pitch, yaw, gripper opening) and base movements (xy-position, yaw).
- **Dataset:** Training utilizes a large dataset of over 115,000 human-collected demonstrations spanning 13 skills, over 100 objects, three tables, and three locations. The dataset features a fixed camera orientation but randomized initial base positions.
- **Factor Implementation:**
 - **Lighting:** Achieved using bright LED light sources with different colored filters to introduce varied hues and shadows.
 - **Distractor Objects:** New, unseen objects are introduced as distractors.
 - **Background/Table Texture:** Patterned paper is used to cover the acrylic wall (background) and cabinet top (table), introducing new visual textures.
 - **Camera Pose:** Varied by changing the robot's head orientation.

#### 3.3. Factor World (Simulated Benchmark) Setup

- **Foundation:** Built upon the Meta World benchmark, which is extended to include environmental diversity.
- **Tasks:** A suite of 19 tasks is created, with the study focusing on three representative tasks: pick-place, bin-picking, and door-open.
- **Factor Configuration:** Each of the 11 implemented factors can be customized with numerous configurations:
 - **Textures (table, floor, objects):** Sampled from 162 texture images (81 for train, 81 for evaluation) and continuous RGB values.
 - **Distractor Objects:** Sampled from 170 object meshes (100 train, 70 eval) from Google's Scanned Objects Dataset.
 - **Lighting:** Continuous ambient and diffuse values are sampled within [0.2, 0.8].
 - **Positions (object, camera, table):** Sampled from continuous ranges, categorized into "Narrow," "Medium," and "Wide" radii (Table 1 in Appendix A.1.1). Object initial positions are randomized per episode.
- **Demonstrations:** Scripted expert policies from the Meta World benchmark are used to collect demonstrations (2000 for pick-place, 1000 for bin-picking and door-open).
- **Observations:** 84x84 RGB images, robot end-effector position, and distance between fingers from the last two time-steps.
- **Actions:** Desired change in 3D end-effector position and gripper state (open/close).

#### 3.4. Algorithms and Generalization Improvement Methods

- **Real Robot Policy:** Utilizes the RT-1 architecture, a Transformer-based model that tokenizes images, text, and actions, trained with a language-conditioned imitation learning objective.
- **Simulated Policy:** Employs a vanilla behavior cloning approach with a convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture similar to and.
- **Generalization Methods Investigated:**
 - **Data Augmentation:**
 - **Real Robot:** Random crops (300x300 from full-resolution images) and random photometric distortions (adjusting brightness, saturation, hue, contrast, and applying cutout/Gaussian noise).
 - **Factor World:** Shift augmentations (random 84x84 crops from a 4-pixel padded image) and color jitter augmentations (adjusting brightness, contrast, saturation, hue with a factor of 0.2 and 30% probability).
 - **Pretrained Representations (Factor World only):** Evaluated CLIP and R3M (both ResNet50 versions) for encoding image observations, with a BatchNorm layer and a three-layer feedforward policy head.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Protocol

- **Real Robot Evaluation:** Policies are tested on 2 new lighting conditions, 3 new sets of distractor objects, 3 new table textures, 3 new backgrounds, and 2 new camera poses. For each factor, 2 evaluation trials are conducted per task (out of 6 tasks), with randomized object and distractor positions. Success rates are averaged across 12 trials.
- **Factor World Evaluation:** Training is conducted with varying numbers of training environments (N = 5, 20, 50, 100). Generalization is assessed by shifting a factor (or pair of factors) by sampling 100 new values for the factor(s) of interest.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate:** The primary metric for real robot evaluations and also reported for Factor World.
 - **Generalization Gap:** Defined as P_T - P_F, where P_T is the success rate on training environments and P_F is the success rate under factor shifts.
 - **Percentage Difference (Factor Pairs):** For pairs of factors, calculated as (P_{A+B} - min(P_A, P_B)) / min(P_A, P_B), normalizing the combined performance relative to the harder individual factor.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several empirical observations regarding generalization in imitation learning for visual robotic manipulation.

#### 4.1. Impact of Environment Factors on Generalization

- **Individual Factors:**
 - **Real Robot:** New camera positions (45.8% success rate) and new table textures (52.8%) presented the most significant drops in performance. New distractor objects (80.6%) and new lighting conditions (83.3%) caused moderate decreases. New backgrounds had the least impact (88.9%), with performance close to the original training environment (91.7%). A direct comparison using the same patterned paper showed that new table textures were harder to generalize to than new backgrounds.
 - **Factor World (Simulation):** The simulated results largely corroborated the real robot findings regarding the relative difficulty of factors. New backgrounds, distractors, and lighting were found to be easier to generalize to, while new table textures and camera positions were harder. Notably, new object textures were approximately as difficult as new camera positions, and new table positions were as challenging as new table textures. A difference was observed for backgrounds, which were harder in simulation than distractors and lighting, potentially attributed to higher background diversity already present in the real robot training dataset. Increasing the number of training environments from 5 to 100 significantly reduced the generalization gap across all factors.
 - **Continuous Factors:** For continuous factors like camera and table positions, linearly increasing the range (radius) over which variations were sampled approximately doubled the generalization gap in simulation. This suggests that maintaining consistent camera and table positions is beneficial for performance, and broader variations may require substantially more diverse training data.

- **Pairs of Factors:**
 - **Real Robot:** When tested with combined shifts (e.g., new table texture + new distractors, or new table texture + new background), the performance was comparable to that with only the harder individual factor (new table texture). This suggested an absence of a strong compounding effect for these factor combinations.
 - **Factor World (Simulation):** Across 21 pairs of factors, 16 pairs showed no significant compounding effect on generalization performance, with the relative percentage difference in success rates falling between -6% and 6%. This implies that generalizing to a combination of two factors is generally not substantially harder or easier than generalizing to the more challenging of the two individual factors. Interestingly, two pairs (object texture + distractor, and light + distractor) exhibited synergistic effects, making generalization to the combination somewhat easier compared to the harder individual factor. These findings suggest that environmental factors can often be addressed independently.

#### 4.2. Effect of Data Augmentation and Pretrained Representations

- **Random Crop Augmentation:**
 - **Real Robot:** Random crop augmentation consistently improved generalization across multiple environmental factors. This improvement was observed not only for spatial factors like camera position, which is an intuitive benefit of cropping, but also for non-spatial factors such as distractor objects and table textures.
 - **Factor World (Simulation):** Similar improvements were seen in simulation, particularly for camera position shifts.
- **Photometric Distortion Augmentation:**
 - **Real Robot:** This augmentation primarily enhanced performance on texture-based factors, specifically improving generalization to new table textures.
 - **Factor World (Simulation):** In simulation, photometric distortions were found to improve performance on object, table, and background textures.
- **Pretrained Representations (R3M, CLIP):**
 - **Factor World (Simulation):** While R3M and CLIP achieved good success rates on the training environments, they generally struggled to generalize to new, similar environments, resulting in a large generalization gap across many factors. An exception was observed for CLIP, which showed an improvement over a trained-from-scratch CNN when generalizing to new object textures. This indicates that while these representations may be effective for in-domain performance, their benefits for out-of-distribution generalization in robotics tasks are not consistently evident.

#### 4.3. Investigating Different Strategies for Data Collection

- **Augmenting Visual Diversity with Out-of-Domain Data:**
 - **Real Robot:** Training with a full dataset that included "out-of-domain" demonstrations (e.g., from tasks like opening a fridge or operating a cereal dispenser) significantly improved generalization performance across various environmental shifts compared to training only on "in-domain" data for the "pick" skill. This suggests that leveraging visual diversity from other robotic tasks, even if not directly relevant to the current skill, can be more effective for in-domain generalization than pre-training on non-robotics datasets.
- **Prioritizing Visual Diversity over In-Domain Quantity:**
 - **Real Robot:** A smaller dataset that prioritized visual diversity from out-of-domain demonstrations, while having fewer "in-domain" "pick" skill demonstrations than an "in-domain only" dataset of the same total size, performed comparably across most generalization factors. The main exception was scenarios with new distractors, where the "in-domain only" policy showed superior performance (75.0% vs. 44.4% success rate). This indicates that visual diversity can be a significant factor, sometimes compensating for a reduction in specific in-domain data quantity for certain generalization challenges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a quantitative and systematic understanding of the generalization challenges in visual robotic manipulation, offering actionable insights for the development of more robust policies.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Quantification of Generalization Difficulty:** By decomposing environmental variations into specific factors and measuring their individual and combined impacts, the study provides a principled ranking of generalization difficulty across both simulated and real-world robot setups. This consistent ordering (e.g., camera position and table texture being consistently challenging) is a significant finding that can guide researchers in prioritizing which factors to address.
- **Introduction of Factor World Benchmark:** The development of Factor World provides a valuable, controlled simulated environment for future research. This benchmark, with its extensive configurability and numerous factor variations, enables fine-grained evaluation of new models and algorithms, thereby facilitating more targeted improvements in generalization.
- **Insights for Data Augmentation and Representation Learning:** The findings confirm the utility of simple data augmentation techniques, such as random cropping and photometric distortions, even for non-spatial generalization challenges. However, the study also indicates that while pre-trained representations like CLIP and R3M perform well on training environments, they do not consistently bridge the generalization gap in new, similar robotic environments, highlighting a specific area for further research tailored to robotics.
- **Guidance for Data Collection Strategies:** The demonstration that visual diversity derived from out-of-domain robotic data significantly improves in-domain generalization is an important finding. It suggests that future dataset collection efforts should consider prioritizing broad visual diversity across various robot interaction contexts, rather than solely focusing on a narrow range of in-domain variations or relying on non-robotics pre-training. This could lead to more efficient and effective data curation.
- **Foundation for Targeted Algorithm Development:** The observation that most environmental factors do not exhibit strong compounding effects on generalization suggests that addressing these factors individually might be a viable and effective strategy. This simplifies the problem space and encourages the development of algorithms specifically designed to tackle identified difficult factors, rather than requiring solutions that manage complex interactions of many factors simultaneously.

**Potential Impact:**
The insights from this research can inform various aspects of robotic learning:
- **Improved Model Design:** Future robotic policy architectures and training methodologies can be explicitly designed to be more robust to the most challenging environmental factors identified (e.g., camera and table position variations, object and table textures).
- **Efficient Data Collection:** Robotics practitioners can make more informed decisions about how to collect and curate datasets, emphasizing specific types of diversity (e.g., visual diversity from out-of-domain tasks) that yield the greatest generalization benefits, potentially reducing the cost and effort of data acquisition.
- **More Robust Robot Deployments:** By understanding and mitigating the effects of common environmental shifts, robots can be deployed in a wider range of real-world scenarios with greater reliability and less need for costly re-training or environment-specific calibration.
- **Standardized Benchmarking:** Factor World can serve as a standard benchmark, enabling direct comparisons of generalization capabilities across different robotic learning approaches and accelerating progress in the field.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge limitations, including the focus on a few representative tasks and environment factors within the imitation learning setting. The extensive experimental requirements for real robot evaluations constrained the scope of the study. Future work could leverage Factor World to explore additional tasks, a wider array of factors, and generalization within the reinforcement learning paradigm. Furthermore, the observed performance degradation on training environments when policies were exposed to more varied data suggests that investigating higher-capacity models (e.g., those based on ResNet or Vision Transformer architectures) capable of better fitting diverse training data could be a fruitful direction.
