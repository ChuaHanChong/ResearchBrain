# GraspSense: Physically Grounded Grasp and Grip Planning for a Dexterous Robotic Hand via Language-Guided Perception and Force Maps

> [!summary] Summary
> A system named GraspSense integrates language understanding, 3D perception, and a "force map" to enable dexterous robotic hands to plan grasps and regulate grip forces based on an object's spatially non-uniform mechanical properties. This approach consistently selected structurally robust contact regions and adaptively applied forces, preventing damage while ensuring stable object retention in simulations.

## Problem

- Existing grasp planning largely relies on geometric and kinematic properties, overlooking an object's spatially non-uniform mechanical strength.
- Applying forces to structurally weak points, even with geometrically stable grasps, can lead to object deformation or damage.
- Grasp selection and grip force regulation are often treated as separate problems, lacking explicit consideration of an object's local mechanical limits.

## Method

- The **GraspSense** pipeline integrates ==natural language understanding==, robust ==3D perception==, a ==physics-informed force map== construction, and advanced grasp planning with adaptive grip execution.
- A ==force map module== estimates ==spatially distributed admissible contact loads== for a reconstructed 3D object model, using a ==geometric approximation of local wall thickness==.
- The system introduces a ==force-map-aware re-ranking== of grasp candidates and an ==impedance-based grip execution== strategy that adaptively scales ==per-finger stiffness== based on local admissible forces and user-defined interaction modes.

## Results

- Force-map-aware grasp selection consistently prioritized structurally reinforced zones (e.g., cup rims, goblet stems), achieving an **8x to 12x** improvement in minimum admissible contact force compared to baseline methods.
- The proposed selection method resulted in **zero force-bound violations**, while the baseline showed violations in **1-2 fingers** per grasp, indicating a higher risk of damage.
- The adaptive impedance controller achieved stable object retention without force-bound violations, successfully calibrating per-finger forces and scaling grip intensity according to operator-specified interaction modes (e.g., "gently" vs. "firmly").

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating an object's spatially non-uniform mechanical properties into grasp selection and force regulation is critical for robust and damage-free manipulation.
> - Geometrically similar grasps can have vastly different mechanical safety profiles, requiring explicit force-map awareness for optimal selection.
> - Adaptive, per-finger impedance control, informed by local object strength, enables nuanced grip strategies that comply with an object's fragility and user intent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial advancements, particularly with the introduction of multi-fingered dexterous hands capable of a wide range of grasps. However, a persistent challenge remains in enabling robots to robustly interact with everyday objects, especially those with varying shapes, fragility, and material properties.

Current grasp planning paradigms, ranging from analytical force-closure methods to data-driven approaches like DexGraspNet, predominantly focus on geometric and kinematic properties such as stability, reachability, and collision avoidance. While these criteria are foundational, they often overlook the spatially non-uniform mechanical properties of objects. This oversight can lead to situations where a geometrically stable grasp might still damage a fragile object if contact forces are applied to a weak region. Prior work in force-aware and compliant manipulation, such as impedance control, has enabled robots to regulate contact forces, but these methods typically rely on generic compliance parameters rather than object-specific structural information. Similarly, structural analysis in grasping has often focused on modeling contact mechanics or force-deformation relationships for deformable objects, rather than pre-computing admissible load maps for informing grasp selection.

Concurrently, advancements in perception, 3D reconstruction, and language-guided control have enabled robots to interpret natural language commands and perceive their environment with increasing fidelity. Tools like SAM for 2D segmentation, YOLO-World for open-vocabulary detection, and SAM3D for 3D reconstruction provide robust means to acquire object models. Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated for high-level task planning and semantic understanding.

This research addresses a specific gap by proposing a system that integrates these disparate capabilities. It moves beyond purely geometric grasp planning by incorporating a detailed understanding of an object's spatially heterogeneous mechanical properties into both grasp selection and grip force regulation. Unlike previous systems, which might consider material properties generally or use generic force limits, this work introduces a "force map" that provides a localized, per-surface-region bound on mechanically safe contact forces. By doing so, it reframes dexterous manipulation as a physically grounded joint planning problem that accounts for object structure, rather than solely relying on geometric validity or generic force control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that existing dexterous grasp planners are limited in their ability to interact robustly and safely with real-world objects, particularly those with spatially heterogeneous mechanical properties. While a grasp may be geometrically stable, applying force to a structurally weak point can lead to deformation or damage. This limitation is particularly relevant for multi-fingered hands, which can achieve numerous kinematically feasible configurations, many of which might be equivalent under traditional stability metrics but vastly different in their mechanical safety.

The core problem identified is the "gap between geometric grasp planning and physically informed manipulation," where grasp selection and grip force regulation are often treated as independent subproblems without explicit consideration of the object's local mechanical strength.

The key objectives of this work are to develop a pipeline that unifies various robotic capabilities to overcome this limitation. Specifically, the objectives are:

1. **To develop a force map construction module:** This module aims to estimate spatially distributed admissible contact loads for a reconstructed 3D object model. It uses a physics-informed geometric approximation of local wall thickness to provide a per-surface-region bound on mechanically safe contact forces, preventing irreversible deformation.
2. **To introduce a physically grounded grasp selection criterion:** This criterion extends classical multi-criteria grasp ranking (which includes stability, reachability, and collision avoidance) by incorporating a force-map-aware re-ranking stage. This allows the system to select, among geometrically equivalent candidates, the one whose contact regions are most mechanically admissible.
3. **To implement an impedance-based grip execution strategy:** This strategy is designed to scale per-finger stiffness according to the locally admissible force at each contact point. This enables a spatially non-uniform grip strategy that is compliant with the object's local structure while maintaining reliable retention.
4. **To integrate a full pipeline:** The system aims to provide an end-to-end solution, from a natural language command to grip execution, within a simulated environment (Isaac Sim). This integration is demonstrated on various cup-like objects made of paper, plastic, and glass, highlighting the system's ability to adapt to different material properties and fragility levels.

Ultimately, the work is motivated by the goal of transforming dexterous manipulation from a purely geometric challenge into a physically grounded interaction planning problem, crucial for future humanoid and general-purpose robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed GraspSense system operates as a five-stage pipeline, transforming natural language commands into physically grounded grasp-and-grip plans for a five-fingered robotic hand.

**A. Task Understanding via Language Model (Qwen)**
The pipeline begins with a natural language command. A large language model, Qwen, is used in inference mode to parse this command. It extracts three key components:
1. **Target object identity (O):** e.g., "paper cup," "plastic cup," "glass goblet." This information is forwarded to the perception module and structural analysis for material model conditioning.
2. **Action type (a):** e.g., "pick up," "pick and place," "hand over." This provides task-level context.
3. **Interaction mode (λ):** e.g., "gently," "default," "firmly." This is mapped to a normalized force scaling factor λ ∈.
The output is a tuple C = (O, a, λ), which propagates through the system.

**B. Object Perception and 3D Reconstruction**
1. **Object Detection and Segmentation:** YOLO-World, an open-vocabulary detection model, identifies the target object based on O from the input image. The resulting point serves as a spatial prompt for SAM (Segment Anything Model), which generates a pixel-accurate binary mask of the object. This stage is designed for robust operation in cluttered environments.
2. **3D Reconstruction (SAM3D):** The masked RGB image is then fed to SAM3D for object-centered 3D reconstruction. SAM3D extracts visual features to infer a coarse 3D structure, which is subsequently refined into a polygonal mesh. The reconstructed object is exported as a.glb format mesh.
3. **GLB-to-USD Conversion and Scene Import:** The.glb mesh is converted to.usd for use in Isaac Sim. During this conversion, several properties are assigned: geometric normalization (rescaling to real-world dimensions), an SDF (Signed Distance Field) mesh collider for accurate contact localization, and material physics properties (density ρ, static friction μs, dynamic friction μd, restitution coefficient er) retrieved from a lookup table keyed by O. The object's mass is computed automatically. The USD asset is then inserted into the simulation scene.

**C. Force Map Construction: Physics-Informed Geometric Approximation**
This stage generates a force map, which estimates the locally admissible lateral contact force for each point on the object's surface—the maximum force that can be applied without causing irreversible deformation. This uses a physics-informed geometric approximation instead of a full Finite Element Method (FEM) simulation.
1. **Principal Axis Decomposition:** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is applied to the mesh vertices to establish a principal coordinate frame. For cup-like objects, one axis typically aligns with the vertical symmetry.
2. **Cylindrical Discretization and Wall-Thickness Estimation:** The object's lateral surface is discretized into height layers (L) and angular bins (A). For each cell (l, a), local wall thickness τ(l, a) is estimated. This involves a two-stage raycasting procedure: an inward ray from outside the object to find the outer-surface point (p1) and its normal, followed by a second ray along the in-plane projection of the normal to find the inner-wall point (p2). The distance between p1 and p2 gives the thickness. Multiple probing directions enhance robustness. The raw thickness map is then completed and smoothed.
3. **Edge Refinement:** Near object boundaries (e.g., rims), the initial thickness estimation can be artificially low. An adaptive refinement stage addresses this by doubling angular resolution and expanding probing directions, allowing the algorithm to correctly identify reinforced edges.
4. **Admissible Force Computation:** A base admissible force Fbase(l, a) is computed, which increases with local wall thickness. For structurally reinforced layers (local force maxima), a Gaussian vertical bonus is added to neighboring layers, reflecting their stiffening influence. The final force map is then calculated as F(l, a) = min([Fbase(l, a) + Σs∈S bs(l)] · km, Fclamp), where km is a material-dependent coefficient and Fclamp is a hard safety ceiling.
5. **Projection onto Mesh Vertices:** The 2D force map F is projected onto the object's 3D mesh vertices using bilinear interpolation in cylindrical coordinates. These per-vertex admissible force values are stored in the USD model as a vertex-interpolated primvar for efficient runtime lookup. A companion color primvar visualizes this map as a heatmap.

**D. Grasp Candidate Generation, Functional-Goal Filtering, and Force-Map-Aware Re-Ranking**
1. **Grasp Representation:** Each grasp candidate for the Shadow Hand is defined by its hand base position (t), 6D rotation (r6D), and 24 target joint angles (θ).
2. **Candidate Generation via DexGraspNet:** Grasp candidates are generated using DexGraspNet, which employs gradient-based optimization of a composite energy function that includes terms for force-closure (stability), hand-object penetration, self-collisions, and joint-angle limits. Candidates are then validated in Isaac Gym, and unstable ones are discarded, yielding a set of physically feasible grasps G.
3. **Functional-Goal Filtering:** A functional criterion, cF, evaluated using TaskGrasp, filters the feasible grasps G based on the scene observation and target task (y). Candidates that do not meet a probability acceptance threshold τF are removed, resulting in G*.
4. **Force-Map-Aware Re-Ranking:** This is a central contribution. For the geometrically and functionally suitable grasps in G*, this stage selects the candidate with the most mechanically admissible contact regions.
 - **Contact region identification:** Fingertip positions for each grasp are obtained via forward kinematics and mapped to containing triangles on the object mesh.
 - **Admissible force at contact:** The admissible force F_i,k at each contact point is computed using barycentric interpolation over the vertices of its corresponding mesh triangle.
 - **Force-map score:** The mechanical quality of a grasp g_i is quantified by S_F(g_i) = min_k F_i,k − α · Var_k(F_i,k). This score rewards grasps where the weakest contact point has a high admissible force and penalizes high variance in admissible forces across fingers, promoting uniform load distribution. α controls this trade-off.
 - **Interaction mode modulation:** If the operator specified a "delicate" interaction (small λ), candidates with any contact point where F_i,k < λ · F_max are additionally discarded before re-ranking.
 - **Final grasp selection:** The grasp g* that maximizes S_F(g_i) is selected for execution.

**E. Pre-Grasp Motion Generation and Grip Execution**
1. **Pre-Grasp Motion Generation:** A pre-grasp configuration is created by retracting the hand along the palm approach axis with fingers in an open pose. A diffusion-based motion planner generates a trajectory between this pre-grasp configuration and the selected grasp g*, which is then executed by a joint-space PD controller.
2. **Grip Execution via Impedance Control:** Once contact is established, the system transitions to grip execution, aiming for sufficient retention force without exceeding locally admissible bounds.
 - **Contact state estimation:** The physics engine provides per-finger contact points, normals, and impulses. The admissible force Fmax_k,j is looked up for each contact, and the actual normal force Fest_k,j is estimated from the impulse. For each finger, the most critical contact point (highest load ratio Fest_k,j / Fmax_k,j) determines the per-finger control parameters: Fmax_k, nk (unit normal), and fk(t) (estimated force vector).
 - **Adaptive impedance controller:** A Cartesian impedance controller implements the grip. The normal stiffness k_n,k(t) for each finger is adaptively set based on the force map and interaction mode (λ): k_n,k(t) = γ_n * F_tar_k(t) / δ_n,max. Damping is co-tuned. This ensures that fingers contacting weaker surface regions operate with lower stiffness, while those on stronger regions can exert greater force, realizing a spatially non-uniform grip strategy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted in Isaac Sim using a Shadow Hand model, generating 50 DexGraspNet candidates per object.

**A. Experiment 1: Force-Map-Aware Grasp Selection**
Three objects were evaluated: a paper cup (7.6 g), a plastic cup (1.9 g), and a glass goblet (155 g). Different structural zones of these objects exhibited varying admissible lateral forces (Table I). For instance, the paper cup's rim allowed 41.2 N, while its wall allowed only 4.8 N. For the glass goblet, the bowl allowed 69 N, compared to the stem's 565 N.

After initial feasibility and functional-goal filtering, the top-M=5 candidates were compared using two selection strategies: a Baseline (highest utility score U(g) without force-map information) and the Proposed Method (maximizing the force-map score S_F(g_i)).

**Results (Table II):**
- **Zone Selection:** The proposed method consistently selected structurally reinforced zones for contact. For the cups, it prioritized the rim, and for the goblet, it selected the stem. In contrast, the baseline method frequently selected the weaker wall or bowl regions.
- **Minimum Admissible Force (F*min):** The proposed method significantly improved the minimum admissible contact force at the weakest contact point. Improvements were 8.6x for the paper cup (from 4.8 N to 41.2 N), 12.3x for the plastic cup (from 0.3 N to 3.7 N), and 8.2x for the glass goblet (from 69 N to 565 N).
- **Force-Bound Violations:** The proposed method resulted in zero force-bound violations in all tested configurations. The baseline method, however, showed violations (1/5 fingers for paper, 2/5 for plastic, 1/5 for goblet), indicating it would likely cause damage.

**Qualitative Illustration (Glass Goblet):** The goblet example demonstrated that two grasps could be geometrically similar (differing by less than 3% on classical metrics) but mechanically distinct. The force-map-aware re-ranking unambiguously selected a grasp on the stronger stem (F*min = 565 N) over a grasp on the weaker bowl (F*min = 69 N), as depicted in Figure 3.

**B. Experiment 2: Grip Force Regulation via Impedance Control**
This experiment used the plastic cup (admissible wall load of 0.3 N) to evaluate the effectiveness of the adaptive impedance controller. The force-map-selected grasp was fixed, and three controller conditions were tested:
1. **Under-stiffness (0.3·K_base):** This condition ignored the force map and used insufficient stiffness.
2. **Over-stiffness (2.0·K_base):** This condition also ignored the force map and used excessive stiffness.
3. **Proposed Controller (K_k = K_base·F*k/Fmax·λ, with λ=0.7):** This condition implemented the per-finger adaptive stiffness based on the force map and interaction mode.

**Results (Table III & IV, Figure 4):**
- **Under-stiffness:** The controller failed to generate sufficient friction, leading to object slippage and failure to retain the object.
- **Over-stiffness:** While the object was retained, the grip forces significantly exceeded the admissible load (f_max = 0.74 N compared to F*wall = 0.3 N), with 4 out of 5 fingers violating their force bounds. This would likely cause visible wall deformation.
- **Proposed Controller:** This controller achieved stable object retention with a safety margin of 1.43 and no force-bound violations (f_max = 0.21 N). This demonstrated successful per-finger calibration of forces according to local mechanical properties.
- **Interaction Mode (λ) Modulation:** Further tests with the proposed controller showed successful scaling of grip forces based on the operator's interaction mode. For "gently" (λ=0.3), the maximum force was 0.09 N, yielding a margin of 3.33. For "default" (λ=0.7), f_max was 0.21 N with a margin of 1.43. For "firmly" (λ=1.0), f_max was 0.29 N, resulting in a margin of 1.03. All modes achieved successful retention without violations, validating the end-to-end propagation of the language command to compliant grip execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in dexterous robotic manipulation by addressing the critical gap between purely geometric grasp planning and physically informed interaction. Its core contribution is the integration of spatially distributed mechanical properties, via a "force map," directly into both grasp selection and grip execution.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:

- **Enhanced Safety and Reliability:** By explicitly accounting for the spatially non-uniform mechanical properties of objects, the system can select grasps and apply forces that prevent object deformation or damage. This is particularly crucial for manipulating fragile, thin-walled, or heterogeneous materials, which are common in everyday environments and industrial settings. The demonstrated 8x to 12x improvement in minimum admissible contact force and the elimination of force-bound violations highlight this enhanced safety.
- **Physically Grounded Manipulation:** The work reframes dexterous manipulation from a purely kinematic and geometric problem into a physically grounded joint planning problem. This shift enables robots to perform more human-like, intuitive, and robust interactions by understanding not just where to grasp, but also how much force can be applied at each contact point.
- **Unified Pipeline for Intelligent Robotics:** The integration of advanced components—natural language understanding (LLMs), robust 3D perception and reconstruction (YOLO-World, SAM, SAM3D), state-of-the-art dexterous grasp generation (DexGraspNet), and adaptive impedance control—into a single coherent pipeline represents a comprehensive solution for intelligent robotic interaction. This provides a blueprint for developing more autonomous and versatile robotic systems.
- **Adaptive and Compliant Interaction:** The adaptive impedance control strategy, which scales per-finger stiffness based on local object strength and operator intent, enables a nuanced and compliant grip. This allows robots to handle objects with varying fragility and to execute tasks with different levels of force requirement (e.g., "gently" versus "firmly") effectively and safely.
- **Foundation for Future Humanoid Systems:** The approach of combining language-guided understanding with physical grounding is highly relevant for the development of future humanoid robots. Such robots will need to interact with a wide array of objects in unstructured human environments, where material properties and potential fragility are critical considerations.

Potential impacts include:
- **Industrial Automation:** Safer handling of delicate components, food items, or precision instruments.
- **Healthcare Robotics:** Assisting with tasks involving fragile medical equipment or patient interaction.
- **Service Robotics:** Enabling robots to perform household chores or elder care tasks that involve handling diverse everyday objects safely.
- **Logistics and Warehousing:** Reducing damage to goods, especially those with sensitive packaging or contents.

Future work aims to deploy this pipeline on physical robotic hardware, generalize it to more complex object geometries beyond cup-like shapes, and develop goal-aware grasp generation that inherently biases sampling towards mechanically favorable regions, further streamlining the planning process.
