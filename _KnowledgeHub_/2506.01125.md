# iRonCub 3: The Jet-Powered Flying Humanoid Robot

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia developed iRonCub 3, a jet-powered humanoid robot, which achieved its first controlled liftoff. This work validates the concept of aerial humanoid mobility, demonstrating the robot becoming airborne for brief periods while highlighting critical challenges in jet dynamics modeling and sensor robustness.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots are primarily restricted to terrestrial locomotion, limiting their operational scope in three-dimensional environments.
- Integrating full-body aerial mobility into a humanoid form introduces complex issues in robot control, state estimation, and system integration.
- Current aerial manipulators or hybrid aerial-terrestrial robots often lack the comprehensive physical interaction capabilities inherent to humanoids.

## Method

- The **iRonCub 3** robot was developed, based on the iCub3 humanoid platform, and augmented with four ==JetCat P250 Pro== turbines on custom forearms and a jetpack.
- A software architecture was implemented, featuring Unscented Kalman Filters for thrust and base pose estimation, and a Linear Parameter Varying Model Predictive Control for flight.
- A dedicated outdoor experimental facility was established, including safety protocols and heat management, for operating the jet-powered humanoid robot.

## Results

- Simulation results validated the control framework's ability to track desired center of mass position and orientation during takeoff and trajectory following.
- Experimental liftoff of the physical iRonCub 3 was achieved, with the robot briefly becoming airborne while maintaining roll, pitch, and yaw angles within approximately 5 degrees.
- Observed deviations, such as a horizontal drift of up to 60 cm, were attributed to the sim-to-real gap caused by simplified jet dynamics models, vibration-induced sensor noise, and inaccuracies in force-torque sensor calibration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The successful first controlled liftoff of iRonCub 3 confirms the fundamental feasibility of integrating jet propulsion with a full-body humanoid robot.
> - Achieving stable and repeatable aerial maneuvers requires precise modeling of highly dynamic jet propulsion and robust sensor performance amidst significant vibration.
> - Comprehensive mechanical and software integration is critical for developing multi-modal humanoid robots capable of operating in complex 3D environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots have traditionally focused on terrestrial locomotion and manipulation, operating in environments designed for human interaction. The integration of aerial mobility into these platforms represents an evolving area of research, aiming to expand their operational capabilities into three-dimensional spaces.

Existing research in this domain has explored several approaches:
- **Aerial Manipulators:** These systems typically involve multirotor platforms equipped with robotic arms or grippers, enhancing their ability to interact physically with environments.
- **Hybrid Aerial-Terrestrial Systems:** This category includes robots that combine different locomotion modes, such as hexapod-quadrotor hybrids, quadrotors with wheels or legs, bio-inspired insect robots capable of both flight and ground movement, and reconfigurable structures that adapt their morphology for varied locomotion.
- **Humanoids with Thrust Augmentation:** Some studies have investigated humanoid robots augmented with thrusters to support bipedal motion or facilitate short bursts of flight. These often use thrusters to assist ground-based movements rather than enabling sustained flight.

The iRonCub project, to which this paper contributes, represents a more ambitious trajectory. It seeks to integrate aerial, terrestrial, and manipulation capabilities within a single, full-body humanoid platform, specifically leveraging the iCub humanoid robot. This approach distinguishes itself by aiming for a unified flight capability inherent to the humanoid form, rather than simply equipping an aerial robot with humanoid-like manipulators or using thrusters for gait assistance. The current work, introducing iRonCub 3, builds upon earlier iterations like iRonCub-Mk1 and the iCub3 platform, pushing the boundaries of what is considered achievable for humanoid robots in dynamic, multi-modal environments. The challenge lies in addressing the unique control, estimation, and system integration complexities posed by a bipedal, human-scale robot employing jet propulsion for flight.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the iRonCub project is to enable a humanoid robot to achieve flight while retaining its full-body humanoid form and capabilities. This endeavor presents unique challenges in control, estimation, and system integration compared to conventional aerial vehicles or hybrid robot systems.

The specific objectives of this article are:
- To present iRonCub 3, the latest iteration of the jet-powered humanoid robot, detailing its current mechanical and software architecture.
- To highlight the design and integration of its propulsion system, its control framework, and the experimental infrastructure developed for its operation.
- To validate the robot’s control and estimation framework through simulation, demonstrating its ability to perform takeoff and track a reference trajectory in a virtual environment.
- To experimentally demonstrate the first liftoff of a jet-powered humanoid robot, marking a foundational step toward aerial humanoid mobility.
- To describe the design considerations for an experimental area suitable for operating jet-powered humanoid robots, addressing the inherent complexities and safety requirements.

The motivation behind pursuing aerial humanoid mobility stems from the potential for these robots to operate effectively in complex environments designed for humans, where purely ground-based or conventional aerial systems face limitations. Potential applications span several high-impact domains:
- **Search and Rescue:** Flying humanoids could navigate collapsed buildings, steep terrain, or flooded zones, combining flight with physical interaction capabilities like opening doors or lifting debris.
- **Infrastructure Inspection and Maintenance:** These robots could access hard-to-reach locations such as bridges, high-rise structures, or industrial plants, performing dexterous tasks like turning valves or manipulating tools.
- **Disaster Response:** In hazardous environments (e.g., nuclear facilities, chemical plants), flying humanoids could operate in human-centric spaces while minimizing exposure risks.
- **Telepresence and Human-Avatar Systems:** The ability to navigate complex 3D environments while mimicking human motion could be beneficial for virtual interaction, remote inspection, or training scenarios.

By leveraging human-like morphology, aerial humanoid robots aim to combine the spatial access and reach of flying robots with the intuitive physical interaction capabilities of humanoids, potentially expanding the scope of robotic deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research involved the development of a jet-powered humanoid robot, iRonCub-Mk3, and a control architecture to enable its flight.

**Robot Platform (iRonCub-Mk3):**
The iRonCub-Mk3 platform is based on the iCub3 humanoid robot. It is equipped with four JetCat P250 Pro turbines: two mounted on custom-designed forearms and two integrated into a custom jetpack.

**Mechanical Design:**
- **Jetpack Design:** The jetpack was custom-manufactured and integrated with the iCub3’s central block using load-bearing brackets. Design considerations included ease of assembly/disassembly, minimal volume, structural integrity to withstand jet engine forces, and minimal modification to the iCub3. Slight tilt angles were incorporated for stability and to mitigate exhaust heat impact on the robot’s lower body.
- **Forearms Design:** The prototype configuration removed the robot’s hands to prioritize flight capability testing, with jets mounted below the elbows. The forearms were designed for future reintegration of manipulation capabilities.
- **Structural Analysis:** Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis was conducted on both the jetpack and forearms. Boundary conditions were fixed at assembly points, and axial loads of 750N (three times the peak thrust per turbine) were applied to ensure structural integrity against jet engine forces.

**Software Architecture:**
The high-level software architecture comprises three main components: Base Pose Estimator, Thrust Estimator and Jet Control, and Flight Controller. These components communicate using the YARP middleware and run on the robot’s onboard computer. All software was initially tested in a Gazebo simulation environment, leveraging YARP Gazebo plugins to ensure consistent interfaces between simulation and hardware. Noise from real-world turbine vibrations was added to simulated sensor data to reduce the sim-to-real gap.

- **Thrust Estimation and Jet Control:** An Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) was implemented to estimate thrust. It combines measurements from force-torque (FT) sensors on the robot’s arms and two additional FT sensors on the jetpack with a second-order nonlinear dynamic model that predicts thrust based on throttle input. The FT sensors were calibrated in situ. The dynamic model was developed using a dedicated test bench for controlled jet engine experiments. The Jet Control module transmits throttle commands to the turbines at 10 Hz.
- **Base Pose Estimation:** A UKF fuses data from an Xsens MTI-670G Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) and a RealSense T265 depth camera. This provides estimates of the robot’s position, orientation, linear velocity, and angular velocity at 200 Hz.
- **Flight Controller:** The control system utilizes a Linear Parameter Varying Model Predictive Control (MPC) strategy. This MPC integrates the centroidal momentum equations with the nonlinear second-order model of jet dynamics to compute optimal joint positions and jet throttle commands for trajectory tracking. It is designed to accommodate the different actuation frequencies of the jets (10 Hz) and joints (1000 Hz). A limitation of this MPC is its reliance on Euler angles, which are susceptible to singularities, though this is mitigated by focusing on Vertical Take-Off and Landing (VTOL) maneuvers that avoid a 90-degree pitch. The controller’s linearisation also limits its suitability for aggressive maneuvers.
 - **Take-off phase:** During ground contact, the centroidal momentum equation is modified to include contact forces. A parameter `α` is introduced to regulate the percentage of body weight compensated by thrust, starting from 0 and reaching 1 upon liftoff.

**Experimental Infrastructure:**
Experiments were conducted in a dedicated outdoor flight area on the terrace of the Center for Robotics and Intelligent Systems (CRIS) at the Italian Institute of Technology. The facility includes three zones: the iRonCub Area for experiments, a Control Room for monitoring and software execution, and a Storage Bunker for equipment and fuel.
- **iRonCub Area:** The robot is suspended from a 2-meter crane, providing maneuverability while being securely tethered for safety. The robot’s lower section is covered with heat-resistant Aerogel to protect it from jet exhaust. Raised platforms with cement tiles minimize heat buildup during tests. Jet fuel is supplied from tanks in an explosion-proof container.
- **Control Room:** Laptops connect to the robot via SSH for remote software execution. A separate machine handles data logging and video recording. Real-time telemetry visualization aids monitoring. Safety protocols require at least two team members in the Control Room and one near the experimental area to directly monitor and report issues.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents results from both simulation and physical experiments with the iRonCub-Mk3 robot.

**Simulation Results:**
The overall software infrastructure was tested in a Gazebo simulation. The robot was tasked with performing a takeoff, ascending approximately 1 meter, and following a square trajectory while maintaining a constant desired orientation.
- The controller demonstrated the ability to track the desired center of mass position and base orientation.
- For the specified "slow and smooth trajectory," the robot exhibited good tracking performance. This confirmed the effectiveness of the control and estimation pipeline in an idealized computational environment, providing a foundation for real-world deployment.

**Experimental Results on the Real Robot:**
A controlled liftoff experiment was performed with the physical iRonCub-Mk3.
- **Thrust Profiles:** Analysis of thrust intensities from the four turbines indicated that the take-off phase initiated around 5 seconds into the experiment and concluded at approximately 30 seconds. During this period, the parameter `α`, regulating the percentage of body weight compensated by thrust, increased from 0 to 1.
- **Tracking Performance:**
 - The robot maintained its roll, pitch, and yaw angles within approximately 5 degrees throughout the test.
 - However, a noticeable horizontal drift was observed, with a displacement of about −20 cm along the x-axis and approximately 60 cm along the y-axis.
 - The vertical (z) coordinate began to increase approximately 2.5 seconds after the command, indicating liftoff. The robot remained airborne for approximately 3 seconds, briefly touched down, and then lifted off again, overshooting the target altitude for another 3 seconds.
 - The robot subsequently lost altitude and was automatically powered down by the flight controller when the orientation error exceeded a predefined threshold.
- **Observed Deviations and Challenges:** The deviations from expected behavior were attributed to several factors contributing to the sim-to-real gap:
 - The Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework relies on a simplified model of jet dynamics, which did not fully capture the highly dynamic behavior of the real turbines.
 - Vibrations induced by the jet propulsion system propagated through the robot's structure, affecting onboard inertial and velocity estimation sensors, leading to noisy measurements. This degradation in sensor data impacted control performance.
 - Inaccuracies in the calibration of the force-torque sensors further complicated thrust estimation, compounding the control difficulties.

Despite these challenges, the experiment successfully demonstrated a controlled liftoff of the jet-powered humanoid robot, representing an initial but significant step toward achieving aerial humanoid mobility.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the iRonCub 3, a jet-powered humanoid robot, and reports on its first controlled liftoff experiments. The work is significant for several reasons:
- **Validation of Aerial Humanoid Concept:** The successful liftoff, even with observed deviations, provides empirical validation for the fundamental concept of a full-body humanoid robot achieving flight via jet propulsion. This advances the iRonCub project’s long-term goal of unifying aerial, terrestrial, and manipulation capabilities within a single humanoid platform.
- **System Integration and Architectural Design:** The paper details the mechanical integration of jet engines into a humanoid form (jetpack and forearm mounts) and outlines a comprehensive software architecture for control and estimation. This provides a blueprint for future developments in complex multi-modal robot design.
- **Identification of Key Challenges:** The experimental results clearly highlight critical technical challenges inherent in this domain. These include the accurate modeling of highly dynamic jet propulsion systems, the impact of significant vibrations on onboard sensor performance and estimation accuracy, and the robustness of control systems in the presence of real-world disturbances and modeling inaccuracies. Explicitly identifying these challenges provides clear directions for future research.
- **Development of Specialized Infrastructure:** The design and implementation of a dedicated experimental area, including safety protocols and heat management solutions for jet-powered robots, contributes practical knowledge essential for conducting such high-risk experiments.

The potential impact of this research extends to various domains where robots require both aerial access and human-like interaction capabilities. These include search and rescue operations in complex, inaccessible terrains, inspection and maintenance tasks on large structures or in hazardous industrial environments, and disaster response scenarios where human-robot collaboration is crucial. By laying the groundwork for aerial humanoid mobility, this work contributes to the development of highly versatile robots that could navigate and operate in environments specifically designed for human beings, thereby opening new frontiers in robotics deployment. Future work, focusing on improving estimation, enhancing controller robustness, and achieving repeatable full take-off and landing, is positioned to address the identified challenges and move closer to these envisioned applications.
