# Multimodal Spatial Language Maps for Robot Navigation and Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Multimodal Spatial Language Maps enable robots to build rich 3D spatial representations by fusing visual, audio, and language features from pre-trained foundation models. This allows for zero-shot navigation and manipulation to open-vocabulary, spatially-grounded, and multimodal goals, significantly improving disambiguation in complex environments.

## Problem

- Traditional robot maps lack semantic understanding for natural language commands and spatial precision for non-object-centric goals.
- Existing language-grounded navigation methods often lack persistent, reusable map representations and struggle to adapt to different robot embodiments.
- Robot perception systems largely overlook valuable sensory inputs like audio, limiting their ability to resolve ambiguities in complex environments.

## Method

- A general framework called "**Multimodal Spatial Language Maps**" is introduced, implemented as VLMaps (==Visual-Language==) and AVLMaps (Audio-Visual-Language).
- Maps are constructed by fusing dense features from pre-trained multimodal foundation models (VLMs, ALMs) with 3D environmental reconstructions.
- A Large Language Model (LLM) is employed for high-level planning, translating natural language commands into executable API calls for spatial and multimodal goal localization, including cross-modal reasoning via heatmap multiplication.

## Results

- Visual-Language Maps (VLMaps) achieved a **62%** success rate for single spatial subgoals in simulation, outperforming baselines and successfully generating tailored obstacle maps for different robot embodiments.
- Audio-Visual-Language Maps (AVLMaps) demonstrated a **77.5%** success rate for sound goal navigation in simulation and substantially higher recall (e.g., **70.59%** recall@2m) for cross-modal disambiguation.
- The system achieved a **50%** overall success rate for language-based multimodal navigation on a real mobile robot and high success rates for tabletop manipulation tasks, indicating real-world applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing dense, spatially grounded features from multiple foundation models (vision, audio, language) into a unified 3D map enables robust open-vocabulary, multimodal goal understanding.
> - Large Language Models can effectively serve as a high-level planner, translating complex natural language commands into robot actions by interacting with a rich spatial map.
> - Leveraging complementary information across modalities (e.g., visual and audio cues) significantly enhances goal disambiguation in ambiguous environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the exciting and rapidly evolving intersection of robotic mapping, multimodal perception, and language-grounded robotics, heavily leveraging recent advancements in large foundation models.

Traditionally, robot navigation has relied on **geometric mapping** techniques (e.g., SLAM), creating precise 3D reconstructions or 2D occupancy grids. While excellent for path planning and collision avoidance, these maps inherently lack semantic understanding, making it challenging for robots to interpret high-level human commands or navigate to abstract goals. The first major evolution was **semantic mapping**, which augmented geometric maps with predefined object categories. However, these systems were limited by fixed vocabularies and struggled to generalize to unseen objects or situations.

The advent of **vision-language models (VLMs)** like CLIP and **large language models (LLMs)** has revolutionized this field. Recent works have focused on **open-vocabulary semantic mapping** (e.g., LM-Nav, CoW, OpenScene, CLIP-Fields), enabling robots to identify and navigate to objects described in natural language, even if they weren't explicitly trained on those objects. This paper directly builds upon this trend, particularly drawing from the authors' previous "VLMaps" work, which already established a strong baseline for fusing VLM features with 3D reconstructions.

However, the authors identify critical limitations in existing open-vocabulary navigation methods:
1. **Disconnection from mapping:** Many VLM-based navigation strategies use VLMs as "critics" to match observations but don't deeply integrate language semantics into a persistent, spatial map of the environment. This leads to a lack of spatial precision for non-object-centric goals (e.g., "in between the sofa and TV").
2. **Lack of persistent representation:** Maps often aren't reusable across different robot embodiments or for long-term tasks.
3. **Neglect of other modalities:** Most systems focus almost exclusively on visual and language cues, overlooking the rich information available in other sensory inputs like audio.

This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a *unified, multimodal spatial map representation*. It integrates not just vision and language but also **audio**, a relatively underexplored modality for semantic mapping and navigation. While **multimodal navigation** has seen research in specific areas like audio-visual navigation (AVN) or audio-visual-language embodied navigation (AVLEN), these often focus on approaching sound sources reactively or in simulated environments, without building a persistent, semantically rich map. This work aims to bridge this by incorporating sound semantics directly into a 3D spatial memory.

Furthermore, the paper leverages the burgeoning field of **pre-trained zero-shot models in robotics**. It not only uses VLMs (LSeg, OVSeg) and LLMs (text-davinci-003) for perception and high-level reasoning but also specifically integrates **audio-language models (ALMs)** like AudioCLIP and CLAP. This highlights a cutting-edge approach that aims to combine the strengths of various foundation models into a cohesive robotic system. The concurrent work like ConceptFusion is acknowledged, but this paper differentiates itself by *integrating* audio information into the map structure itself, rather than solely using audio as a query modality.

In essence, this research pushes the boundaries of robot cognition by creating a richer, more human-like spatial understanding of the world, moving beyond isolated perception modules to a truly integrated, language-queryable, and multimodal map.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this work stems from observing how humans navigate and interact with their environment. Humans build sophisticated "cognitive maps" that integrate information from diverse sensory modalities (sight, sound, etc.) and leverage natural language for spatial reasoning. They can localize landmarks at various ontological levels (e.g., a "book," "on the shelf," "in the living room") and infer navigational possibilities from the environment's layout. This remarkable human ability forms the aspirational benchmark for autonomous robots.

The paper identifies several critical limitations in existing robot navigation and mapping paradigms, which collectively serve as the primary motivations:

1. **Lack of Spatial Precision and Open-Vocabulary Grounding:** Traditional geometric maps lack semantic understanding, while earlier language-grounded systems struggle with generalization or require extensive data. More recent VLM-based navigation methods, while open-vocabulary, often operate disjointly from environment mapping, failing to provide the fine-grained spatial precision needed for complex commands like "in between the sofa and TV" or "three meters to the east of the chair."
2. **Limited Reusability and Adaptability:** Existing maps often cannot be persistently shared across different robot embodiments (e.g., a mobile robot vs. a drone) or adapt dynamically to varying task requirements (e.g., defining different obstacle types for different robots).
3. **Unexploited Multimodality (especially Audio):** Most advanced robot perception systems heavily rely on vision and language, neglecting other valuable sensory inputs. Acoustic signals, in particular, offer complementary information for disambiguation and situational awareness (e.g., the sound of a microwave indicating a task completion, or a baby crying signaling a location of interest). Integrating audio semantically into a spatial map is largely an open question.
4. **Inefficient Data Collection and Fine-tuning:** Learning-based navigation policies typically demand copious amounts of training data, limiting their real-world applicability and zero-shot generalization capabilities.

To address these motivations and limitations, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a general mapping framework called "Multimodal Spatial Language Maps"** that is:
 - **(i) Spatial:** Capable of localizing precise spatial goals, not just object centroids.
 - **(ii) Multimodal:** Integrating information from multiple sensing modalities beyond just vision and language.
 - **(iii) Reusable across different robot embodiments:** Allowing the same map to generate tailored information (e.g., obstacle maps) for diverse robots.
 - **(iv) Readily extensible:** Designed to accommodate additional sensing modalities in the future.
2. **Introduce two concrete instances of this framework:**
 - **Visual-Language Maps (VLMaps):** Fusing pre-trained visual-language features with a 3D reconstruction, enabling spatial language goal localization and dynamic obstacle map generation.
 - **Audio-Visual-Language Maps (AVLMaps):** Extending VLMaps to unify 3D spatial representation with audio, visual, and language cues through fusion of features from pre-trained multimodal foundation models.
3. **Enable Zero-Shot Spatial and Multimodal Goal Navigation:** Allow robots to understand and execute natural language commands that specify open-vocabulary, spatially-grounded, and multimodal goals without requiring task-specific data collection or model fine-tuning.
4. **Enhance Goal Disambiguation:** Leverage diverse sensory inputs to significantly improve the robot's ability to disambiguate ambiguous goals in complex environments (e.g., "the table where you heard coughing" when multiple tables exist).
5. **Demonstrate Real-World Applicability:** Validate the proposed maps and navigation capabilities in both simulated and real-world settings, across different robotic platforms (mobile robots and tabletop manipulators).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core idea behind Multimodal Spatial Language Maps is to build a rich, persistent 3D spatial map of an environment by fusing dense features from pre-trained multimodal foundation models with a 3D reconstruction. The authors propose two main instances: VLMaps (Visual-Language Maps) and AVLMaps (Audio-Visual-Language Maps).

#### 3.1. Visual-Language Maps (VLMaps)

VLMaps primarily focus on integrating visual and language information into a spatial map.
- **Map Construction:**
 - The robot explores the environment, collecting RGB-D (color and depth) video streams and camera odometry (pose information).
 - For each RGB image, a pre-trained Visual-Language Model (VLM), specifically **LSeg** (which maps pixel-level features to the CLIP feature space), is used to compute dense pixel-level embeddings.
 - These 2D pixel embeddings are then back-projected onto the 3D surface of the environment using corresponding depth data and transformed into the world frame using odometry.
 - These 3D points and their associated visual-language features are then projected onto a 2D top-down grid map. For grid cells where multiple 3D points project, their embeddings are averaged, resulting in a dense 2D feature map `M` where each cell contains a rich visual-language embedding. This averaging across multiple views enhances robustness.
- **Open-Vocabulary Landmark Localization:**
 - Given a natural language query (e.g., "chair," "sofa"), a pre-trained **CLIP text encoder** converts it into a language embedding.
 - The map embeddings `M` are then compared (using cosine similarity) with the language embedding.
 - The cell with the highest similarity score corresponds to the most likely location of the queried concept, effectively providing an open-vocabulary semantic segmentation of the map.
- **Open-Vocabulary Obstacle Map Generation:**
 - VLMaps can dynamically generate tailored obstacle maps for different robot embodiments (e.g., a ground robot vs. a drone).
 - Users provide a list of natural language obstacle categories (e.g., "table," "chair" for a ground robot; just "wall," "stairs" for a drone).
 - The landmark localization process identifies these obstacles on the map. By taking the union of the corresponding segmentation masks and filtering out floor/ceiling points, a binary obstacle map is created. This allows robots to plan paths efficiently based on their physical constraints and capabilities.
- **Zero-Shot Spatial Goal Navigation with LLMs:**
 - **High-Level Planning:** A Large Language Model (LLM), specifically **OpenAI's text-davinci-003**, is used to interpret complex natural language commands (e.g., "move first to the left side of the counter, then move between the sink and the oven").
 - **Code Generation:** The LLM is primed with few-shot examples (context prompt) demonstrating how to translate such commands into executable Python code, utilizing a predefined library of navigation APIs (e.g., `move_to_left(object_name)`, `move_in_between(obj_a, obj_b)`). This leverages the LLM's code-writing capabilities.
 - **Spatial Reasoning:** The API functions use the VLMap to localize the objects mentioned in the command. Spatial relations (e.g., "left of," "in between") are resolved by applying predefined geometric offsets relative to the localized object(s).
 - **Low-Level Execution:** The computed spatial goal coordinates are fed into an off-the-shelf navigation stack (e.g., ROS navigation package) that uses the embodiment-specific obstacle map (generated from VLMap) for path planning and execution (e.g., `move forward 0.1m`, `turn left 5 degrees`).

#### 3.2. Audio-Visual-Language Maps (AVLMaps)

AVLMaps extend VLMaps by incorporating audio information, aiming for a truly multimodal spatial representation. The core structure involves maintaining a 3D voxel map (instead of 2D grid) where each occupied voxel is associated with multimodal features.
- **Multi-Module Heatmap Generation:** AVLMaps utilize four distinct modules, each responsible for generating a 3D voxel heatmap (indicating probability of target location) based on a specific modality or type of query:
 - **Visual Localization Module:** Localizes a query image. It uses a hierarchical localization scheme (NetVLAD for global scene matching, SuperPoint/SuperGLUE for local feature matching) to estimate the query image's 3D camera pose, which then forms the center of a decaying probability heatmap (`H_v`).
 - **Object Localization Module:** Similar to VLMaps, but maintains 3D voxel-wise visual-language features (from LSeg/OpenSeg). Queries with natural language object descriptions (e.g., "sofa") generate an object heatmap (`H_o`). This module also informs the 3D semantic voxel map used for obstacle generation.
 - **Area Localization Module:** For coarser spatial concepts (e.g., "the kitchen"). It leverages a sparse topological map of **CLIP visual features** associated with robot poses. A language query (e.g., "bedroom area") is compared to these CLIP features, generating an area heatmap (`H_a`) where higher scores indicate better matches.
 - **Audio Localization Module:** Processes the robot's audio stream. It segments audio (e.g., by silence detection) and encodes each segment using pre-trained **Audio-Language Models (ALMs)** like AudioCLIP or CLAP. These audio features are associated with the robot's pose at the time of recording. A language query about a sound (e.g., "the sound of door knocks") is encoded and matched with stored audio features, resulting in an audio heatmap (`H_s`).
- **Cross-Modality Reasoning:** This is a crucial innovation for disambiguation. When a command refers to multiple modalities (e.g., "the sound of baby crying near the sofa"), the heatmaps generated by the relevant modules are combined via **element-wise multiplication** (`H_target = H_1 ⊙ ... ⊙ H_L`). This effectively leverages complementary information to narrow down ambiguous locations. A key aspect is assigning different "decay rates" to heatmaps: a higher rate for the primary target (more precise localization) and a lower rate for auxiliary information (broader constraint).
- **Multimodal Goal Navigation from Language (Enhanced LLM Interface):**
 - The LLM (text-davinci-003) is again used with few-shot prompting, but with an expanded API library that allows it to:
 - `load_image(path)`: Integrate image queries.
 - `get_major_map(modality_query)`: Generate heatmaps for primary targets with high decay.
 - `get_map(modality_query)`: Generate heatmaps for auxiliary constraints with low decay.
 - `get_max_pose_3d(heatmap)`: Extract the highest probability 3D coordinate from a heatmap.
 - The LLM generates Python code that orchestrates calls to these APIs, including performing element-wise multiplication for cross-modal reasoning and simple arithmetic for spatial relationships (e.g., averaging two positions for "in between").
 - The resulting 3D goal position is then projected to 2D for path planning using the standard navigation stack. Real-world audio undergoes denoising as a pre-processing step.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted in both simulation (Habitat, AI2THOR) and real-world settings (mobile robot, tabletop manipulator), demonstrate the efficacy and advantages of Multimodal Spatial Language Maps.

- **Zero-Shot Spatial Goal Navigation (VLMaps):**
 - VLMaps significantly outperform baseline open-vocabulary navigation methods (LM-Nav, CoW, CLIP Map) in tasks requiring precise spatial understanding. For instance, in Habitat, VLMaps achieved a 62% success rate for single spatial subgoals and a 10% success rate for sequences of four subgoals, whereas baselines typically failed for sequential tasks (0%). This validates VLMaps' ability to ground language to fine-grained spatial locations beyond just object centroids.

- **Cross-Embodiment Navigation (VLMaps):**
 - In the AI2THOR simulator, VLMaps successfully generated tailored obstacle maps for different robot embodiments (LoCoBot vs. Drone) from a single map. The drone, when using its customized "drone map" (which ignores ground-level obstacles like tables), achieved comparable path efficiency (SPL) to the LoCoBot with its "ground map," while significantly outperforming a drone forced to use a "ground map." This highlights the practical utility of the reusable map representation for multi-robot scenarios.

- **Multimodal Goal Navigation (AVLMaps - Simulation):**
 - **Sound Goal Navigation:** AVLMaps achieved a high independent success rate of 77.5% for navigating to sound goals specified by language (e.g., "the sound of a baby crying"), demonstrating its effective integration and understanding of sound semantics.
 - **Visual and Object Goals Navigation:** The system successfully navigated to sequences involving visual and object goals, with a 71.5% success rate for the first subgoal and 25% for a sequence of three, indicating robust multimodal comprehension.

- **Cross-Modal Goal Indexing (AVLMaps - Simulation):**
 - **Area-Object Disambiguation:** AVLMaps, particularly with its CLIP sparse map for general concepts, showed superior recall (e.g., 35.56% recall@2m) compared to VLMaps and ConceptFusion when disambiguating objects based on their region (e.g., "an object X in the area of Y").
 - **Object-Sound Disambiguation:** In ambiguous scenarios (multiple sound sources of the same type), AVLMaps achieved substantially higher recall (e.g., 70.59% recall@2m with AudioCLIP) than unimodal baselines, effectively using object information to pinpoint the correct sound location (e.g., "a sound X near object Y").
 - **Visual-Object Disambiguation:** Similarly, visual cues significantly improved object localization, with AVLMaps achieving over 60% recall@0.5m, demonstrating how an image can disambiguate an object goal.

- **Multimodal Ambiguous Goal Navigation (AVLMaps - Simulation):**
 - AVLMaps demonstrated a 46.2% success rate for navigating to ambiguous sound goals and 55.5% for ambiguous object goals in sequential tasks. This represents a significant improvement (e.g., 24.2% higher for sound goals) over multimodal baselines that do not leverage the same cross-modal reasoning framework.

- **Scaling Experiment:**
 - The modularity of AVLMaps was tested by swapping underlying foundation models. Performance in sound goal navigation notably improved by 10% when upgrading from AudioCLIP to the more advanced CLAP, confirming that AVLMaps can evolve with better-performing models.
 - Improvements from newer Visual Language Models (OVSeg, SAM+CLIP) for dense pixel-level features were less pronounced than expected, suggesting that further research is needed in VLM architectures specifically tailored for robot mapping.

- **Real-World Mobile Robot Experiments:**
 - AVLMaps achieved a 50% overall success rate for language-based multimodal navigation in a real room with challenging ambiguities (duplicate objects, artificially inserted sounds).
 - Qualitative results visually confirmed the system's ability to localize ambiguous objects, sounds, and visual goals as 3D heatmaps and effectively fuse them for disambiguation. Failures were primarily attributed to imperfections in individual perception modules (e.g., VLM misclassifying rare objects).

- **Real-World Tabletop Manipulation Experiments:**
 - AVLMaps demonstrated excellent performance in table-top goal reaching tasks, with high success rates (100% for visual/sound goals, 9/13 for object goals, and 9/10 for ambiguous goals) under stricter tolerance (within 10 cm). This extends the applicability of the framework to fine-grained manipulation, requiring detailed semantic understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Multimodal Spatial Language Maps" framework represents a significant advancement in robotic perception, cognition, and human-robot interaction, with several profound implications:

1. **Towards Human-like Spatial Cognition for Robots:** By creating a unified 3D spatial map that integrates visual, audio, and language cues, this work moves robots closer to human-like cognitive mapping. This enables robots to build richer internal representations of their environment, moving beyond purely geometric or limited semantic understanding.
2. **Intuitive and Natural Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability to understand and act upon open-vocabulary, spatially-grounded, and multimodal natural language commands (e.g., "go to the table where you heard coughing," "in between the sofa and TV") makes robot control far more intuitive and accessible for non-expert users. This reduces the need for complex programming or predefined interaction protocols.
3. **Enhanced Robustness and Disambiguation in Complex Environments:** The core strength of multimodal fusion, particularly demonstrated through cross-modal reasoning, is its ability to resolve ambiguities. In real-world settings rife with similar-looking objects or recurring sounds, leveraging complementary sensory information to pinpoint a specific goal is critical for reliable robot operation. This significantly improves navigation success rates in challenging, ambiguous scenarios.
4. **Zero-Shot Generalization and Reduced Data Dependency:** By building upon powerful pre-trained foundation models (VLMs, ALMs, LLMs), the system exhibits impressive zero-shot capabilities. This drastically reduces the laborious and costly process of collecting and annotating vast amounts of task-specific data, accelerating robot deployment and adaptability to novel environments and tasks.
5. **Reusable and Extensible Robotic Memory:** The map is designed to be persistent and reusable across different robot embodiments, addressing a key challenge in multi-robot systems or when adapting robots for varied tasks. Its modular and extensible architecture means it can readily incorporate new sensing modalities (e.g., temperature, tactile feedback) and benefit from future advancements in foundation models, ensuring long-term relevance and adaptability.
6. **Bridge Between Perception and Action:** The framework effectively bridges the gap between high-level human commands and low-level robot actions by leveraging LLMs to generate executable code that directly interacts with the spatial map. This provides a robust and flexible pipeline for robot planning and control.
7. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The paper openly discusses its limitations, such as handling transient sounds, accurate sound source localization, and dynamic scenes. These identified challenges serve as clear and inspiring directions for future research in multimodal robotics, encouraging the community to address these complex, real-world problems.

In summary, "Multimodal Spatial Language Maps" represent a substantial leap towards creating more intelligent, versatile, and human-centric robots. By endowing robots with a comprehensive, adaptable, and intuitive understanding of their physical world, this research paves the way for a new generation of autonomous systems capable of operating effectively and seamlessly in complex human environments.
