# HATS: A Human-Agent Teleoperation System for Multi-Arm Data Collection

> [!summary] Summary
> HATS is a human-agent teleoperation system letting one operator collect four-arm manipulation demonstrations. It decouples control by role: the human pilots two primary arms via a leader-follower interface, while a training-free MLLM copilot (AnyGrasp perception, Qwen-VL grounding, Gemini planner, motion primitives) drives two assistive arms, with real-time voice intervention. Across seven real tasks it reaches 86.4% average success versus 90.0% for expert dual-human teams.

## Problem

- Most teleoperation rigs support only single-arm or dual-arm setups, so demonstrations for tasks needing three or four coordinated arms cannot be collected at scale.
- Single-operator multi-arm control forces mode-switching between arms, imposing heavy cognitive load and yielding discontinuous, inconsistent demonstration trajectories.
- Multi-operator control cuts individual workload but adds labor cost, communication latency, and inconsistent inter-operator actions that degrade demonstration quality.

## Method

- ==Pilot-Copilot decoupling==: human teleoperates two primary arms through a kinematically isomorphic leader-follower interface, while an ==MLLM== agent controls two assistive arms on supportive sub-tasks.
- Perception plus planning stack: ==AnyGrasp== with DBSCAN clustering yields grasp poses, ==Qwen-VL== grounds placement targets, and a ==Gemini== planner emits validated, cached plans with collision-aware sequential scheduling.
- Motion-primitive library (Pick, Place, Pour, Go Home) composed from Cartesian, joint, and gripper control, plus an asynchronous ASR supervision thread parsing voice commands that preempt the plan.

## Results

- **HATS** averaged **86.4%** success across seven tasks versus 50.7% for solo mode-switching and 35.0% for the full-agent baseline, approaching the **90.0%** dual-human ceiling.
- Diffusion Policy trained on HATS data reached **71.3%** task completion rate versus 70.3% for dual-human data; the hybrid deployment (learned primary arms, primitives for assistive arms) hit **86.9%**.
- Voice intervention succeeded in **90%** of collision stress tests (sole failure from ASR error) while the unsupervised baseline failed all; 13 novice users averaged **86.58%** success with reduced NASA-TLX load.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing a high-dimensional control space by functional role, not by arm index, lets one operator exceed their concurrency limit without mode-switching penalties.
> - Agent assistance can beat human teams on tightly coupled tasks by removing interpersonal communication latency, but fully autonomous collection still fails on complex manipulation.
> - Caching validated plans and running only perception per trial keeps MLLM latency out of the high-frequency control loop, though predefined primitives limit adaptability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic systems capable of performing complex manipulation tasks often necessitates the coordination of multiple robotic arms. Such multi-arm systems can operate in large workspaces, improve task efficiency through parallelization, and enable novel manipulation capabilities that are beyond the scope of bimanual systems. A critical requirement for achieving these capabilities is the collection of real-world demonstrations to train data-driven multi-arm manipulation policies.

Current teleoperation systems predominantly focus on single-arm or dual-arm configurations. The limited existing multi-arm teleoperation frameworks generally fall into two categories, each presenting distinct challenges:
1. **Single-operator control of multi-arm systems:** These systems often impose a high cognitive burden on the human operator, frequently requiring mode-switching between arms. This can lead to discontinuous or inconsistent demonstrations, thereby affecting the quality of data for policy learning. Previous approaches in this category typically rely on classical shared autonomy mechanisms or mode-switching paradigms, which often involve rigid symmetric mappings or task-specific constraints, limiting adaptability in unstructured environments.
2. **Multi-operator control of multi-arm systems:** While distributing the workload, this setup incurs high labor costs and significant coordination demands. Communication delays and inconsistencies between operators can negatively impact the quality and coherence of collected demonstrations.

Recent advancements in shared autonomy have moved towards data-driven human-in-the-loop paradigms, with some systems exploring zero-shot intent recognition using vision-language models. Concurrently, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown promise in robotic planning, particularly for task decomposition and long-horizon reasoning. However, their real-time deployment in high-frequency teleoperation, especially in human-in-the-loop control, has been limited due to concerns regarding latency, reliability, and safety.

HATS addresses this research gap by proposing a human-agent teleoperation system that combines the strengths of human dexterity and MLLM-based autonomous assistance. It aims to overcome the trade-off between operator cognitive load and multi-operator coordination costs by decoupling the control space and leveraging MLLMs for real-time assistive arm control, thereby enabling a single human operator to collect data for complex multi-arm tasks efficiently and effectively.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective multi-arm teleoperation system that enables a single human operator to collect high-quality demonstrations for training coordinated multi-arm manipulation policies. This objective is motivated by several challenges and requirements in multi-arm robotics:

1. **Addressing the demand for multi-arm manipulation:** Many real-world tasks, such as handling large objects or performing collaborative actions, necessitate more than two robotic arms. Enabling robots to perform these tasks efficiently requires robust multi-arm manipulation capabilities. The motivation here is to provide a scalable method for generating the training data necessary for these advanced robotic systems.

2. **Overcoming limitations of existing teleoperation systems:**
 - **High cognitive load for single operators:** Traditional single-operator multi-arm systems require the human to constantly switch control modes between arms, leading to increased mental effort and potentially inconsistent, low-quality demonstrations. This creates a bottleneck in data collection efficiency and quality.
 - **High cost and coordination issues for multiple operators:** Employing multiple human operators for multi-arm teleoperation, while reducing individual workload, incurs significant labor costs and introduces coordination challenges such as communication delays and conflicting actions. This can hinder the scalability and practicality of data collection efforts.
 The research is motivated to find a middle ground that mitigates these issues.

3. **Leveraging MLLMs for real-time robotic assistance:** While MLLMs have demonstrated capabilities in high-level planning and task decomposition, their practical application in real-time, human-in-the-loop teleoperation has been constrained by latency and safety concerns. The motivation is to integrate MLLMs effectively into a teleoperation framework to provide intelligent, training-free assistance for sub-tasks, thereby offloading work from the human operator without compromising real-time performance or safety.

4. **Ensuring high-quality data for downstream policy learning:** The ultimate goal of teleoperation is often to collect diverse and high-quality demonstrations for imitation learning. The motivation is to design a system that produces demonstrations with characteristics favorable for policy training, such as spatial consistency, temporal smoothness, and high success rates, which are crucial for the generalization and robustness of learned policies.

In summary, the key objectives are to reduce the cognitive burden on a single human operator, minimize the coordination costs of multi-operator setups, and generate high-quality data for multi-arm policy learning, all while leveraging the planning capabilities of MLLMs in a practical and safe real-time system.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HATS (Human–Agent Teleoperation System) framework is designed to enable a single human operator, assisted by an MLLM-based agent, to collect data for multi-arm manipulation tasks. The system operates on a "Pilot-Copilot" paradigm, decoupling the control space such that the human directly teleoperates two primary arms, while an assistive agent controls two supportive arms. The system integrates four interconnected modules: Perception, Planning, Collaboration, and Supervision.

#### 3.1. Visual Perception

This module is responsible for extracting task-relevant spatial configurations from visual inputs.
- **Grasp Detection:** The system utilizes AnyGrasp, a general-purpose grasp detection algorithm, which processes RGB-D observations to predict a set of candidate grasp poses. To enhance robustness, DBSCAN is applied to cluster the translations of these candidate grasps, identifying a dominant cluster. The geometric centroid of this cluster determines the final target position, and the orientation from the nearest candidate grasp within the cluster defines the target rotation.
- **Semantic Placement:** For tasks requiring object placement based on linguistic descriptions, Qwen-VL (a Vision-Language Model) is employed for open-vocabulary localization. Given a natural language instruction and an RGB image, the VLM predicts the pixel coordinates of the target placement region. These 2D coordinates, along with corresponding depth values, are then converted into 3D points in the camera frame using the camera's intrinsic matrix.

The extracted grasp poses and spatial coordinates are then forwarded to the planning module as geometric preconditions.

#### 3.2. LLM-driven Task Planning

The central planner in HATS is Gemini 3.1-Flash-lite, which translates high-level instructions into executable robot actions. Planning is framed as a constrained code generation task.
- **Task Decomposition and Assignment:** The LLM planner analyzes task requirements and decomposes the original task into sequential sub-tasks. It then assigns these sub-tasks to the two assistive arms, instantiating them with parameters derived from the perception module. A collision-aware scheduling rule is enforced, dictating sequential execution if target spatial distances are below a safety threshold.
- **Motion Primitive Selection:** Based on the assigned sub-tasks, the planner selects appropriate motion primitives from a predefined library.
- **Two-stage Validation Mechanism:**
 - **Structural Validator:** During the initial planning stage, this validator checks if the generated plan adheres to the required schema and parameter constraints. Invalid plans trigger replanning with error feedback to the LLM.
 - **Perception-aware Validator:** Before each data collection episode, this validator verifies if the current scene matches the cached plan template by checking perception outputs (e.g., number of grasp poses). A mismatch triggers automatic replanning, ensuring adaptability to dynamic scene changes while reusing validated plans for efficiency.

#### 3.3. Human-Agent Collaboration

This module orchestrates the concurrent operation of human-controlled and agent-controlled arms.
- **Human Control:** The human operator teleoperates the two primary arms using a "Leader-Follower" setup, allowing for direct kinematic control.
- **Agent Control:** The agent converts the structured plan generated by the LLM planner into low-level hardware commands for the two assistive arms, which execute their assigned sub-tasks automatically.
- **Motion Primitives Library:** The system employs a library of motion primitives, encompassing both atomic functions (Cartesian Control, Joint Control, Gripper Control) and composite behaviors. Composite primitives, such as "Pick" (decomposed into approach, descend, close, lift steps) and "Place" (descent, release, retreat), enable robust execution. More complex skills like "Pour" can be composed from these atomic actions, providing extensibility without altering the core control framework.

#### 3.4. Human-in-the-Loop Supervision

To ensure safety and facilitate error recovery during task execution, HATS incorporates a real-time voice supervision mechanism.
- **Voice Command Processing:** Paraformer-Realtime, an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model, continuously transcribes the operator's voice commands.
- **Intervention Mechanism:** The ASR output is parsed to extract key tokens (e.g., target arm, action like "pause," "retry," or "stop"). These tokens are mapped to corresponding motion primitives or system-level triggers for the designated assistive arm.
- **Priority and Error Recovery:** Voice commands take priority, immediately suspending subsequent actions of the assistive arms. This allows for interactive error recovery (e.g., "retry" for a failed grasp) and safety interventions (e.g., "stop" to halt all assistive arms and clear the execution queue).
- **Synchronization:** Human-agent synchronization is achieved through both implicit coordination (predictable timing and motion patterns of assistive arms) and explicit voice intervention at critical junctures.

The hardware platform consists of four lightweight 7-DOF Agilex Piper robotic arms, an Intel RealSense D455 for high-resolution depth data (AnyGrasp), and two Intel RealSense D435 cameras for third-person observations. The teleoperation interface is a Leader-Follower setup inspired by GELLO, complemented by a portable microphone for voice commands.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of HATS was evaluated through extensive experiments involving seven real-world multi-arm tasks, categorized by the degree of temporal and physical coupling: Independent (Clear), Weakly Coupled (Pack, Draw, Clean, Pour), and Strongly Coupled (Spread, Lift). The system was benchmarked against three baselines: Solo-Switching (single human, mode-switching), Full-Agent (agent-only control), and Dual-Human (two expert human operators).

#### 4.1. System Evaluation and Efficiency

- **Overall Performance:** HATS achieved an average success rate of 86.4% and an average completion time of 10.25 seconds across all tasks, which is comparable to the expert Dual-Human baseline (90.0% success rate, 9.95 seconds completion time).
- **Temporal Efficiency:** In decoupled tasks (Clear, Pack, Draw, Clean), HATS demonstrated improved temporal efficiency compared to Solo-Switching by eliminating mode-switching pauses through parallel execution. For instance, in "Clear," HATS completed the task in 11.46s compared to Solo-Switching's 16.57s.
- **Concurrency in Coupled Tasks:** Solo-Switching proved entirely ineffective for strongly coupled tasks (Spread, Lift), resulting in 0/20 success rates due to the inability to control multiple arms concurrently. In contrast, HATS successfully completed these tasks with success rates comparable to Dual-Human (e.g., 18/20 for Lift). HATS also demonstrated faster completion times than Dual-Human in "Spread" (6.86s vs. 7.73s) and "Lift" (5.45s vs. 6.19s), attributed to the absence of interpersonal communication latency.
- **Necessity of Human Involvement:** The Full-Agent baseline, while succeeding in structurally simple tasks (e.g., Clear, Lift), failed in complex manipulations requiring nuanced understanding (e.g., Pack, Pour, Draw, Clean), underscoring the importance of human intervention for high-quality demonstration collection.

#### 4.2. Downstream Policy Evaluation

To assess the quality of collected data, two imitation learning algorithms, Diffusion Policy (DP) and 3D Diffusion Policy (DP3), were trained using 100 demonstrations per task from HATS and baseline data sources.
- **Comparable Performance:** Policies trained on HATS data achieved average Task Completion Rates (TCR) comparable to or slightly exceeding those trained on Dual-Human demonstrations (e.g., for DP, HATS achieved 71.3% TCR versus Dual-Human's 70.3%). This suggests that HATS provides training data of a quality suitable for complex multi-arm manipulation policy learning.
- **Hybrid Deployment:** A hybrid deployment combining learned policies for primary arms and primitive-based control for assistive arms achieved the highest success rates, indicating a practical approach for real-world multi-arm deployment.
- **Trajectory Quality:** Analysis revealed that HATS-generated trajectories for assistive arms exhibited lower spatial variance and smoother motion compared to Dual-Human demonstrations. HATS consistently reduced the Mean Spatial Standard Deviation across tasks (e.g., 54.0% improvement in Lift), and cross-sectional analyses showed more concentrated spatial distributions. The Mean Jerk metric indicated that assistive arm motions maintained fluidity comparable to human experts. These qualities contribute to more stable and accurate policy convergence.

#### 4.3. System Efficiency and Safety

- **Latency Management:** The system manages latency by caching LLM planner outputs per task (3.6s planner latency), executing perception (AnyGrasp 0.8s, VLM 4.5s) once per trial, and running motion primitives in real-time (<0.01s). An asynchronous ASR thread (1.5s latency for event detection) enables rapid human intervention.
- **Safety Interventions:** In collision stress tests, HATS achieved a 90% intervention success rate via voice commands, with failures primarily attributed to ASR transcription errors. A physical emergency stop served as a final safety measure.

#### 4.4. User Study

A user study involving 13 novice participants demonstrated that HATS enabled them to achieve an average success rate of 86.58%, closely matching the 89.42% achieved by expert Dual-Human teams. Subjective workload assessment using a simplified NASA-TLX indicated that HATS significantly reduced physical and temporal demands on the operator compared to a Solo-Switching setup.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study on LLM Planner

An ablation study comparing the LLM-driven planner with a Rule-based Scheduler demonstrated the LLM planner's superior adaptability. When scene variations (added or removed objects) were introduced, the rule-based system failed to adapt. In contrast, HATS, through its perception-aware validator, detected mismatches, triggered replanning, and achieved 100% completion. This highlights the LLM planner's scalability and ability to autonomously generate collision-free sequences for complex tasks without requiring manual scripting.

#### 4.6. Failure Analysis

Analysis of 20 failure cases revealed three primary sources:
- **Kinematic and control instability (65%):** The majority of failures were attributed to the low-level control limitations of the low-cost Piper manipulators, specifically issues with the internal Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver converging to unreasonable joint configurations or hitting singularities.
- **Grasping inaccuracy (20%):** Failures occurred when the gripper could not secure an object due to positional errors from AnyGrasp or object dimensions exceeding gripper limits.
- **Teleoperation and coordination error (15%):** Human-related errors included synchronization latency in tightly coupled tasks and high cognitive load during fine manipulation, leading to manual control errors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HATS framework presents a notable advancement in multi-arm teleoperation, offering a scalable and efficient solution for collecting manipulation data. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Scalable Multi-Arm Data Collection:** HATS addresses a critical bottleneck in robotics research by providing a method for a single operator to efficiently collect demonstrations for tasks requiring more than two robotic arms. This is crucial for training data-driven policies for increasingly complex real-world manipulation scenarios that are beyond the capabilities of bimanual systems.
- **Reduced Operator Burden and Cost:** By decoupling the control space and leveraging an MLLM-based agent, HATS significantly reduces the cognitive and physical workload on a single human operator. This mitigates the challenges of high cognitive load in single-operator setups and the high labor costs and coordination complexities associated with multi-operator systems, making multi-arm teleoperation more accessible and economically viable for research and industrial applications.
- **High-Quality Demonstration Generation:** The empirical evaluations demonstrate that HATS collects high-quality demonstrations characterized by spatial consistency and motion fluidity, comparable to or exceeding those generated by expert dual-human teams. This is important for the efficacy and generalization capabilities of downstream imitation learning policies, as high-quality data can lead to more stable and robust policy convergence.
- **Practical Application of MLLMs in Real-Time Robotics:** The work showcases a practical and effective integration of MLLMs (Gemini, Qwen-VL) into a real-time human-in-the-loop robotic system for assistive planning and control. By structuring the MLLM's role into high-level planning and incorporating robust validation and human supervision mechanisms, HATS successfully navigates challenges of latency, reliability, and safety typically associated with deploying large language models in dynamic, interactive robotic environments.
- **Enhanced System Adaptability and Safety:** The two-stage validation mechanism in the planning module allows HATS to adapt to scene variations without requiring manual replanning, enhancing its robustness. The real-time voice supervision mechanism provides a crucial layer of safety and error recovery, allowing operators to intervene instantly and prevent failures or correct agent behaviors, thus increasing task success rates in dynamic environments.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The current HATS system relies on structured task decomposition, predefined motion primitives, and explicit voice supervision. While this design ensures stability, it inherently trades off rapid adaptability in highly dynamic scenarios and the ability to proactively infer human intent. Future research could focus on integrating World Action Models and learned coordination policies to achieve more fluid and tightly coupled human-agent collaboration. This would involve moving towards systems capable of proactive intent inference, allowing for more intuitive and less explicit human-agent synchronization. Furthermore, addressing the identified hardware limitations by deploying HATS on more robust robotic platforms with advanced kinematic control could further enhance its performance and reliability.
