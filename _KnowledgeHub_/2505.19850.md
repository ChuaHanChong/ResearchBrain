# DISCOVER: Automated Curricula for Sparse-Reward Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A new curriculum learning method, DISCOVER, addresses sparse-reward reinforcement learning by strategically selecting intermediate goals that balance achievability, novelty, and relevance to the final task. This approach from ETH Zürich and Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems consistently outperforms existing goal-conditioned RL techniques and scales to higher-dimensional environments.

## Problem

- Efficient exploration in sparse-reward, long-horizon tasks is challenging due to infrequent feedback and intractable random exploration in high-dimensional spaces.
- Existing goal selection strategies often prioritize either exploration or exploitation, lacking a principled way to integrate target relevance alongside achievability and novelty.
- Many theoretically-backed exploration methods for multi-goal RL suffer from dependencies on the total volume of the goal space, limiting scalability.

## Method

- **DISCOVER** proposes a ==goal selection== strategy that quantifies goal utility as a sum of Achievability, Novelty, and Relevance to the target goal.
- An ensemble of critic networks estimates value and uncertainty for these three principles, guiding the selection of intermediate goals from previously achieved states.
- The method incorporates an online adaptation strategy for a key parameter, adjusting the balance between achievability/novelty and relevance to maintain a target goal achievement rate.

## Results

- DISCOVER consistently outperforms state-of-the-art goal selection strategies on complex `antmaze` and `arm` tasks, particularly in challenging configurations.
- The method scales effectively to 6-dimensional `pointmazes`, achieving success rates where undirected baselines fail entirely beyond 3 dimensions.
- Visualizations confirm that DISCOVER's agents direct their goal selection efforts towards the target region after an initial exploration phase, contrasting with undirected approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly integrating target relevance into goal selection provides a crucial "sense of direction" for exploration, enabling agents to focus on skills applicable to the ultimate objective.
> - The approach is theoretically grounded, connecting to UCB sampling and offering a bound on episodes needed to reach the target that is independent of the overall goal space volume.
> - Online parameter adaptation allows the method to automatically tune its exploration strategy based on learning progress, optimizing for efficient skill acquisition.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

DISCOVER addresses one of the most fundamental and persistent challenges in reinforcement learning (RL): **efficient exploration in sparse-reward, long-horizon tasks**. In such scenarios, an agent rarely receives feedback, making it exceedingly difficult for traditional RL methods to discover successful behaviors. This problem is exacerbated in high-dimensional state and action spaces, where random exploration becomes computationally intractable.

The paper situates itself within the landscape of **goal-conditioned RL** and **multi-goal learning**. Prior work, like Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) and Universal Value Function Approximators, transformed sparse-reward problems into multi-goal ones, allowing agents to learn from achieved (even if unintended) goals. However, a crucial aspect within this framework is **goal selection**: deciding *which* intermediate goals the agent should attempt to achieve next to maximize overall learning progress. Existing goal selection strategies often prioritize either exploration (e.g., novelty-seeking) or exploitation (e.g., reaching already known goals). Most methods, particularly those focused on intrinsic motivation or curiosity [e.g., 52, 27], aim to explore the entire state space or maximize novelty generally, without explicit direction towards a specific, ultimate target task. This "undirected" exploration struggles when the search space is vast and only specific paths lead to the target.

DISCOVER distinguishes itself by explicitly integrating **relevance** to the final target goal into the goal selection process, alongside achievability and novelty. This directed approach is crucial for overcoming the "curse of dimensionality" that plagues undirected exploration in high-dimensional goal spaces. The paper also draws a parallel to **Test-Time Training (TTT)** and **Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)**, framing DISCOVER as a method for robustly training an agent specifically for a single, hard target task at "test-time," potentially leveraging pre-trained priors but primarily bootstrapping from scratch. This connection highlights the paper's contribution to online adaptation and continuous self-improvement for initially unsolvable tasks.

Furthermore, DISCOVER connects to the principled framework of the **exploration-exploitation dilemma**, specifically drawing inspiration from **Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) sampling** in bandits. This theoretical grounding provides a strong analytical basis for its exploration strategy, a notable differentiator from many heuristic-driven exploration methods in deep RL. By proving bounds independent of the total volume of the goal space, DISCOVER presents a more scalable solution for complex tasks than prior theoretically-backed exploration methods that often suffer from dependencies on the size of the entire goal space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind DISCOVER are:

1. **To enable efficient exploration and learning in challenging sparse-reward, long-horizon reinforcement learning tasks.** These tasks are often considered the "core premise" of RL but remain intractable for many existing algorithms due to the difficulty of credit assignment and finding initial reward signals.
2. **To develop a novel goal selection strategy that provides a "sense of direction" towards the final target task.** The authors argue that merely solving many sparse-reward tasks is insufficient; instead, agents need to strategically learn simpler, relevant tasks that build skills directly applicable to the harder target.
3. **To balance achievability, novelty, and relevance during goal selection.** Previous methods often focused on only two of these principles. DISCOVER aims to integrate all three to guide exploration more effectively, ensuring goals are neither too easy (lacking novelty) nor too hard (unachievable), and crucially, contribute to the ultimate objective.
4. **To achieve this directed exploration *without leveraging any prior information* about the environment or task structure.** DISCOVER is motivated to bootstrap its sense of direction entirely from the agent's own learned value estimates, making it broadly applicable.
5. **To provide theoretical guarantees for the efficiency of the proposed method.** By establishing a formal connection to UCB bandits, the authors aim to demonstrate that DISCOVER can achieve the target goal within a bounded number of episodes, with a dependence that scales favorably with the problem's complexity (e.g., linear in target distance, independent of goal space volume).
6. **To significantly improve sample efficiency compared to state-of-the-art exploration methods.** The practical motivation is to create agents capable of solving more complex and larger-scale problems than currently possible, moving towards "self-improving agents with superhuman ability."

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DISCOVER operates within the **goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL)** framework, formalized as a multi-goal Markov Decision Process (M). The agent learns a goal-conditioned policy $\pi(s, g)$ that maximizes rewards, where rewards are sparse and given only upon reaching a specified goal $g$. The core innovation lies in the `SelectGoal` function, which determines the next intermediate goal $g_t$ for the agent to pursue.

The method quantifies **Goal Utility** as a combination of three principles:
$$ \text{Goal utility} = \text{Achievability} + \text{Novelty} + \text{Relevance} $$

These principles are estimated using an **ensemble of critic networks** from a standard deep RL algorithm (specifically, TD3 in their implementation). The ensemble provides both a mean value estimate ($V$) and a measure of uncertainty (standard deviation, $\sigma$).

1. **Achievability:** Quantified by $V(s_0, g)$, which reflects how easily the policy can reach goal $g$ from the initial state $s_0$. Higher values indicate easier, more achievable goals.
2. **Novelty:** Quantified by $\sigma(s_0, g)$, the uncertainty in reaching goal $g$ from $s_0$. High uncertainty suggests the agent has not reliably achieved this goal, making it a promising candidate for novel experience.
3. **Relevance:** Quantified by $V(g, g^\star) + \beta_t \sigma(g, g^\star)$, where $g^\star$ is the target goal. This term estimates how "close" an intermediate goal $g$ is to the final target $g^\star$ and the agent's uncertainty about this "distance". By prioritizing goals $g$ from which the target $g^\star$ seems readily achievable (high $V(g, g^\star)$) or where there's high uncertainty (high $\sigma(g, g^\star)$), DISCOVER directs exploration towards the ultimate objective.

The `SelectGoal` function chooses $g_t$ from the set of previously achieved goals $G_{ach}$ by maximizing the following objective:
$$ g_t = \arg\max_{g \in G_{ach}} \alpha_t V(s_0, g) + \beta_t \sigma(s_0, g) + (1-\alpha_t) [V(g, g^\star) + \beta_t \sigma(g, g^\star)] $$
Here, $\alpha_t$ and $\beta_t$ are schedulable coefficients. In practice, $\beta_t$ is simplified to 1 in most experiments, and the uncertainty term $\sigma(g, g^\star)$ is replaced by $\sigma(s_0, g)$ as it showed little positive effect.

**Automatic Online Parameter Adaptation:** To manage the trade-off between achievability/novelty and relevance, DISCOVER employs a simple online adaptation strategy for $\alpha_t$. It adjusts $\alpha_t$ to maintain a fixed target goal achievement rate $p^\star$ (empirically found to be around 50%). If the recent achievement rate $p_t$ is too low, $\alpha_t$ increases (prioritizing achievability); if too high, $\alpha_t$ decreases (prioritizing relevance).

**Theoretical Foundation:** The paper formally connects DISCOVER to **UCB sampling** in a simplified linear bandit setting. Under assumptions of linear value functions, noisy feedback, goal space containing optimal paths, and goal achievability, DISCOVER provides a theoretical guarantee. The number of episodes $N$ until the target goal $g^\star$ becomes achievable is bounded by $N \le e^{O(D d^2 / \kappa^3)}$, where $D$ is the optimal distance from the initial state to the target, $d$ is the feature space dimensionality, and $\kappa$ is the expansion rate of achievable goals. Critically, this bound is **independent of the volume of the entire goal space**, depending only on the "shortest distance" $D$, thereby avoiding the curse of dimensionality for high-dimensional problems.

**Implementation Details:**
- Uses the **TD3** actor-critic algorithm for policy updates.
- Employs an **ensemble of critics** (e.g., 6 critics) to estimate mean values and quantify uncertainty (variance).
- Utilizes **Hindsight Experience Replay (HER)** for efficient learning from off-policy trajectories and goal relabeling.
- Includes a random exploration phase after achieving a commanded goal, where actions are selected uniformly.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of DISCOVER is conducted across three complex, sparse-reward environments: `antmaze` (quadruped navigation), `arm` (robotic manipulation), and custom `n-dimensional pointmazes` (loco-navigation in varying dimensions). Both "simple" and "hard" configurations are tested for each environment.

Key findings include:

1. **Superior Performance on Complex Control Tasks:** DISCOVER consistently and significantly outperforms state-of-the-art goal selection strategies (HER, DISCERN, MEGA, Achievability + Novelty) across all evaluated environments, particularly in their "hard" configurations (Figure 3). This highlights the critical role of the "relevance" term in solving challenging tasks.
2. **Effective in High-Dimensional Search Spaces:** Undirected goal selection strategies struggle and fail to achieve 10% success rates in pointmazes beyond 3 dimensions. In contrast, DISCOVER successfully scales to 6-dimensional pointmazes (Table 1), demonstrating its ability to mitigate the curse of dimensionality by focusing exploration on relevant directions.
3. **Directed Exploration Towards the Target:** Visualizations of goal selection in the `antmaze` (Figure 4) clearly show DISCOVER's agents, after an initial exploration phase, quickly identifying and concentrating their goal selection efforts in the region leading towards the target goal. This contrasts sharply with baselines that explore the entire state space undirectedly.
4. **Ability to Leverage Prior Knowledge:** DISCOVER can integrate prior information (e.g., a hand-designed L2 distance to the target or a pre-trained critic from a similar environment). This integration further accelerates exploration, particularly in environments with obstacles (Figure 5), suggesting the method is robust and can benefit from available guidance.
5. **Subgoal Selection Enables Deep Exploration:** Comparisons against standard RL algorithms that do not explicitly select intermediate exploratory goals (e.g., TD3, SAC, RND, SAC+MaxInfoRL) reveal that these methods fail entirely to solve the target tasks, even in simpler configurations (Figure 6). This underscores that goal-conditioned approaches with strategic subgoal selection are essential for deep exploration in long-horizon, sparse-reward settings.
6. **Direction Estimates Become Useful Early:** The Pearson correlation between DISCOVER's learned relevance estimate $V(g, g^\star)$ and the true L2-distance to the target (a proxy for ground truth path) increases rapidly early in training (Figure 16a), indicating that the agent learns a useful sense of direction even before reaching the target for the first time.
7. **Robustness to Noisy Direction Estimates:** Even with substantial Gaussian noise added to target estimates in high-dimensional pointmazes, DISCOVER remains effective, demonstrating that precise directional knowledge is not always necessary for significant gains over undirected methods (Figure 9).
8. **Optimality of Adaptation Strategy:** The online adaptation strategy for $\alpha_t$ outperforms fixed parameter settings, achieving optimal performance when targeting a goal achievement rate of approximately 50%, consistent with prior work (Figure 10).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DISCOVER represents a significant step forward in tackling the long-standing challenge of sparse-reward reinforcement learning. Its main contributions and potential impacts are:

1. **Overcoming Deep Exploration Hurdles:** By systematically combining achievability, novelty, and, crucially, target **relevance** into goal selection, DISCOVER enables agents to solve tasks that are beyond the reach of prior state-of-the-art exploration methods. This is vital for applications where rewards are inherently sparse (e.g., completing a complex task in robotics, solving a mathematical problem).
2. **Principled and Theoretically Sound Exploration:** The formal connection to UCB bandits and the theoretical bound that is independent of the goal space's volume provide a strong foundation. This makes DISCOVER a more robust and scalable solution for high-dimensional problems compared to many heuristic-driven approaches.
3. **Reduced Human Supervision:** The ability to bootstrap a sense of direction without prior domain-specific knowledge or reward shaping makes DISCOVER highly autonomous. This reduces the need for human expert intervention, a major bottleneck in applying RL to real-world problems.
4. **Advancement in Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL):** DISCOVER's effectiveness in "test-time training" a policy for a single, hard target task opens new avenues for agents that can continuously self-improve on difficult, initially unsolvable problems.
5. **Broad Applicability:** While demonstrated in continuous control, the core idea of directed curriculum learning via bootstrapping value functions is general. The paper highlights potential applications in domains with highly complex goal spaces, such as **mathematical theorem proving or program synthesis with large language models**, where effective search and skill acquisition are paramount.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies several promising future directions, including:
 - **More efficient uncertainty estimation:** Addressing the computational overhead of critic ensembles, especially for large models.
 - **Goal generation:** Moving beyond selecting from achieved goals to actively generating novel, relevant goals.
 - **Hierarchical planning:** Integrating DISCOVER into hierarchical RL architectures for even deeper exploration and skill transfer.
 - **Experience reuse:** Developing mechanisms to leverage experience from one target task for future, related tasks.

**Limitations** acknowledge that DISCOVER relies on the generalization capabilities of value networks and incurs computational overhead from training critic ensembles for uncertainty estimation. However, these are common challenges in deep RL, and the proposed approach demonstrates practical effectiveness despite them. Overall, DISCOVER offers a powerful and principled approach to guide deep exploration in complex, sparse-reward settings, pushing the boundaries of what autonomous agents can learn to achieve.
