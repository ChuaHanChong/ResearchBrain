# Expressive Whole-Body Control for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC San Diego developed Expressive Whole-Body Control (ExBody), a framework that enables humanoid robots to generate diverse and expressive whole-body motions by selectively imitating human motion capture data. The method combines robust lower-body locomotion with upper-body expressiveness, successfully transferring various human gestures and dances to a real Unitree H1 robot and demonstrating superior stability and expressivity compared to traditional imitation techniques.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to generate diverse, natural, and expressive whole-body motions, often producing stiff or task-specific movements lacking fluidity.
- Existing deep reinforcement learning approaches for humanoids primarily focus on robust locomotion, neglecting the expressiveness observed in human motion.
- Leveraging large-scale human motion capture (MoCap) data from the computer graphics community for real robots is challenging due to significant differences in robot kinematics, degrees of freedom, and physical limitations compared to idealized character models.

## Method

- The authors propose ==Expressive Whole-Body Control (ExBody)==, a goal-conditioned motor policy that combines large-scale human motion capture data with deep reinforcement learning for robust Sim2Real transfer.
- ExBody employs a relaxed imitation strategy: it encourages imitation of human motion for the humanoid's upper body (arms, torso) to achieve expressiveness, while the lower body prioritizes robustly following root movement commands for stable locomotion.
- Training is conducted in a massively parallel physics simulator (Isaac Gym) using Guiding State Initialization (RSI) and a reward function that balances expressive goal tracking, root movement tracking, and physical regularization terms for stability and plausibility.

## Results

- ExBody accurately tracked diverse upper-body expressive goals and root movement commands from human motion data, enabling the Unitree H1 robot to perform various gestures, dancing, and interactions while walking.
- The approach achieved superior performance in simulation metrics (e.g., higher mean episode linear velocity and key body tracking rewards) and demonstrated robust real-world deployment on the Unitree H1 robot without fine-tuning, successfully replaying complex MoCap and text-to-motion trajectories.
- Experiments showed that Guiding State Initialization (RSI) and motion-derived goals were critical for successful learning, while baselines attempting full-body imitation or relying solely on adversarial methods without proper regularization exhibited significantly worse performance and instability on the physical robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A relaxed imitation strategy, prioritizing upper-body expressiveness while maintaining robust lower-body locomotion, is essential for successfully transferring diverse human motions to real humanoid robots with hardware constraints.
> - Utilizing large-scale human motion data for diverse state initialization (Guiding State Initialization - RSI) and deriving root movement goals directly from this data is crucial for efficient and robust deep reinforcement learning of complex humanoid behaviors.
> - Direct full-body imitation from human motion capture data or naive application of adversarial motion prior (AMP) methods often leads to unstable or infeasible control on physical humanoid robots, highlighting the need for specific regularization and adaptive control strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics, particularly for humanoid systems, has historically struggled with generating diverse, natural, and expressive motions. Traditional approaches to whole-body control often rely on complex dynamics modeling and optimization, which are computationally intensive, highly engineered, and tend to produce stiff, singular, or task-specific movements that lack the fluidity and personality observed in human motion. These methods are also highly sensitive to real-world dynamics changes and hardware limitations.

More recently, deep reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for developing robust locomotion policies for legged robots, including bipeds. However, much of this work has focused on achieving stable and agile locomotion (e.g., walking on challenging terrains, jumping, navigating obstacles), often prioritizing task completion over the richness or expressiveness of movement. While some RL-based methods have incorporated imitation learning from small datasets of reference motions, scaling this to highly diverse human behaviors on complex humanoid platforms remains a significant challenge.

Simultaneously, the computer graphics community has made significant strides in physics-based character animation, generating visually appealing and reactive character behaviors by leveraging large-scale motion capture (MoCap) datasets. Techniques like adversarial motion priors (AMP) have been effective in synthesizing diverse stylized motions. However, as the authors highlight, these graphics-oriented approaches often operate with idealized character models (e.g., high DoF, unrealistic actuator gains, privileged information) that do not translate well to real-world robots due to fundamental differences in degrees of freedom (DoF), torque limits, and sensing capabilities. Direct application of these methods to physical robots frequently results in brittle, unstable, or infeasible control policies.

"Expressive Whole-Body Control (ExBody)" bridges this critical gap by attempting to merge the expressive richness from human motion capture data (graphics domain) with the robustness and real-world feasibility required for physical humanoid robots (robotics domain). It acknowledges the limitations of direct imitation and proposes a novel strategy that selectively mimics human motion where it contributes to expressiveness (upper body) while enforcing robust task-based control where physical constraints are paramount (lower body locomotion). This approach distinguishes itself by being one of the first to tackle real-world, learning-based humanoid control for *diverse and expressive* motions, rather than focusing solely on locomotion or limited manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable humanoid robots to generate rich, diverse, and expressive whole-body motions in the real world**. The authors are motivated by the observation that while robots are increasingly capable of performing specific tasks, their movements often lack the "grace and personality" that characterize human and animal motions. This absence of expressivity limits natural human-robot interaction and the robot's ability to convey intent or emotional valence.

The core motivations and challenges addressed by the paper include:

- **Bridging the expressivity gap:** Moving beyond singular, task-optimized motions to allow humanoids to exhibit a wider range of natural and expressive behaviors.
- **Leveraging large-scale human motion data:** Tapping into the vast repositories of human motion capture data (like CMU MoCap) from the graphics community to imbue robots with human-like expressivity.
- **Overcoming the Sim2Real gap and hardware limitations:** Directly imitating human motions from MoCap data on real humanoid robots is problematic due to differences in degrees of freedom, kinematic structures, and the physical capabilities (e.g., torque limits, actuator gains) of real hardware. Graphics models often assume much higher DoF and less realistic physical constraints.
- **Achieving robust real-world deployment:** Ensuring that learned expressive policies are not only visually convincing but also physically stable and robust enough to operate effectively on diverse terrains and interact with humans.
- **Developing a generalizable control policy:** Aiming for a single control policy that can handle a wide repertoire of motions and adapt to various root movement commands, rather than training separate policies for each specific task or motion style.

In essence, the authors seek to build humanoids that can move "as expressive as humans," which is crucial for their seamless integration into human environments and for fostering more intuitive and engaging interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach: Expressive Whole-Body Control (ExBody)

The paper proposes **Expressive Whole-Body Control (ExBody)**, a novel approach that combines large-scale human motion capture data with deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) within a simulated environment for robust Sim2Real transfer to a human-sized robot.

The core of ExBody revolves around a **goal-conditioned motor policy** $\pi: G \times S \rightarrow A$, where the goal space $G$ comprises both an *expression target* ($g_e$) and a *root movement target* ($g_m$).

Key components of the methodology include:

- **Relaxed Imitation Strategy:** This is the most crucial innovation. Instead of attempting to precisely mimic *all* joint angles and keypoints from human motion data (which is infeasible for real robots due to DoF mismatch and physical constraints), ExBody adopts a relaxed approach:
 - **Upper Body Imitation:** The policy is encouraged to imitate the reference motion for the humanoid's upper body (arms and torso, 9 DoFs and 18 keypoints of shoulders, elbows, hands) to achieve expressiveness.
 - **Lower Body Robust Control:** For the two legs, the imitation constraint is relaxed. Instead, the lower body is primarily tasked with robustly following root movement commands (linear velocity, roll/pitch/yaw, and body height) provided by the reference motion. This decoupling ensures stable locomotion while allowing for upper-body expressivity.
- **Large-Scale Motion Data Curation and Retargeting:**
 - The **CMU MoCap dataset** is used as the primary source of human motion data (around 780 motion clips, 3.7 hours total).
 - A **semi-automatic filtering process** selects motions suitable for a humanoid robot without complex environmental interactions (e.g., excluding heavy object lifting, rough terrain traversal).
 - **Motion Retargeting:** Human motions are adapted to the Unitree H1 robot's specific kinematic structure (19 DoF, including 3-DoF shoulder/hip joints) by mapping local joint rotations. This process acknowledges the DoF mismatch and converts spherical human joints to the H1's revolute joint structure.
- **Reinforcement Learning Framework:**
 - **Simulation Environment:** Training is conducted in **Isaac Gym**, a massively parallel GPU-based physics simulator, which is critical for efficient RL training and robust Sim2Real transfer through randomization.
 - **Guiding State Initialization (RSI):** During training, environments are initialized by randomly sampling states ($g = [g_e, g_m]$) from the curated and retargeted motion dataset. This diverse initialization strategy is hypothesized to encourage exploration and provide a natural curriculum for policy learning, preventing the policy from getting stuck in local optima or falling immediately.
 - **Reward Function Design:** The reward function incentivizes:
 - **Expression Goal Tracking:** Minimizing the difference between the robot's upper body joint positions and keypoint positions and the reference motion.
 - **Root Movement Goal Tracking:** Minimizing errors in linear velocity, roll, pitch, and yaw relative to the reference motion.
 - **Regularization Terms:** Additional terms penalize undesirable behaviors like high joint acceleration, large action rates, self-collision, joint limit violations, excessive vertical velocity, and deviation from default lower body posture, ensuring physical plausibility and stability.
- **Sim2Real Transfer:** The policy trained in the randomized simulation environment is directly deployed on the real Unitree H1 humanoid robot without further fine-tuning, demonstrating the effectiveness of the training methodology.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments in both simulation and the real world to validate ExBody's effectiveness and compare it against various baselines.

**A. Performance on Tracking Expression ($g_e$) and Root Movement ($g_m$):**
- **Simulation:** ExBody successfully learns to produce root movement state distributions that align well with the training dataset, indicating effective tracking of intuitive commands. More importantly, the trained policy generalizes to *randomly sampled* root movement commands, allowing for flexible joystick control in the real world. While the policy's state distribution for roll and pitch is somewhat narrower than the sampled commands (attributed to robot's physical properties), it covers most of the commanded velocity range.
- **Upper Body Expressivity:** Qualitative visualization (Fig. 6) shows that the learned ExBody policy accurately reproduces the diverse hand positions and upper body movements observed in the retargeted human motion dataset, demonstrating high fidelity in tracking expressive goals.
- **Real World:** The robot successfully replayed complex CMU MoCap trajectories and even motions generated from text-to-motion diffusion models, showcasing robust, responsive, and precise tracking performance for expressive actions like dancing, hugging, high-fives, and various gestures while walking.

**B. Benefits of Large-Scale Data and Guiding State Initialization (RSI):**
- **Quantitative Metrics (Table IV):** ExBody achieved the best overall performance in simulation, particularly in mean episode linear velocity tracking reward (MELV), mean episode roll/pitch tracking reward (MERP), and mean episode key body tracking reward (MEK), while maintaining long mean episode lengths (MEL).
- **Importance of RSI:** The "No RSI" baseline performed disastrously (MEL of ~0.2), indicating that initializing the robot in diverse states sampled from human motion data is crucial for exploration and learning, preventing the policy from failing immediately.
- **Value of Motion-Derived Goals:** The "Random Sample" baseline (sampling $g_m$ uniformly) yielded significantly worse performance than using motion-derived goals. This suggests that the natural temporal correlations and structured distribution of human motion data provide a beneficial "curriculum" that aids the RL training process, making policy learning more efficient and robust.

**C. Validation of Relaxed Lower Body Imitation:**
- **"Full Body Tracking" Baseline:** This baseline, attempting to mimic *all* human joint angles and keypoints, resulted in substantially worse performance across all metrics (Table IV). Qualitatively, it led to noticeable artifacts in the robot's lower body movements, such as executing multiple steps to stabilize when the reference motion suggested a single step. This finding strongly supports ExBody's core hypothesis that relaxing the imitation constraint on the lower body and prioritizing robust locomotion is essential for real-world humanoid control given current hardware limitations.

**D. Comparison with Adversarial Methods (AMP):**
- **Simulation & Real World (Table IV & V, Fig. 7):** Baselines incorporating Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), commonly used in graphics for stylized character control, showed mixed results. While "ExBody + AMP" sometimes achieved slightly higher MERP, it significantly sacrificed linear velocity tracking (MELV) compared to ExBody.
- **Real-World Stability:** Crucially, real-world tests demonstrated that AMP-based policies resulted in less stable gaits (higher mean absolute roll and pitch angles, indicating more shaking), often exhibiting "straight-leg" walking with inadequate foot clearance and a tendency to stumble. In contrast, ExBody maintained a stable stepping gait.
- **Necessity of Regularization:** "ExBody + AMP NoReg" (AMP without additional regularization terms) performed even worse, exhibiting high-frequency jittery movements, indicating that for complex robotic systems, AMP alone is insufficient without careful reward engineering and regularization. This highlights a key divergence from graphics applications where models might be more forgiving.

In summary, ExBody effectively leverages large-scale human motion data to imbue humanoids with expressiveness while maintaining robust real-world locomotion, a feat that traditional direct imitation or graphics-centric adversarial methods struggle to achieve on physical hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Expressive Whole-Body Control (ExBody)" framework represents a significant step forward in humanoid robotics, particularly in closing the gap between the visually rich character animations from computer graphics and the stringent requirements for robust control on physical robots. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Truly Expressive Humanoid Behavior:** By successfully transferring diverse and expressive human motions to a real humanoid robot, ExBody paves the way for robots that can move more naturally, gracefully, and with greater personality. This moves beyond the purely functional and singular movements often seen in robotic tasks, unlocking a new dimension of human-robot interaction.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** Robots capable of expressive whole-body movements can communicate intent, emotion, and engage in more intuitive and natural interactions with humans. Imagine a robot that can genuinely dance, hug, or express frustration through its posture—this greatly enhances user experience and allows for more nuanced collaborative tasks.
- **Bridging Robotics and Graphics:** The work offers a robust methodology for leveraging the vast and continually growing datasets of human motion from the graphics community to benefit physical robotics. It provides a blueprint for how to selectively extract and adapt "expressive" features while respecting the "physical" realities and limitations of robot hardware, offering a more practical bridge between these two disciplines.
- **Advancing Generalizable Humanoid Control:** ExBody demonstrates the feasibility of training a single, generalizable control policy that can handle a wide range of diverse motions and root movement commands. This reduces the need for task-specific controllers and moves towards more versatile and adaptable humanoid systems capable of performing multiple functions effectively in complex, unstructured environments.
- **Robustness in the Wild:** The emphasis on training with massively parallel simulation (Isaac Gym) and robust root movement control (rather than full-body imitation) ensures that the learned policies are not only expressive but also stable and robust in real-world scenarios, including varied terrains and physical interactions. This robust foundation is critical for deploying humanoids outside controlled laboratory settings.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The approach can serve as a foundational method for future research in several areas, including:
 - **Complex Manipulation:** Integrating expressive whole-body control with sophisticated manipulation tasks, allowing robots to pick up objects with more human-like flair or perform assembly tasks with greater dexterity.
 - **Navigation in Dynamic Environments:** Enabling humanoids to navigate complex and dynamic environments while performing expressive gestures or communicating non-verbally.
 - **Humanoid Learning from Diverse Sources:** Opening doors for learning from even broader motion datasets, potentially including those generated by generative AI models (as demonstrated by the text-to-motion examples).

In conclusion, ExBody is a pivotal contribution that brings humanoids closer to truly mimicking human motion in its fullness—not just in terms of locomotion, but in the subtle, expressive nuances that make human movement so rich and compelling. This advancement has profound implications for the future design and deployment of humanoid robots in society.
