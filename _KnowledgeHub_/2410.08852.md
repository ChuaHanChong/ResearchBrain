# Conformalized Interactive Imitation Learning: Handling Expert Shift and Intermittent Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> The ConformalDAgger framework enables robots to adaptively quantify uncertainty and strategically query human experts during interactive imitation learning, even with intermittent feedback. Utilizing the new Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT) algorithm, this approach helps robots detect distribution shifts and quickly adapt policies, resulting in increased expert feedback requests (e.g., from ~20% to ~60% in simulation upon expert shift) and improved learning efficiency in simulated and hardware tasks.

## Problem

- Robot policies trained on expert demonstrations often degrade in performance due to distribution shifts when deployed in dynamic or new environments, or when the expert's behavior changes.
- Prior uncertainty quantification methods in interactive imitation learning (IIL) do not adapt their estimates online using received feedback, potentially leading to overconfident predictions in the face of deployment-time shifts.
- Existing online conformal prediction algorithms assume continuous access to ground-truth labels, which is not suitable for interactive robot learning where human feedback is inherently intermittent.

## Method

- Developed Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT), a novel online ==conformal prediction== algorithm that extends Quantile Tracking (QT) to handle intermittent labels by scaling updates with the observation probability and only applying them when labels are observed.
- Integrated IQT into an interactive imitation learning framework, named ConformalDAgger, which treats the robot's policy as the base model for IQT and uses the calibrated uncertainty (prediction interval size) to trigger expert queries.
- Designed an adaptive querying mechanism where the robot's decision to query the expert is based on the size of the calibrated uncertainty interval, allowing for targeted and efficient feedback collection.

## Results

- In simulated expert shift tasks, ConformalDAgger adaptively increased expert feedback requests (e.g., from approximately **20%** to **60%** of trajectory length) and achieved lower miscoverage rates (max **0.4** vs. baselines max **1.0**), leading to faster policy alignment with the shifted expert.
- On a 7-degree-of-freedom robotic manipulator performing a sponging task, ConformalDAgger significantly increased robot-requested interventions (up to ~35 timesteps of human feedback) when the expert changed their wiping strategy, enabling the robot to learn the new pattern more effectively.
- ConformalDAgger maintained minimal querying behavior and exhibited performance comparable to other methods in stationary expert scenarios, demonstrating that it does not excessively query when uncertainty is genuinely low.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT) maintains asymptotic coverage guarantees for uncertainty estimates even when ground-truth labels are observed probabilistically or intermittently, providing theoretical reliability for online interactive learning.
> - Calibrated uncertainty estimates derived from online conformal prediction can effectively detect distribution shifts, particularly changes in expert policy, serving as a robust signal for adaptively querying human experts.
> - Directly incorporating intermittent human feedback into the online uncertainty estimation process allows for real-time adaptation of the robot's confidence, leading to more efficient data collection and faster policy retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a core challenge in imitation learning (IL), specifically distribution shift, which often limits the reliability of robot policies when deployed in dynamic or new environments. Traditional IL methods train robot policies on expert demonstrations, but performance can degrade if the deployment environment or the expert's behavior deviates from the training data.

Interactive imitation learning (IIL), particularly frameworks like DAgger (Dataset Aggregation), aim to mitigate this distribution shift by allowing the robot to query a human expert for additional feedback online. Prior uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods in IIL, such as ensemble disagreement or Monte Carlo dropout, typically rely on the initial training data to estimate uncertainty. These methods often do not adapt their uncertainty estimates online using the feedback received during deployment, potentially leading to overconfident predictions in the face of deployment-time shifts. Furthermore, such methods treat requested labels primarily for later retraining, rather than as a signal for real-time uncertainty adaptation.

Conformal prediction, a distribution-free UQ method, has gained traction in robotics for providing guarantees for complex learned models, particularly in non-i.i.d. and non-stationary data settings common in robotic applications. Existing online conformal prediction algorithms, such as Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) and Quantile Tracking (QT), assume continuous access to ground-truth labels. However, in interactive robot learning, human feedback is inherently intermittent, posing a challenge for direct application of these online conformal methods.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of interactive imitation learning and online conformal prediction, seeking to develop a UQ framework that can leverage intermittent online expert feedback to adapt the robot's uncertainty estimates, thereby improving its ability to detect and respond to distribution shifts, including changes in expert policy. This contrasts with prior work by explicitly incorporating the online, intermittent nature of human feedback into the uncertainty quantification process itself, aiming for more reliable and adaptive robot behavior during deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an interactive imitation learning framework that can robustly handle distribution shifts encountered during robot deployment by actively and adaptively quantifying uncertainty and soliciting expert feedback online. The authors identify several motivations and specific objectives:

1. **Addressing Overconfident Uncertainty Estimates:** Prior uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods in interactive IL, such as those based on ensemble disagreement or Monte Carlo dropout, tend to rely on the initial training data to estimate uncertainty. This approach can lead to overconfident uncertainty estimates when the robot encounters distribution shifts during deployment, as these methods do not typically adapt their UQ online based on new expert feedback. The motivation is to overcome this limitation by developing a UQ method that is dynamic and responsive to deployment-time data.

2. **Leveraging Online Expert Feedback for Uncertainty Adaptation:** The human feedback (action labels) received during deployment in an interactive IL setting is a valuable signal. Current methods often treat this feedback primarily as data for later policy retraining, rather than using it to update the robot's understanding of its own uncertainty in real-time. A key motivation is to devise a mechanism that actively incorporates this intermittent human feedback into the robot's online uncertainty estimation, allowing the robot to adjust its confidence levels based on new information from the expert.

3. **Ensuring Reliable Uncertainty Quantification for Black-Box Policies:** Modern imitation learning often employs complex, end-to-end "black-box" neural network policies. Quantifying uncertainty for such models in a reliable and distribution-free manner, especially in non-i.i.d. and non-stationary online settings, is a significant challenge. The motivation is to draw upon techniques like online conformal prediction, which can construct prediction intervals for black-box models without strong distributional assumptions, and adapt them for the specific challenges of interactive IL.

4. **Developing a Conformal Prediction Algorithm for Intermittent Labels:** A fundamental gap identified in existing online conformal prediction literature (e.g., Adaptive Conformal Inference, Quantile Tracking) is the assumption of continuous access to ground-truth labels. In interactive IL, expert feedback is inherently intermittent. Therefore, a specific technical objective is to extend online conformal prediction to scenarios where labels are observed probabilistically or intermittently. This led to the development of Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT), aiming to maintain asymptotic coverage guarantees even with probabilistic label access.

5. **Designing an Adaptive Querying Mechanism for Interactive IL:** With a reliable online uncertainty measure in hand, the goal is to develop an interactive IL algorithm that uses these calibrated uncertainty estimates to strategically query the expert. This querying mechanism should enable the robot to detect states of high uncertainty, particularly those arising from distribution shifts (e.g., changes in the expert's strategy), and selectively ask for more expert input. This aims to minimize expert effort while maximizing the informativeness of received feedback, accelerating policy adaptation.

6. **Empirical Validation in Robotic Tasks:** The ultimate motivation is to demonstrate the practical utility and robustness of the proposed framework in both simulated and real-world robotic tasks. This involves evaluating its ability to detect expert shifts, increase interventions appropriately, and enable the robot to more quickly learn new behaviors compared to existing uncertainty-aware DAgger methods.

In summary, the work is motivated by the need for more adaptive and reliable uncertainty quantification in interactive imitation learning, particularly when facing deployment-time distribution shifts and intermittent human feedback. The core objectives are to develop a novel conformal prediction algorithm for intermittent labels and integrate it into an interactive IL framework to enable intelligent, uncertainty-aware expert querying and rapid policy adaptation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research involves two main components: first, the development of a novel online conformal prediction algorithm designed to handle intermittent labels, and second, the integration of this algorithm into an interactive imitation learning framework.

#### 3.1. Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT)

The first core technical contribution is extending online conformal prediction to settings where ground-truth labels are observed intermittently. The authors adapt the Quantile Tracking (QT) algorithm (Angelopoulos et al., 2024), which provides distribution-free prediction intervals and asymptotic coverage guarantees for streaming data.

- **Setting:** The algorithm operates on an arbitrary sequence of data points $(x_t, y_t)$, which are not necessarily independent and identically distributed (I.I.D.). The goal is to construct prediction sets $C_t(x_t)$ for the true label $y_t$ with a specified coverage probability ($1-\alpha$). A base prediction model $\hat{f}: X \rightarrow Y$ provides an initial prediction $\hat{y}_t$.
- **Nonconformity Score:** A bounded nonconformity score function $s(x_t, y_t)$ is used to quantify the error of the base model's prediction. Lower scores indicate higher accuracy.
- **Quantile Tracking (QT) Background:** In standard QT, an estimated $(1-\alpha)$-quantile $q_t$ of the score sequence is tracked. Prediction intervals are formed as $C_t(x_t) = \{y \in Y : s(x_t, y_t) \leq q_t\}$. The quantile $q_t$ is updated via online gradient descent: $q_{t+1} = q_t + \gamma_t(\text{err}_t - \alpha)$, where $\gamma_t > 0$ is a step size and $\text{err}_t = \mathbb{1}_{y_t \notin C_t(x_t)}$ is the miscoverage indicator. QT guarantees asymptotic coverage $1-\alpha$.
- **Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT) Formulation:** To account for intermittent labels, a binary random variable $\text{obs}_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is introduced, indicating whether the label $y_t$ is observed at time $t$. A probabilistic observation model $p_t := P(\text{obs}_t=1 | x_t)$ defines the likelihood of receiving feedback. The key modification in IQT is to scale the update term by $p_t$ and only apply the update if the label is observed:
 $q_{t+1} = q_t + \frac{\gamma_t}{p_t}(\text{err}_t - \alpha)\text{obs}_t$
 If $\text{obs}_t=0$ (label not observed), $q_{t+1} = q_t$, meaning the quantile estimate remains unchanged.
- **Asymptotic Coverage Guarantees:** The paper provides Proposition 1, demonstrating that IQT maintains asymptotic coverage guarantees, meaning $\lim_{T \rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{1}_{y_t \in C_t(x_t)}$ approaches $1-\alpha$, even with intermittent ground-truth access. This guarantee holds regardless of the underlying data generation process, provided $p_t > 0$.
- **Practical Considerations for $\gamma_t$:** For the step size, prior QT works use $\gamma_t = \frac{\text{lr}}{\hat{B}_t}$, where $\hat{B}_t$ is a maximum nonconformity score over a look-back window. The authors investigate two variants: IQT-pi (p-independent update, where $\gamma_t$ cancels $p_t$) and IQT-pd (p-dependent update, where $\gamma_t$ does not cancel $p_t$).
- **Empirical Evaluation of IQT:** IQT was empirically evaluated on standard time series benchmarks (Amazon, Google stocks, Elec2 dataset) using various base prediction models (Autoregressive, Prophet, Theta, Transformer). Different observation frequencies ($p_t = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9$) were tested. Metrics included marginal coverage, longest miscoverage error sequence, and mean prediction interval size.

#### 3.2. ConformalDAgger

The second component integrates IQT into an interactive imitation learning framework, named ConformalDAgger.

- **Framework Overview:** ConformalDAgger treats the robot's policy $\pi_r$ as the base model for IQT. An initial policy $\pi_r^0$ is trained on demonstrations $D_0$. During deployment, the robot generates state-action pairs $(x_t, a_r^t)$, and IQT calibrates uncertainty. When uncertainty (measured by prediction interval size) is high, the robot queries the expert for feedback. The received expert actions $a_h^t$ are used to update IQT and are aggregated for retraining $\pi_r$.
- **Observation Model in IIL:** The intermittent label observation likelihood $p_t = P(\text{obs}_t=1 | x_t)$ is decomposed into human-gated and robot-gated feedback components: $p_t = P(\text{obs}_h^t=1 | x_t) + P(\text{obs}_r^t=1 | x_t) - P(\text{obs}_h^t=1 | x_t)P(\text{obs}_r^t=1 | x_t)$.
 - $P(\text{obs}_h^t=1 | x_t)$ represents the probability of unsolicited human intervention, modeled as a small constant $c$.
 - $P(\text{obs}_r^t=1 | x_t)$ represents the robot's active query, informed by its uncertainty.
- **Uncertainty Quantification for Actions:** IQT is applied to the robot's action space. For continuous actions, upper and lower residual nonconformity scores are defined: $s_{lo}^t = a_r^t - a_h^t$ and $s_{hi}^t = a_h^t - a_r^t$. Separate quantiles, $q_{lo}^t$ and $q_{hi}^t$, are tracked to form a prediction interval $C_t(x_t) = [a_r^t - q_{lo}^t, a_r^t + q_{hi}^t]$. If expert action $a_h^t$ is observed, $\text{err}_{lo}^t$ and $\text{err}_{hi}^t$ are computed, and $q_{lo,hi}^{t+1}$ are updated via the IQT rule. If not observed, quantiles remain unchanged.
- **Robot-Gated Feedback Mechanism:** The robot's decision to query the expert is based on the size of the calibrated uncertainty interval, $u(x_{t+1}) := ||C_{t+1}(x_{t+1})||_2$. In simulation, $P(\text{obs}_r^t | x_t) = \sigma(\beta[u(x_t) - \tau])$, where $\tau$ is an uncertainty threshold and $\beta$ is a temperature. In hardware, a hard threshold is used.
- **ConformalDAgger Algorithm (Algorithm 1):** The overall algorithm iteratively deploys the robot, collecting intermittently observed expert state-action pairs (when the robot queries or human intervenes). This data is aggregated into a replay buffer, and the learner policy is retrained for subsequent episodes. The IQT quantiles are reset at the beginning of each interactive deployment episode.

This dual-pronged approach, first addressing the theoretical challenge of intermittent labels in UQ and then applying it to the practical problem of interactive robot learning, constitutes the core methodology.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented main findings from both simulated interactive imitation learning experiments and hardware deployments.

#### 4.1. Simulated Interactive Imitation Learning Experiments

The simulation experiments used a 4D robot goal-reaching task to study scenarios of distribution shift due to changes in expert preference. The robot policy was a neural network. Four expert policy scenarios were examined: Stationary, Shift, Drift, and Environment Shift. ConformalDAgger was compared against EnsembleDAgger, LazyDAgger, and SafeDAgger.

- **Takeaway 1: Expert Shift Scenario:** When the expert's goal shifted (e.g., at episode 5), ConformalDAgger immediately increased its requests for expert feedback. For instance, interventions increased from approximately 20% to 60% of the trajectory length after the shift. This contrasts with EnsembleDAgger and SafeDAgger, which showed smaller increases (remaining around 30%). This increased querying by ConformalDAgger correlated with a lower miscoverage rate (max 0.4, converging to 0.2) compared to baselines (max 1.0, converging to 0.4). Consequently, ConformalDAgger's retrained policies exhibited minimized decision and trajectory deviations, indicating faster alignment with the shifted expert. LazyDAgger also queried frequently but demonstrated higher decision and trajectory deviation possibly due to less consistent querying across episodes or different query effectiveness.

- **Takeaway 2: Expert Drift Scenario:** In scenarios where the expert's goal slowly drifted over multiple episodes, ConformalDAgger consistently increased feedback requests at each drift point. While EnsembleDAgger showed similar query levels at the first and last shifts, ConformalDAgger maintained a consistently lower miscoverage rate and led to more rapid policy adaptation (lower decision and trajectory deviation) on average. This was hypothesized to be due to ConformalDAgger's more consistent querying across different random seeds.

- **Takeaway 3: Stationary Expert Scenario:** Without any distribution shift, all methods (ConformalDAgger, EnsembleDAgger, LazyDAgger, SafeDAgger) maintained minimal querying behavior, staying close to the inherent human-gated intervention probability (20%). All achieved similar performance in aligning with the expert's stationary policy. This indicates that ConformalDAgger does not excessively query when uncertainty is genuinely low.

- **Takeaway 4: Environment Shift Scenario:** Under covariate shift (changing robot start position), ConformalDAgger and EnsembleDAgger showed comparable performance, balancing intervention frequency with policy learning quality marginally better than LazyDAgger or SafeDAgger.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

Hardware experiments were conducted on a 7-degree-of-freedom robotic manipulator performing a sponging task, utilizing a Diffusion Policy for visuomotor control. The goal was to wipe a line drawing on a whiteboard. Expert shift was induced by changing the human teleoperator's wiping strategy from a straight line to a zig-zag pattern.

- **Intervention Behavior:** ConformalDAgger significantly increased the cumulative number of robot-requested interventions (up to ~35 timesteps of human feedback) when interacting with the shifted expert (zig-zag pattern) compared to the stationary expert (straight line). This response was observed as uncertainty intervals grew sufficiently large to trigger robot queries.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** EnsembleDAgger and SafeDAgger did not request as many interventions as ConformalDAgger or LazyDAgger when the expert shifted. While LazyDAgger showed a similar intervention profile to ConformalDAgger, its queries were less reliable across episodes, resulting in insufficient data for learning the shifted strategy. The paper suggests this could be because EnsembleDAgger's and SafeDAgger's uncertainty estimates, uncalibrated to deployment-time data, might not detect the need for additional feedback during critical phases of the task (e.g., when the sponge contacts the whiteboard).
- **Qualitative Policy Improvement:** Qualitatively, the robot policy retrained with data from ConformalDAgger's interventions exhibited a more distinct zig-zag pattern, closely aligning with the shifted expert's strategy. Policies from EnsembleDAgger and SafeDAgger, which queried less, did not learn the zig-zag pattern as effectively. This suggests that ConformalDAgger's adaptive querying mechanism effectively gathered necessary data for policy adaptation.

Overall, the findings demonstrate ConformalDAgger's ability to adaptively quantify uncertainty, detect shifts in expert policy (or environment), and strategically solicit feedback. This leads to more efficient data collection and faster policy adaptation in both simulated and real-world robotic scenarios, particularly in the presence of distribution shifts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel approach to uncertainty quantification in interactive imitation learning, with several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Reliability of Robot Policies under Distribution Shift:** By enabling robots to adaptively quantify uncertainty and actively seek feedback, ConformalDAgger addresses a fundamental limitation of imitation learning: performance degradation due to distribution shift. This is crucial for deploying robots in real-world, dynamic environments where the training distribution may not perfectly match deployment conditions, leading to more robust and reliable autonomous systems.

2. **Theoretical Foundation for Intermittent Feedback:** The introduction of Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT) provides a principled, distribution-free method for quantifying uncertainty in settings with intermittent labels. This extends the applicability of online conformal prediction to a wider range of real-world scenarios beyond interactive IL, such as human-robot collaboration, active learning, and online control where ground-truth observations are often sparse or delayed. The asymptotic coverage guarantees offer a strong theoretical basis for the reliability of the uncertainty estimates.

3. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction and Efficiency:** ConformalDAgger's ability to strategically query experts based on calibrated uncertainty can lead to more efficient human-robot interaction. By asking for help primarily when truly uncertain or when a shift occurs, the system minimizes unnecessary interventions, reducing human workload and cognitive burden. This targeted querying ensures that the expert's valuable time and effort are utilized effectively to provide the most informative feedback.

4. **Faster Policy Adaptation and Learning:** The empirical results demonstrate that ConformalDAgger allows the robot to adapt its policy more quickly to new expert strategies or environmental changes. By rapidly detecting shifts and acquiring relevant data, the robot can learn new behaviors and correct erroneous ones with fewer deployment episodes and interventions than some existing methods. This accelerates the iterative learning process in interactive IL.

5. **Scalability to Complex Robotic Tasks:** The successful deployment on a 7DOF robotic manipulator performing a visuomotor sponging task, utilizing a state-of-the-art Diffusion Policy, indicates the scalability of ConformalDAgger to high-dimensional and complex real-world control problems. This suggests its potential for application in various advanced robotic applications requiring human guidance, such as manufacturing, assistive robotics, and exploration.

6. **Broader Impact on Uncertainty Quantification in AI:** The concept of modeling and integrating intermittent feedback into online uncertainty quantification has implications beyond robotics. Many AI systems interact with humans or environments where labels or ground truth are intermittently available. IQT provides a framework that could be adapted to these domains, leading to more robust and reliable AI systems that can quantify and adapt their uncertainty in the face of partial information.

In summary, this work advances the field of interactive imitation learning by providing a theoretically grounded and empirically validated method for adaptive, online uncertainty quantification. Its impact is likely to be seen in the development of more reliable, efficient, and adaptable robotic systems that can learn effectively from human experts in complex and changing environments.
