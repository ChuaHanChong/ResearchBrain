# Flattening Hierarchies with Policy Bootstrapping

> [!summary] Summary
> Subgoal Advantage-Weighted Policy Bootstrapping (SAW) is an algorithm for offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning that trains a single, non-hierarchical policy for long-horizon tasks. SAW eliminates the need for a generative model over the subgoal space, achieving strong performance in complex environments such as a 35% success rate in `humanoidmaze-giant-navigate`.

## Problem

- Scaling Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) to complex, long-horizon tasks is challenging due to sparse rewards and temporal discounting.
- Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) methods, while effective, introduce significant architectural complexity, particularly from the need to learn a generative model over high-dimensional subgoal spaces.
- Current offline GCRL approaches face limitations in scalability and unified task representation, hindering their application as general 'foundation policies'.

## Method

- The ==Subgoal Advantage-Weighted== Policy Bootstrapping (**SAW**) algorithm was developed, introducing a flat (non-hierarchical) objective for a goal-conditioned policy.
- SAW eliminates the need for a generative model over the subgoal space by directly estimating expectations via advantage-weighted importance sampling on in-trajectory waypoint states.
- The method combines a one-step Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR) objective with a policy bootstrapping component that leverages a separately trained subpolicy for stable action targets.

## Results

- SAW matched or surpassed state-of-the-art baselines across 20 state- and pixel-based offline GCRL datasets, achieving a **73%** success rate in `antmaze-giant-navigate`.
- The algorithm was the first to demonstrate non-trivial success (**35%** success rate) in `humanoidmaze-giant-navigate`, significantly improving upon the previous best of **12%**.
- SAW scaled effectively to pixel-based locomotion tasks like `visual-antmaze-large` and achieved state-of-the-art performance in state-based manipulation tasks, outperforming existing methods in 5 `scene` tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Empirical analysis revealed that the success of hierarchical methods in offline GCRL is significantly due to improved sampling efficiency from training low-level policies on nearby subgoals, in addition to better value function signal-to-noise ratio.
> - Hierarchical policy optimization can be reframed as probabilistic inference over optimal subgoal states, providing a unified theoretical framework.
> - Directly using advantage-weighted importance sampling for subgoals from the dataset, rather than learning a generative model, removes a major source of complexity and enhances scalability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work operates within the field of Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically focusing on Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) in an offline setting. GCRL specifies tasks by desired outcomes, thereby eliminating the need for manually defined reward functions and enabling the training of general policies capable of achieving diverse goals. Offline GCRL extends this paradigm to existing datasets of reward-free trajectories, a concept that has been compared to self-supervised objectives used for training foundation models in computer vision and natural language processing.

A significant challenge in scaling GCRL, particularly to long-horizon tasks, involves the combination of sparse rewards and temporal discounting. These factors can obscure the utility of primitive actions with respect to distant goals, making it difficult to learn accurate value functions and effective policies.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) is a common approach to address these long-horizon challenges. HRL methods exploit the recursive subgoal structure inherent in goal-reaching tasks by employing multiple levels of policies: high-level policies generate intermediate subgoals, and low-level policies execute primitive actions to achieve these subgoals. HRL has demonstrated strong empirical performance in both online and offline GCRL, especially for tasks with long horizons and sparse rewards.

However, HRL also presents limitations. Its modular policy architectures, tied to specific temporal abstractions, can hinder unified task representations and necessitate the learning of a generative model over the subgoal space to bridge different policy levels. This generative modeling problem can be complex, especially in high-dimensional observation spaces, often requiring additional processing to ensure subgoal realism and reachability. Alternatively, learning in compact latent subgoal spaces introduces nonstationary input distributions for low-level actors, which can destabilize training. The choice of objective for learning these representations further adds to the design complexity of HRL methods.

This research aims to address these limitations by developing a flat (non-hierarchical) goal-conditioned policy that can incorporate the benefits of hierarchies without their associated complexity. It builds upon the idea of policy bootstrapping, particularly related to Reinforcement learning with Imagined Subgoals (RIS), which also uses subgoal-conditioned policies to train a flat goal-conditioned policy. The key differentiation of the present work is its method for eliminating the need for a generative model over the (sub)goal space, which is a major source of complexity in both HRL and previous bootstrapping approaches like RIS.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an algorithm for training a single, flat (non-hierarchical) goal-conditioned policy that can effectively solve complex, long-horizon tasks in offline GCRL settings. This objective is motivated by several factors:

- **Addressing HRL Complexity:** Existing hierarchical RL (HRL) methods, despite their empirical success in long-horizon goal-reaching, introduce significant additional complexity. This complexity arises from their reliance on modular, timescale-specific policies and the necessity of learning a generative model for the subgoal space. The authors aim to eliminate these complexities while retaining the performance benefits.
- **Scaling to High-Dimensional Goal Spaces:** The generative modeling problem inherent in HRL, particularly for predicting intermediate goals in high-dimensional observation spaces, poses a significant hurdle to scalability. Learning suitable subgoal representations and simultaneously optimizing policies can lead to nonstationary input distributions and slow/unstable training. A core motivation is to develop an approach that scales effectively to such environments by removing the need for a generative subgoal model.
- **Distilling HRL Advantages:** The authors empirically analyze a state-of-the-art hierarchical method (HIQL) to identify the fundamental reasons for its success beyond improved value function signal-to-noise ratio. They find that training a low-level policy on nearby subgoals significantly improves sampling efficiency. This insight motivates an approach that can capture these benefits—better signal-to-noise ratio and improved sampling efficiency from nearby goals—within a unified, flat policy architecture.
- **Unified Task Representation:** HRL's modularity, with policies fixed to specific levels of temporal abstraction, precludes a unified task representation. The research seeks to develop a single, one-step policy capable of handling a wide range of goal horizons, thus offering a more unified and potentially generalizable approach.
- **Enhancing Offline GCRL Applicability:** The practical challenges of offline GCRL in learning effective policies for long-horizon behaviors question its scalability as a general objective for learning "foundation policies." By providing a more scalable and less complex method, the work aims to enhance the applicability of offline GCRL for pretraining generalist policies on large, reward-free datasets.

In summary, the motivation is to achieve the long-horizon performance of hierarchical methods using a conceptually simpler, flat policy by circumventing the challenges associated with generative subgoal models and modular architectures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Subgoal Advantage-Weighted Policy Bootstrapping (SAW), builds on insights from probabilistic inference and an empirical analysis of hierarchical methods.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

The work addresses offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, where the agent learns a policy `π` from a fixed dataset `D` of reward-free trajectories `τ = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1..., s_T)`. The objective is to maximize the expected discounted sum of goal-conditioned rewards `R(s_t, g)`.

- **Offline Value Learning:** The method utilizes Goal-Conditioned Implicit Value Learning (GCIVL), an action-free variant of Implicit Q-Learning (IQL). GCIVL minimizes an expectile regression loss `L_GCIVL` to train a value function `V_ψ(s, g)`, avoiding overestimation for out-of-distribution actions. It employs an action-free estimator `r(s, g) + γ V̄(s', g)`.
- **Offline Policy Extraction:** Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR) is used to extract policies. AWR reweights state-action pairs from the dataset based on their exponentiated advantage `exp(α(Q̄(s,a,g) - V(s,g)))` to maximize `log π(a|s,g)`, thereby keeping the learned policy within the support of the data. The action-free `Q̄` estimate is used for advantage computation.

#### 3.2. Understanding Hierarchies in Offline GCRL

An empirical investigation of Hierarchical Implicit Q-Learning (HIQL) was conducted to identify its core advantages for offline GCRL. HIQL learns high-level policies over subgoals and low-level policies over primitive actions from a single goal-conditioned value function using AWR.

- **Value Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR):** Previous work attributed HRL success to improved SNR in value functions for distant goals. To test this, a baseline called Goal-Conditioned Waypoint Advantage Estimation (GCWAE) was proposed. GCWAE uses a high-level policy to generate "imagined" subgoals and approximates the advantage of actions with respect to these subgoals. While GCWAE improved over one-step policy learning, it still underperformed HIQL, suggesting other factors were at play.
- **Sampling Efficiency for Closer Goals:** The analysis revealed that low-level policies in HIQL benefit from training on nearby subgoals (k-step future states from the dataset). For these shorter horizons, the dataset is more likely to contain high-advantage actions, leading to better sampling efficiency compared to distant goals or "imagined" subgoals generated by a high-level policy. This insight suggests that solutions from constrained policy optimization depend on the action sampling distribution.
- **Test-Time Policy Bootstrapping:** Hierarchical methods are reframed as performing "test-time policy bootstrapping," where a high-level policy predicts optimal subgoals, and a low-level subpolicy's action distribution conditioned on these subgoals is used to estimate the full goal-conditioned policy.

#### 3.3. Subgoal Advantage-Weighted Policy Bootstrapping (SAW)

The insights from the empirical analysis are unified into a novel objective for a flat policy. The approach views hierarchical policy optimization as probabilistic inference over optimal subgoal states.

- **Hierarchical RL as Inference:** A probabilistic model is constructed with an optimality variable `U` for subgoals, where `p(U=1|τ, {w}, g)` is proportional to `exp(β * sum(γ^t * A(s_t, w_t, g)))`. Policies are considered in a factored hierarchical form. An Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) on the optimality likelihood is derived, which, after expansion and approximation with dataset samples, results in an objective involving KL divergences for policy optimization. This framework can also recover RIS and the bilevel HIQL objective through specific design choices.
- **Eliminating the Subgoal Generator:** A key innovation in SAW is to eliminate the need for learning a generative model `π_h(w|s,g)` over the subgoal space. Instead of minimizing the KL divergence between a parametric generative model and a sample-based posterior `q_h(w|s,g)`, SAW directly estimates the expectation over `q_h(w)` using Bayes' rule. This leads to `p(w|s, g, U=1) ∝ p_D(w|s) * exp(A(s,w,g))`, where `p_D(w|s)` is the dataset distribution of subgoals. This effectively uses advantage-weighted importance sampling on in-trajectory waypoint states from the dataset, removing the complexity of a generative subgoal model.
- **The SAW Objective:** The final SAW objective `J(θ)` combines this advantage-weighted bootstrapping term with a one-step AWR signal.
 `J(θ) = E[exp(αA(s,a,g)) log π_θ(a|s,g) - exp(βA(s,w,g)) D_KL(π_θ(a|s,g)||π_sub(a|s,w))]`
 Here, `α` and `β` are inverse temperature hyperparameters. The first term represents a one-step AWR objective, and the second term is the policy bootstrapping component. `π_sub(a|s,w)` is a separately trained subpolicy that acts as a stable action target, trained with AWR on goals sampled from k-steps into the future. This objective dynamically balances the two terms, with the bootstrapping term gaining emphasis as the goal horizon increases and one-step value signals become noisier.
- **Training Scheme (Algorithm 1):** The full training scheme involves three main phases:
 1. Training the value function `V_ϕ` using GCIVL.
 2. Training the target subpolicy `π_ψ` using AWR on `(s_t, a, w)` sampled from the dataset.
 3. Training the main policy `π_θ` using the combined SAW objective.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Simplified Advantage Estimates:** A simplified advantage estimate `V(w, g) - V(s_t, g)` is used, approximating the progress towards the goal.
- **Goal Sampling:** Value networks are trained with a mixture of current state goals, future in-trajectory goals (geometric sampling), and random goals. Policies are trained with uniformly sampled future states from the same trajectory.
- **Target Subpolicy:** A separate, smaller policy network `π_sub` is trained on k-step future goals, rather than an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the online policy parameters.
- **Architectures:** Networks consist of 3-layer MLPs, with additional Impala encoders for pixel-based observations. A goal encoder (MLP followed by a length-normalized linear projection) is used for the value function and main policy, enhancing performance in state-based locomotion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SAW was conducted across 20 state- and pixel-based offline GCRL datasets from the OGBench suite, covering locomotion and manipulation tasks, with performance averaged over 5 state-goal pairs per dataset.

- **Overall Performance:** SAW consistently matched or surpassed state-of-the-art baselines across a wide variety of tasks.
- **Long-Horizon Locomotion:** SAW demonstrated strong performance in long-horizon tasks, achieving a 73% success rate in `antmaze-giant-navigate` and becoming the first method to achieve non-trivial success (35% success) in `humanoidmaze-giant-navigate`, compared to a previous state-of-the-art of 12%.
- **Impact of Subgoal Representations:**
 - Training SAW's target subpolicy with HIQL's subgoal representations (from intermediate value function layers) negatively impacted performance in giant maze environments compared to training directly on observations.
 - HIQL itself failed to learn meaningful behaviors when predicting subgoals directly in the raw observation space (i.e., without learned representations), underscoring the trade-off: compact representations are crucial for high-level policy tractability but can constrain expressiveness.
- **Comparison with Offline RIS:** An offline variant of RIS (`RIS_off`), which bootstraps on generated subgoals, performed significantly worse than SAW, particularly in locomotion, suggesting the benefit of SAW's approach to avoid generative subgoal models.
- **Pixel-Based Locomotion:** SAW maintained strong performance with visual observations, scaling better than its hierarchical counterpart in `visual-antmaze-large`. However, a performance drop in `visual-antmaze-giant` was observed, along with value function training divergence for both HIQL and SAW in visual giant mazes, indicating challenges in scaling offline value learning to very long horizons with high-dimensional visual inputs.
- **State-Based Manipulation:** SAW achieved state-of-the-art performance in `cube` environments and significantly outperformed existing methods in the 5 `scene` tasks, which require complex compositional reasoning. It was noted that expectile regression-based value learning methods were sensitive to hyperparameters in `cube-single-play-v0`.
- **Pixel-Based Manipulation:** SAW and HIQL showed near-equivalent performance in visual manipulation tasks, suggesting that representation learning is a primary bottleneck in these environments, rather than long-horizon reasoning.
- **Ablation Study (One-step AWR term):** Removing the one-step AWR term in SAW primarily affected short-horizon, stitching-heavy tasks. Performance in longer-horizon locomotion and manipulation tasks was largely unaffected, supporting the hypothesis that policy bootstrapping is the main contributor to SAW's success in complex tasks.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** SAW demonstrated robustness to hyperparameter selection in locomotion tasks but was more sensitive to the value learning expectile parameter `τ` and the KL divergence temperature `β` in manipulation tasks.
- **Stitching/Explore Datasets:** SAW showed competitive performance with HIQL in `pointmaze-stitch` and `antmaze-stitch` but lagged in `explore` datasets and `humanoidmaze` stitching environments. This suggests that the generalization capabilities of generative models may become more important when data is suboptimal or sparse.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces Subgoal Advantage-Weighted Policy Bootstrapping (SAW), which represents an advancement in offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) with several significant implications:

- **Enhanced Scalability for Long-Horizon Tasks:** SAW demonstrates the ability to solve complex, long-horizon tasks, particularly in high-dimensional state and action spaces, where previous state-of-the-art methods have struggled or failed. This includes achieving non-trivial success in environments like `humanoidmaze-giant-navigate`. This improved scalability is critical for applying GCRL to real-world problems requiring extended sequential decision-making.
- **Simplification of Policy Architectures:** By effectively "flattening hierarchies," SAW provides a method to achieve the benefits of hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) for long-horizon control without the added complexity of modular, timescale-specific policies or the need for a generative model over the subgoal space. The elimination of the generative subgoal model is presented as a key factor enabling scalability to high-dimensional control.
- **Unified Theoretical Framework:** The work offers a unified derivation that connects SAW to existing hierarchical methods (HIQL) and other bootstrapping approaches (RIS) within a common probabilistic inference framework. This theoretical unification provides a deeper understanding of the relationships between these algorithms and the underlying principles that contribute to their effectiveness.
- **Advancement Towards Generalist Policies:** Offline GCRL is considered a promising approach for pretraining generalist or "foundation" policies on large, reward-free datasets. By addressing critical scalability challenges, SAW contributes to the development of more robust and broadly applicable policies that can achieve a wide range of goals efficiently.
- **Improved Stability in Long-Horizon Learning:** The research suggests that policy bootstrapping, particularly with stable action targets provided by subpolicies, can reduce variance in target estimates and policy gradients for long-horizon tasks where value functions are inherently noisy. This could lead to more stable and efficient training processes in complex environments.
- **Conceptual Link to Cognitive Science:** The authors draw a parallel between policy bootstrapping and the "chunking theory" of learning from neuroscience, suggesting that breaking down complex skills into "chunks" and then blending them into smoother movements is a mechanism for acquiring progressively longer and more complex skills. This connection offers a potential avenue for further interdisciplinary research.

In summary, SAW represents a step towards making offline GCRL more practical and scalable for complex, long-horizon control tasks by offering a simpler, yet effective, policy learning objective that leverages the strengths of hierarchical reasoning without its typical architectural overhead.
