# MARBLE: A Hard Benchmark for Multimodal Spatial Reasoning and Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from EPFL and ETH Zurich developed MARBLE, a new benchmark designed to assess multi-step multimodal spatial reasoning and planning in large language models using game-inspired tasks. Evaluations revealed that current state-of-the-art models perform near-randomly on core tasks like distinguishing correct multi-step plans or assembling 3D puzzles, demonstrating limitations in both structured perception and combinatorial reasoning capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing multimodal benchmarks often focus on shallow forms of reasoning or conflate perception with deeper multi-step reasoning, failing to rigorously challenge multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on complex planning and spatial understanding.
- There is a critical gap in hard benchmarks that specifically stress multi-step planning and spatial reasoning under physical constraints, which is essential for developing intelligent systems that can generalize and interact adaptively with complex environments.
- MLLMs' capacity for complex reasoning that integrates diverse modalities, especially vision and spatial understanding, remains nascent and poorly understood, hindering progress towards human-like intelligence.

## Method

- Introduces ==MARBLE==, a challenging game-inspired multimodal reasoning benchmark with two distinct tasks: ==M-Portal== (abstracted Portal 2 spatial planning) and ==M-Cube== (3D mechanical puzzle assembly).
- M-Portal requires MLLMs to evaluate the correctness of multi-step solution plans or fill in missing steps, incorporating screenshots and textual instructions, and evaluating the entire reasoning process.
- M-Cube challenges models to assemble a 3D cube from rendered images of interlocking pieces, with a ==programmatic solution validator== provided to assess the correctness of proposed assemblies and support iterative refinement.

## Results

- On M-Portal's plan correctness task, all evaluated MLLMs and text-only LLMs achieved near-random F1 scores of approximately **6%**, indicating an inability to reliably distinguish correct from incorrect multi-step plans.
- All advanced MLLMs completely failed on the harder CUBE task within M-Cube, achieving **0%** accuracy, and even on the simplified CUBE-easy task, only one model (GPT-o3) achieved a substantial accuracy (**72.0%**), while others performed significantly lower.
- A specific perception task for M-Cube revealed that models achieved **0%** accuracy in perfectly extracting structured visual information (5x5 array) from a single 3D-rendered jigsaw piece image, highlighting a foundational perceptual limitation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current MLLMs exhibit fundamental weaknesses in structured visual perception, unable to perfectly extract structured information from images (e.g., converting a 3D-rendered piece to a 2D array representation).
> - Navigating vast combinatorial search spaces for multi-step planning and problem-solving remains a significant bottleneck for state-of-the-art MLLMs, even when perceptual challenges are simplified.
> - While tool use and iterative feedback can offer marginal improvements on simplified tasks, they do not compensate for a lack of fundamental reasoning capability in highly complex multimodal scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of MARBLE is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of multimodal artificial intelligence, particularly concerning Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). While MLLMs have demonstrated impressive capabilities in integrating information from various modalities, primarily text and vision, their ability to perform complex, multi-step, and spatially grounded reasoning remains a significant open challenge.

Prior research in multimodal reasoning has largely focused on tasks that, according to the authors, emphasize "relatively shallow forms of reasoning." Benchmarks like ScienceQA, MathVista, and MMMU, while instrumental in showcasing MLLMs' proficiency in integrating visual and linguistic understanding for question answering, often prioritize factual retrieval or single-step inferences. The authors argue that these benchmarks frequently "conflate perception (e.g., interpreting an image or diagram) with reasoning (e.g., drawing logical inferences, comparing evidence, or crafting a multi-step plan)," thereby reducing complex reasoning to pattern matching. Furthermore, many abstract reasoning benchmarks in domains such as advanced mathematics or code generation tend to overlook the crucial aspect of multimodal embodiment and interaction with spatially and physically constrained environments.

A recent effort, MM-Escape, introduced an escape room-inspired benchmark. However, the authors point out that even frontier models achieved near-perfect scores on this task (up to 100% escape rate), indicating it did not sufficiently challenge their reasoning capabilities in a complex, multi-step setting. This highlights a critical gap: the lack of "hard benchmarks that stress multi-step planning and spatial reasoning under physical constraints."

MARBLE directly addresses this gap by introducing tasks that are "cognitively demanding" and explicitly designed to scrutinize MLLMs' capacity for deep, structured reasoning beyond simple perception or retrieval. By focusing on "reasoning trajectories and plans" rather than just final answer accuracy, MARBLE aims to provide a diagnostic tool for understanding model failures and driving foundational advances. The paper positions MARBLE as an "ARC-like test" for multimodal reasoning, drawing an analogy to the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) which challenged AI systems in abstract pattern recognition, pushing the boundaries of general intelligence. This work thus represents a crucial step towards developing MLLMs that can genuinely generalize across domains and interact adaptively with complex, real-world environments, a cornerstone for building more capable and robust intelligent systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the MARBLE research is to rigorously evaluate and subsequently advance the multi-step, multimodally grounded reasoning abilities of contemporary MLLMs. The authors identify a critical bottleneck in the current state of AI: while LLMs have made strides in step-by-step reasoning within text-only domains, MLLMs' capacities for complex reasoning that integrates diverse modalities, especially vision and spatial understanding, are still nascent and poorly understood.

The specific key objectives driving the creation of MARBLE include:

- **Scrutinizing Deep Multimodal Reasoning:** To develop a benchmark that specifically targets the ability of MLLMs to reason carefully and step-by-step through complex multimodal problems, moving beyond superficial forms of reasoning or direct information retrieval.
- **Emphasizing Multi-step Planning under Constraints:** To introduce tasks that necessitate the understanding and crafting of multi-step plans, while explicitly accounting for spatial, visual, and physical constraints inherent in complex environments.
- **Focusing on Reasoning Trajectories:** Unlike many existing benchmarks that only evaluate final answer accuracy, MARBLE aims to assess the correctness and coherence of the entire reasoning process or "chain of thought," providing mechanisms to evaluate intermediate step fidelity.
- **Identifying Current MLLM Limitations:** Through rigorous empirical evaluation, to expose the specific weaknesses and bottlenecks of state-of-the-art MLLMs in handling these cognitively demanding multimodal reasoning challenges.
- **Spurring Future Research:** To serve as a probing benchmark that illuminates the path for developing the next generation of MLLMs with significantly enhanced capabilities in multi-step multimodal reasoning and planning.

The primary motivation stems from the observation that human reasoning is inherently multimodal and sequential, constantly integrating information from various senses to draw conclusions and plan actions. Achieving such a level of complex, multi-step, multimodally grounded reasoning is deemed "critical for building intelligent systems that can generalize across domains and interact adaptively with complex environments." The authors believe that just as difficult challenges have historically propelled AI progress, a truly challenging benchmark for multimodal reasoning is necessary to catalyze foundational advancements in MLLM capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MARBLE is meticulously designed as a "challenging game-inspired multimodal reasoning benchmark" focusing on process-oriented, multi-step reasoning. It comprises two distinct yet complementary tasks: M-Portal and M-Cube.

**Benchmark Design Principles:**
The benchmark challenges MLLMs to:
- Decompose complex multimodal prompts into interpretable intermediate steps.
- Align information across diverse inputs (images, text).
- Craft multi-step plans that adhere to spatial and physical constraints.

**M-Portal Task:**
- **Inspiration:** The puzzle video game *Portal 2*, known for its complex spatial reasoning and physics-based mechanics (e.g., momentum conservation with portals).
- **Problem Abstraction:** Instead of direct game interaction, the task abstracts Portal 2 maps into visual question-answering problems. Models are provided with map screenshots, textual environment instructions, and challenged to evaluate or complete multi-step solution plans.
- **Data Collection:** Human annotators, experienced in *Portal 2*, selected 16 high-quality, self-contained maps from community test chambers. For each map, illustrative screenshots, textual instructions, a ground-truth "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) solution, and five deliberately mistaken steps were generated. The mistakes were designed to be independent, allowing for combinatorial creation of candidate plans.
- **Evaluation Subtasks:**
 - **Plan Correctness (Harder):** A binary classification task where the model determines if a given candidate CoT plan (potentially dozens of steps long, with up to five mistaken steps) is correct ("yes" or "no"). This task has a significant class imbalance (1 correct out of 32 candidates generated from 5 mistakes).
 - **Fill-the-Blanks (Easier):** The model is given a partial CoT plan with masked steps and must select the correct missing steps from a set of candidate options (correct steps and distractors), placing them in the right order.

**M-Cube Task:**
- **Inspiration:** The *Happy Cube* mechanical puzzle, involving assembling a 3D cube from six jigsaw-style pieces.
- **Problem Complexity:** Requires assigning each piece to a cube face with the correct orientation (rotation and/or flip). The search space is combinatorially vast (6! assignments * 8^6 orientations), with very few valid solutions.
- **Data Generation:** All samples are synthetically generated. A 5x5x5 cube is disassembled into 6 interlocking pieces, characterized by bump-and-gap patterns. These pieces are then shuffled and rendered from random 3D viewpoints as input images for the MLLM.
- **Solution Validator:** Crucially, a programmatic solution validator is provided. Instead of direct ground-truth comparison (due to rotational symmetries), the validator tests if a model's proposed assembly plan forms a perfect cube, identifying conflicts like mismatched edges. This tool supports an iterative refinement process.
- **Evaluation Subtasks:**
 - **CUBE (Full Complexity):** Models receive rendered images of the pieces and must arrange all 6 pieces without any hints.
 - **CUBE-easy (Simplified):** A simplified version to isolate reasoning from perception. Inputs are 2D arrays (textual representation) instead of images, solutions do not require flipping pieces, and a partial solution with 4 pre-arranged pieces is provided, reducing the search space significantly.

**Evaluated Models:** The study extensively evaluates 12 state-of-the-art MLLMs, including prominent open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL-72B, InternVL3-78B, Llama-4-Scout) and closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-o3, GPT-o4-mini, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-pro, Seed1.5-VL). Additionally, three text-only models (DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-R1-0528, Qwen3) are included by converting visual inputs to text, providing a baseline for the impact of multimodal inputs. Experiments were conducted on a single node with 8 Nvidia H200 GPUs for open-source models and via respective APIs for closed-source models, with careful control of hyperparameters (e.g., temperature, max tokens, reasoning effort).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 12 state-of-the-art MLLMs on the MARBLE benchmark reveals profound limitations in their multimodal spatial reasoning and planning capabilities.

**Overall Performance on MARBLE:**
- **M-Portal:** All evaluated models (MLLMs and text-only LLMs) performed very poorly on the `plan correctness` task, achieving near-random F1 scores of approximately 6%, indicating they could not reliably distinguish correct from incorrect multi-step plans. On the easier `fill-the-blanks` task, 8 out of 12 models slightly outperformed random chance, but even the best-performing model, GPT-o3, managed only 17.6% accuracy. This performance rapidly declined with an increasing number of masked steps, dropping from around 70% accuracy for a single blank to less than 1% for four or more blanks. Providing "hint images" offered only a marginal improvement (from 7.6% to 8.6% F1 for Seed1.5-VL on plan correctness), suggesting the problem's inherent difficulty.
- **M-Cube:** The results were even more striking on M-Cube. All advanced MLLMs completely failed on the harder `CUBE` task, achieving 0% accuracy despite extensive token usage for reasoning. On the simplified `CUBE-easy` task, while six models outperformed random guess, only GPT-o3 achieved a substantial accuracy of 72.0%, with the next best (GPT-o4-mini) at a mere 16%.

**Disentangling Perception and Reasoning Bottlenecks:**

- **Perception Bottleneck:** The authors conducted a specific perception task within M-Cube, asking MLLMs to convert a 3D-rendered jigsaw piece image into a 5x5 2D array representation (0 for gap, 1 for bump). Surprisingly, all models performed poorly, achieving only around 70% accuracy per cell. Critically, this translated to 0% accuracy on the entire 5x5 piece, meaning no model could perfectly extract the structured visual information from the input image. This finding suggests a fundamental weakness in structured visual perception, preceding any complex reasoning.
- **Reasoning Bottleneck (Vast Search Space):** To isolate the reasoning challenge from perceptual errors, visual inputs for M-Cube were manually converted into textual 2D arrays. Even with perfect perception, the complexity of the combinatorial search space proved daunting. For DeepSeek-R1, accuracy on CUBE-easy dropped from 57% with only one missing piece to 0% when three or more pieces were missing. This highlights that navigating vast combinatorial solution spaces is a significant bottleneck for current MLLMs.

**Tool Use and Iterative Refinement:**
The study explored the use of a solution validator tool for M-Cube, providing either binary or detailed diagnostic feedback. On `CUBE-easy`, GPT-o4-mini's performance improved significantly with feedback, increasing from 10% to 28% accuracy after five rounds with detailed feedback (outperforming binary feedback). However, on the more challenging `CUBE` dataset, tool use offered no improvement, with performance remaining at 0%. This indicates that while tool assistance can aid in simpler problems, it doesn't compensate for a lack of fundamental reasoning capability in highly complex scenarios. The authors also note that many current models struggle to effectively retain and build upon previous reasoning steps in multi-round interactions.

**Token Usage:** Advanced reasoning models generally used significantly more output tokens for their reasoning processes than open-source models, particularly for the `fill-the-blanks` task on M-Portal. Interestingly, some models (GPT-4o, GPT-o3, GPT-o4-mini) used fewer tokens on the harder `CUBE` task than on `CUBE-easy`, which the authors hypothesize might be due to the visual input of CUBE leading to less in-depth reasoning attempts.

In summary, the results overwhelmingly demonstrate that current state-of-the-art MLLMs are far from capable of robustly handling complex, multi-step multimodal reasoning and planning problems, suffering from both foundational perceptual limitations and challenges in navigating expansive reasoning search spaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of MARBLE marks a significant contribution to the field of artificial intelligence, particularly in the realm of multimodal language models and reasoning. Its primary significance lies in filling a critical void in the evaluation landscape for MLLMs.

**Key Significance of MARBLE:**
- **Diagnostic Power:** Unlike previous benchmarks that often conflate perception with deep reasoning or focus solely on final answer accuracy, MARBLE is designed to be highly diagnostic. By scrutinizing reasoning trajectories and planning processes under explicit spatial and physical constraints, it provides a much clearer picture of where MLLMs struggle—whether it's at the level of raw perception, combinatorial search, or the logical sequencing of steps.
- **"Hard" Benchmark for Progress:** The empirical findings unequivocally demonstrate that MARBLE is indeed a "hard" benchmark, with state-of-the-art models performing at or near random levels on its core tasks. This extreme difficulty is not a drawback but a strength; historically, such challenging problems (e.g., ImageNet for computer vision, ARC for abstract reasoning) have been instrumental in driving significant breakthroughs and architectural innovations in AI. MARBLE is poised to play a similar role for multimodal reasoning.
- **Focus on Embodied Intelligence:** By grounding its tasks in "game-inspired" environments like Portal 2 and Happy Cube, MARBLE shifts the focus towards reasoning in "spatially and physically constrained environments." This is crucial for developing AI systems that can interact with and understand the physical world, moving beyond purely abstract or linguistic reasoning, which is essential for embodied AI and robotics.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Driving Next-Generation MLLMs:** The stark performance gaps revealed by MARBLE are a clarion call for researchers to develop fundamentally new approaches to multimodal reasoning. This benchmark will likely serve as a common testbed for evaluating new architectures, training methodologies, and reasoning paradigms, accelerating progress in areas like causal inference, hierarchical planning, and robust perception for MLLMs.
- **Advancing Reasoning Capabilities:** The findings highlight that current MLLMs not only struggle with the complexity of multi-step reasoning but also with seemingly basic "structured perception" tasks (e.g., converting an image to a grid array). This dual bottleneck suggests that future research needs to concurrently address both robust visual understanding and sophisticated logical inference.
- **Refining Tool Use and Interactive AI:** The experiments with the solution validator tool underscore the potential of iterative refinement and tool-assisted reasoning. While current models struggle to leverage such tools effectively in complex scenarios, MARBLE provides a framework for developing models that can learn to "think with images," gather feedback from the environment, and adapt their strategies, mimicking human problem-solving processes more closely.
- **Real-World Applications:** Enhanced multimodal reasoning capabilities, spurred by benchmarks like MARBLE, have immense potential for positive societal impact. Applications span diverse domains such as advanced robotics (e.g., complex manipulation tasks), healthcare (e.g., interpreting medical images and patient data for diagnosis and treatment planning), accessibility (e.g., intelligent assistance for visually impaired individuals navigating complex spaces), and education (e.g., interactive learning environments for STEM subjects).

While acknowledging the risk of overfitting to dataset-specific patterns, the authors rightly point out that the abstract puzzle domains chosen for MARBLE mitigate direct societal risks. By providing a rigorous and challenging benchmark, MARBLE ensures that future multimodal AI systems are not only powerful but also robust, reliable, and beneficial when deployed in real-world, complex scenarios.
