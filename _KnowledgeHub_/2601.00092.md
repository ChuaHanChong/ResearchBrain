# Spatial4D-Bench: A Versatile 4D Spatial Intelligence Benchmark

> [!summary] Summary
> Huawei Technologies researchers and academic collaborators introduced Spatial4D-Bench, a large-scale, multi-task benchmark designed to evaluate 4D spatial intelligence in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). Evaluations across 18 tasks revealed a substantial 17-22% performance gap between MLLMs and humans in dynamic reasoning, with models struggling particularly in spatiotemporal continuity and physical plausibility despite proficiency in some basic object perception tasks.

## Problem

- Existing Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) struggle with comprehensive 4D spatial intelligence, often acting as "frame-based observers" rather than understanding dynamic, evolving scenes.
- Prior spatial intelligence benchmarks are often limited in scale and diversity, predominantly focusing on static 3D scenes and neglecting the crucial temporal dimension for evaluating spatiotemporal awareness.
- There is a lack of rigorous, multi-faceted evaluation tools to precisely diagnose MLLMs' limitations in complex spatial and temporal reasoning, such as route planning and physical plausibility.

## Method

- Developed ==Spatial4D-Bench==, a versatile 4D spatial intelligence benchmark containing approximately ==40,000 question-answer pairs== spanning ==18 distinct tasks==.
- Tasks are organized into a hierarchical taxonomy of six cognitive categories, progressing from basic object/scene understanding to advanced spatiotemporal and physical reasoning, utilizing diverse real-world video data.
- A four-stage construction pipeline ensures benchmark quality and diversity by unifying data from multiple sources, employing hybrid human and automatic QA generation, and thorough human review processes.

## Results

- Humans achieved an average score of **78.02%** on the benchmark, outperforming the best proprietary MLLM (GPT-5 at **60.90%**) by approximately **17%** and the best open-source model (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B at **56.17%**) by **22%**.
- MLLMs showed substantial performance degradation in reasoning tasks, scoring only **32.83%** on 'Route Plan' and **30-40%** on 'Physical Plausibility Reasoning,' contrasting sharply with human scores of **91.67%** and **66.67%** respectively.
- While MLLMs occasionally surpassed human performance on perception-related tasks like object size estimation (e.g., Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B at **80.10%** vs. human **74.61%**), they performed near random chance on tasks requiring intuitive physics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLLMs exhibit a significant overall gap in 4D spatial intelligence compared to human performance, indicating they lack coherent 4D world models and an intuitive understanding of physics.
> - MLLMs struggle notably with spatiotemporal continuity and long-term spatial memory, with performance degrading as video length increases, pointing to limitations in current information retrieval and state representation architectures.
> - Visual ablation studies demonstrated that incomplete visual frames can act as "adversarial distractors," sometimes causing MLLMs to prioritize flawed visual evidence over correct language priors, leading to confident hallucinations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly with the advent of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), has been progressing towards capabilities that mimic human cognitive abilities, including spatial understanding and reasoning. Spatial cognition in humans involves the perception, interpretation, mental representation, and interaction with environmental spatial characteristics, and is often assessed through various standardized tests. Analogously, researchers are developing MLLMs to unify language and vision, with the objective of achieving human-level spatial understanding.

Recent MLLMs have demonstrated performance across a range of multimodal tasks; however, the extent to which they achieve human-level spatial cognition remains an area of ongoing investigation. To address this, several spatial intelligence benchmarks have been developed. Examples include SpatialRGPT, 3DSRBench, VSI-Bench, STI-Bench, LEGO-Puzzles, MMSI-Bench, MIRAGE, SpaCE-10, InternSpatial, VLM4D, and SIBench. These existing benchmarks generally focus on foundational spatial reasoning capabilities such as room size estimation, object distance reasoning, and object counting, predominantly assessing 3D spatial intelligence in static scenes.

A notable limitation of many existing benchmarks is their scale and diversity, which can restrict a comprehensive evaluation of MLLM capabilities. Furthermore, the real world is a 4D environment where spatial and temporal aspects are continuously evolving. While some benchmarks like STI-Bench, VSI-SUPER, and VLM4D have incorporated aspects of spatiotemporal reasoning, their evaluation data can be limited in diversity or scale. This limitation poses a challenge in thoroughly assessing MLLMs' 4D spatial reasoning and identifying the gap between current MLLM performance and human-level 4D spatial intelligence, which involves perceiving and processing object movement and dynamic change over time.

Spatial4D-Bench is presented as an advancement in this landscape by addressing the limitations of prior work. It aims to provide a large-scale, multi-task, and comprehensively structured benchmark that explicitly targets 4D spatial intelligence. Unlike benchmarks focused primarily on 3D static environments, Spatial4D-Bench emphasizes the dynamic, time-evolving aspects of spatial reasoning. By offering a broader range of tasks, including those previously under-investigated, and a significantly larger dataset, this work seeks to enable a more robust evaluation of MLLMs' spatial cognitive abilities, encompassing the versatility observed in human spatial intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce Spatial4D-Bench, a versatile 4D spatial intelligence benchmark, to comprehensively assess the 4D spatial reasoning abilities of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs).

The motivation for developing Spatial4D-Bench stems from several factors:

- **Understanding MLLM Spatial Intelligence:** Humans naturally possess 4D spatial intelligence, enabling a broad spectrum of spatial reasoning abilities. A key motivation is to investigate the extent to which current MLLMs can achieve comparable human-level 4D spatial intelligence.
- **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** Current spatial intelligence benchmarks are often limited in scale or task diversity. Many predominantly focus on 3D spatial intelligence and static scenes, neglecting the evaluation of spatiotemporal awareness, which is crucial for understanding the inherently 4D nature of the real world where objects move and change over time. This lack of comprehensive evaluation hinders a complete understanding of MLLM capabilities in dynamic environments.
- **Need for Comprehensive Assessment:** There is a need for a benchmark that can comprehensively evaluate various aspects of spatial perception, understanding, and reasoning. Spatial4D-Bench is designed to offer a structured and comprehensive evaluation suite that parallels the versatility of human spatial intelligence, covering a wider spectrum of tasks.
- **Identification of MLLM Deficiencies:** By providing a detailed and challenging benchmark, the researchers aim to reveal specific limitations of MLLMs in various 4D spatial reasoning aspects. Examples cited include route planning, action recognition, and physical plausibility reasoning. Identifying these gaps is intended to provide insights into areas requiring further model development.
- **Facilitating Future Development:** The ultimate motivation is that the findings and the benchmark itself will offer valuable insights to the research community, thereby facilitating the development of more capable MLLMs that can approach human-level 4D spatial intelligence. The benchmark is positioned as a tool to drive progress in this field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Spatial4D-Bench is a large-scale, multi-task 4D spatial intelligence benchmark comprising approximately 40,000 question-answer (QA) pairs across 18 well-defined tasks. These tasks are systematically organized into six cognitive categories, adhering to human spatial cognition principles. The benchmark utilizes a diverse collection of data, encompassing indoor and outdoor environments, various objects, actions, and scenes viewed from both egocentric and allocentric perspectives.

The construction of Spatial4D-Bench involves a four-stage pipeline:

#### 3.1. Data Collection

The initial stage involves gathering data from a variety of publicly available datasets to ensure diversity and comprehensiveness. These datasets include Charades-Ego, ARKitScenes, ScanNet, ScanNet++, 3RScan, RoomTour3d, EPIC-KITCHENS, ADL, EgoTaskQA, HoloAssist, IndustReal, YouCook2, Video-MME, VideoPhy-2, and nuScenes. These sources contribute data modalities such as text, RGB videos, and point clouds (the latter primarily used for QA annotation). A human verification loop is integrated to filter out low-quality scans or incomplete annotations, maintaining data reliability.

#### 3.2. Data Unification

In the second stage, the heterogeneous data collected from various sources are converted into a unified metadata structure, consistent with protocols used in previous benchmarks like VSI-Bench. This standardization supports consistent downstream task generation and facilitates error tracing during human review. Specific preprocessing steps are performed for certain tasks; for instance, room names are tagged on frames from RoomTour3D for the route plan task, and video clips of varying lengths are extracted from datasets like EPIC-KITCHENS for action prediction and spatial memory tasks to meet particular QA design requirements. Human review and verification are also applied during this stage to ensure data unification correctness.

#### 3.3. QA Pairs Generation

QA pairs are generated through a hybrid approach combining human annotation and automatic template-based generation:
- **Human Annotation:** For complex spatiotemporal understanding and high-level cognition tasks, such as object attribute estimation, spatial memory, state change detection, egocentric reasoning, route plan, action prediction, and physical plausibility reasoning, well-educated annotators are recruited to design QA pairs. This approach is favored for these tasks due to the higher quality of human-generated questions.
- **Automatic Template-Based Generation:** For tasks involving rigid geometric properties like object counts, dimensions, and distances, automated generation is employed using predefined templates and ground-truth labels from the unified metadata.
- **Instruction Formats:** For tasks involving 3D bounding boxes and coordinate systems, multiple instruction formats are provided, including object 3D bounding boxes, text descriptions of objects/rooms, or a combination, to test models with diverse input types.
- **Continuous Review:** Throughout this stage, humans continuously review and verify the generated QA pairs to refine, correct, and filter out incorrect or ambiguous questions, options, and answers.

#### 3.4. Final Human Review

The final stage involves a review by experienced AI researchers to identify and filter any remaining ambiguous or incorrect QA pairs. Detected errors trigger a feedback loop, tracing issues back to the source metadata for correction. This iterative refinement process ultimately leads to the selection of approximately 40,000 high-quality QA pairs for the Spatial4D-Bench.

#### 3.5. Task Taxonomy

The 18 tasks are systematically organized into a hierarchical taxonomy of six core categories, progressing from object/scene-level perception to high-level dynamic spatial/spatiotemporal reasoning:

- **Object Understanding (9,302 QA pairs):** Evaluates perception of intrinsic physical properties. Includes Object Size Estimation, Object Attribute Estimation, Object Counting, and Affordance Estimation (requiring 3D grounding for functional descriptions).
- **Scene Understanding (7,296 QA pairs):** Assesses comprehension of global semantic and geometric context. Encompasses Room Size Estimation, Scene Classification, and 3D Grounding (object detection and localization in 3D space).
- **Spatial Relationship Understanding (8,286 QA pairs):** Focuses on resolving geometric topology and metric layout between entities. Comprises Absolute Distance Estimation, Relative Distance Estimation, and Relative Orientation Estimation.
- **Spatiotemporal Relationship Understanding (10,686 QA pairs):** Incorporates the temporal dimension to evaluate tracking object states and dynamics. Includes Action Recognition, Appearance Order, Spatial Memory (tracking occluded objects), and State Change Detection.
- **Spatial Reasoning (2,000 QA pairs):** Assesses the agent's ability to reason about its own state and plan movements. Consists of Egocentric Reasoning (inferring observer's spatial state) and Route Plan (long-horizon trajectory planning across multiple rooms).
- **Spatiotemporal Reasoning (1,735 QA pairs):** Represents the highest level of spatial cognition, involving abstracting perceptual data into predictive models and physical laws. Tasks are Action Prediction and Physical Plausibility Reasoning (identifying violations of physical laws).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation on Spatial4D-Bench involved benchmarking a diverse set of state-of-the-art MLLMs, including proprietary models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5-Pro) and open-source models (VideoLLama3, Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3-VL, InternVL3.5) with model sizes ranging from 7B to 241B parameters. All evaluations were conducted in a zero-shot setting, using exact matching/fuzzy matching for multiple-choice answers and Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA) for numerical answers. Human-level performance (based on qualified evaluators) and chance-level baselines (random and frequency-based) were used for comparison.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Gap

A significant performance gap exists between MLLMs and human-level 4D spatial intelligence. The best proprietary model, GPT-5, achieved an average score of 60.90%, while the top-performing open-source model, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B, scored 56.17%. In contrast, human evaluators achieved an average score of 78.02%, outperforming GPT-5 by approximately 17 percentage points and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B by 22 percentage points. This suggests that current MLLMs may function more as "frame-based observers" than "world observers," lacking an intuitive physics engine or coherent temporal memory required for effective causal reasoning and understanding of object permanence and dynamics.

#### 4.2. MLLM Strengths: Understanding-Related Tasks

MLLMs demonstrated competitive, and in some instances, superior performance compared to humans on fundamental object and scene understanding tasks. For example, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B achieved 80.10% on object size estimation, surpassing the human baseline of 74.61%. Similarly, MLLMs sometimes outperformed humans on object counting. This is attributed to MLLMs leveraging vast amounts of pre-training data to acquire prior knowledge for precise metric estimation from 2D projections, a task humans may struggle with without explicit reference scales. Human performance on tasks like object counting could also be affected by low-resolution videos, jittering, inconsistent frames, and subjective categorization discrepancies.

#### 4.3. MLLM Weaknesses: Reasoning-Related Tasks

The primary source of the human-AI performance gap was identified in spatial reasoning and spatiotemporal reasoning tasks. MLLMs showed substantial performance degradation in these categories. For spatiotemporal reasoning, even top-tier MLLMs lagged significantly behind humans (e.g., GPT-5 scored 32.83% on route plan compared to human 91.67%). This approximately 60% gap indicates a fundamental limitation in MLLMs' ability to maintain a coherent 4D world model over extended temporal sequences. In physical plausibility reasoning, MLLMs scored close to random chance (30–40%), whereas humans achieved 66.67%, suggesting that MLLMs struggle to ground visual perception in fundamental physical laws.

#### 4.4. Benchmark Challenge Analysis

A comparison with VSI-Bench on overlapping tasks indicated that Spatial4D-Bench is more challenging. While performance on perception-related tasks like object size and room size was comparable across benchmarks, there were substantial divergences in more complex reasoning tasks. For instance, models exhibited a 22% to 29% performance drop on the route plan task in Spatial4D-Bench compared to VSI-Bench, attributed to the requirement for long-horizon planning and 4D spatial understanding over longer sequences and more complex state transitions in Spatial4D-Bench.

#### 4.5. Further Analysis and Discussion

- **Temporal Context and Spatial Memory:** Performance on spatial memory tasks degraded as video length increased (e.g., Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B’s accuracy dropped from 53.81% for 5-minute videos to 42.50% for 30-minute videos). This highlights limitations of fixed-frame sampling in current MLLM architectures and suggests a need for adaptive sampling or streaming memory architectures for 4D spatial intelligence.
- **Visual Ablation Study:** A study on Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B revealed several patterns:
 - **Necessity of 4D Signals:** Removing visual input resulted in an average performance drop of 23.40%, confirming that 4D spatial intelligence tasks require explicit temporal integration beyond language models alone.
 - **"Blind" Leading "One-Eyed":** On eight tasks, text-only input surprisingly outperformed single-frame image input. This suggests that for tasks requiring global or temporal continuity, a single, potentially misleading random frame can act as an adversarial distractor, overriding reliable language priors.
 - **Route Plan Failure:** Providing full video input for route plan showed negligible improvement over the text-only baseline (12.00% vs 13.67%), indicating MLLMs struggle to construct coherent spatial maps from egocentric videos, effectively reverting to random guessing or language priors.
- **Qualitative Case Analysis:** Case studies with GPT-5’s self-explanation capabilities provided insights into failure modes:
 - **Spatiotemporal Continuity:** MLLMs can prioritize semantic priors over specific visual temporal actions, leading to errors in action prediction (e.g., "placing in sink" vs. "washing"). They succeed when explicit cues (like OCR) are available.
 - **Knowledge-Perception Gap in Intuitive Physics:** MLLMs may possess abstract knowledge of physical laws but lack the visual grounding to detect subtle violations in pixel space, relying on textual plausibility of options rather than visual dynamics.
 - **Spatial Hallucination:** Perceptual ambiguity, such as texture similarity, can lead to confident hallucinations in spatial memory tasks, where models construct coherent but false narratives.
 - **Egocentric Route Plan:** MLLMs struggle with long-horizon spatial consistency and relative orientation in route planning, often hallucinating spatial layouts that fit internal narratives but contradict visual reality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Spatial4D-Bench represents a notable contribution to the evaluation of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) in the context of spatial intelligence. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Comprehensive Evaluation Framework:** Spatial4D-Bench provides a large-scale, multi-task, and structured benchmark that comprehensively assesses MLLMs' 4D spatial reasoning abilities. By covering 18 diverse tasks across six cognitive categories, it offers a more holistic evaluation than many existing benchmarks, which often have limitations in scale, diversity, or focus on static 3D environments. This comprehensive scope is critical for understanding the breadth of human-like spatial cognition.
- **Exposure of Core MLLM Limitations:** The benchmark has been instrumental in exposing critical limitations of current MLLMs in 4D spatial intelligence. Key findings indicate significant gaps in areas such as temporal coherence, physical grounding, long-horizon planning, egocentric reasoning, and the ability to appropriately weigh conflicting visual evidence versus language priors. These are fundamental cognitive abilities for intelligent agents operating in dynamic, real-world environments.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The detailed findings, particularly the identification of specific failure modes and the qualitative analysis of MLLM reasoning processes (e.g., through self-explanations), offer actionable insights for the research community. This information can guide the development of new architectural designs, training methodologies, and data curation strategies aimed at improving MLLMs' spatiotemporal reasoning, intuitive physics, and robust spatial memory.
- **Advancing Human-Level AI:** By establishing a clearer picture of where MLLMs currently stand relative to human performance in 4D spatial intelligence, Spatial4D-Bench contributes to the broader goal of achieving human-level AI. It highlights areas where substantial efforts are still required, such as moving from "frame-based observers" to "world observers" that can maintain a coherent 4D mental model over time.
- **Stimulating Architectural Innovation:** The findings related to temporal context modeling and the impact of video length suggest that a paradigm shift from fixed-context windows to adaptive sampling or streaming memory architectures may be necessary for MLLMs to effectively handle long-duration 4D spatial tasks.
- **Validating the Need for Multimodality:** The visual ablation study demonstrates that 4D spatial intelligence tasks genuinely require explicit temporal integration of visual inputs, validating the design choice of the benchmark and the necessity of multimodal data for such complex reasoning.

In summary, Spatial4D-Bench serves as a robust diagnostic tool, revealing the current bottlenecks of MLLMs in critical aspects of 4D spatial intelligence. Its release is intended to facilitate targeted advancements, fostering the development of more capable MLLMs that can navigate, understand, and reason within dynamic, time-evolving environments with greater proficiency.
