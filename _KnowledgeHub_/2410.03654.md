# Learning Humanoid Locomotion over Challenging Terrain

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley developed a learning-based control system, combining sequence modeling pre-training with reinforcement learning fine-tuning, that enables a bipedal robot to traverse diverse and difficult terrains previously challenging for humanoids. This single controller, deployed on a Digit humanoid robot, successfully navigates steep streets up to 31% grade, rough hiking trails for over 4 miles, and loose sand without terrain-specific tuning.

## Problem

- Humanoid locomotion over challenging and unpredictable terrains is difficult due to their inherent instability.
- Existing control methods either struggle with the complexities of natural terrain (model-based) or are limited to gentle terrains (learning-based).
- Developing a single controller capable of robustly navigating diverse and extreme terrains for bipedal robots remained an open challenge.

## Method

- A transformer network serves as the core control system, processing proprioceptive observations to output target joint positions and PD gains.
- A two-stage training methodology is introduced: pre-training with sequence modeling on flat-ground walking data.
- The pre-trained model is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning in simulation environments with diverse challenging terrains and domain randomization.

## Results

- The Digit humanoid robot successfully traversed diverse real-world terrains, including steep San Francisco streets (up to **31%** grade), over 4 miles of hiking trails, and previously unseen loose beach sand.
- The proposed two-stage approach (HT-2) demonstrated superior performance over prior methods, achieving higher success rates on challenging terrains and improved gait stability and energy efficiency.
- The controller exhibited emergent adaptive behaviors, such as implicitly learning terrain representations and automatically adjusting its gait and posture based on terrain properties.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single learning-based controller can effectively handle a wide range of diverse and challenging terrains for bipedal robots.
> - Combining sequence modeling pre-training with reinforcement learning fine-tuning substantially improves sample efficiency and learning stability.
> - The controller can implicitly learn to recognize different terrain types and adapt its gait and posture solely from proprioceptive feedback, without explicit terrain information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Humanoid locomotion across challenging and unpredictable terrains represents one of the most difficult problems in robotics. While quadrupedal robots have made significant strides in navigating uneven terrain, bipedal robots face inherently greater challenges due to their less stable configuration. The paper "Learning Humanoid Locomotion over Challenging Terrain" by researchers from UC Berkeley presents a breakthrough approach that enables a bipedal robot to traverse diverse and difficult terrains previously considered nearly impossible for humanoid robots.

The researchers developed a learning-based control system that combines sequence modeling pre-training with reinforcement learning fine-tuning, resulting in a controller that enables a humanoid robot to navigate steep hills, rough terrain, rocky paths, and even loose sand. Most remarkably, their approach created a single controller capable of handling all these diverse terrains without terrain-specific tuning or adaptation.

### 2. Research Context and Significance

Traditionally, robot locomotion controllers have followed one of two approaches: model-based control methods that rely on precise dynamics models and simplified abstractions, or learning-based methods that directly map sensory observations to control actions. Model-based approaches excel in structured environments but struggle with the complexities of natural terrain, while learning-based methods show promise for adaptability but have primarily succeeded on relatively gentle terrains.

This work represents a significant advancement by:

1. Demonstrating that a single learning-based controller can handle diverse challenging terrains
2. Introducing a two-stage training process that dramatically improves sample efficiency and performance
3. Showcasing successful real-world deployment on some of the steepest streets in San Francisco (up to 31% grade) and hiking trails with varied conditions
4. Identifying emergent capabilities in the learned controller, including terrain-specific gait adaptations

The approach builds upon recent work in applying transformer networks to robotics problems. However, unlike previous research, this paper specifically addresses the challenge of locomotion over difficult terrain and introduces a novel training methodology that yields superior performance.

### 3. Technical Approach

At the core of the system is a transformer network that processes sequences of proprioceptive observations (joint positions, velocities, etc.) and previous actions to predict the next control action. This architecture provides several advantages:

1. The ability to model complex temporal relationships in the walking dynamics
2. Capacity to learn from diverse datasets including expert demonstrations
3. Natural capability for in-context learning, enabling adaptation to terrain without explicit terrain sensing

The transformer takes proprioceptive observations as input, which notably do not include explicit terrain information or visual data. Instead, the model learns to infer terrain properties solely from how the robot's body responds to the terrain during locomotion.

The control output consists of target joint positions and PD gains, which are sent to the robot's low-level controller. This approach strikes a balance between high-level decision-making and low-level stability control.

### 4. Two Stage Training Methodology

The paper introduces an innovative two-stage training approach that significantly improves learning efficiency and performance:

#### 4.1. Stage 1: Pre-training with Sequence Modeling

In the first stage, the transformer model is pre-trained on a dataset of flat-ground walking trajectories using sequence modeling. This dataset includes:

- Trajectories from existing controllers and policies
- Human motion capture data
- Data from previous versions of the controller

The pre-training objective is to predict the next action given the history of observations and actions, essentially learning to imitate successful walking behaviors. This approach is more data-efficient and stable than reinforcement learning, allowing the model to develop a foundation of walking skills before tackling more challenging terrains.

#### 4.2. Stage 2: Fine-tuning with Reinforcement Learning

After pre-training, the model is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning in simulation environments with diverse challenging terrains. These environments include:

- Flat terrain (baseline)
- Hills and slopes (up to 35% grade)
- Rough surfaces with height variations
- Obstacle-filled terrains with blocks and steps
- Various terrain materials with different friction properties

The reinforcement learning process uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a reward function that encourages forward progress, stability, and energy efficiency. Domain randomization is applied during training to improve sim-to-real transfer.

The authors demonstrate that this two-stage approach yields dramatically improved sample efficiency compared to training from scratch. The pre-trained model reaches high performance levels with significantly fewer environment steps.

### 5. Results and Key Findings

The trained controller demonstrates remarkable capabilities when deployed on a Digit humanoid robot in real-world environments:

1. **Versatile Terrain Navigation**: The robot successfully traverses hiking trails with various terrains including dirt, rocks, grass, and mud.

2. **Extreme Grade Handling**: The robot climbs and descends some of San Francisco's steepest streets with grades up to 31%.

3. **Unseen Terrain Adaptation**: The robot adapts to terrain types not seen during training, such as loose sand, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities.

4. **Robust Performance**: The controller maintains stability even when encountering unexpected terrain changes or transitions between terrain types.

One of the most impressive demonstrations involves the robot completing over 4 miles of hiking across multiple trails in Berkeley Hills:

The robot's ability to adapt its gait for different terrains is particularly noteworthy. For example, when walking on sand versus a firm trail, the robot automatically adjusts its foot placement and force application.

### 6. Emergent Behaviors

Analysis of the learned controller reveals several emergent behaviors that weren't explicitly programmed:

1. **Terrain Representation Learning**: The model learns to cluster its internal representations based on terrain type, despite never receiving explicit terrain information. This indicates the model implicitly learns to recognize different terrains based solely on how they affect the robot's dynamics.

2. **Kinematic Adaptation**: The robot automatically adjusts its posture and gait parameters based on terrain slope, leaning into uphill climbs and modifying its stepping pattern for downhill descents.

3. **Dynamic Adaptation**: The controller modifies joint stiffness, foot placement, and force application based on terrain material properties, using different strategies for firm versus soft surfaces.

4. **Consistent Gait Timing**: Despite terrain variations, the controller maintains relatively consistent gait timing, indicating a learned fundamental walking rhythm that adapts to conditions without losing its basic structure.

These emergent behaviors demonstrate that the transformer architecture, combined with the two-stage training method, enables sophisticated adaptive behaviors without requiring explicit programming or terrain sensing.

### 7. Comparative Performance

The researchers compared their approach (which they call HT-2) against a previous state-of-the-art learning-based controller (HT) across various metrics:

The results show that the proposed approach significantly outperforms the previous system in several key areas:

1. **Success Rate on Challenging Terrain**: The new controller achieves much higher success rates on difficult terrains, particularly on steep uphill slopes where the previous controller's performance drops dramatically at higher grades.

2. **Gait Stability**: The proposed approach shows lower stride-to-stride variability on rough terrain, indicating more consistent and stable walking.

3. **Energy Efficiency**: The new controller demonstrates better mechanical cost of transport, especially when carrying payloads.

4. **Drift Reduction**: Both angular and linear drift are reduced, indicating better heading control and path following ability.

### 8. Real World Applications

The successful deployment in real-world environments demonstrates the practical applicability of the approach. The robot completed several challenging real-world tests:

1. **Urban Navigation**: Climbing and descending the steepest streets in San Francisco, including grades of up to 31%.

2. **Nature Trail Hiking**: Completing multiple hiking trails in Berkeley Hills with a total distance of over 4 miles, traversing varied terrain including dirt, grass, rocks, and mud.

3. **Beach Walking**: Successfully walking on loose beach sand, a terrain type not explicitly included in the training environments.

4. **Terrain Transitions**: Smoothly transitioning between different terrain types without losing stability.

These demonstrations illustrate the potential for deploying humanoid robots in real-world applications such as search and rescue, inspection in natural environments, and delivery services in urban areas with challenging topography.

### 9. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive capabilities, the approach has several limitations that point to directions for future research:

1. **Visual Perception**: The current controller is "blind," relying solely on proprioceptive feedback. Incorporating visual information could allow the robot to anticipate upcoming terrain changes and further improve performance.

2. **Global Navigation**: The controller focuses on local locomotion rather than global navigation or path planning. Integrating the locomotion controller with higher-level navigation would enable more autonomous operation.

3. **Terrain-Specific Optimization**: While the controller generalizes well across terrain types, it does not explicitly optimize for terrain-specific objectives such as energy efficiency on different surfaces.

4. **Limited Manipulation Capabilities**: The research focuses exclusively on locomotion; future work could integrate locomotion with manipulation skills for more versatile humanoid robots.

The authors suggest that incorporating visual perception would be particularly valuable for enabling anticipatory control, allowing the robot to adjust its gait before encountering terrain changes rather than reacting to them.

### 10. Conclusion

The research presented in "Learning Humanoid Locomotion over Challenging Terrain" represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robotics. By combining sequence modeling pre-training with reinforcement learning fine-tuning in a transformer architecture, the researchers have developed a control system that enables unprecedented performance on diverse challenging terrains.

The controller's ability to handle steep hills, rough surfaces, and even loose sand with a single unified policy demonstrates the power of learning-based approaches for complex robot control problems. The emergent behaviors, including terrain-specific gait adaptations and implicit terrain representation learning, highlight how sophisticated capabilities can arise from relatively simple training objectives.

The successful real-world deployment—traversing miles of hiking trails and conquering San Francisco's steepest streets—proves the practical utility of the approach and suggests exciting possibilities for future applications of humanoid robots in unstructured environments.

This work provides a strong foundation for future research in humanoid locomotion, particularly in integrating visual perception and developing unified models for both locomotion and manipulation. As these capabilities advance, we may soon see humanoid robots operating confidently in the diverse and challenging environments humans navigate every day.
