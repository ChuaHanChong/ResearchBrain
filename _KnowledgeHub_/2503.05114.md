# Look Before You Leap: Using Serialized State Machine for Language Conditioned Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Johns Hopkins University and the University of Arkansas developed a state-aware imitation learning framework that uses Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate and filter Finite State Machines (FSMs) for robust robotic demonstration collection. This method achieved up to 98.0% success rates on complex long-horizon tasks like Towers of Hanoi, significantly outperforming control methods that struggled to generalize with random environment initializations.

## Problem

- Current LLM-based imitation learning for robotics struggles with long-horizon tasks requiring precise, state-dependent reasoning due to policies failing to generalize to dynamically evolving states.
- Training data generated from randomly initialized object placements often leads to cascading errors as policies encounter unseen or structurally inconsistent environmental states.
- Existing methods lack a systematic approach to ensure comprehensive coverage of all possible, structurally consistent intermediate states during demonstration collection.

## Method

- Introduced a 'state-aware' imitation learning framework that systematically integrates LLM-generated and filtered ==Finite State Machines (FSMs)== with environment-conditioned robot policy training.
- Developed a State Machine Serialization Language (SMSL), a textual FSM representation generated by LLMs through a multi-step process of identifying states, operations, and valid transitions via filtering.
- Modified task demonstration code to enable environment passing between executions and deterministic object referencing, ensuring state consistency while allowing initial state randomization under constraints.

## Results

- Achieved significantly higher success rates on complex long-horizon tasks, with the proposed method reaching **98.0%** for Towers of Hanoi and Chess, and **96.2%** for River Crossing at 1000 demonstrations.
- The control method (simulating GenSim with random object placements) consistently underperformed, achieving only **58.0%** for Towers of Hanoi, **5.5%** for River Crossing, and **44.3%** for Chess at 1000 demonstrations.
- Demonstrated robust FSM navigation across the entire state space, validating the approach for tasks where intermediate states are as critical as final goal states, unlike control methods that suffered catastrophic failures in state-dependent puzzles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging LLMs to *indirectly* generate and filter formal FSM definitions ensures logical consistency and correctness in task planning, overcoming challenges of direct, unstructured LLM plan generation.
> - Systematic, state-aware demonstration collection, covering all valid FSM transitions, is crucial for training robust policies for long-horizon tasks that involve dynamically evolving environments.
> - Decoupling low-level object instantiation from high-level task logic, while preserving diversity through constrained initial state randomization, significantly improves generalization in complex manipulation scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of robotic manipulation, imitation learning, and the burgeoning field of Large Language Model (LLM)-grounded robotics. Recent advancements have seen LLMs being increasingly integrated into robotic systems for high-level task planning and execution, offering the potential for automating complex tasks through natural language instructions. Approaches such as those that use LLMs to generate task demonstration code for imitation learning (e.g., GenSim,) have gained traction, extending the paradigm of language-conditioned policies for goal setting and concept transfer.

However, the paper identifies a critical limitation in these existing frameworks: their success heavily depends on the comprehensiveness of the demonstration set. Current methods often rely on an assumption that all objects are randomly spawned in simulation during demonstration collection. While this randomization can be beneficial for short-horizon or loosely constrained tasks where precise positioning or ordering is less critical, it introduces significant challenges for "long-horizon" tasks that require a sequence of precise interactions and where the environment's state dynamically evolves. In such scenarios, policies trained on randomly initialized demonstrations struggle with generalization, leading to cascading errors when encountering previously unseen or structurally inconsistent environmental states. The paper explicitly illustrates this with tasks like Towers of Hanoi, where random initializations fail to capture the sequential spatial reasoning required.

The proposed work directly addresses this gap by introducing a "state-aware" approach to demonstration generation. It builds upon the strengths of LLMs for planning and code generation but enhances the robustness and reliability by incorporating formal state machine logic. This differentiates it from existing LLM-robotics paradigms that might focus on emergent planning capabilities or broad generalization from diverse but unstructured data. Instead, this paper argues for a more structured, deterministic approach to demonstration collection, particularly for tasks where environmental consistency and sequential dependency are paramount. By explicitly leveraging LLM-generated FSMs, the research aligns with efforts to make LLM-based robot task planning more deterministic and capable of situated awareness, pushing the boundaries of what complex, long-horizon tasks LLM-powered robots can reliably perform. It represents a move towards making LLM-based robotic systems not just capable of *generating* plans, but also of *ensuring the correctness and coverage* of the underlying data for robust policy learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from a critical weakness in current LLM-based imitation learning frameworks for robotic manipulation: their inability to reliably handle long-horizon tasks that demand precise, state-dependent reasoning and interaction. The paper articulates this problem by highlighting that existing approaches, which typically train on demonstrations generated from randomly initialized object placements, frequently fail when confronted with dynamically evolving environmental structures during execution. This leads to poor generalization and cascading errors, particularly when the training data does not cover how to act in all possible, structurally consistent intermediate states of a complex task.

The authors' core objectives are therefore to:
1. **Enhance Robustness and Generalization:** Develop a framework that overcomes the limitations of demonstration coverage, ensuring that the robot's policy can generalize effectively to situations involving dynamically changing environmental states in long-horizon tasks.
2. **Ensure State-Awareness in Policy Training:** Introduce a mechanism that makes the imitation learning process explicitly aware of the precise geometric and logical constraints of complex, multi-step tasks.
3. **Prevent Cascading Failures:** Design a system that minimizes the occurrence of errors that propagate through sequential actions, which is common when policies trained on random data encounter unforeseen but structurally significant environmental configurations.
4. **Leverage LLMs for Structured Planning:** Utilize the advanced capabilities of LLMs not for direct, unstructured plan generation, but for systematically creating and managing Finite State Machine (FSM) definitions that guide robust demonstration collection.

These objectives are guided by two key principles: "divide and concur" and "look before you leap." "Divide and concur" refers to breaking down complex tasks into manageable sub-actions and reasoning about intermediate environmental structures. "Look before you leap" emphasizes the proactive identification and training on all (or most) predicted valid states, rather than relying on a reactive policy that might encounter novel, unhandled states. Ultimately, the research aims to pave the way for LLM-powered robots to reliably perform real-world tasks where environmental states must be rigorously tracked and propagated across extended sequences of actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework introduces a "state-aware" imitation learning pipeline that systematically integrates Finite State Machines (FSMs) with environment-conditioned robot policy training, all orchestrated by Large Language Models (LLMs). The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

**A. SMSL Generation via LLM-Based States, Operations, and Transitions Filtering:**
The core of the method is the generation of a State Machine Serialization Language (SMSL), a textual representation of FSMs that defines task workflows. To overcome the challenges of LLMs directly generating comprehensive FSMs for complex tasks, an *indirect* generation approach is used:
1. **Operation and State Candidate Generation:** An LLM first analyzes the high-level task description to list independent operations (e.g., "move Object A to Position B") and generates symbolic state representations (e.g., "State A"). It then enumerates all possible candidate states through a Cartesian product, initially ignoring constraints.
2. **State Filtering:** The LLM constructs filter functions based on explicit task constraints. These filters are applied to the candidate states to rule out invalid configurations, resulting in a set of valid states.
3. **Operation Assignment and Transition Filtering:** For each valid state, the LLM identifies permissible operations. It then generates code to infer state transitions based on valid state-operation pairs, predicting the resultant (goal) state. A second application of state and operation filters verifies the validity of these goal states, removing any transitions that lead to invalid configurations.
4. **SMSL Formatting:** The remaining valid transitions are then formatted into the SMSL text (e.g., JSON), representing the complete FSM for the task. This systematic, LLM-guided filtering process ensures the logical consistency and correctness of the FSM.

**B. State-Aware Task Demonstration Code Design:**
To address the limitations of existing approaches (like GenSim) that reset the environment and randomly spawn objects for each demonstration, the authors introduce a modified task code format featuring three key improvements:
1. **Randomized Environment Under Constraints (Initial State Only):** The environment is randomized only for the *initial state* and strictly under predefined constraints, ensuring compliance with symbolic state requirements.
2. **Environment Passing:** Rather than repeatedly resetting and spawning objects, the environment is directly passed between task code executions, preserving its state.
3. **Deterministic Object Referencing:** A persistent dictionary maps scene entities to runtime object IDs, ensuring consistent referencing across multiple episodes.
These changes decouple low-level object instantiation from high-level task logic, allow LLMs to inspect geometric features when necessary, and maintain dataset diversity while guaranteeing state consistency.

**C. Prompt Engineering for Task Generation Agents:**
A multi-agent LLM pipeline transforms the symbolic operations from the SMSL into executable task demonstration scripts:
1. **Agent 0 (Human/LLM): Initial-State Environment Setup Script:** This agent generates scripts to spawn objects at random poses *only for the initial state* under geometric constraints and establishes persistent mappings for object IDs. This script is verified to ensure consistency with the initial state's configurations.
2. **Agent 1: Task Specification Generation:** An LLM reads the initial state setup script and task description to produce a structured JSON task specification. This includes an entity list, entities requiring geometric inspection, and a natural language task summary.
3. **Agent 2: Task Code Generation:** The final LLM agent generates the actual task code using APIs (e.g., CLIPort), drawing on the JSON specification and, if necessary, inspecting URDF files for geometric features to calculate precise placement offsets.

**D. Demonstration Dataset Generation:**
The generation of the training dataset is a two-phase process:
1. **Path Generation:** A graph search algorithm (depth-first search maximizing state coverage or multiple minimum-length paths) is applied to the constructed FSM. This generates sequences of operations that cover all (or exemplary) states, guiding the exploration of environment configurations.
2. **Demonstration Collection:**
 - The environment is initialized to the first state in a generated path, and its object poses (state configurations) are stored.
 - Operations are applied sequentially, and the resulting state configurations are recorded after each step. This process continues along the paths until all relevant configurations are collected and stored in a state dictionary. This approach ensures deterministic state reconstruction.
 - Finally, the system iterates through *all transitions* defined in the SMSL (allowing parallel execution). For each transition, it reconstructs the environment using the stored configurations, executes the corresponding LLM-generated task code, and collects the required data (RGB-D observations, language goals, actions) for imitation learning (e.g., CLIPort). Crucially, the environment is then initialized in *random poses* that are *consistent with the constraints* for the *next* state, preserving diversity.

This comprehensive methodology ensures that the generated demonstrations accurately represent all valid state transitions and configurations within the defined task, making the training data robust to the dynamic environmental changes inherent in long-horizon manipulation tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the proposed methodology was conducted on three complex tabletop manipulation tasks, each representing a distinct Finite State Machine (FSM) with varying levels of complexity: Towers of Hanoi, River Crossing, and a Chess puzzle (swapping two pieces on a board with impassable blocks). These tasks were chosen to highlight scenarios where intermediate states are as crucial as final goal states, reflecting real-world applications with many valid transitional configurations. The objective was not merely to reach a specific goal, but to demonstrate robust FSM navigation across its entire state space.

The proposed method was compared against a control condition, which simulated existing approaches (like GenSim) by randomly placing all objects on the table during demonstration collection. Both methods were trained with 200, 500, and 1000 demonstrations per operation, and the average success rate across all operations served as the evaluation metric.

The results, summarized in Table II of the paper, vividly illustrate the effectiveness of the state-aware approach:

- **Towers of Hanoi (tolerance 0.01m, 15 degrees):**
 - **Our Method:** Achieved success rates of 84.9% with 200 demonstrations, improving to 94.2% with 500, and reaching a high of **98.0%** with 1000 demonstrations.
 - **GenSim (Control):** Showed significantly lower performance, with success rates hovering around 58-60% (58.7%, 60.6%, 58.0%) across all demonstration numbers. This indicates a clear ceiling on performance due to the lack of state awareness.

- **River Crossing (tolerance 0.06m, 15 degrees):**
 - **Our Method:** Started at 71.6% with 200 demonstrations, increasing to 94.7% with 500, and achieving **96.2%** with 1000 demonstrations.
 - **GenSim (Control):** Experienced catastrophic failure, with success rates as low as 2.7% (200 demos), marginally increasing to 7.7% (500 demos), and 5.5% (1000 demos). This demonstrates a near-complete inability to solve the task.

- **Chess (tolerance 0.01m, 15 degrees):**
 - **Our Method:** Achieved 89.0% with 200 demonstrations, rising to 92.9% with 500, and reaching **98.0%** with 1000 demonstrations.
 - **GenSim (Control):** Showed poor performance, with success rates ranging from 39.4% to 44.3% across the different demonstration counts, indicating significant generalization issues.

**Discussion of Findings:**
The results unequivocally demonstrate that the proposed method, which leverages serialized FSMs for state-aware demonstration generation, dramatically improves the success rate of robotic manipulation in long-horizon, state-dependent tasks. In contrast, the control condition, relying on random object placements, consistently underperformed, and in the case of the River Crossing puzzle, almost entirely failed. This validates the paper's central hypothesis: randomized training distributions are insufficient for tasks requiring sequential spatial reasoning and structural consistency, especially when objects are subject to dynamically evolving spatial constraints. The ability of the proposed framework to achieve near-100% success rates, even with tasks exhibiting complex FSM configurations, underscores the critical importance of rigorously tracking and propagating environmental states. This robust performance allows LLM-powered robots to move beyond loosely constrained tasks towards performing real-world applications where precise and reliable interactions are non-negotiable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Look Before You Leap" offers a significant advancement in the field of language-conditioned robotic manipulation, particularly by addressing a critical limitation of current LLM-based imitation learning frameworks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Gap for Long-Horizon, State-Dependent Tasks:** The most immediate impact is enabling LLM-powered robots to reliably execute complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks that require precise interactions and sensitive state awareness. Previously, these tasks were prone to cascading errors due to the inability of policies trained on randomly initialized environments to generalize to dynamically evolving states. This work provides a robust solution, opening up a wider array of real-world applications for LLM-robot integration.

2. **Enhanced Robustness and Determinism:** By introducing the State Machine Serialization Language (SMSL) and an LLM-guided filtering process, the framework ensures a highly deterministic and logically consistent planning process. This reduces the ambiguity and potential for errors often associated with purely generative LLM approaches, leading to more reliable and predictable robot behavior, which is crucial for safety-critical or high-precision applications.

3. **Improved Data Efficiency and Quality for Imitation Learning:** The state-aware demonstration generation ensures comprehensive coverage of all valid states and transitions within a task's FSM. This systematic approach to data collection creates higher-quality training datasets that are tailored to the task's inherent structure. While the paper still uses a significant number of demonstrations (up to 1000 per operation), the *quality* and *relevance* of these demonstrations are drastically improved, leading to superior policy generalization compared to less structured data collection methods.

4. **Automated, Structured Demonstration Generation:** The LLM-driven pipeline for generating SMSL, task specifications, and task code significantly automates the traditionally labor-intensive process of creating diverse and comprehensive demonstration datasets. This automation reduces human effort and expertise required, making it easier to scale robotic training to new, complex tasks.

5. **Foundation for Verified Robotic Systems:** The explicit use of Finite State Machines provides a formal basis for the robot's behavior. This structured approach could pave the way for future research into formal verification methods for LLM-grounded robotic policies, enhancing trust and safety in autonomous systems. Given the authors' background and funding in biomedical fields, this aspect is particularly impactful for applications like surgical robotics or laboratory automation, where rigorous validation is paramount.

6. **Advancement in LLM-Robotics Integration:** This work demonstrates a sophisticated way to integrate LLMs into robotic control beyond simple command interpretation or unstructured planning. By using LLMs to *indirectly* generate and refine formal task representations (FSMs), the approach leverages the reasoning capabilities of LLMs in a structured manner, pushing the frontier of intelligent agent design in robotics.

In conclusion, "Look Before You Leap" offers a foundational contribution to making LLM-powered robotic manipulation more reliable, generalizable, and applicable to the complex, state-dependent tasks found in real-world environments. Its emphasis on structured, state-aware data generation for imitation learning sets a new benchmark for developing robust robotic policies.
