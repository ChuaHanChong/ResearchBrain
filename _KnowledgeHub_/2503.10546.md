# KUDA: Keypoints to Unify Dynamics Learning and Visual Prompting for Open-Vocabulary Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> KUDA enables robots to understand and execute natural language instructions for manipulating diverse objects by unifying vision-language models and dynamics learning through a keypoint-based representation. This framework successfully handles rigid, deformable, and granular materials, achieving 48 out of 60 successful manipulations and outperforming baseline methods on various complex tasks.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to interpret complex natural language instructions and translate them into precise, physics-aware actions for manipulating diverse objects due to the inherent 'representation gap' between high-level language and low-level control.
- Handling objects with varied physical behaviors, such as rigid bodies, deformable ropes, and granular materials, poses challenges for traditional dynamics models and generalizable manipulation strategies.
- Defining concrete target states or cost functions for planning algorithms from abstract natural language commands is difficult, especially for complex spatial arrangements.

## Method

- **KUDA** employs a keypoint-based representation, where visually identifiable points sampled on object surfaces unify visual prompting and dynamics learning, providing a compact and interpretable form for both VLMs and planning.
- The framework uses visual prompting and a prompt library for few-shot VLM integration, enabling the VLM to interpret natural language instructions and generate keypoint-based code defining target object configurations.
- A two-level closed-loop planning mechanism combines neural dynamics models for predicting keypoint evolution and ==Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)== for trajectory generation, with periodic VLM re-querying for high-level error correction and adaptation.

## Results

- KUDA successfully performed manipulation tasks across a wide variety of objects, including rigid blocks, deformable ropes (e.g., forming fractions), and granular materials (e.g., collecting coffee beans).
- The framework handled complex natural language instructions, such as 'Move the T shape to make a word ROBOT,' demonstrating its ability to process multi-step and conceptual commands.
- KUDA achieved a success rate of 48 out of 60 trials across various tasks, quantitatively outperforming baseline methods like MOKA and VoxPoser in terms of manipulation success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Keypoints serve as a unified intermediate representation, bridging the high-level understanding of vision-language models (VLMs) with low-level dynamics learning and precise control for diverse object manipulation.
> - Integrating visual prompting with VLMs allows for effective interpretation of natural language instructions and generation of executable code for target keypoint configurations.
> - A two-level closed-loop planning mechanism, combining neural dynamics models for low-level execution with high-level VLM re-querying, enhances robustness and adaptability to dynamic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Robots that can understand and execute complex manipulation tasks from natural language instructions are a longstanding goal in robotics research. The challenge lies in bridging the gap between high-level language commands and the precise, physics-aware control needed for successful manipulation of diverse objects. Recent advances in large vision-language models (VLMs) have enhanced robots' ability to interpret instructions, but they often fall short when dealing with complex object dynamics and physical interactions.

This paper presents KUDA (Keypoints to Unify Dynamics Learning and Visual Prompting), a framework that addresses these limitations by integrating dynamics learning with visual prompting through a unified keypoint-based representation. KUDA enables robots to perform manipulation tasks on a wide variety of objects—from rigid blocks to deformable ropes and granular materials like coffee beans—all from natural language instructions.

### 2. Challenges in Open-Vocabulary Manipulation

Open-vocabulary robotic manipulation faces several significant challenges:

1. **Dynamic Complexity**: Objects in the real world exhibit diverse physical behaviors. Rigid objects move predictably, while deformable objects like ropes and granular materials like coffee beans have complex dynamics that are difficult to model.

2. **Representation Gap**: There's a fundamental disconnect between the high-level understanding of VLMs and the low-level control required for precise manipulation. VLMs can interpret instructions but struggle to translate them into precise robot actions.

3. **Target Specification**: Translating natural language commands into concrete target states or cost functions for planning algorithms is non-trivial, especially for complex manipulation tasks involving multiple objects or specific spatial arrangements.

4. **Environmental Adaptability**: Real-world environments are dynamic and unpredictable, requiring robots to continuously adapt their plans based on current observations.

### 3. KUDA Framework Overview

KUDA addresses these challenges through a novel approach that uses keypoints as a unified representation to bridge dynamics learning and visual prompting. The framework consists of several integrated components:

The workflow begins with an RGB-D observation of the scene and a natural language instruction. A large vision model identifies and segments objects in the scene, and keypoints are sampled on these objects. The Vision-Language Model (VLM), guided by a prompt library, interprets the instruction and generates target specifications using the keypoint representation. These specifications are translated into code that defines the desired spatial relationships between keypoints. The system then uses neural dynamics models and model-based planning to generate robot actions that achieve the target state.

### 4. Keypoint-Based Representation

The core innovation of KUDA is using keypoints as a unified intermediate representation:

1. **Definition**: Keypoints are distinctive points on objects that are visually identifiable. They can be sampled using methods like Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) from object masks.

2. **Advantages**:
 - Keypoints are intuitive for VLMs to interpret and express
 - They can be directly tracked across time to model dynamics
 - They provide a compact representation for diverse objects
 - They can be translated into cost functions for planning

3. **Implementation**: The system uses the Segment Anything model for semantic mask segmentation and then applies FPS to obtain keypoints on object surfaces. A reference point (usually the centroid) is also identified for each object to serve as a coordinate origin.

The mathematical representation of keypoints is straightforward. For each object, the system tracks a set of keypoints $\{p_1, p_2..., p_n\}$ and a reference point $C$. Target specifications are expressed as arithmetic relationships between these points, such as:

```python
# Move red cubes to the left
p1 = C + [-20, 0, 0]
p3 = C + [-20, 0, 0]
p5 = C + [-20, 0, 0]

# Move purple cubes to the right
p2 = C + [20, 0, 0]
p4 = C + [20, 0, 0]
p6 = C + [20, 0, 0]
```

This representation is both interpretable by VLMs and useful for dynamics modeling and planning.

### 5. Visual Prompting and VLM Integration

KUDA leverages visual prompting to enhance the visual grounding capabilities of VLMs:

1. **Visual Markers**: Keypoints are visualized as colored markers on RGB images, serving as visual prompts that help the VLM understand the spatial configuration of objects.

2. **Prompt Library**: The system maintains a library of successful prompt examples for different tasks. When faced with a new task, a prompt retriever selects the most relevant examples using CLIP embeddings to match the current observation and instruction with stored examples.

3. **Few-Shot Learning**: The selected examples are used for few-shot prompting of the VLM, showing it how to translate similar instructions into keypoint-based target specifications.

4. **Code Generation**: The VLM generates executable code that defines the target configuration of keypoints. This code serves as a formal specification of the goal state that can be used to derive cost functions for planning.

The prompt structure follows a pattern that includes:
- The current observation with keypoints visualized
- The language instruction
- A few-shot demonstration of similar tasks
- A request to generate code for the target keypoint configuration

### 6. Two-Level Closed-Loop Planning

KUDA implements a two-level closed-loop planning mechanism to ensure robust execution:

1. **Low-Level Closed Loop**:
 - **Neural Dynamics Models**: Predict how keypoints will evolve over time in response to robot actions
 - **Model-Based Planning**: Uses the Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) algorithm to generate robot trajectories based on the cost function derived from the target specifications
 - **Execution**: The robot executes the planned trajectory, and the system continuously tracks keypoints to update the model's predictions

2. **High-Level Closed Loop**:
 - The VLM is re-queried periodically with the current observation and instruction
 - Target specifications are updated based on the current state of the environment
 - This enables the system to correct errors and adapt to dynamic environments

The cost function for planning is defined as the distance between predicted keypoint positions and their target positions:

$C(s_t, a_t) = \sum_{i} w_i ||p_i(s_t, a_t) - p_i^{target}||_2^2$

where $p_i(s_t, a_t)$ is the predicted position of keypoint $i$ after applying action $a_t$ in state $s_t$, $p_i^{target}$ is the target position, and $w_i$ is a weight coefficient.

### 7. Experimental Results

KUDA was evaluated on a diverse set of manipulation tasks involving different object types and complex instructions:

The experiments demonstrated KUDA's capabilities in handling:

1. **Rigid Objects**: Moving colored blocks to specific locations, arranging them in patterns, or stacking them.

2. **Deformable Objects**: Manipulating ropes to form shapes like fractions (1/9) or following curved paths.

3. **Granular Materials**: Collecting scattered coffee beans into a designated area.

4. **Complex Instructions**: Following multi-step or conceptually complex instructions like "Move the T shape to make a word 'ROBOT'" or "Move the cubes to the sticker that writes 'pink'."

Quantitative evaluation showed that KUDA significantly outperformed baseline methods (MOKA and VoxPoser) in terms of success rate across different task categories. Out of 60 total trials, KUDA achieved 48 successful manipulations, demonstrating its effectiveness and versatility.

### 8. Error Analysis

To understand the limitations of the system, the researchers conducted a detailed error analysis:

The analysis revealed several sources of failure:

1. **VLM Errors (2/60)**: Cases where the VLM provided under-specified targets that didn't capture the full intent of the instruction.

2. **Perception Errors (6/60)**: The most common source of failures, including:
 - Camera depth estimation issues (1/6)
 - Object overlap difficulties (3/6)
 - Failure to detect adjacent objects (3/6)
 - Tracking failures with dense object piles (2/6)

3. **Dynamics Model Errors (1/60)**: Rare cases where the dynamics model failed to predict the correct states, particularly for complex interactions.

This analysis highlights that perception remains the primary bottleneck for the system, with dynamics modeling and VLM interpretation being relatively robust.

### 9. Significance and Future Directions

KUDA represents a significant advancement in open-vocabulary robotic manipulation for several reasons:

1. **Unified Representation**: By using keypoints to bridge dynamics learning and visual prompting, KUDA establishes a common language between high-level understanding and low-level control.

2. **Versatility**: The system demonstrates competence across a wide range of objects and tasks, including challenging cases like deformable objects and granular materials that previous approaches struggled to handle.

3. **Robustness**: The two-level closed-loop planning mechanism enhances the system's ability to recover from errors and adapt to changes in the environment.

4. **Practical Applications**: The ability to follow natural language instructions makes KUDA applicable to various real-world scenarios, from manufacturing and logistics to healthcare and home robotics.

Future work could focus on:

1. Improving perception systems to address the main source of failures
2. Extending the approach to more complex multi-step tasks requiring longer-horizon planning
3. Incorporating tactile feedback to enhance manipulation of delicate or small objects
4. Reducing the computational requirements to enable real-time execution on more accessible hardware

KUDA demonstrates how unifying different AI approaches—vision-language models and dynamics learning—through an appropriate intermediate representation can address complex robotics challenges that neither approach could solve alone.
