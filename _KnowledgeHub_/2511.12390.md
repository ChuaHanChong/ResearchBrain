# Learning Adaptive Neural Teleoperation for Humanoid Robots: From Inverse Kinematics to End-to-End Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A neural teleoperation framework for humanoid robots, developed at the Georgia Institute of Technology, maps VR controller inputs directly to joint commands, significantly outperforming traditional Inverse Kinematics (IK) methods. The system achieved a 52% lower tracking error under external forces and 45% smoother motions on a Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating robust and natural control through a combination of imitation learning and reinforcement learning with a force adaptation curriculum.

## Problem

- Traditional VR teleoperation for humanoid robots relies on an Inverse Kinematics (IK) + PD control pipeline, leading to several limitations.
- The IK+PD approach suffers from 'force blindness', neglecting external forces during physical interaction, and produces 'motion artifacts' or jerky movements.
- It also experiences 'solver failures' near kinematic singularities or joint limits and lacks dynamic adaptation to varying payloads, disturbances, or user preferences.

## Method

- A single, end-to-end recurrent neural network policy replaces the traditional IK+PD pipeline, directly mapping VR controller inputs and robot proprioceptive states to joint commands.
- The training methodology involves three stages: initial behavioral cloning from IK demonstrations, reinforcement learning fine-tuning with PPO for motion smoothness and tracking, and a curriculum for force adaptation.
- Domain randomization and asymmetric actor-critic training are employed to facilitate effective sim-to-real transfer to a ==Unitree G1== humanoid robot.

## Results

- The learned policy achieved a **52%** lower tracking error under external force disturbances (**2.3** cm vs. **4.8** cm) and produced **45%** smoother motions than the IK+PD baseline on a Unitree G1 robot.
- It demonstrated superior force robustness, maintaining an **89%** task success rate in interactive tasks compared to **71%** for the baseline, and required **21%** lower average torques.
- An **87%** user preference for the learned policy was recorded, with users citing a 'smoother feel' and 'more predictable' behavior, and successful sim-to-real deployment with minimal performance degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The learned policy implicitly compensates for external forces using only proprioceptive feedback, eliminating the need for explicit force sensing or complex model-based impedance control.
> - A recurrent architecture (LSTM) is crucial for maintaining temporal consistency, enabling anticipatory control, and generating smooth, natural robot trajectories.
> - Combining imitation learning for warm-starting with phased reinforcement learning (first for smoothness, then for force adaptation) provides an efficient and effective strategy for learning robust, high-dynamic teleoperation policies.
