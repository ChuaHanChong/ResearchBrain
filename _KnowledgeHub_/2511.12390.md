# Learning Adaptive Neural Teleoperation for Humanoid Robots: From Inverse Kinematics to End-to-End Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A neural teleoperation framework for humanoid robots, developed at the Georgia Institute of Technology, maps VR controller inputs directly to joint commands, significantly outperforming traditional Inverse Kinematics (IK) methods. The system achieved a 52% lower tracking error under external forces and 45% smoother motions on a Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating robust and natural control through a combination of imitation learning and reinforcement learning with a force adaptation curriculum.

## Problem

- Traditional VR teleoperation for humanoid robots relies on an Inverse Kinematics (IK) + PD control pipeline, leading to several limitations.
- The IK+PD approach suffers from 'force blindness', neglecting external forces during physical interaction, and produces 'motion artifacts' or jerky movements.
- It also experiences 'solver failures' near kinematic singularities or joint limits and lacks dynamic adaptation to varying payloads, disturbances, or user preferences.

## Method

- A single, end-to-end recurrent neural network policy replaces the traditional IK+PD pipeline, directly mapping VR controller inputs and robot proprioceptive states to joint commands.
- The training methodology involves three stages: initial behavioral cloning from IK demonstrations, reinforcement learning fine-tuning with PPO for motion smoothness and tracking, and a curriculum for force adaptation.
- Domain randomization and asymmetric actor-critic training are employed to facilitate effective sim-to-real transfer to a ==Unitree G1== humanoid robot.

## Results

- The learned policy achieved a **52%** lower tracking error under external force disturbances (**2.3** cm vs. **4.8** cm) and produced **45%** smoother motions than the IK+PD baseline on a Unitree G1 robot.
- It demonstrated superior force robustness, maintaining an **89%** task success rate in interactive tasks compared to **71%** for the baseline, and required **21%** lower average torques.
- An **87%** user preference for the learned policy was recorded, with users citing a 'smoother feel' and 'more predictable' behavior, and successful sim-to-real deployment with minimal performance degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The learned policy implicitly compensates for external forces using only proprioceptive feedback, eliminating the need for explicit force sensing or complex model-based impedance control.
> - A recurrent architecture (LSTM) is crucial for maintaining temporal consistency, enabling anticipatory control, and generating smooth, natural robot trajectories.
> - Combining imitation learning for warm-starting with phased reinforcement learning (first for smoothness, then for force adaptation) provides an efficient and effective strategy for learning robust, high-dynamic teleoperation policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing, with humanoid robots being a significant area of interest due to their potential to operate in human-centric environments. Teleoperation, particularly via virtual reality (VR) interfaces, has emerged as a crucial paradigm for controlling these complex robots, allowing human operators to intuitively leverage their intelligence for task planning and execution in challenging or remote scenarios. This work sits at the intersection of VR teleoperation, advanced robot control, and artificial intelligence, specifically reinforcement learning.

Traditionally, VR teleoperation systems for humanoid robots have relied on a two-stage pipeline: Inverse Kinematics (IK) solvers to convert desired end-effector poses from VR controllers into robot joint angles, followed by Proportional-Derivative (PD) controllers to track these joint targets. While intuitive, this conventional IK+PD approach suffers from fundamental limitations. These include "force blindness," where IK solvers neglect external forces, leading to poor performance during physical interaction; "motion artifacts," resulting in jerky and unnatural movements; "solver failures" near kinematic singularities or joint limits; and a general "lack of adaptation" to varying payloads, user preferences, or task dynamics due to fixed control gains.

The broader research landscape has witnessed remarkable success in robot control through reinforcement learning (RL). RL policies have enabled legged robots to achieve unprecedented agility and robustness over challenging terrains, and have empowered manipulators to perform dexterous in-hand manipulation and complex tool use. This paper positions itself by asking whether these RL successes can be translated to overcome the inherent limitations of traditional teleoperation.

Existing learning-based approaches for robot control often focus on autonomous execution or replay of teleoperated demonstrations (e.g., ACT, ALOHA), rather than real-time human-in-the-loop assistance. Other work, like FALCON, addresses force adaptation for autonomous loco-manipulation but is not designed for the specific demands of real-time human control, which requires responsiveness, transparency, and user adaptability. This research distinguishes itself by focusing squarely on *real-time teleoperation for humanoid upper-body manipulation*, developing an end-to-end learned policy that directly maps VR inputs to robot joint commands. By doing so, it aims to imbue teleoperation with the benefits of learned control—implicit force adaptation, motion smoothness, and context-dependent behavior—thereby making human-robot collaboration more natural, robust, and efficient in dynamic and unpredictable environments. It represents a significant step towards more sophisticated and intuitive control paradigms for complex robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the acknowledged shortcomings of conventional VR teleoperation systems for humanoid robots. While VR teleoperation offers an intuitive way for humans to command robots in complex tasks, the underlying control mechanisms, specifically the Inverse Kinematics (IK) + PD control pipeline, present several critical limitations that hinder performance and user experience. These limitations include:

1. **Force Blindness:** Traditional IK solvers primarily deal with geometry, ignoring external forces. This leads to poor performance and instability when the robot interacts physically with the environment or objects, a common requirement in manipulation tasks.
2. **Motion Artifacts:** Discrete IK solutions and manually tuned, fixed PD gains often produce jerky, unnatural, and energy-inefficient motions, increasing wear on the robot and diminishing the operator's sense of control.
3. **Solver Failures:** IK solvers can fail to find solutions or converge slowly, particularly near kinematic singularities or joint limits, leading to abrupt halts or unpredictable behavior.
4. **Lack of Adaptation:** Fixed PD gains cannot adapt dynamically to changes in payload, unforeseen disturbances, varied user preferences, or evolving task requirements, making the system rigid and non-responsive.

Inspired by the remarkable successes of reinforcement learning (RL) in learning complex, adaptive, and robust control policies for various robotic tasks, the overarching objective of this work is to fundamentally improve humanoid robot teleoperation. The authors aim to achieve this by replacing the constrained IK+PD pipeline with a learning-based neural teleoperation framework.

The **key objectives** of this research are explicitly stated as:

- **Develop a neural teleoperation architecture** that learns end-to-end mappings from VR controller inputs directly to robot joint commands, with the crucial capability of implicitly handling force adaptation.
- **Propose a comprehensive training methodology** that combines the efficiency of imitation learning from existing IK demonstrations for initial policy warm-up, followed by sophisticated reinforcement learning fine-tuning to optimize for motion smoothness and robustness to external forces.
- **Conduct extensive sim-to-real experiments** on a realistic humanoid robot platform (Unitree G1) to quantitatively demonstrate significant improvements in tracking accuracy, motion smoothness, and force robustness compared to conventional IK baselines.
- **Analyze the emergent learned behaviors** to understand how the neural policy achieves force compensation and potentially develops user-adaptive control strategies.

Ultimately, the goal is to create a teleoperation system that is more natural, robust, and intuitive for human operators, thereby expanding the applicability and effectiveness of humanoid robots in a wider range of real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is to replace the traditional two-stage IK+PD teleoperation pipeline with a single, end-to-end learned neural policy. This policy directly maps real-time observations from the VR controller and the robot's proprioceptive state to joint commands, leveraging the power of deep reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Neural Architecture

The learning-based policy ($\pi_\theta$) is structured as a recurrent neural network designed to process diverse inputs and generate coherent actions. It comprises three main components:

1. **VR Input Encoder:** This component processes the poses (position and orientation) of the VR hand controllers. To ensure invariance to the absolute VR coordinate frame and focus on relative motion, it encodes the transformation between the initial grip pose (when the user engages control) and the current pose. This provides a compact and robust representation of the user's intended movement.
2. **Proprioception Encoder:** This is a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that processes the robot's internal state. Inputs include current joint positions ($q_{robot}$), joint velocities ($\dot{q}_{robot}$), and the previous action ($a_{t-1}$). A history of 5 timesteps for these proprioceptive observations is fed into the encoder to provide essential temporal context, allowing the policy to infer dynamics and react to disturbances.
3. **LSTM Policy Head:** The latent representations from both the VR Input Encoder and the Proprioception Encoder are concatenated and fed into an Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layer. The LSTM maintains a hidden state ($h_{t-1}$), which is crucial for handling temporal dependencies, generating smooth trajectories, and enabling anticipatory control. The LSTM then outputs the robot's joint commands (e.g., target joint positions or torques). The network comprises approximately 2.5 million parameters and can infer actions at >500 Hz on a CPU, ensuring real-time performance.

#### 3.2. Training Methodology

The training procedure is structured in three progressive stages to efficiently learn complex, robust, and natural control policies:

1. **Stage 1: Imitation Learning from IK Demonstrations:**
 - **Data Collection:** An operator teleoperates the robot in a simulation environment using the conventional IK+PD baseline for various manipulation tasks. State-action pairs (robot state, VR commands, IK-generated joint actions) are recorded.
 - **Behavioral Cloning:** The neural policy is initially trained via behavioral cloning (supervised learning) to mimic these recorded IK solutions. This provides a "warm-start" initialization, teaching the policy basic task-relevant behaviors and accelerating subsequent reinforcement learning. The loss function minimizes the squared error between the policy's output and the IK-generated actions.

2. **Stage 2: RL Fine-Tuning with Smoothness Rewards:**
 - **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for reinforcement learning.
 - **Reward Function:** A composite reward function is designed to shape the policy's behavior:
 - **Tracking Reward ($r_{track}$):** Penalizes deviations between the robot's actual end-effector pose and the target pose commanded by the VR controller. This ensures the robot accurately follows user intent.
 - **Smoothness Reward ($r_{smooth}$):** Explicitly penalizes high joint accelerations and jerks. This is crucial for producing natural, fluid motions, reducing wear, and improving energy efficiency.
 - **Energy Regularization ($r_{energy}$):** Penalizes excessive joint torques, encouraging the policy to find minimum-effort solutions.
 - This stage refines the policy learned during imitation, introducing adaptability and optimizing for desired motion characteristics.

3. **Stage 3: Force Adaptation Curriculum:**
 - **Curriculum Learning:** To instill robustness against external forces, random force disturbances are progressively applied to the robot's end-effectors during training. The magnitude of these forces gradually increases, forcing the policy to learn to implicitly compensate for them using its proprioceptive feedback (joint positions, velocities, etc.).
 - This curriculum ensures the policy develops an internal model of its dynamics and how to maintain desired poses and trajectories even under unexpected interactions, without requiring explicit force sensing or estimation at the policy input level.

#### 3.3. Sim-to-Real Transfer

To ensure the learned policies translate effectively from simulation to the real robot, standard domain randomization techniques are employed during training. These include varying:
- **Dynamics parameters:** Link masses, friction coefficients, and motor gains within plausible ranges.
- **System latency:** Introducing random control delays.
- **Observation noise:** Adding noise to joint encoder readings.
- **External perturbations:** Applying random pushes to the robot's torso.
Additionally, asymmetric actor-critic training is used, where the critic has access to privileged information (like true external forces and exact dynamics parameters) during training to aid learning, while the actor (the deployed policy) relies solely on available observations.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform:** Unitree G1 humanoid robot (35-DOF, 14 arm joints). The policy operates at 50Hz, with low-level motor controllers interpolating commands at 250Hz.
- **VR Interface:** Meta Quest 3 controllers, streamed via WebRTC for low latency (<30ms). Users grip to activate arm control and use the trigger for gripper control.
- **Simulation Environment:** IsaacGym with MuJoCo physics, running at 100Hz.
- **Tasks:** Evaluated on "Pick and Place" (0.5kg box), "Door Opening" (varying resistance), and "Bimanual Coordination" (grasping and inserting a rod).
- **Metrics:** Tracking error (end-effector position), motion smoothness (joint acceleration magnitude), task success rate, and user subjective ratings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling quantitative and qualitative evidence demonstrating the superiority of the proposed learning-based neural teleoperation framework over traditional IK+PD control.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Comparison

A direct comparison on the Unitree G1 robot across various tasks yielded significant improvements:

- **Tracking Accuracy:** The learned policy achieved a **34% lower tracking error** (1.4 cm vs. 2.1 cm) in scenarios without external forces. Critically, under external force disturbances (0-40N), the learned policy demonstrated **52% lower error** (2.3 cm vs. 4.8 cm) compared to IK+PD, highlighting its superior implicit force adaptation.
- **Motion Smoothness:** The learned approach produced **45% smoother motions** (4.6 vs. 8.3 joint acceleration magnitude) than the IK+PD baseline. This was corroborated by user feedback describing the motions as more "natural" and "fluid."
- **Force Robustness:** In tasks involving external forces, such as door opening, the IK+PD baseline's success rate dropped significantly to 71%, whereas the learned policy maintained a high **89% success rate**, showcasing robust performance under interaction.
- **Energy Efficiency:** Due to smoother trajectories and anticipatory control, the learned policy required **21% lower average torques**, suggesting improved energy efficiency and reduced mechanical wear.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies confirmed the importance of key components in the training methodology:

- **Force Curriculum:** Removing the force adaptation curriculum led to a substantial degradation in performance under force disturbances (error increased from 2.3 cm to 3.8 cm, success rate dropped from 89% to 76%), validating its critical role in learning robust force compensation.
- **LSTM Architecture:** Replacing the LSTM with a purely feedforward MLP resulted in **53% worse smoothness** (8.9 vs. 5.8), indicating that the recurrent nature of the LSTM is essential for maintaining temporal consistency and generating fluid trajectories.
- **Smoothness Reward:** Without explicit smoothness rewards, the policy generated jerky motions despite achieving tracking success, emphasizing the need for this reward component.
- **Imitation Initialization:** Training the policy from scratch without the initial imitation learning stage took 3 times longer to converge and achieved poorer final performance (error increased from 2.3 cm to 4.2 cm, success rate dropped from 89% to 73%), proving the value of warm-starting with IK demonstrations.

#### 4.3. User Study

A user study with 8 participants (mix of experienced and novices) revealed a strong preference for the learned policy:

- **87% of users preferred the learned policy**, citing a "smoother feel," "less fighting the robot," and "more predictable" behavior. Subjective ratings were consistently higher for naturalness, responsiveness, and ease of use.

#### 4.4. Sim-to-Real Deployment

The learned policy successfully transferred to the real Unitree G1 hardware with minimal performance degradation. The tracking error increased slightly from 2.3 cm in simulation to 2.7 cm on the real robot, demonstrating the effectiveness of domain randomization and the overall training approach. The policy maintained smooth, natural motions and adapted to external forces across all tested manipulation tasks in the real world.

#### 4.5. Failure Cases and Limitations

Despite significant advancements, the study identified certain limitations:
- **Rapid Motions:** The policy exhibited lagging behavior for extremely fast VR controller movements (>1.5 m/s) due to physical dynamics constraints, though it still outperformed the erratic IK+PD baseline in such scenarios.
- **Occlusion Recovery:** Recovery from lost end-effector tracking (e.g., arm behind body) took 2-3 seconds, suggesting a need for explicit occlusion handling.
- **Multi-User Adaptation:** The current policy does not explicitly adapt to different users, highlighting an area for future personalized control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in the field of humanoid robot teleoperation, offering a robust, natural, and adaptive control paradigm that fundamentally overcomes the limitations of traditional methods. Its potential impact spans several critical areas:

1. **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** By enabling more intuitive, responsive, and robust control, this work significantly improves the efficacy and naturalness of human-robot collaboration. Operators can focus more on higher-level task planning rather than micromanaging robot movements or struggling with control artifacts, reducing cognitive load and increasing overall efficiency. This is crucial for applications in dynamic, unstructured environments where human intelligence is indispensable.

2. **Broader Applicability of Humanoid Robots:** The improved robustness to external forces and ability to handle physical interactions expands the range of tasks humanoid robots can reliably perform. Applications in warehouse logistics, manufacturing, disaster response, maintenance in hazardous environments, and even space exploration, where physical interaction and adaptability are paramount, become more feasible. The smooth, energy-efficient motions also contribute to longer operational lifespans and reduced maintenance costs for these complex robots.

3. **Methodological Advancement in Robot Control:** The proposed training methodology—combining imitation learning for warm-starting, reinforcement learning with tailored rewards for smoothness and tracking, and a curriculum for force adaptation—provides a powerful blueprint for developing complex, robust robot skills. This approach is generalizable and could inspire similar methodologies for other challenging robot control problems, particularly those requiring fine motor skills, adaptability, and human-in-the-loop interaction.

4. **Implicit Force Compensation:** A key insight is the policy's ability to implicitly compensate for external forces using only proprioceptive feedback and a force curriculum. This eliminates the need for explicit force sensing or complex model-based impedance control at the policy level, simplifying the system architecture while delivering superior performance. This finding highlights the power of end-to-end learning to discover complex control strategies from data.

5. **User Experience and Trust:** The demonstrated improvements in motion smoothness and operator preference are vital for building trust and acceptance of humanoid robots. A robot that moves naturally and responds predictably is more likely to be embraced by human operators and the general public, fostering better integration into society and workplaces.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a strong foundation for several exciting future directions. These include integrating visual feedback for more sophisticated task understanding and obstacle avoidance, incorporating haptic feedback to the operator for an even richer telepresence experience, extending control to whole-body contacts and dexterous hands, and developing meta-learning techniques for rapid adaptation to new tasks, environments, or individual user preferences.

In conclusion, this research not only presents a significant technical achievement in learning-based robot control but also offers a compelling vision for the future of humanoid teleoperation. By making robot control more natural, robust, and intuitive, it brings us closer to a future where humanoid robots can effectively augment human capabilities across a myriad of complex real-world applications.
