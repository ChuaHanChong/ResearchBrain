# CHORUS: Decentralized Multi-Embodiment Collaboration with One VLA Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University introduce CHORUS, a framework enabling decentralized collaboration among diverse robot teams using a single Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy. This approach improves reactive coordination by leveraging VLA priors, scales to three heterogeneous robots, and outperforms centralized VLA policies and from-scratch imitation learning methods.

## Problem

- Centralized multi-robot control approaches face scalability limitations and require constant inter-robot communication at inference, leading to latency and robustness issues.
- Existing decentralized methods often rely on strong inference-time assumptions like shared proprioceptive state or third-person camera views, complicating real-world deployment.
- Leveraging powerful single-robot Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model priors for truly decentralized, multi-embodiment, and mobile multi-robot collaboration remained an open challenge.

## Method

- **CHORUS** adapts a pretrained VLA backbone (specifically, π_{0.5}) into a single, ==shared policy== that controls multiple robots.
- Each robot runs an independent copy of the policy, conditioned only on its local observations and a unique robot-identifying prompt, eliminating inference-time communication.
- Training data consists of individual robot trajectories extracted from synchronously logged multi-robot demonstrations, which are then pooled to fine-tune the shared VLA policy using a flow-matching loss.

## Results

- CHORUS achieved a 64 percentage point higher mean task success rate compared to a decentralized diffusion policy baseline, demonstrating the value of VLA priors.
- The shared policy in CHORUS improved handover recovery rate by **40%** (17/20 vs. 9/20) over separate policies when a teammate was perturbed, indicating enhanced reactive coordination.
- The framework successfully scaled to a three-robot team for a complex basket transport task, achieving a **90%** success rate without any architectural modifications, showcasing its scalability and robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pretrained visuomotor priors from VLA models are highly effective for multi-robot collaboration, significantly improving performance over learning from scratch, even for out-of-distribution tasks.
> - Training a single, weight-shared policy across diverse robots fosters a shared representation that enhances reactivity and adaptation to dynamic teammate behavior.
> - Decentralized execution with local observations maintains input distributions closer to the VLA backbone's pretraining data, leading to superior performance compared to centralized approaches requiring concatenated, multi-robot observations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards enabling robots to perform complex tasks through collaboration. This work addresses the specific challenges of achieving coordination in mobile multi-robot settings, particularly for heterogeneous teams (i.e., teams with diverse robot morphologies, sensors, and kinematics).

Current approaches to multi-robot collaboration generally fall into two categories:
- **Centralized methods:** These methods typically condition a single policy on the combined observations of an entire team and produce actions for all robots. While offering a comprehensive view, they scale poorly with increasing team size due to expanding context windows and action spaces. Furthermore, they necessitate inter-robot communication at inference time to gather team-wide observations, which can introduce latency and robustness issues in real-world deployments. Examples include approaches that leverage shared latent action spaces or learning through collaborative teleoperation.
- **Decentralized methods:** These involve training and deploying a separate policy for each robot, allowing each robot to act based on its local observations. This approach inherently scales better with team size as the context window for each policy remains constant. Decentralized methods also tolerate asynchronous execution and minor temporal mismatches. However, the primary challenge for decentralized systems is partial observability, where each robot must coordinate effectively with teammates despite having only local information. Prior learning-based decentralized methods, such as MIMIC-D and LatentToM, often address this by introducing shared assumptions at execution, such as conditioning on proprioceptive state from teammates, relying on shared third-person cameras, or employing online alignment procedures. These assumptions can impose practical constraints at inference time.

A separate but related area is the development of **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models** in single-robot manipulation. Models like OpenVLA and RT-2 have demonstrated robust visuomotor priors and generalization capabilities across various tasks, scenes, and embodiments, often trained on diverse datasets. While some VLA-based bimanual manipulation can be seen as a simplified form of multi-robot collaboration, these setups typically involve physically coupled arms controlled in a centralized manner. The extent to which these powerful single-robot VLA priors can be directly leveraged for *decentralized*, *multi-embodiment*, *mobile* multi-robot collaboration without inference-time communication has been an open question.

The CHORUS framework positions itself at the intersection of these domains. It seeks to combine the benefits of decentralized execution (scalability, robustness to asynchronicity) with the strong visuomotor priors of pretrained VLA models, while overcoming the limitations of previous decentralized methods that required explicit information sharing or alignment at inference. It also distinguishes itself from higher-level task decomposition and role assignment methods that use large language models by focusing on the low-level execution aspect.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable effective and reactive collaboration among diverse robot teams in a decentralized manner, leveraging the capabilities of pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in existing multi-robot collaboration paradigms:

1. **Scaling Challenges of Centralized Control:** Centralized policies, while conceptually providing a global view, face practical limitations. As the number of robots in a team increases, the combined observation space and action space grow proportionally. This leads to increased computational demands and potential issues with context window management. Furthermore, centralized approaches inherently require real-time communication among robots to aggregate observations, which can be a bottleneck for latency and reliability in dynamic real-world environments. The goal is to develop a method that avoids these scaling and communication overheads.

2. **Inference-Time Assumptions in Decentralized Control:** While decentralized policies offer better scalability and robustness to asynchronicity, they traditionally struggle with partial observability. Prior decentralized learning-based methods have often mitigated this by introducing requirements at inference time, such as sharing proprioceptive state, relying on a common third-person camera view, or employing online alignment procedures. These assumptions can complicate deployment, increase system complexity, and negate some of the advantages of decentralization. A key motivation is to eliminate such inference-time dependencies, allowing each robot to operate truly independently based solely on its local observations.

3. **Inefficiency of Per-Robot Policy Training:** Training a separate policy for each robot in a diverse team, even with decentralized execution, can be computationally intensive and less data-efficient. This approach typically requires maintaining and updating N distinct policies, where N is the number of robots. The researchers aim to explore if a single, shared policy can effectively control multiple, diverse robot embodiments, thereby reducing training cost and potentially fostering better coordination through shared representations.

4. **Leveraging Pretrained VLA Priors for Collaboration:** Pretrained VLA models have demonstrated significant progress in single-robot manipulation, exhibiting robust visuomotor understanding and generalization. The underlying motivation is to investigate whether these rich priors, developed in single-robot contexts (including bimanual tasks, which can be viewed as a coupled form of collaboration), are sufficient to enable reactive, decentralized collaboration in a multi-robot, mobile setting. This involves exploring if fine-tuning a VLA model on multi-robot data can facilitate coordination purely from local observations, without requiring explicit inter-robot communication.

In summary, the specific objectives guiding the development of CHORUS are:
- To propose a framework that adapts a single VLA backbone for controlling diverse, multi-robot teams.
- To enable fully decentralized execution where each robot runs an independent copy of the policy, conditioned only on its local observations and a robot-identifying prompt.
- To demonstrate that such a framework can outperform decentralized, from-scratch imitation learning methods in collaborative tasks.
- To assess if sharing a single policy across robots enhances reactivity to teammate behavior.
- To compare the performance of this decentralized VLA approach against centralized VLA policies that have access to more comprehensive team-wide information.
- To evaluate the framework's scalability to larger teams comprising three heterogeneous robots without requiring architectural modifications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CHORUS framework is designed to adapt a pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) backbone for decentralized, multi-embodiment collaboration using a single shared policy. The methodology addresses three key aspects: data collection, policy design and training, and decentralized deployment.

**Policy Design and Architecture:**
The core of CHORUS is a single, shared policy, $\pi_\theta(A_r | o_r, c_r)$, which is responsible for controlling every robot in the team. This policy is built upon the $\pi_{0.5}$ pretrained VLA backbone.
- **Cross-embodiment Input Format:** The policy leverages the VLA backbone's inherent ability to handle diverse robot morphologies. It processes variable numbers of image tokens per observation and outputs padded action vectors of a fixed dimension (32), accommodating robots with different observation and action space sizes without architectural changes.
- **Robot-Identifying Prompt:** To allow a single policy to control multiple robots, a unique robot-identifying prompt ($c_r$) is prepended to the model input at each timestep. This prompt explicitly names the embodiment (e.g., `<YAM>`, `<Kinova>`) and can specify its role, providing the policy with the necessary context to generate appropriate actions for the specific robot it is controlling.

**Multi-Robot Demonstrations and Task Design:**
- **Data Collection:** Training data is collected using single-robot mobile manipulators (Kinova, ARX, YAM) via the TidyBot++ teleoperation interface. For two-robot tasks, a single human operator controls both robots using two phones. For three-robot tasks, a second human operator assists.
- **Synchronous Logging:** Each multi-robot episode is recorded as a synchronously logged trajectory, $\xi = \{(o_t^r, a_t^r)\}_{r \in [N], t \in [T]}$, where $o_t^r$ is robot $r$'s observation and $a_t^r$ is its commanded action at time $t$.
- **Per-Robot Training Tuples:** From these episodes, individual training tuples $(o_t^r, A_t^r, c_r)$ are extracted for each robot. $A_t^r$ represents a chunk of future actions (horizon H) for robot $r$. All such per-robot tuples from all robots and episodes are pooled into a single training dataset $D$. Crucially, the model is never exposed to joint observations across robots during training; each tuple contains only one robot's local observation.
- **Collaboration Strategy:** During data collection, collaboration strategies are chosen to ensure that each robot's local observations (from its onboard cameras) provide sufficient visual information about its teammates and the workspace to enable successful decentralized task completion.

**Policy Training:**
- **Robot Sampler:** Training batches are formed by drawing single-robot tuples $(o_t^r, A_t^r, c_r)$ independently from the pooled dataset $D$. To address varying robot control frequencies and ensure balanced training, sampling weights are applied (e.g., upweighting data from slower robots).
- **Training Objective:** The policy is fine-tuned using the flow-matching loss, $L(\theta)$, inherited from the $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone (Equation 2). This loss measures the difference between the policy's predicted velocity field and the actual velocity field of noised action chunks. The expectation for the loss is taken over single-robot tuples.
- **Optimization:** Fine-tuning is performed using LoRA adapters (rank 16 for the VLM, rank 32 for the action expert) applied to the pretrained backbone. The AdamW optimizer is used with a cosine learning rate schedule.

**Decentralized Deployment:**
- **Independent Execution:** At inference time, each robot operates independently by running its own copy of the CHORUS policy. At each timestep $t$, robot $r$ samples its next action chunk $A_t^r$ from $\pi_\theta(\cdot | o_t^r, c_r)$ using only its local observation $o_t^r$ and identity prompt $c_r$.
- **No Inter-Robot Communication:** A fundamental aspect of CHORUS's deployment is the complete absence of information exchange between robots at runtime. Coordination is intended to emerge solely through each robot's visual perception of its teammates and the environment.
- **Asynchronous Operation:** The decentralized nature naturally supports asynchronous execution. Robots can operate at different control frequencies, with chunk sizes scaled proportionally to ensure they plan over the same time horizon. This allows robots to progress at their own rates, tolerating minor temporal discrepancies without requiring a centralized synchronizer.
- **Flexible Hosting:** Policies can be hosted independently on each robot or batched on a shared compute instance.

**Evaluation Tasks:**
The framework was evaluated on a suite of real-world multi-embodiment collaborative tasks:
- **Basket Lift:** Two robots (ARX, Kinova) collaborate to lift and place a laundry basket.
- **Tape Measure:** Two mobile manipulators (YAM, Kinova) work together to measure an object with a tape measure.
- **Book Handover:** A YAM robot hands over a book to a Kinova robot.
- **3-Robot Move:** A three-robot team (Kinova, two YAMs) transports a basket through a doorway.
For each task, 25-45 demonstrations were collected, and 10-18 real-world rollouts were performed per policy, including scenarios with distractor objects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated CHORUS against various baselines to assess the impact of the pretrained backbone, weight-sharing, and comparison to centralized control, as well as scalability.

**1. Effect of the Pretrained Backbone (Q1):**
CHORUS and its ablation, CHORUS (w/o Weight Sharing), demonstrated a significant performance advantage over a decentralized diffusion policy, which serves as a standard from-scratch imitation learning baseline. CHORUS achieved a 64 percentage point improvement in mean task success rate compared to the decentralized diffusion baseline (Figure 4). The diffusion policies exhibited two primary failure modes: confusion caused by distractor objects in tasks like tape measurement, and a characteristic mismatch pattern where one robot would initiate its part of the collaboration before the teammate had synchronized, leading to task failure (e.g., basket slipping during lifting). These results indicate that the visuomotor priors from a pretrained VLA backbone provide a substantial benefit for multi-robot collaboration, even in tasks that are considered out-of-distribution from the pretraining data.

**2. Effect of Weight-Sharing on Teammate Reactivity (Q2):**
To evaluate the benefit of a single shared policy over training separate policies per robot (CHORUS w/o Weight Sharing), an experiment was conducted on the mobile handover task. In this experiment, the YAM arm was subjected to scripted lateral perturbations while holding a book, and the Kinova robot, running the policy, was required to adapt its movements to successfully grasp the book.
Over 20 perturbation trials, CHORUS successfully recovered the handover in 17 instances, while CHORUS (w/o Weight Sharing) succeeded in 9 instances (Figure 5). This represents a 40% point improvement in recovery rate for the weight-shared CHORUS policy. The primary failure observed with the non-weight-shared policy was "early grasping," where the Kinova attempted to grasp the book at a pre-trained, in-distribution location rather than tracking the YAM's perturbed position. This finding suggests that a single set of weights trained on the perspectives of all robots can induce a shared representation that improves reactivity and adaptation to dynamic teammate behavior.

**3. Comparison to Centralized Policy Framework (Q3):**
CHORUS was compared against a VLA Centralized baseline, which conditions a single policy on the concatenated observations of both robots and generates joint actions for the entire team. Despite the centralized policy having access to strictly more information, CHORUS matched or exceeded its performance, particularly outperforming it in mean success rate across the evaluated tasks (Figure 6). This unexpected outcome is attributed to two factors:
- **Distribution Shift from Pretraining:** The centralized observation format, requiring the policy to process two top views and two wrist views from different robots, deviates significantly from the $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone's pretraining distribution (typically four camera slots tied to a single mobile manipulator's front, back, base, and wrist views). This semantic mismatch degrades the VLA's ability to leverage its strong priors.
- **Increased Input Dimensionality and Sensitivity:** Conditioning on the team's entire state means that even minor desynchronization between teammates can push the input further from the training data distribution. This increased input dimensionality also makes the policy more susceptible to performance degradation, consistent with prior research on behavior cloning.
In contrast, CHORUS, by processing only local observations, maintains inputs that more closely align with its pretraining distribution, thereby better retaining its learned priors.

**4. Scaling to Three-Robot Teams (Q4):**
The CHORUS framework was successfully deployed and evaluated on a three-robot team comprising a Kinova and two YAM mobile manipulators in a "3-robot move" task (transporting a basket through a doorway). The task involved complex coordination: the Kinova opening a door, while two YAMs collaboratively lift and transport a basket through the doorway, requiring one YAM to infer the Kinova's status from the other YAM's behavior and mutual adjustments to avoid collisions.
CHORUS achieved a 90% success rate in this three-robot setting without any architectural modifications to the policy. This result demonstrates the framework's scalability to larger and more heterogeneous robot teams using the same training recipe.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CHORUS framework represents an advancement in multi-robot collaboration by proposing a decentralized approach that leverages pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Decentralized Multi-Embodiment Collaboration:** CHORUS provides a method for achieving reactive and effective collaboration among diverse robot teams without the need for inter-robot communication or explicit alignment procedures at inference time. This addresses a critical challenge in multi-robot systems, allowing individual robots to operate independently based on their local observations, which simplifies deployment and increases robustness in real-world, dynamic environments.
- **Effective Utilization of VLA Priors:** The research demonstrates that the strong visuomotor priors embedded in pretrained VLA models, initially developed for single-robot manipulation, are highly transferable and beneficial for complex multi-robot collaborative tasks. This suggests a broader applicability for foundation models in robotics, extending their utility beyond single-agent scenarios to collective intelligence.
- **Improved Reactivity and Training Efficiency:** By training a single, weight-shared policy across multiple robots, CHORUS not only reduces the computational cost associated with training separate policies for each embodiment but also enhances the robots' ability to react to and coordinate with dynamic teammate behaviors. This improved reactivity, as evidenced by the perturbation experiments, is a valuable characteristic for fluent collaboration.
- **Practical Scalability:** The framework's ability to control heterogeneous teams of up to three robots without requiring architectural modifications highlights its potential for scaling to larger and more complex robot deployments. The constant context window size for each robot and the absence of inter-robot communication at runtime are fundamental advantages for scalability.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages the robotics community to focus on collecting larger and more diverse multi-robot collaborative datasets, which could further unlock the capabilities of VLA models in this domain. It also implicitly highlights the need for careful consideration of task requirements; while CHORUS excels in many collaborative tasks, it acknowledges that scenarios demanding strict, instantaneous synchronization might still necessitate centralized control.

In conclusion, CHORUS offers a practical and effective paradigm for decentralized multi-robot collaboration, paving the way for more adaptable, scalable, and autonomous robotic teams in various applications, from logistics and manufacturing to domestic assistance.
