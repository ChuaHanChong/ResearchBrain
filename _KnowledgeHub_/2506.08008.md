# Hidden in plain sight: VLMs overlook their visual representations

> [!summary] Summary
> UC Berkeley researchers found that Vision-Language Models frequently underperform on vision-centric tasks because their language model components fail to fully leverage the high-quality visual representations available from their vision encoders, instead often defaulting to linguistic priors. The study demonstrated that fine-tuning the LLM significantly improves visual utilization and reduces reliance on these priors.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) exhibit significantly worse performance on vision-centric tasks compared to their standalone vision encoders, often performing near random chance.
- There is an unreliable rank-ordering of vision encoders when integrated into VLMs; top-performing standalone encoders do not consistently yield the best VLMs.
- The underlying reason for VLMs failing to effectively use demonstrably rich visual information from their encoders remained unclear.

## Method

- Developed a ==dual evaluation strategy== comparing direct ==visual encoder readouts== with full VLM performance on a suite of "vision-centric" tasks.
- Conducted ==diagnostic analyses== to probe potential bottlenecks: ==degradation of visual representations, brittleness to linguistic prompts==, and the LLM's ability to utilize visual information.
- Systematically ==fine-tuned== individual ==VLM components (vision encoder, projector, LLM)== on vision-centric tasks to isolate the source of performance limitations.

## Results

- A universal and substantial performance drop was observed from direct vision encoder evaluation to full VLM evaluation across all vision-centric tasks, often to near-chance levels.
- VLMs' answer distributions closely matched those generated when given blank visual inputs, confirming a reliance on linguistic priors over visual evidence.
- Fine-tuning the LLM component on vision-centric tasks yielded the largest performance improvements, alongside improved visual attention and mitigation of language biases, compared to fine-tuning other VLM components.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task-relevant visual information is largely preserved within the VLM's internal representations, indicating that the visual input itself is not degraded by the projector or LLM layers.
> - VLMs often default to "blind" language priors or biases when answering, ignoring available visual evidence, which significantly contributes to their underperformance.
> - The language model (LLM) component is the primary bottleneck, as it fails to effectively attend to, integrate, and reason over the rich visual features it receives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in large language models (LLMs) and large-scale vision encoders have converged to create Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which aim to perform multimodal tasks by integrating visual and linguistic information. The prevailing architectural paradigm for open-source VLMs typically involves three main components: a pre-trained vision encoder (e.g., CLIP, DINOv2), a projector layer to align vision features with the language space, and a large language model (LLM) for reasoning and text generation. This flexible architecture has enabled VLMs to tackle diverse tasks, often specified through natural language prompts, and has even been suggested as a novel method for understanding and leveraging vision model representations.

However, the behavior of VLMs in "vision-centric" tasks—those that primarily rely on visual input rather than external knowledge or linguistic reasoning—has revealed puzzling inconsistencies. Previous work (e.g., Tong et al., Fu et al.) has observed that while VLMs excel at tasks requiring general knowledge or complex reasoning often inherent in the LLM, they often perform poorly, sometimes at random chance, when success depends purely on image understanding. This is particularly puzzling because the underlying large vision models (e.g., DINOv2) embedded within these VLMs have been shown to perform exceptionally well on many of these very same vision-centric tasks when probed directly (Banani et al., Zhan et al.).

Furthermore, a critical observation in the current landscape is the lack of correlation, and even a "rank order shift," between the standalone performance of a vision encoder and its performance when integrated into a VLM. For instance, DINOv2, often considered a top-performing vision encoder on spatial and low-level visual tasks, has been observed to underperform within a VLM context compared to vision encoders like CLIP, leading to premature conclusions that CLIP-style pretraining might be inherently superior for VLMs (Tong et al.).

This paper directly addresses these critical inconsistencies and the observed "disconnect" between the formidable visual representations extracted by vision encoders and their seemingly underutilized state within the complete VLM architecture. It contributes to the ongoing debate about the actual source of VLM limitations—whether it lies in the vision backbone, the projection layer, or the language model's ability to integrate and leverage multimodal information. By meticulously comparing direct vision encoder readouts with VLM performance on carefully selected vision-centric tasks, the authors provide a crucial diagnostic analysis that reframes our understanding of VLM capabilities and guides future research directions in multimodal AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this research stems from a crucial observation in the VLM landscape: despite the impressive capabilities of standalone large vision encoders on a variety of visual tasks, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) built upon them frequently exhibit substantially worse performance, often dropping to near-chance levels, when evaluated on purely vision-centric benchmarks. This performance discrepancy is further complicated by an unreliable rank-ordering of vision encoders; models that excel when directly probed may perform disproportionately poorly when integrated into a VLM.

Given this "disconnect," the primary objective of this study is to systematically diagnose the underlying reasons for this failure. The authors aim to understand *why* VLMs fail to effectively utilize the rich visual information that is demonstrably present and accessible within their own vision encoders.

To achieve this, the research sets out to investigate three main potential bottlenecks within the VLM architecture:
1. **Degradation of Vision Representations:** Is the task-relevant visual information lost or degraded as it passes through the VLM's projector and LLM layers?
2. **Brittleness to Task Prompt:** Are VLMs highly sensitive to the way visual tasks are formulated in language prompts, hindering their ability to interpret and act on visual cues?
3. **Language Model's Role in Utilizing Visual Information:** Is the large language model (LLM) component the primary limiting factor, failing to properly attend to, integrate, or reason over the visual representations it receives, instead defaulting to its inherent language priors?

By systematically analyzing these potential failure points, the authors seek to provide clarity on the actual limitations of current open-source VLMs and offer actionable insights for their future development and evaluation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors design a comprehensive evaluation setup to compare and diagnose the performance of VLMs against their constituent visual encoders on tasks demanding pure visual understanding.

#### 3.1. Model Selection and Configuration

The study primarily focuses on a suite of four VLMs from the Prismatic VLMs framework (Karamcheti et al. 2024), chosen because their vision encoders remain frozen and identical to their original pre-trained versions. This allows for a direct ablation of the VLM components' impact on vision representations. These include:
- **DINOv2 L/14:** A self-supervised vision-only pre-trained model.
- **ViT-IN1k L/16:** A supervised vision-only pre-trained model (on ImageNet).
- **CLIP L/14:** A vision-language pre-trained model.
- **SigLIP L/14:** Another vision-language pre-trained model.
Each VLM pairs its respective vision backbone with Vicuña v1.5 as the LLM, and the projection layers are trained using the LLaVA v1.5 data mixture.
To ensure the generalizability of their findings, the authors also evaluate widely-used instruction-tuned VLMs where vision encoders are specifically trained within the VLM context: Qwen-VL, Phi-3-V, and InternVL.

#### 3.2. Vision-Centric Task Selection

The core of the methodology lies in the use of "vision-centric" tasks, which are carefully chosen to be solvable purely from visual input, minimizing reliance on linguistic knowledge or domain expertise. These tasks are drawn from established benchmarks such as CV-Bench (Tong et al. 2024a), BLINK (Fu et al. 2024), and MOCHI (Bonnen et al. 2024). The selected tasks span a range of visual abilities, from low-level perception to higher-level understanding:
- **Depth Estimation:** Determining which of two objects is closer to the camera.
- **Correspondence-based Tasks (Semantic, Object Affordance, Low-level Matching):** Identifying a corresponding point in a second image given a reference point, testing different levels of visual similarity (semantic, functional, pixel-level).
- **3D Object Awareness:** Identifying the odd object out among multiple views of mostly identical objects.
- **Art Style:** Matching the artistic style of a reference image to one of two options.
All tasks are formatted into multiple-choice VQA-style questions for VLM evaluation.

#### 3.3. Dual Evaluation Strategy

For each task, performance is assessed using two distinct strategies:
- **"Visual" Evaluation (Direct Readout):** This method directly probes the vision encoder's representations to determine task performance, establishing a "ceiling" of visual information availability. For example, a DPT head is trained for depth estimation, cosine similarity of patch features for correspondence, and Gram matrices for art style. This approach largely operates zero-shot or with minimal task-specific training on the vision features.
- **"VLM" Evaluation (VQA Format):** This is the standard evaluation where the full VLM processes the image(s) and a text prompt to generate an answer in a multiple-choice VQA format.

#### 3.4. Diagnostic Analyses of Failure Points

The authors conduct targeted experiments to pinpoint the source of the performance drop:
- **Vision Representation Integrity:** They probe intermediate layers of the VLM (projector and LLM layers) using the "Visual" evaluation methods. This assesses whether task-relevant visual information degrades as it propagates through the VLM's pipeline.
- **Prompt Sensitivity:** They introduce learnable prefix embeddings (prompt-tuning) to the VLM's input prompt. By training these embeddings on a small set of task examples, they evaluate if a better "encoding" of the task via the prompt can significantly improve VLM performance.
- **LLM Utilization of Visual Information:**
 - **Component Fine-tuning:** They systematically fine-tune individual components of the VLM (ViT, projector, LLM) on 5,000 examples of each vision-centric task. LoRA-tuning is used, and parameter counts are controlled (16.7M parameters) across all fine-tuning settings to ensure fair comparison. This helps identify which component's improvement yields the largest performance gains.
 - **Attention Map Analysis:** They visualize differences in attention maps before and after LLM fine-tuning to understand if the LLM learns to focus more on salient visual regions.
 - **"Blind" Baseline Comparison:** They evaluate the VLMs with blank visual inputs to determine the models' default answer biases (language priors) and compare these "blind" answer distributions to VLM predictions with actual images. Total variation (TV) distance is used to quantify these distributional differences and assess how fine-tuning affects them.

By employing this rigorous methodology, the authors systematically deconstruct the VLM's performance on vision-centric tasks, isolating and identifying the primary bottleneck.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yields several crucial findings that collectively pinpoint the root cause of VLMs' underperformance on vision-centric tasks:

1. **Universal Performance Drop and Rank-Order Shift (Initial Observations):**
 - **Significant Performance Degradation:** Across all vision-centric tasks, there is a consistent and substantial drop in accuracy when moving from a direct "Visual" evaluation of the vision encoder to a "VLM" evaluation using the full model. Performance frequently plummets from near-ceiling (e.g., DINOv2 at 88.7% on Depth Estimation) to near-chance levels (e.g., Low-level Matching dropping by 45.5%). This trend holds even for instruction-tuned open-source VLMs like Qwen-VL, Phi-3-V, and InternVL, suggesting it's a fundamental issue.
 - **Unreliable Rank-Ordering:** The vision encoder that performs best in a standalone "Visual" evaluation (often DINOv2) does not necessarily lead to the highest-performing VLM. DINOv2, despite being superior visually on most tasks requiring spatial awareness, experiences a disproportionately large performance decrease within a VLM, leading to a shifted ranking where other encoders (like CLIP or SigLIP) sometimes appear superior in VLM evaluations.

2. **VLM Choices Reflect "Blind" Language Priors:**
 - When presented with multiple-choice questions, VLMs' answer distributions are non-uniform. Crucially, these distributions are remarkably similar to those generated by the VLMs when given *blank visual inputs* ("blind" baselines). This strongly suggests that VLMs often ignore the visual input and instead rely heavily on biases inherent in their language model components, effectively guessing based on linguistic priors rather than visual evidence.

3. **Integrity of Vision Features Maintained Throughout VLM Layers:**
 - A critical finding is that the task-relevant visual information *does not degrade* as it passes through the projector and subsequent LLM layers. When intermediate layers of the VLM are probed using direct "Visual" evaluation methods, the accuracy remains high, indicating that the information necessary to solve the visual tasks is preserved and accessible within the model's internal representations. This eliminates the degradation of visual features as the primary bottleneck.

4. **Prompt-Tuning Yields Minimal Improvements:**
 - Attempts to improve VLM performance by fine-tuning learnable prefix embeddings (prompt-tuning) result in only marginal gains. A single prefix embedding offers a slight improvement, but adding more embeddings provides diminishing returns and does not bridge the performance gap between VLM and direct visual evaluation. This suggests that VLM performance is not significantly bottlenecked by the precise formulation or encoding of the linguistic prompt.

5. **Language Model Underutilization is the Key Bottleneck:**
 - **Largest Gains from LLM Fine-tuning:** When individual VLM components are fine-tuned on the vision-centric tasks (controlling for parameter count), fine-tuning the LLM component consistently provides the largest performance increase. This improvement is significantly greater than fine-tuning the vision encoder (ViT) or the projector layers.
 - **Improved Visual Attention:** Analysis of attention maps shows that fine-tuning the LLM leads to an increased focus on salient visual regions (e.g., reference points, multiple-choice labels) that are critical for solving correspondence tasks. This indicates the LLM learns to better attend to and utilize the visual information when explicitly trained to do so.
 - **Alleviating Language Prior:** Fine-tuning the LLM also effectively mitigates the "blind" language biases observed initially. The answer distributions of LLM-tuned models become much closer to the ground truth distributions, demonstrating that the LLM learns to overcome its default linguistic priors and genuinely integrate visual evidence.

In summary, the authors conclude that VLMs' failure on vision-centric tasks is not due to poor visual representations or prompt sensitivity, but rather a fundamental inability of the LLM to effectively leverage and attend to the high-quality visual information that is readily available within the model. The LLM often defaults to its inherent language biases instead of truly "seeing."

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has profound significance for the development, evaluation, and conceptual understanding of Vision-Language Models. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Redefining VLM Failure Modes:** The study fundamentally shifts the understanding of VLM limitations on visual tasks. It conclusively demonstrates that the bottleneck is not in the vision encoder's ability to extract robust visual features, nor in their degradation within the VLM pipeline, nor even in the prompt's formulation. Instead, the primary culprit is the *language model's inability to effectively attend to, integrate, and reason over these high-quality visual representations*, often defaulting to its internal language priors. This finding is critical for guiding future research efforts.

2. **Rethinking VLM Evaluation Methodologies:** The paper serves as a crucial cautionary tale regarding the interpretation of VLM performance on Visual Question Answering (VQA) benchmarks, especially those that claim to assess "vision capabilities." The observed disconnect between direct visual readout and VLM performance, coupled with the rank-ordering shifts, implies that current VQA-style evaluations can significantly underestimate and misrepresent the true visual understanding capabilities embedded within the vision encoders. Researchers should be cautious when drawing conclusions about vision model strengths or design choices (e.g., preferring CLIP-style over DINOv2) based solely on VLM benchmarks, as these may reflect the LLM's biases and integration inefficiencies more than the vision encoder's intrinsic power.

3. **Guiding VLM Architectural and Training Design:** The finding that fine-tuning the LLM component yields the most substantial performance gains offers clear actionable insights for future VLM development. Instead of solely focusing on developing more powerful vision encoders or improving projector layers, research efforts should prioritize training strategies that compel the LLM to better leverage and attend to visual inputs, overcome its language priors, and truly integrate multimodal information. This might involve novel LLM architectures, attention mechanisms, or instruction-tuning regimes specifically designed for robust visual-linguistic fusion. The paper implicitly suggests that simply ensembling stronger vision encoders (as some prior works propose) might be an inefficient solution if the LLM cannot effectively utilize the richer inputs.

4. **Advancing Multimodal AI Research:** By diagnosing a core issue in existing VLMs, this work opens new avenues for research into more robust and genuinely multimodal AI systems. It underscores the challenge of integrating information across vastly different modalities and highlights the need for dedicated mechanisms that ensure linguistic processing truly benefits from, rather than overrides, visual perception. The benchmark tasks and diagnostic evaluation strategies presented in this paper will be valuable tools for future investigations into improving visual understanding within VLMs.

In conclusion, "Hidden in plain sight" provides a pivotal diagnostic analysis, revealing that despite their impressive "sight" through powerful vision encoders, many VLMs remain functionally "blind" to their own visual representations. This work is a significant contribution towards building VLMs that not only "see" but also truly "understand" their visual world, moving beyond superficial multimodal integration to genuinely grounded visual reasoning.
