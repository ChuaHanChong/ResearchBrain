# CTS: Concurrent Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning for Legged Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> A concurrent teacher-student reinforcement learning framework, CTS, enables robust and agile legged locomotion for robots using only proprioceptive feedback. It achieves up to 21.85% lower velocity tracking error and higher robustness compared to conventional teacher-student methods, validated on quadrupedal and bipedal robots through extensive real-world experiments.

## Problem

- Developing robust and agile controllers for legged robots is challenging due to their complex, nonlinear dynamics and intricate contact interactions.
- Conventional two-stage teacher-student reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms are inefficient, requiring a fully trained teacher before student training can begin.
- Student policies in traditional teacher-student setups often rely on pure imitation (supervised learning), which can lead to suboptimal performance when the student's observation space is significantly different from the teacher's privileged information.

## Method

- The Concurrent Teacher-Student (**CTS**) framework concurrently trains a teacher policy (with privileged information) and a student policy (with proprioceptive observations) using an asymmetric actor-critic architecture.
- The student policy is guided by its own ==reinforcement learning== objectives, rather than solely supervised imitation, with a shared policy and critic network that learn from data collected by both teacher and student agents.
- A proprioceptive encoder for the student is updated by minimizing a reconstruction loss between its latent representation and that of the privileged encoder, allowing it to infer relevant state information from its limited observations.

## Results

- The CTS student policy significantly reduced velocity tracking error by up to **21.85%** on challenging terrains and improved robustness by **5.04%** to **6.47%** in push recovery tests compared to the two-stage teacher-student method in simulation.
- Policies trained with CTS demonstrated effective sim-to-real transfer and robust performance on real-world quadrupedal robots (Unitree A1, Aliengo) and the highly challenging point-foot bipedal robot (LimX Dynamics P1).
- Real-world experiments showcased the robots' ability to adapt to complex, unseen environments, including navigating moving platforms, recovering from stumbles, adapting to missing steps, and ascending stairs, indicating strong generalization capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Concurrent teacher-student training, where the student policy learns with its own reinforcement learning objectives rather than solely through imitation, leads to superior performance and robustness compared to conventional sequential teacher-student methods.
> - Allowing the student to actively learn under its proprioceptive observation constraints, while being guided by a privileged teacher, enables it to infer and utilize implicit task-relevant information for robust "blind locomotion".
> - Tailored reward functions, such as the `feet_regulation` reward and biped-specific penalties, are crucial for generating natural, stable, and adaptable gaits across diverse legged robot platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged locomotion is a foundational problem in robotics, enabling robots to navigate complex, unstructured terrains where wheeled or tracked robots struggle. However, developing robust and agile controllers for legged robots is inherently challenging due to their highly nonlinear and hybrid dynamics, coupled with complex contact interactions with the environment.

In recent years, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a dominant paradigm, achieving remarkable success in training legged robots to exhibit dynamic and agile behaviors. This paper specifically positions itself within the widely adopted **teacher-student (T-S) learning paradigm** for legged locomotion.

**Conventional Teacher-Student Paradigm:**
Traditionally, the T-S paradigm operates in a two-stage process:
1. **Teacher Training:** A "teacher" policy is first trained using RL. This teacher policy has access to "privileged information," such as precise terrain details, contact forces, and accurate inertial parameters, which are typically unavailable to a real robot's onboard sensors. This privileged access allows the teacher to learn optimal, high-performance behaviors in simulation.
2. **Knowledge Transfer (Student Training):** Once the teacher is well-trained, a "student" policy is then trained via supervised learning (SL) to imitate the teacher. The student policy operates solely on proprioceptive feedback (e.g., joint angles, velocities, IMU data), which is available on real hardware. This typically involves the student learning to reconstruct latent representations from the teacher's encoder or directly imitating the teacher's action outputs. This two-stage approach has been instrumental in achieving sim-to-real transfer and enabling robust locomotion in complex terrains. Notable works like RMA and those leveraging Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) have built upon this foundation.

**Limitations of Conventional T-S and Related Work:**
The paper identifies key limitations of the conventional two-stage T-S approach:
- **Sequential Training:** The two-stage process can be inefficient and time-consuming, requiring a fully trained teacher before student training can commence.
- **Pure Imitation:** The student's objective is often purely imitative (supervised learning). The authors argue that since the student has different, less complete observations than the teacher, perfect imitation is difficult and may not yield an *optimal* policy for the student's limited observation space. The student might struggle to adapt if the teacher's policy relies heavily on privileged information not reflected in the student's observations.

The paper also references **representation learning** approaches, that leverage learned representations (e.g., via VAEs or contrastive learning) to enhance locomotion performance, often using regression objectives for state representation. Another related work mentioned is **Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA)**, which integrates some aspects of teacher and student training into a single stage, but primarily focuses on training encoders via supervised learning, not the policy network itself through RL conditioned on the proprioceptive encoder's input.

**CTS's Position:**
The proposed **Concurrent Teacher-Student (CTS)** framework seeks to overcome these limitations by:
- **Concurrent Training:** Training teacher and student policies simultaneously, streamlining the process.
- **RL Objective for Student:** Crucially, the student policy is guided by its own reinforcement learning objectives (maximizing expected discounted return), rather than solely imitating the teacher. This allows the student to learn a more effective policy given its proprioceptive-only observations.
- **Systematic Integration:** CTS aims to systematically integrate information from both teacher and student strategies, conceptually combining the teacher-student paradigm with the core ideas of representation learning.

By addressing the efficiency and optimality challenges of prior T-S methods, CTS aims to push the boundaries of robust and agile locomotion for various legged robots, particularly in blind locomotion scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a novel and more effective reinforcement learning framework, **CTS (Concurrent Teacher-Student)**, to achieve robust and agile legged locomotion for robots operating on uneven terrains, relying solely on proprioceptive feedback.

The key motivations driving the development of CTS are:

- **Overcoming Limitations of Conventional Two-Stage Teacher-Student RL:** The prevalent teacher-student paradigm, while effective, typically involves a two-stage training process. First, a teacher policy with access to "privileged information" (e.g., terrain heightmap, contact forces) is trained via RL. Second, a student policy, restricted to only proprioceptive observations (available on real robots), is trained via supervised learning to imitate the teacher. This sequential process can be inefficient and may lead to suboptimal student policies if the student merely attempts to perfectly mimic a teacher whose decisions are based on information the student cannot perceive.
- **Streamlining the Training Process:** The two-stage training is inherently cumbersome. The authors are motivated to create a more "streamlined" approach by training both teacher and student concurrently, thus potentially reducing overall training time and complexity.
- **Empowering the Student with RL Objectives:** A core motivation is to move beyond mere imitation for the student policy. The authors hypothesize that guiding the student's training with its own reinforcement learning objectives, rather than solely supervised imitation, will result in a "better policy." This is because the student, due to differences in observation space from the teacher, might find perfect imitation suboptimal or even detrimental to its performance in the real world where certain privileged information is absent. By letting the student learn to maximize its own reward, it can develop strategies that are robust to its observational constraints.
- **Exploiting Interplay between Teacher and Student:** The paper aims to systematically integrate information from both teacher and student strategies. This concurrent training allows for a dynamic interplay where the teacher, with its privileged information, guides the learning direction, while the student actively explores and learns optimal behaviors under its own constraints, rather than passively receiving knowledge transfer. This mutual influence is expected to enhance overall policy performance.
- **Enhancing Robustness and Agile Locomotion:** The ultimate goal is to achieve superior performance in terms of velocity tracking error reduction and robustness against external disturbances on challenging real-world terrains, as compared to state-of-the-art methods.
- **Demonstrating Universality Across Robot Platforms:** The authors are motivated to validate the versatility and generalizability of their approach across different types and sizes of legged robots, including quadrupedal robots (Unitree A1, Aliengo) and the more challenging, underactuated point-foot bipedal robot (LimX Dynamics P1). This demonstrates the method's potential for broad applicability in the field of legged robotics.

In essence, the motivation is to develop a more efficient, robust, and generalizable reinforcement learning framework for legged robots by rethinking the traditional teacher-student paradigm through concurrent training and an RL-centric approach for the student.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces **CTS: Concurrent Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning**, a novel architecture and training scheme designed to enable robust legged locomotion using only proprioceptive feedback.

**A. Concurrent Teacher-Student Architecture:**
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic Framework:** The core of CTS is an asymmetric actor-critic framework, where teacher and student policies are trained concurrently.
- **Two Agent Groups:** Parallel agents are divided into a "teacher group" and a "student group."
 - **Teacher Group:** Agents have access to full state information ($s_t$), including privileged information like terrain details. They utilize a **privileged encoder** ($E^t_\theta$) to encode these full states into a latent representation ($z^t_t$).
 - **Student Group:** Agents only have access to proprioceptive observations ($o_t$), mimicking real-world sensor limitations. They employ a **proprioceptive encoder** ($E^s_\theta$) to encode a historical sequence of proprioceptive observations ($o^s_{t-H:t}$) into a latent representation ($z^s_t$).
- **Shared Policy and Critic Networks:** Crucially, agents in both groups share the *same* **policy network** ($\pi_\theta$) and **critic network** ($V_\phi$).
 - The policy network outputs actions ($a_t$) given the proprioceptive observation ($o_t$) and the latent representation ($z_t$) (which comes from either the privileged or proprioceptive encoder).
 - The critic network estimates the state value ($V_\phi(s_t, z_t)$) for given states and latent representations.
- **Network Structure:** All modules (encoders, policy, critic) are implemented as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) activation. The latent representation is L2-normalized onto a unit hypersphere.

**B. Training Pipeline:**
The training process is based on a modified Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, adapting it for concurrent teacher-student learning.
- **Data Collection:** Trajectories ($D^t$ for teacher, $D^s$ for student) are collected from interactions with the environment using their respective encoders and the shared policy network.
- **Policy Network Update (Shared $\pi_\theta$):** The policy network is updated using PPO's clipped objective function. The key innovation is that the policy gradient combines data samples from *both* the teacher group and the student group: $L_{ppo,t}(\theta, \theta_t)$ and $L_{ppo,s}(\theta)$. This means the shared policy learns from the rich data generated by the privileged teacher as well as the proprioceptive-constrained student, enabling a concurrent learning process.
- **Critic Network Update (Shared $V_\phi$):** The critic network is trained via regression to minimize the mean-squared error between its estimated value and the Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) returns, using trajectories from *both* groups.
- **Proprioceptive Encoder Update (Student's $E^s_\theta$):** This is where the supervised learning aspect for the student comes in. The proprioceptive encoder is updated by minimizing a **reconstruction loss** ($L_{rec}(\theta_s)$). This loss measures the squared difference between the latent representation generated by the proprioceptive encoder ($E^s_\theta(o^s_{t-H:t})$) and the latent representation generated by the privileged encoder ($E^t_\theta(s^t_t)$). This step allows the student's encoder to learn to infer relevant state information from proprioceptive data by mimicking the privileged encoder's output.
- **Concurrent Iterations:** The algorithm iterates between updating the shared policy/critic networks using PPO objectives (leveraging both teacher and student data) and updating the student's proprioceptive encoder using the reconstruction loss. This online iterative scheme allows the teacher's performance to improve, providing a "moving target" that the student is concurrently guided towards, ultimately leading to better student policy performance.

**C. Reward Design:**
A unified, adaptable reward structure is used, with specific adjustments for different robot types.
- **Common Rewards (Quadruped & Biped):** Linear/angular velocity tracking, penalties for Z-velocity, XY-angular velocity, joint acceleration, power, torque, base height, action rate/smoothness, collision, joint limits.
- **Novel `feet_regulation` Reward ($r_{fr}$):** Introduced to encourage smooth, natural foot trajectories (vertical takeoff/touchdown) by penalizing horizontal foot velocity when the foot is low to the ground. This addresses a common issue in RL-trained gaits where feet tend to drag.
- **Biped-Specific Adjustments:**
 - **Reduced Z-velocity penalty & Base Orientation Reward:** To handle the higher underactuation and stability challenges of bipeds, the Z-velocity penalty is decreased, and a base orientation reward is added to penalize roll/pitch angles, promoting a level posture.
 - **`feet_distance` Penalty ($r_{fd}$):** Penalizes feet placing too close to the centerline, mitigating leg collisions which are common in bipeds seeking to minimize CoM torque.
 - **Periodic Rewards (`feet_contact_force`, `feet_velocity`):** Inspired by, these rewards encourage regular, periodic gaits by penalizing contact forces during swing phase and velocities during stance phase, ensuring specified contact patterns for self-balancing in point-foot bipeds.

**D. Environment Setup and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
- **Simulator:** IsaacGym is used for massively parallel training (8192 agents).
- **Agent Distribution:** Teacher and student groups are set at a 3:1 ratio (6144 teacher, 2048 student agents) to maintain the teacher's leadership in skill learning.
- **Curriculum Learning:** A terrain curriculum is employed, starting with easier slopes and progressively introducing more difficult terrains (rough slopes, stairs, discrete obstacles) as robot performance improves. Velocity command ranges are also increased incrementally.
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied to bridge the sim-to-real gap, randomizing inertial parameters (mass, CoM), friction, restitution, PD gains, motor strength, and action delay.

This comprehensive methodology, particularly the concurrent training with RL objectives for the student and the sophisticated reward design for various platforms, represents a significant departure from and improvement upon conventional teacher-student RL approaches for legged locomotion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents both quantitative simulation comparisons and qualitative real-world experimental results to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed CTS framework.

**A. Simulation Evaluation Results:**

The authors compare CTS against several baselines:
- **Oracle:** Policy trained with full privileged state input (upper bound performance).
- **Baseline:** Policy with proprioceptive encoder, trained purely by PPO (no privileged information or teacher guidance).
- **EstimatorNet:** Policy concurrently trained with an explicit estimator network for body velocity and feet height.
- **Two-stage Teacher-Student (T-S):** Conventional approach where the teacher is trained first, then the student is trained via supervised learning (latent reconstruction + action imitation).
- **Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA):** A single-stage method where encoders are trained with supervised learning, but the policy network is not updated via RL on proprioceptive input.

All methods were trained under the same configuration, network scale, and random seeds for fair comparison.

- **Learning Curves (Average Terrain Level):** Fig. 4 illustrates the average terrain level achieved by agents during training.
 - **Teacher in CTS:** Performs nearly identically to the Oracle, indicating that concurrent training with the student does not degrade the teacher's learning performance.
 - **Student in CTS:** Consistently outperforms the student policy from the two-stage T-S method. This is a crucial finding, supporting the hypothesis that training the student with RL objectives, even with a "moving target" teacher, yields better performance than pure imitation.
 - **Baseline & EstimatorNet:** Show similar final performance. EstimatorNet learns faster initially due to explicit estimation guidance.
 - ROA's curve is excluded due to its policy-switching nature, which doesn't directly reflect individual policy mobility.

- **Velocity Tracking Performance:** Fig. 5 quantifies the average velocity tracking error across four challenging terrain types (slopes, rough slopes, stairs, discrete obstacles).
 - **CTS Student Superiority:** The student policy trained with CTS significantly reduces velocity tracking error compared to the two-stage T-S student. Improvements are substantial: 17.85% on slopes, 19.12% on rough slopes, 7.9% on stairs, and 21.85% on discrete obstacles.
 - **Importance of Privileged Information:** On stair terrain (most challenging for tracking), methods directly guided by privileged information (CTS, Oracle, T-S) perform significantly better than Baseline and EstimatorNet, highlighting the value of such information for complex terrain navigation.

- **Robustness (Push Recovery Survival Rates):** Fig. 6 evaluates policies' ability to withstand strong external disturbances (random pushes inducing ~2.5m/s velocity change).
 - **CTS Robustness:** CTS demonstrates greater robustness across all terrain types compared to the two-stage T-S method, with survival rates higher by 5.04% to 6.47%.
 - **Two-stage T-S Limitation:** The staged training student exhibits the poorest robustness. This supports the argument that a student trained purely on imitation, without its own RL objective, may lack emphasis on crucial aspects like balance maintenance, making it more vulnerable to external disturbances due to the information gap with the teacher.
 - **EstimatorNet Performance:** Performs quite well in robustness, possibly benefiting from explicit estimation of base linear velocity.

**B. Real-World Experiments:**

The student policy (trained with CTS) was deployed on various hardware platforms:
- **Unitree A1 (Quadruped)**
- **Aliengo (Larger Quadruped)**
- **LimX Dynamics P1 (Challenging Point-Foot Bipedal Robot)**

The experiments showcase the small sim-to-real gap and the method's universality and superior performance:

- **Quadruped Locomotion on Moving Platform (Fig. 7):** An Aliengo robot successfully navigates a moving platform.
 - **Adaptation:** The robot instinctively retracts and lifts its leg upon encountering the obstacle, then accurately steps onto the platform. This demonstrates strong generalization capabilities, as this specific scenario was not explicitly trained.
 - **Dynamic Balance:** It maintains balance as the platform slides, showcasing reactive balance.
 - **Foot Placement Adaptation:** During descent, the hind leg dynamically adjusts its placement (S-shaped swing trajectory) to align with the robot's motion.

- **Quadruped Locomotion with Stairs and Missing Steps (Fig. 8):** The robot adapts to unexpected changes.
 - **Stumble Recovery:** When stumbling on a step, the robot quickly lifts its leg to step onto the stair.
 - **Missing Step Adaptation:** Upon stepping into empty space, the robot immediately senses the terrain change, extends its front legs to find support, and safely returns to the ground, demonstrating rapid adaptation to unseen terrain.

- **Biped Locomotion with Stairs (Fig. 9):** Demonstrates effectiveness on the highly challenging P1 bipedal robot.
 - **Obstacle Detection and Adaptation:** When the right leg hits a step during swing, the policy detects the obstacle via proprioception, causing the foot to land prematurely. Subsequently, the left leg is raised directly to overcome the obstruction.
 - **Height Perception and Gait Adjustment:** After the initial contact, the policy perceives the stair height, allowing subsequent swing trajectories to have sufficient height for smooth ascent. This highlights the policy's ability to extract and utilize implicit terrain information from proprioceptive inputs, even for a biped.

Overall, the results clearly demonstrate that CTS achieves significant improvements in velocity tracking and robustness in simulation. Furthermore, the extensive real-world experiments on diverse and challenging robot platforms confirm the practical applicability and high performance of the learned policies in unstructured environments, validating the method's ability to bridge the sim-to-real gap effectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Concurrent Teacher-Student (CTS) Reinforcement Learning framework represents a significant advancement in the field of legged robot locomotion, particularly for achieving robust and agile movement over complex terrains using only proprioceptive feedback.

**Key Significance of the Work:**

1. **Enhanced Learning Efficiency and Performance:** By enabling concurrent training of teacher and student policies, CTS streamlines the learning process compared to conventional two-stage methods. More importantly, it demonstrates that guiding the student policy with **reinforcement learning objectives (maximizing discounted return)**, rather than just pure supervised imitation of the teacher, leads to substantially better performance. The quantitative results show up to a 20% reduction in velocity tracking error and improved robustness (higher survival rates under pushes) compared to the traditional two-stage teacher-student approach. This is a crucial insight: allowing the student to learn actively under its own constraints, even from a dynamically improving teacher, yields superior outcomes.

2. **Robustness to Information Gaps:** The method effectively addresses the challenge of partial observability in real-world scenarios. The student policy, relying solely on proprioceptive inputs, learns to infer task-relevant information implicitly from the teacher's guidance and its own interaction with the environment. This leads to robust "blind locomotion" capabilities, meaning the robots can navigate complex terrains without explicit terrain maps or external visual sensors.

3. **Generalizability Across Robot Platforms:** A major strength of CTS is its demonstrated universality. The framework successfully enabled robust and agile locomotion on a range of platforms: quadrupeds of different sizes (Unitree A1, Aliengo) and, critically, a highly challenging, underactuated point-foot bipedal robot (LimX Dynamics P1). This cross-platform validation is a strong indicator of the method's versatility and potential for broader application in legged robotics. The tailored reward design for bipeds further showcases the adaptability of the framework.

4. **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The extensive real-world experiments confirm that policies trained in simulation using CTS transfer effectively to physical robots. This is a testament to the robust training methodology, including the curriculum learning and comprehensive domain randomization strategies, which successfully mitigate the notorious sim-to-real gap. The ability of robots to handle moving platforms, adapt to missing steps, and maintain balance on stairs in real-world scenarios validates the practical utility of the approach.

5. **Novel Reward Design:** The introduction of the `feet_regulation` reward to promote smooth, natural foot trajectories and the specific adaptations for bipedal stability (e.g., `feet_distance`, `feet_contact_force`, `feet_velocity`) are valuable contributions to the reward engineering aspect of RL for legged locomotion. These elements contribute to the generation of more robust and aesthetically pleasing gaits.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Advancement of Autonomous Robotics:** CTS paves the way for more autonomous and capable legged robots in real-world unstructured and hazardous environments (e.g., disaster relief, exploration, industrial inspection). Robots equipped with such policies can robustly traverse diverse terrains without relying on heavy or complex external sensing, making them more practical and deployable.
- **New Paradigm for RL-based Control:** The concurrent teacher-student paradigm, with the student actively learning through RL, could become a new standard for training complex robot behaviors, moving beyond purely imitative learning for partial observability.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitation—the reliance on physical leg-obstacle interaction for adaptation—points directly to future research directions. Integrating exteroceptive inputs (e.g., vision, LiDAR) into the locomotion system for proactive gait planning and obstacle negotiation *before* physical contact would be the logical next step. This work provides a strong foundation upon which such perceptive locomotion systems can be built.
- **Commercial Applications:** Given the affiliation with LimX Dynamics, the practical impact on commercial legged robot development is evident. The ability to train robust and generalizable locomotion policies efficiently could accelerate the deployment of advanced legged robots in various industries.

In conclusion, CTS offers a powerful and effective solution for developing highly capable legged robot controllers. Its innovations in concurrent training, RL-driven student learning, and rigorous real-world validation signify a substantial step forward in making legged robots truly autonomous and adaptable to the complexities of the physical world.
