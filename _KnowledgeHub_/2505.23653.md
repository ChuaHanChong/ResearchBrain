# How do Transformers Learn Implicit Reasoning?

> [!summary] Summary
> Ye et al. from Tsinghua University, Peking University, and Siemens AG investigate how Transformers acquire implicit multi-hop reasoning by training them from scratch in a controlled symbolic environment. They identify a three-stage generalization trajectory, showing that successful reasoning correlates with the consistent cosine-space clustering of intermediate entity representations, while challenging the assumption that functional internal states must be explicitly decodable.

## Problem

- Understanding the internal processes by which Large Language Models (LLMs) acquire and perform implicit multi-hop reasoning.
- Distinguishing genuine internal reasoning within LLMs from mere memorization or superficial shortcut learning.
- Addressing the limitations of existing research, which either lack control in pre-trained LLMs or sufficient mechanistic granularity in symbolic datasets.

## Method

- Training ==decoder-only Transformers== from scratch in a highly controlled symbolic environment, extended with fine-grained query-level control and targeted omissions.
- Developing novel diagnostic tools: =='cross-query semantic patching'== to causally identify semantically transferable intermediate representations, and a =='cosine-based representational lens'== to analyze the structural consistency of internal representations.
- Systematically tracking model performance across distinct training phases and various ==in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD)== query types, including ==2-hop== and ==3-hop== reasoning.

## Results

- Models achieve robust generalization for first-hop OOD queries (Test-OI), which is explained by OOD-derived representations aligning with ID-based clusters, but consistently fail for second-hop OOD queries (Test-IO, Test-OO) due to a lack of query-level compositional supervision.
- Cross-query semantic patching successfully localized transferable intermediate entity representations primarily at the `r₁` token in middle layers of the Transformer, with patching success rates increasing as reasoning capabilities emerge.
- Intermediate representations corresponding to the same entity coalesce into tight clusters in cosine space (high ID Cohesion) during in-distribution generalization, and OOD-derived representations align with these clusters (high OOD Alignment) during cross-distribution generalization, indicating a representational unification mechanism for generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit reasoning emerges through a consistent three-phase generalization trajectory: memorization, in-distribution generalization ('grokking'), and a newly identified cross-distribution generalization.
> - Successful reasoning, particularly generalization, is strongly correlated with the geometric regularity and consistent clustering of intermediate entity representations in cosine similarity space.
> - The explicit decodability of intermediate hidden states into tokens does not reliably indicate their functional role in reasoning; representations can be decodable even before reasoning capabilities emerge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated at the crucial intersection of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, interpretability, and mechanistic understanding of neural networks. Recent advancements have showcased LLMs' impressive capabilities in complex, multi-step reasoning tasks, often facilitated by techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, which verbalizes intermediate steps. However, a significant area of inquiry, and the focus of this paper, lies in **implicit reasoning**, where LLMs produce correct answers without explicitly articulating their internal thought processes.

The broader research landscape grapples with the "black box" nature of LLMs. While LLMs excel behaviorally, understanding *how* they arrive at their conclusions remains a formidable challenge. This paper directly addresses this by investigating the underlying mechanisms of implicit reasoning, aiming to distinguish genuine internal reasoning from mere memorization or shortcut learning.

Previous studies exploring implicit reasoning often rely on pre-trained LLMs, whose vast and opaque training data makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about the genesis of reasoning abilities. Researchers cannot precisely control for prior knowledge or accidental shortcuts learned during pre-training. To circumvent this, a growing body of work, including this paper, adopts **controlled symbolic environments** where Transformers are trained from scratch on synthetic datasets. This approach, pioneered by works like Wang et al., allows for fine-grained experimental control, enabling researchers to isolate specific training signals and observe the emergence of complex behaviors.

The paper also extends the concept of **grokking**, a phenomenon where models generalize long after overfitting the training data, by identifying a third stage of "cross-distribution generalization." This refines our understanding of how generalization unfolds over extended training periods. Furthermore, it contributes to the mechanistic interpretability agenda by introducing novel diagnostic tools—**cross-query semantic patching** and a **cosine-based representational lens**—that aim to overcome the limitations of traditional methods like linear probing or causal patching, which often provide only correlational insights or rely on potentially flawed assumptions about explicit decodability of internal states.

By systematically investigating the developmental stages of implicit reasoning, the necessary training conditions (e.g., query-level exposure), and the internal representational structures (e.g., cosine-space clustering), this work offers fundamental insights into why LLMs might exhibit phenomena like the "two-hop curse" (where single-hop knowledge doesn't readily transfer to multi-hop reasoning) and provides a more nuanced understanding of how latent computations support complex problem-solving. It directly informs the ongoing debate about whether LLMs truly "reason" or simply retrieve and recombine memorized patterns.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to demystify the internal processes by which Large Language Models (LLMs) acquire and perform **implicit multi-hop reasoning**. The authors are motivated by a fundamental question: "How do LLMs acquire and perform implicit reasoning during training and inference?" This core inquiry branches into several specific aims:

1. **Distinguish Genuine Reasoning from Memorization:** A primary motivation is to ascertain whether LLMs genuinely conduct step-by-step reasoning internally or merely recall answers from memorized knowledge. Both behaviors can yield correct outputs, but reflect fundamentally different cognitive processes. Understanding this distinction is crucial for building robust and trustworthy AI systems.
2. **Uncover Internal Mechanisms of Implicit Reasoning:** The paper seeks to shed light on the "internal processes of implicit reasoning" within the Transformer architecture, specifically in the context of multi-hop compositional queries where intermediate entities are not explicitly verbalized.
3. **Address Limitations of Existing Research Settings:** Current studies on implicit reasoning often suffer from two major drawbacks:
 - **Lack of Control in Pre-trained LLMs:** Analyses of pre-trained LLMs offer limited experimental control, making it difficult to isolate the exact training data responsible for emergent reasoning abilities or to rule out the reliance on prior knowledge or superficial shortcuts.
 - **Limited Granularity in Symbolic Datasets:** While symbolic datasets trained from scratch provide better control, previous works often focus on dataset-level trends, lacking the fine-grained experimental control and behavioral resolution needed for deeper mechanistic analysis at the query level. The authors aim to overcome these limitations by constructing a more controlled symbolic environment.
4. **Develop Advanced Diagnostic Tools:** To facilitate a deeper mechanistic analysis in their controlled environment, the researchers aim to introduce novel interpretability tools. Specifically, they propose "cross-query semantic patching" to identify semantically transferable intermediate representations and a "cosine-based representational lens" to analyze the structural consistency of internal representations without making assumptions about their explicit decodability.
5. **Understand the Emergence and Generalization of Reasoning:** The study is motivated to track the developmental trajectory of implicit reasoning during training, identifying distinct phases of learning (memorization, in-distribution generalization, cross-distribution generalization) and the specific conditions (e.g., exposure to atomic facts, compositional structures) that enable or hinder generalization capabilities. This includes understanding why certain forms of generalization (e.g., first-hop OOD) succeed while others (e.g., second-hop OOD) fail.

By addressing these objectives, the paper aims to provide a "comprehensive account of how implicit multi-hop reasoning emerges within LLMs," thereby contributing foundational insights to the field of AI interpretability and guiding future research into enhancing the transparency and capabilities of advanced language models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a rigorous methodology centered on training Transformers from scratch in a highly controlled symbolic environment, complemented by novel diagnostic tools for mechanistic interpretability.

#### 3.1. Model Architecture and Training Setup

The base model used is a decoder-only Transformer, architecturally identical to GPT-2, comprising 8 layers, 768 hidden dimensions, and 12 attention heads. For scalability analysis, a larger Qwen2.5-1.5B model is also employed. Models are trained from scratch using AdamW optimizer, a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, 2000 warm-up steps, and a batch size of 1024. Training extends significantly beyond convergence to observe late-stage generalization, sometimes for up to three weeks on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs.

#### 3.2. Controlled Symbolic Environment and Data Construction

The core of the methodology is an extended symbolic reasoning setup, building upon prior work by Wang et al.. This environment offers fine-grained query-level control and targeted omissions.

- **Vocabulary:** Consists of 2000 entities and 200 relations, each represented by a unique, semantically neutral token.
- **Atomic Triples:** Facts are represented as (e₁, r₁) → e₂, mimicking simple factual relations. These are partitioned into:
 - **In-Distribution (ID) Triples:** Used both standalone and as components of multi-hop training queries.
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Triples:** Appear in training data only standalone, not in multi-hop compositions, enabling OOD reasoning tests. Entities and relations are shared between ID and OOD sets. The default ID/OOD ratio is 95%/5%.
- **2-Hop Queries:** Compositional chains of the form (e₁, r₁, r₂) → e₃, requiring implicit reasoning through an intermediate entity e₂. Queries are categorized for training and testing:
 - **Train-II:** Both hops from ID triples, used during training.
 - **Test-II:** Unseen ID-ID compositions.
 - **Test-OI, Test-IO, Test-OO:** Test queries involving OOD triples in the first, second, or both hops, respectively.
- **Training Configurations:** A base configuration includes all atomic triples (ID and OOD) and Train-II queries. A flexible family of training variants are defined for ablation studies, allowing targeted omission of specific triples or restrictions on compositional roles.
- **3-Hop Extension:** The framework is extended to (e₁, r₁, r₂, r₃) → e₄, involving two intermediate entities. Vocabulary size and OOD ratio are adjusted (1000 entities, 100 relations, 20% OOD) to manage combinatorial complexity and test set sparsity.

#### 3.3. Behavioral Study

The authors track model performance (accuracy) on different query types across training checkpoints. This allows them to observe the emergence of reasoning abilities and identify distinct developmental phases (memorization, in-distribution generalization, cross-distribution generalization). Ablation studies are performed to investigate the necessity of specific training signals, such as the role of atomic ID triples and query-level exposure for second-hop generalization.

#### 3.4. Diagnostic Tools for Internal Mechanisms

- **Cross-Query Semantic Patching:**
 - **Goal:** Causally identify hidden representations that encode semantically valid intermediate entities.
 - **Mechanism:** A hidden vector from a candidate layer/position (e.g., `h⁵_r₁`) of a source query (`e₁`, `r₁`, `r₂`) is patched into the same layer/position of a structurally similar target query (`e₅`, `r₆`, `r₇`).
 - **Success Metric:** If the patched model's output prediction shifts from `r₇(r₆(e₅))` to `r₇(r₁(e₁))`, it confirms that the patched representation carries transferable semantic information of the bridge entity.
- **Explicit Decoding Probing (Logit Lens):**
 - **Goal:** Test the assumption that informative intermediate representations are explicitly decodable into human-interpretable tokens.
 - **Mechanism:** Applies immediate probing (projecting hidden states onto the output vocabulary) and full-run probing (patching hidden states and decoding after full model processing) to assess decodability of the localized intermediate entity representations (`h⁵_r₁`).
- **Cosine-based Representational Lens:**
 - **Goal:** Examine the geometric regularity and consistency of intermediate representations in the hidden space.
 - **Mechanism:** Collects `h⁵_r₁` hidden states for queries sharing the same intermediate entity.
 - **Visualization:** Uses Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) to project high-dimensional vectors into 2D space to visualize clustering patterns.
 - **Quantification:** Introduces two metrics:
 - **ID Cohesion Score:** Average cosine similarity between ID-derived intermediate representations and their centroid, measuring internal consistency.
 - **OOD Alignment Score:** Average cosine similarity between OOD-derived intermediate representations and the ID centroid, measuring cross-distribution unification.

By combining these behavioral observations with mechanistic interpretations derived from their novel diagnostic tools, the authors systematically "close the loop" to explain *how* the observed reasoning phenomena emerge from the model's internal dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study reveals several significant findings regarding the emergence and mechanisms of implicit multi-hop reasoning in Transformers:

1. **Three-Stage Generalization Trajectory:** The models exhibit a consistent three-phase developmental trajectory:
 - **Phase I (Memorization):** The model quickly learns to fit the training data (atomic facts and 2-hop compositions) but shows minimal generalization to unseen queries.
 - **Phase II (In-Distribution Generalization):** After memorization saturates, the model generalizes to unseen ID-ID compositions (Test-II queries). This phase aligns with the "grokking" phenomenon observed in prior work.
 - **Phase III (Cross-Distribution Reasoning):** The model then slowly learns to generalize across distributions, specifically to Test-OI queries (OOD first hop, ID second hop). Generalization consistently fails when the second hop involves OOD triples (Test-IO, Test-OO), indicating a stronger bottleneck in the second relational step.

2. **ID Triples Accelerate, but are Not Strictly Necessary for, ID Generalization:** Surprisingly, models can generalize to Test-II queries even when trained *only* on Train-II (2-hop compositional) queries, without explicit exposure to atomic ID triples. However, including atomic ID triples in the training significantly *accelerates* the onset of this in-distribution generalization.

3. **Second-Hop Generalization Requires Query-Level Training Match:** The model consistently fails to generalize when the second hop of a query involves OOD triples or when specific atomic triples are excluded from *second-hop positions* during training, even if those same triples are present in the training data as atomic facts or in the first hop of other queries. This demonstrates a strong dependency on query-level exposure for second-hop compositional structures. Increased second-hop exposure frequency correlates with earlier learning.

4. **Intermediate Entity Localization:** Using **cross-query semantic patching**, the study successfully localizes intermediate entity representations. These are primarily found at the `r₁` token position in the middle layers (e.g., layer 5 for an 8-layer model). Patching success rates are negligible in Phase I but become prominent as reasoning capabilities emerge.

5. **Explicit Decodability ≠ Implicit Informative:** The traditional assumption that informative hidden states must be explicitly decodable (e.g., via logit lens) is challenged. Intermediate entities remain highly decodable across all training phases, even before reasoning capabilities emerge. Furthermore, there's no significant difference in decodability between ID-derived and OOD-derived representations during Phase II, despite differential reasoning performance. The model appears to prefer non-explicit representations for generalization, abandoning early attempts at explicit decoding.

6. **Geometric Regularity (Cosine-based Clustering) Correlates with Reasoning:** A key mechanistic finding is that successful reasoning correlates with the consistent clustering of intermediate representations in cosine similarity space.
 - **Phase I:** Intermediate entity representations are geometrically scattered.
 - **Phase II:** ID-derived representations form tight clusters (high ID Cohesion Score), aligning with the emergence of Test-II generalization.
 - **Phase III:** OOD-derived representations begin to align with these ID-based clusters (increasing OOD Alignment Score), correlating with the rise in Test-OI performance. This "cosine-space clustering" is proposed as the model's internal mechanism for semantic abstraction and generalization.

7. **Explanations for Behavioral Phenomena:**
 - **OOD Generalization (Phase III) is Driven by ID Supervision:** The clustering of OOD-derived representations is not spontaneous but relies on the dominance of ID supervision. High ID/OOD ratios (e.g., 80% ID) are essential for OOD representations to assimilate into ID clusters and enable Test-OI generalization; lower ratios prevent this.
 - **ID Triples Accelerate Generalization by Constraining Representation Space:** Atomic ID triples, while not strictly necessary, accelerate learning by constraining the `r₁` hidden state to a decodable subspace. Because the `r₁` token position is shared across atomic and 2-hop queries due to causal masking, ID triple supervision directly shapes the same representations used for multi-hop reasoning, thus limiting the search space for generalization.
 - **First-Hop OOD Generalization (Test-OI) is an "Artifact":** The apparent generalization to Test-OI queries (OOD → ID) is not true generalization of the `r_atomic_1` relation. Instead, it's an "illusory" success arising from representational alignment: OOD-derived entities are pulled into the same cosine-space cluster as ID-derived entities (due to ID supervision), effectively "cheating" by reusing shared representation scaffolds. This mechanism relies on shared input contexts and does not extend to deeper reasoning steps.
 - **Failure of Second-Hop OOD Generalization:** This is explained by the lack of such representational anchoring. The second hop cannot rely on shared prefixes or incidental alignment and thus requires direct, query-level compositional supervision.

8. **Scaling Analysis:** With Qwen2.5-1.5B, similar three-stage generalization is observed, but with increased instability in Phase III. Critically, Test-II accuracy decouples from ID Cohesion in larger models, suggesting that while representation alignment is necessary, the execution of the second relational step becomes a separate and later bottleneck for successful ID generalization. OOD Alignment, however, remains tightly coupled with Test-OI accuracy, reinforcing its role in cross-distribution generalization.

These findings collectively provide a detailed, mechanistic view of how implicit reasoning unfolds internally within Transformers, moving beyond purely behavioral observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the fields of LLM interpretability, mechanistic understanding of neural networks, and the broader study of AI reasoning. Its potential impact spans both theoretical understanding and practical implications for model development.

1. **Deepened Mechanistic Understanding of Implicit Reasoning:** The study provides the first comprehensive, mechanistic account of how implicit multi-hop reasoning emerges in Transformers. By linking observable behavioral stages (memorization, ID generalization, cross-distribution generalization) to specific internal representational dynamics (cosine-space clustering of intermediate entities), it moves beyond correlational insights to causal explanations. This clarity is crucial for demystifying the "black box" nature of LLMs.

2. **Novel Interpretability Tools:** The introduction of "cross-query semantic patching" and the "cosine-based representational lens" offers powerful new methods for probing internal representations. These tools address limitations of prior techniques by focusing on semantic transferability and structural consistency rather than relying solely on explicit decodability or superficial causal influence. This could inspire the development of similar, more nuanced diagnostic tools for other complex LLM capabilities.

3. **Refined Understanding of Generalization and "Grokking":** The identification of a third stage of "cross-distribution generalization" extends the existing concept of "grokking." This demonstrates that generalization is not a monolithic event but a multi-stage process with distinct conditions and internal mechanisms, offering a more granular view of how models move from memorization to robust generalization.

4. **Explanation for Known LLM Challenges:** The findings provide plausible mechanistic explanations for observed phenomena in LLMs, such as the "two-hop curse" (where single-hop knowledge struggles to transfer to multi-hop tasks). The requirement for "query-level training match" for second-hop generalization, and the "illusory" nature of first-hop OOD generalization driven by representational alignment, offer concrete reasons for these transferability limitations. This helps clarify why knowledge learned in one context may not be readily available in another, despite seemingly overlapping facts.

5. **Insights for Robust LLM Training:** The study highlights that strong in-distribution supervision (atomic ID triples) is vital not only for teaching facts but also for scaffolding the latent space, enabling the alignment of OOD-derived representations and facilitating cross-distribution generalization. Conversely, it underscores that complex reasoning steps require specific, query-level compositional exposure. These insights can inform the design of more effective training curricula and data augmentation strategies to foster more robust and generalizable reasoning abilities in LLMs, especially for multi-hop tasks.

6. **Challenging Assumptions about Decodability:** By demonstrating that explicit decodability of intermediate states does not necessarily correlate with their functional role in reasoning, the paper prompts a re-evaluation of common interpretability practices. This encourages researchers to explore diverse perspectives, such as geometric regularity, for understanding internal computations, moving beyond human-interpretable token-level decoding.

While the study is conducted in a controlled symbolic environment, and its direct applicability to real-world LLMs (with their vastly more complex knowledge bases and training dynamics) is acknowledged as preliminary, the foundational insights it offers are invaluable. It provides a crucial stepping stone towards achieving more transparent, reliable, and genuinely intelligent large language models by elucidating the intricate internal mechanisms that underpin their impressive, yet often mysterious, reasoning capabilities.
