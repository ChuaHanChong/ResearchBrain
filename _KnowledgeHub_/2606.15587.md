# Perfect Demo Makes Poor Teacher: Learning Robust Alignment from Critical Motion Segments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Zhejiang University and collaborators investigated why fluent human demonstrations often lead to brittle robot performance in fine-grained manipulation, identifying a 'duration–sensitivity mismatch'. They developed STAIR, a novel representation that extracts compact dynamic features from short video clips, enabling policies to achieve 62.2% task success on fluent data, comparable to policies trained on labor-intensive 'deliberate' demonstrations, and reaching 70.0% when combined.

## Problem

- Policies trained on fluent human demonstrations often fail at fine-grained manipulation tasks requiring precise alignment, despite mastering coarse motions.
- Fluent human expert demonstrations lead to an imbalanced learning signal, providing abundant supervision for less sensitive transport phases but insufficient data for critical, highly sensitive alignment phases.
- The lack of demonstrated error recovery in fluent expert data makes robot policies brittle to minor deviations during deployment, as they do not learn how to correct misaligned states.

## Method

- Proposed data-level remedies: 'deliberate demonstrations' (slower, corrective motions near contact) and 'critical-segment resampling' (upweighting frames within task-critical temporal windows in fluent data).
- Introduced STAIR (Spatio-Temporal feature As an Interface for Robot learning), a compact dynamic feature extracted from short video clips via a trained projector, to provide explicit local motion information to the policy.
- Developed a two-stage training protocol for STAIR: first, shaping dynamic features to be action-relevant, then training the Vision-Language Model (VLM) to predict these dynamic features from current observations at inference time.

## Results

- Deliberate demonstrations significantly improved overall success rates for fine-grained tasks from **52.2%** (fluent) to **67.8%** (deliberate) on an independent base policy, with more pronounced gains on harder tasks.
- STAIR, trained solely on fluent demonstrations with a custom VLA base, achieved **62.2%** overall success, recovering most of the performance gains (**64.4%**) observed with deliberate demonstrations alone.
- Combining STAIR with deliberate demonstrations yielded the highest overall success rate of **70.0%** on fine-grained manipulation tasks, and STAIR demonstrated competitive performance on general manipulation benchmarks like SimplerEnv.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fluent expert demonstrations, while efficient for humans, can be suboptimal for robot learning due to a 'duration–sensitivity mismatch', where critical alignment phases are underrepresented.
> - Expanding the *coverage* of recovery states and corrective behaviors through deliberate demonstrations is more impactful for robot learning than merely increasing the *weight* of existing critical segments in fluent data.
> - Explicitly encoding short-horizon dynamic information into a compact feature (STAIR) allows policies to learn robust fine alignment from standard fluent demonstrations, effectively bridging the gap without needing specialized data collection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of robot imitation learning, particularly focusing on fine-grained manipulation tasks such as insertion, stacking, and alignment. Imitation learning, where robots learn policies by observing human demonstrations, has become a mainstream paradigm, especially with the rise of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models typically map language instructions and visual observations to robot actions, often conditioning each action on a single, per-step visual state.

The broader landscape of robot learning from demonstration often assumes that expert demonstrations, characterized by their fluency and efficiency, serve as the optimal source of knowledge. However, this paper challenges that assumption, particularly for tasks requiring high precision. It identifies a specific challenge where the efficiency of human experts can inadvertently diminish the quality of the learning signal for robots.

The paper contributes to several sub-areas of robotics research:
- **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models:** It builds upon existing VLA frameworks but proposes a modification to their input representation, moving beyond static per-step visual states to incorporate local motion dynamics.
- **Robotic Representation Learning:** The research explores how to extract and encode task-relevant information that is often implicit in raw visual data. Previous work in this area has utilized semantic priors, spatial features, or motion-oriented features. This paper specifically focuses on compact dynamic features for capturing short-horizon motion crucial for fine alignment.
- **Data Curation for Robot Learning:** The study investigates the impact of demonstration data characteristics on learning outcomes. While much work focuses on scaling dataset coverage, filtering for quality, or rebalancing data mixtures, this paper delves into how the temporal composition of demonstrations, particularly the distribution of critical vs. non-critical segments, affects policy performance. It also explores data-level interventions like deliberate demonstration collection and critical-segment resampling, distinguishing between the effects of increased supervision weight and expanded state coverage.

By identifying and addressing the "perfect demonstration can make a poor teacher" paradox, this research provides insights into how the design of demonstrations and the visual representations used by policies can be optimized for machine learnability, rather than solely for human execution efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to improve robot success rates in fine-grained manipulation tasks, which often involve precise alignment, contact handling, and recovery from minor errors. The primary motivation stems from an observed failure mode in imitation learning: policies trained on what appear to be "fluent" or "perfect" expert demonstrations often master coarse motions (e.g., approaching a target) but fail in the critical final centimeters where precision is paramount.

The authors formalize this problem as a "duration–sensitivity mismatch." In most fine-manipulation trajectories, a significant majority of frames (e.g., 90%) depict easy free-space transport, while success is determined by a short "alignment phase" (e.g., 10% of frames) near contact. An efficient human expert compresses this decisive segment into a few frames, leading to two critical issues for robot learning:

1. **Imbalanced Supervision:** Standard imitation learning losses typically weight every frame equally. Consequently, the policy receives abundant supervision for the less sensitive transport phase and comparatively little supervision for the highly sensitive alignment phase, where actions have a disproportionately large impact on success or failure.
2. **Lack of Recovery States:** Fluent experts rarely make errors and thus rarely enter or demonstrate recovery from misaligned states. This deprives the learning policy of crucial information on how to correct errors and recover toward the target, making it brittle to minor deviations during deployment.

The paper aims to address this bottleneck at two levels:

- **Data Level:** The objective is to provide more and better supervision for the critical alignment phase. This is explored through two approaches:
 - **Deliberate Demonstrations:** Changing the demonstration protocol to explicitly teach corrective behaviors by slowing down, making small adjustments, and occasionally demonstrating recovery from mild offsets near contact.
 - **Critical-Segment Resampling:** Reweighting existing fluent demonstrations to emphasize frames within task-critical temporal windows, thus increasing the effective training weight on alignment segments without collecting new data.
- **Representation Level:** The objective is to enable the policy to extract more meaningful information from individual frames, particularly regarding local motion and contact cues, which are vital for fine alignment. This is achieved through the introduction of STAIR (Spatio-Temporal feature As an Interface for Robot learning), a compact dynamic feature. The motivation here is that a single static image may not sufficiently convey whether the gripper is drifting, contact has begun, or an error is shrinking, necessitating a representation that summarizes short-horizon dynamics.

Ultimately, the research seeks to demonstrate that a more "pedagogical view" of robot data, optimized for machine learnability rather than solely human efficiency, is crucial for robust fine manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on diagnosing the "duration–sensitivity mismatch" in imitation learning for fine manipulation and proposing remedies at both the data and representation levels.

#### 3.1. Formalizing the Alignment Bottleneck

The paper formalizes the problem by defining a trajectory $\tau = \{(o_t, a_t)\}_{t=1}^T$ as composed of a transport phase $T_{tr}$ and an alignment phase $T_{al}$. The fraction of frames in alignment is denoted $\alpha = |T_{al}|/T$. A standard behavioral cloning (BC) loss is given by $L_{BC} = (1 - \alpha) L_{tr} + \alpha L_{al}$. For fluent demonstrations, $\alpha$ is small, leading to an imbalance in training weight. The action sensitivity $\sigma_\phi \propto \partial P(\text{success})/\partial a_t$ is significantly higher in the alignment phase ($\sigma_{al} \gg \sigma_{tr}$), meaning small errors during alignment are critical, whereas transport errors are often recoverable. This mismatch between duration and sensitivity is identified as the core bottleneck.

#### 3.2. Data-Level Remedies

Two data-level approaches are explored to address the bottleneck:

1. **Deliberate Demonstrations:**
 - **Protocol:** Human operators are instructed to perform the transport phase naturally but adopt a "teacher" role near contact. This involves slowing down, executing small corrective motions, and occasionally approaching the target with a slight offset before recovering.
 - **Effect:** This protocol increases the alignment-frame ratio ($\alpha_{delib} > \alpha_{fluent}$) and, more significantly, broadens the coverage of recovery states and local correction processes, which are often absent in fluent expert data.
2. **Critical-Segment Resampling:**
 - **Protocol:** This method retains the original fluent demonstrations but reweights frames during training. It identifies "key moments" $K$ (e.g., gripper changes, contact, insertion, placement) and defines a critical window $W$ around these moments with a radius $r$. Frames within this window are upweighted by a factor $\lambda$. The loss function becomes $L_{resample} = \frac{1}{\sum_t w_t} \sum_{t=1}^T w_t \ell(\pi_\theta(o_t, l), a_t)$, where $w_t = \lambda$ if $t \in W$ and $1$ otherwise.
 - **Effect:** This approach effectively increases the training contribution of alignment frames without requiring new data collection. However, its limitation is that it cannot introduce novel recovery behaviors or temporal details not originally recorded in the fluent demonstrations.

#### 3.3. Representation-Level Remedy: STAIR (Spatio-Temporal feature As an Interface for Robot learning)

STAIR introduces a compact dynamic feature to provide the action expert with explicit information about local motion during alignment.

- **Dynamic Feature Extraction:** For each training sample, a short dynamic observation clip $N_t = \{o_{t+\delta}\}_{\delta \in \Delta}$ (a neighborhood of frames around the action chunk) is associated with the supervised action. This clip is encoded by a frozen WanVAE encoder $E_{wan}$, and then a trainable projector $P_\psi$ (implemented as a 3D convolution followed by a Q-Former) compresses the video latent into compact dynamic tokens $f_{dyn_t} = P_\psi(E_{wan}(N_t))$. These tokens are designed to capture cues such as movement into contact, slipping, or correction.
- **Training Protocol (Two Stages):**
 1. **Stage I (Feature Shaping):** The WanVAE encoder is frozen. The projector $P_\psi$, an MLP adapter (MLP$_\eta$), the action expert, and selected VLA modules are jointly trained. The projected dynamic tokens $c_{dyn_t} = \text{MLP}_\eta(f_{dyn_t})$ condition the action expert through cross-attention. The action expert uses a flow-matching DiT architecture. The loss is an L2 loss on predicted velocities: $L_I = \mathbb{E}_{s,A} [\|v_\theta(A_s, s, z_t, c_{dyn_t}) - v^*\|^2_2]$, where $z_t$ is the VLM latent and $v^*$ is the target velocity. The gradient flows back through MLP$_\eta$ and $P_\psi$, shaping the dynamic tokens to be action-relevant.
 2. **Stage II (VLM Prediction of Dynamic Feature):** The entire dynamic-feature pathway ($E_{wan}$, $P_\psi$, MLP$_\eta$) is frozen. A QFormer query module is added on top of the VLM hidden states and trained to predict the *same* projected dynamic condition from the current observation and instruction: $\hat{c}_{dyn_t} = q_\phi(\text{hidden}(f_{VLM}(o_t, l)))$. This prediction is supervised with a cosine distance loss: $L_{dyn} = 1 - S(\hat{c}_{dyn_t}, c_{dyn_t})$. The action expert continues training with the *frozen* projected condition $c_{dyn_t}$ as part of a combined loss: $L_{II} = L_{action}(z_t, c_{dyn_t}) + \lambda_{dyn} L_{dyn}$.
- **Inference:** During deployment, the video neighborhood $N_t$ is not available. The policy receives only the current RGB observation $o_t$ and instruction $l$. The VLM produces $z_t$, the trained query module predicts $\hat{c}_{dyn_t}$ from $z_t$, and the action expert uses $(z_t, \hat{c}_{dyn_t})$ to generate actions. This preserves the standard VLA observation interface while benefiting from the learned dynamic feature.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Hardware:** Experiments used a dual-arm PiperX robot platform with one USB head camera and two USB wrist cameras (640x360 resolution, 30 FPS).
- **Tasks:** Six real-world fine-grained manipulation tasks, grouped by increasing alignment difficulty: Stack Bowls (Easy), Stack Lego Blocks (Easy), Insert Flowers (Middle), Insert Pen Cap (Middle), Put Coin into Safe (Hard), and Open the Lock (Hard).
- **Data Conditions:** For each task, 100 demonstrations were collected under three conditions:
 - **Fluent:** Efficient expert demonstrations.
 - **Deliberate:** Slow-at-alignment demonstrations (matched in trajectory count to Fluent, but longer).
 - **Resample:** Critical-segment reweighting applied to the Fluent dataset.
- **Base Policies:** Experiments used two base policies to evaluate the data-level effects:
 - **$\pi_{0.5}$:** An independent, strong VLA policy used to establish the model-agnostic nature of data-level remedies.
 - **Custom VLA Base:** A VLM backbone coupled with a flow-matching DiT action expert (the architecture STAIR builds upon). The "Baseline" here is this custom VLA without the STAIR dynamic-feature pathway.
- **Evaluation:** Policies were evaluated with 15 trials per task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to answer four questions, yielding several key findings.

#### 4.1. Data-Level Remedies are Model-Agnostic (Q1)

Experiments on the independent $\pi_{0.5}$ base policy demonstrated that data-level remedies effectively improve fine alignment performance.
- **Deliberate demonstrations** significantly improved overall success rates compared to fluent demonstrations (from 52.2% to 67.8%). This improvement was more pronounced as task alignment difficulty increased (e.g., 10.0% to 36.7% for hard tasks).
- **Critical-segment resampling** of fluent data also yielded gains over plain fluent data, recovering part of the deliberate demonstration's advantage (from 52.2% to 58.9%).
These results indicate that the benefit of targeted data collection or reweighting is not tied to a specific policy architecture.

#### 4.2. STAIR on a Controlled Architecture (Q2)

Experiments using the custom VLA base policy confirmed the data trends and demonstrated the efficacy of STAIR.
- **Data Trends Reconfirmed:** The deliberate baseline again outperformed the fluent baseline (50.0% to 64.4% overall), with larger gaps in higher-difficulty tasks. Resampling fluent data partially closed this gap (to 56.7%).
- **STAIR's Performance on Fluent Data:** A central finding is that STAIR, when trained *solely on fluent data*, achieved an overall success rate of 62.2%. This performance recovered most of the gain seen with deliberate demonstrations (64.4%) without requiring specialized data collection. For example, on middle-level tasks, STAIR on fluent data matched the deliberate baseline at 70.0% success.
- **Complementary Effects:** STAIR trained on deliberate data achieved the highest overall success rate (70.0%), indicating that the data-level and representation-level remedies are complementary.

A per-task breakdown further highlighted STAIR's impact, particularly on challenging tasks like "Insert Pen Cap," "Put Coin into Safe," and "Open the Lock," where multi-millimeter alignment under partial occlusion is required.

#### 4.3. Why Deliberate Demonstrations Outperform Resampling (Q3)

The research provides an explanation for why deliberate demonstrations consistently outperformed critical-segment resampling, even when both approaches provided a comparable "alignment budget" (e.g., 95 frames for deliberate vs. an effective 88 frames for resampling).
- **Coverage vs. Weight:** While resampling increased the effective weight of existing alignment frames, it did not expand the *diversity* of states covered within the alignment phase. Resampling inherited the narrow state coverage of the original fluent dataset.
- **Expanded Recovery-State Coverage:** Deliberate demonstrations, by contrast, explicitly included off-axis approaches and recovery motions, substantially broadening the coverage of corrective behaviors. This expanded coverage of recovery states, rather than merely an increased number of alignment frames, was identified as the primary reason for the superior performance of deliberate demonstrations. This finding suggests that a policy's success is limited by whether the data exposes the local correction process, which resampling cannot create.

#### 4.4. Generalization on SimplerEnv (Q4)

To assess the broader applicability of the dynamic feature beyond fine alignment, STAIR was evaluated on SimplerEnv, a general manipulation benchmark, against state-of-the-art VLA models.
- **Competitive Performance:** STAIR, trained on large-scale robot data (OXE mixture, then Bridge and Fractal) and operating with only current RGB observation at inference, showed competitive performance across nine tasks on Google Robot and WidowX Robot.
- **Broad Benefits:** The gains were observed across various general manipulation tasks, including grasping and final placement. This indicates that conditioning on a compact dynamic feature is beneficial for general manipulation tasks, not exclusively for the fine-alignment scenarios that motivated its development.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the field of robot imitation learning, particularly for tasks demanding precision and robustness.

**Primary Significance:**
- **Identification of a Critical Failure Mode:** The paper rigorously identifies and formalizes the counterintuitive problem where fluent, efficient expert demonstrations can be suboptimal for robot learning, especially in fine-grained manipulation. This "duration–sensitivity mismatch" highlights a fundamental tension between human efficiency and machine learnability.
- **Disentangling Data Effects:** By systematically comparing deliberate demonstration collection and critical-segment resampling, the work clarifies that improved performance from richer data stems not just from increasing the weight of critical frames, but more critically from expanding the *coverage* of corrective and recovery states. This distinction is vital for guiding future data collection strategies.
- **Novel Representation for Robustness:** The introduction of STAIR provides a new representation-level solution. By distilling short-horizon motion into compact dynamic features, STAIR enables policies to implicitly infer local dynamics, contact cues, and correction progress from ordinary fluent demonstrations. This allows policies to achieve significant performance gains without the costly and labor-intensive process of collecting deliberate, "pedagogical" demonstrations.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Improved Robot Autonomy in Complex Tasks:** The ability to learn robust alignment policies from readily available, albeit "fluent," demonstrations can accelerate the deployment of robots in real-world scenarios requiring precise manipulation, such as assembly, delicate object handling, and intricate human-robot interaction.
- **Guidance for Data Collection:** The findings will influence how demonstration datasets are designed and collected. Rather than simply prioritizing quantity or overall task success in human demonstrations, researchers and engineers may focus on methods that either explicitly expose recovery behaviors or that facilitate the extraction of dynamic cues from existing data. The concept of "pedagogical demonstrations" may become a more deliberate strategy.
- **Advancements in Representation Learning:** STAIR's success in distilling action-relevant dynamic information into a compact feature can inspire further research into developing representations that bridge between raw perceptual data and the nuanced requirements of robot control, particularly for sensitive phases of tasks.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The demonstrated competitiveness of STAIR on general manipulation benchmarks suggests that incorporating local dynamic features could be a broadly applicable strategy for improving the robustness and performance of VLA models across a wider range of robotic tasks, beyond just fine alignment.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point to future research directions. These include the deterministic prediction of dynamic features (limiting multimodal contact futures), reliance on visual dynamics without explicit geometric or tactile feedback, and the manual selection of key-frames and neighborhood sizes. Addressing these limitations, particularly learning task-conditioned temporal selection and integrating multi-modal sensory feedback, presents avenues for further enhancing robust alignment in robotics.
