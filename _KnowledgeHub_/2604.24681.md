# Learning Human-Intention Priors from Large-Scale Human Demonstrations for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and ByteDance developed a hierarchical framework for robotic manipulation that learns human-intention priors from a large-scale human video dataset, HA-2.2M. The approach improves robot control robustness and motion plausibility by factorizing manipulation into embodiment-agnostic spatial planning, latent human-intention modeling, and embodiment-specific robot action generation, achieving a 66.1% average success rate on SimplerEnv tasks, a 22.3% absolute improvement over the previous best baseline.

## Problem

- Robot learning is constrained by the expense and sparsity of robot demonstration data, which limits scale, diversity, and task coverage, and is tied to specific hardware.
- Leveraging abundant human videos for robot control is challenging due to complex, entangled observations and the absence of explicit action labels or robot-executable controls.
- Directly mapping human observations to robot actions can lead to entangled representations, causing valuable human-motion priors to be overwritten by robot-specific learning objectives.

## Method

- Curated **HA-2.2M**, a ==2.2-million-episode human demonstration dataset== with hand-centric spatial supervision and aligned action descriptions.
- Introduced **MoT-HRA**, a hierarchical VLA framework decomposing manipulation into ==embodiment-agnostic 3D trajectory prediction==, a ==latent human-intention model== (MANO-style hand kinematics), and an ==embodiment-specific robot action expert==.
- Implements ==knowledge insulation== via a ==read-only key-value transfer== to prevent downstream robot losses from corrupting upstream spatial and intention representations.

## Results

- **MoT-HRA** achieved superior hand motion generation: **0.136m ADE** and **34.16°** finger joint rotation error on Ego4D.
- Achieved **66.1% average success rate** on SimplerEnv, a **22.3 pp** absolute improvement over the next best baseline (**43.8%**).
- Ablations confirmed each hierarchical component (3D Trajectory, Intention Expert, Knowledge Insulation) incrementally improves performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hierarchically decomposing manipulation into spatial planning, human intention, and robot action effectively disentangles reusable human priors from embodiment-specific controls, preventing interference during training.
> - Large-scale human video data, when processed into hand-centric spatial and kinematic representations, offers a rich, embodiment-agnostic source for learning manipulation intent.
> - A mechanism for "knowledge insulation" is crucial for stable and effective transfer of human-intention priors, allowing robot policies to utilize these priors without destructive modification.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of generalist robotic manipulation is increasingly adopting Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, where a single policy uses visual observations and language instructions to produce executable robot actions. This paradigm has benefited from large pre-trained vision-language models and expanding robot datasets. However, the scalability of this approach is constrained by the inherent limitations of robot demonstration data. Robot trajectories are typically expensive to collect, specific to particular hardware embodiments, and often cover a narrow range of manipulation tasks.

In contrast, human videos offer an abundant and diverse source of object interaction data across various environments, viewpoints, and task styles. Leveraging these human videos to provide supervision for robot control represents a significant potential avenue for scaling up manipulation priors. However, directly using human videos poses challenges. Raw video clips contain entangled information, including scene understanding, human hand motion, camera movement, task progression, and embodiment-specific constraints. Many segments may show hands without purposeful manipulation, and useful interaction clips rarely include temporally aligned action labels or robot-executable controls.

Prior research in human-centric robot learning has begun to address these issues by extracting hand motion, aligning visual observations with physical space, or introducing latent bridges between human videos and robot actions. These efforts indicate that human videos contain transferable physical knowledge. This work builds upon these advancements by addressing a core representation problem: how to effectively separate the *where* an interaction should occur (spatial grounding), the *how* of human motion expressing task intent (human intention), and the *what* of specific robot execution (robot action). This paper positions itself within the explicit 3D reconstruction line of human demonstration learning but introduces a novel hierarchical decomposition to manage the transfer of knowledge from human to robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that can effectively learn human-intention priors from large-scale human demonstration videos and transfer this knowledge to robotic manipulation tasks. The authors aim to overcome the limitations of relying solely on expensive and sparse robot-only datasets by utilizing the rich information available in human videos.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in current robotic manipulation learning:
1. **Cost and Sparsity of Robot Data**: Collecting robot demonstrations is resource-intensive and yields data that is typically hardware-specific and limited in scope, covering only a small fraction of everyday manipulation tasks. This constrains the scalability and generalization capabilities of VLA models.
2. **Complexity of Human Video Data**: While human videos are plentiful, they are not direct robot demonstrations. Raw observations entangle multiple factors such as scene understanding, human hand motion, camera motion, and embodiment-specific actions. Simply treating them as noisy robot demonstrations can lead to suboptimal or brittle policies.
3. **Representation Problem**: A key issue is how to represent human behavior in a way that is beneficial for robot learning. A single, monolithic action representation that collapses scene, intention, and robot-specific execution can cause robot-specific learning losses to overwrite transferable human-motion priors. This can result in policies that are sensitive to changes in visual appearance, viewpoint, object instance, or robot embodiment.

The authors' key observation is that human demonstrations are most valuable when interpreted as structured evidence of manipulation *intent*, rather than merely as noisy substitutes for robot actions. They hypothesize that a human hand trajectory provides an embodiment-agnostic spatial scaffold, while articulated hand motion captures temporal coordination and contact-oriented motor preferences. The subsequent robot controls should then be learned as an embodiment-specific realization of this extracted prior.

This perspective leads to the core motivation for a hierarchical representation. Such a decomposition is intended to preserve the reusable aspects of human behavior while allowing the final policy to adapt to the kinematics and action conventions of a target robot. Specifically, the framework aims to:
- Ground the interaction in 3D space.
- Model human intention in a structured hand-motion space.
- Adapt this intention-aware representation to robot action chunks.

To achieve this, the paper sets two main contributions:
1. To curate a large-scale, high-quality human demonstration dataset, HA-2.2M, from heterogeneous human videos, providing reconstructed hand-centric spatial supervision and aligned action-language episodes.
2. To propose MoT-HRA, a hierarchical VLA framework that factorizes action generation into distinct experts (vision-language, intention, fine-grained action) with mechanisms for knowledge insulation, ensuring that human priors are leveraged without destructive interference during robot policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research comprises two main components: the creation of a large-scale human demonstration dataset called HA-2.2M and the development of a hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework named MoT-HRA.

#### 3.1. HA-2.2M Dataset Curation

HA-2.2M is a dataset containing 2.2 million short human-demonstration episodes reconstructed from a mixture of egocentric and third-person videos from various sources (e.g., Ego4D, EPIC-KITCHENS, Something-Something-V2). The curation pipeline involves three stages:

1. **Coarse Filter**:
 - Initial filtering is performed to identify hand-centric action segments and remove irrelevant content, such as idle gestures or camera motion unrelated to interaction.
 - This stage uses a recall-oriented cascade: first, Gemini labels likely hand-centric segments, followed by a V-JEPA-based classifier trained on these labels. This dual-stage approach aims to remove obvious noise while retaining a broad range of manipulation patterns across diverse viewpoints and environments.

2. **Perspective and Background Reconstruction**:
 - The retained video segments are processed to convert raw videos into image-aligned 3D trajectories and scene context.
 - **Hand Motion Reconstruction**: VitPose is used to localize hands in each frame, and HaMeR estimates absolute-scale MANO hand poses. Crop-to-image translation and scale are recovered to map MANO states back to the original frame, and trajectories are temporally smoothed to reduce jitter.
 - **Scene Context Reconstruction**: Depth Anything 3 predicts relative monocular depth for the background layout. This relative depth is aligned to the hand's absolute scale by fitting DA3 values on hand pixels to the MANO reconstruction, providing scaled scene geometry in a hand-consistent 3D frame.

3. **Fine Filter**:
 - This stage extracts atomic episodes and aligns them with language supervision.
 - A V-JEPA temporal segmentation model, trained on 38K manually annotated segments, predicts action boundaries to ensure clips correspond to coherent manipulation units.
 - To address potential over-segmentation, Gemini merges adjacent clips with continuous intent and generates concise action descriptions. These descriptions can include hand-specific tags for two-handed interactions. This final filtering aims to produce temporally coherent action-language episodes for the HA-2.2M dataset.

#### 3.2. MoT-HRA Framework

MoT-HRA is a hierarchical Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture designed to factorize robot control prediction. It decouples the learning process into an embodiment-agnostic spatial plan and a learned human-intention state. Given an observation image `o`, a language instruction `w`, and a chunk horizon `H`, the model approximates the action distribution `p_θ(a_1:H | o, w)` by sequentially sampling a 3D plan `τ_1:H`, an intention representation `h_int_1:H` (derived from MANO-style pose `m_1:H`), and finally the robot action `a_1:H`.

The architecture consists of a cross-task shared-attention trunk and three task-specific experts:

1. **Unified Architecture and Knowledge Insulation**:
 - The model uses a token sequence comprising visual tokens (`x_img`), text tokens (`x_txt`), and query/noise tokens for 3D trajectory (`z_3d`), MANO intention (`z_m`), and robot action (`z_a`).
 - The shared trunk builds multimodal context. Each expert updates only its dedicated token span.
 - **Read-Only Key-Value Transfer**: A crucial mechanism called "knowledge insulation" is employed, where later experts can read earlier hidden states through read-only key-value caches. This design prevents downstream losses (e.g., robot action loss) from overwriting upstream representations (e.g., spatial grounding or human intention), thereby preserving the learned human motion priors. The attention mask is designed hierarchically to support this, with different attention patterns for each token type.

2. **Vision-Language Expert**:
 - This expert is initialized from a pre-trained multimodal decoder.
 - It predicts an embodiment-agnostic, discretized 3D trajectory (`τ`) as a sequence of `H` waypoints (`τ_h = (b_x_h, b_y_h, b_z_h)`), each coordinate quantized into `B` bins.
 - The generation is autoregressive, predicting each coordinate based on the visual-linguistic context and previous waypoints.
 - Training uses a coordinate-wise cross-entropy loss, `L_3d`. This expert acts as a spatial bottleneck, grounding the interaction geometrically without tying it to a specific robot embodiment.

3. **Intention Expert**:
 - Conditioned on the shared multimodal context and the predicted 3D trajectory, this expert generates a MANO-style human hand pose sequence (`m_1:H`).
 - It uses conditional flow matching to denoise Gaussian noise into the target hand states. Each hand state `m_h` includes wrist translation/orientation and 15 finger joint rotations.
 - The training objective `L_mano` separates wrist and finger losses to prevent the lower-dimensional wrist signal from being dominated by the higher-dimensional joint term. MANO tokens use causal attention within their span. This expert transforms human videos into an interpretable intention space aligned with physical interaction.

4. **Fine Expert**:
 - This expert maps the intention-aware representation (`h_int_1:H`) to the final robot action chunk (`a_1:H`).
 - Similar to the intention expert, it uses conditional flow matching to denoise Gaussian noise into robot actions.
 - The conditioning variable `c_a` includes image-text features, 3D trajectory states, and the latent intention states `h_int`. Notably, the policy uses these latent states (produced during MANO denoising) rather than directly imitating decoded hand poses.
 - Action tokens attend to all preceding modalities and use bidirectional attention within their chunk, enabling joint refinement of temporally coupled controls suitable for chunked robot control.

5. **Joint Training Strategy**:
 - The three experts are trained jointly using a partially supervised multi-task objective `L = λ_3d L_3d + λ_m 1_m L_mano + λ_a 1_a L_act`.
 - Human-demonstration episodes from HA-2.2M primarily supervise the trajectory and intention experts.
 - Robot-manipulation episodes (e.g., from AgiBot-World) supervise the trajectory and fine experts.
 - When training on robot-only samples, the intention expert provides latent conditioning through the read-only interface but receives no MANO loss, and downstream action gradients are not propagated back into the intention cache. This allows the human-motion prior to remain stable while the fine expert adapts it to specific robot control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates MoT-HRA across hand motion generation, simulated manipulation, and real-world robot tasks.

#### 4.1. Hand Motion Generation

- **Setup**: MoT-HRA was compared against Being-H0 and VITRA on held-out egocentric Ego4D and third-person video clips. The evaluation included 200 clips per dataset, with five generations sampled per clip.
- **Metrics**: Wrist accuracy was measured using Average Displacement Error (ADE, meters) and Dynamic Time Warping distance (DTW, meters). Pose fidelity was assessed with wrist rotation error (Rot, degrees) and finger joint rotation error (Joint-Rot, degrees). Lower values indicate better performance.
- **Results**: MoT-HRA demonstrated improved performance across both egocentric and third-person datasets, achieving the lowest ADE, DTW, Rot, and Joint-Rot scores. For instance, on egocentric views, MoT-HRA achieved ADE of 0.136m, DTW of 0.127m, Rot of 28.95 degrees, and Joint-Rot of 34.16 degrees, which are improvements over the baselines. The results suggest that the learned intention prior enhances both global motion and local hand configuration. The generalizability to third-person views, despite a larger appearance gap, further supports the robustness of the prior. Qualitative analysis indicated that MoT-HRA produced smoother and more anatomically plausible motions compared to baselines, which often exhibited wrist jitter, inconsistent trajectories, or implausible finger poses.

#### 4.2. Robotic Manipulation

##### 4.2.1. SimplerEnv Benchmark

- **Setup**: MoT-HRA was evaluated on SimplerEnv-WidowX tasks, which involve variations in illumination, background texture, and camera viewpoint. The benchmark tests visually grounded control and transfer capabilities under distribution shifts.
- **Comparisons**: Baselines included RoboVLMs, OpenVLA-OFT, π0, π0-FAST, SpatialVLA, and ThinkACT.
- **Results**: MoT-HRA achieved the highest average success rate of 66.1% across the four tasks (Put Spoon on Towel, Put Carrot on Plate, Stack Green Block on Yellow Block, Put Eggplant in Yellow Basket), outperforming the next best baseline (ThinkACT) by over 22 percentage points. The gains were particularly notable on tasks requiring accurate spatial grounding and stable placement amid visual changes (e.g., Spoon: 78.1%, Carrot: 62.5%). This supports the hypothesis that the hierarchical intention representation contributes to preserving fine-grained control under distribution shifts.

##### 4.2.2. Real-World Experiments

- **Setup**: The framework was tested on real robots with both a parallel gripper and a dexterous hand, performing long-horizon "Clean" and "Pouring" tasks. The evaluation included out-of-distribution changes in object position, category, and appearance. Each method was post-trained with 150 task-specific trajectories.
- **Comparisons**: Compared against π0 and GigaBrain-0.
- **Results**: MoT-HRA demonstrated more reliable task completion across both robot embodiments and under realistic visual and object variations. The quantitative results, presented in Figure 5 of the paper, illustrate higher success rates for MoT-HRA in these real-world scenarios, suggesting the effectiveness of human-intention priors in complex, generalized robotic tasks.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study investigated the contribution of key architectural components: 3D trajectory prediction, the intention expert, and knowledge insulation.
- **Baseline (No 3D Trajectory, No Intention Expert, No Knowledge Insulation)**: This configuration, akin to a direct VLA architecture, yielded the lowest performance across all metrics (e.g., Ego4D ADE of 0.205m, SimplerEnv Average success rate of 48.4%).
- **Adding 3D Trajectory**: Including the 3D trajectory prediction branch improved both hand-motion metrics (Ego4D ADE to 0.182m) and SimplerEnv average success rate (to 52.1%). This indicates that explicit spatial grounding provides a useful scaffold for downstream control.
- **Adding Intention Expert**: Further introducing the intention expert led to a substantial gain, particularly in articulated pose accuracy and wrist trajectory coherence (Ego4D ADE to 0.140m, SimplerEnv Average to 62.7%). This highlights the importance of explicit human-intention modeling for generating plausible and coordinated human-like motions.
- **Adding Knowledge Insulation**: Incorporating knowledge insulation mechanisms further enhanced the full model's performance (Ego4D ADE to 0.136m, SimplerEnv Average to 66.1%). This suggests that isolating intention abstraction from policy learning mitigates negative interference during large-scale heterogeneous training.

The monotonic improvement observed with the incremental addition of each proposed component supports the claim that the performance gains are attributable to the hierarchical structure and specialized experts, rather than merely increased model capacity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation:

1. **Scalable Data Source for Robot Learning**: The introduction of HA-2.2M, a 2.2-million-episode action-language dataset reconstructed from heterogeneous human videos, represents a substantial step towards overcoming the data scarcity issue in robot learning. By providing temporally coherent episodes with reconstructed hand-centric spatial supervision, HA-2.2M offers a scalable and diverse source of manipulation priors that goes beyond the limitations of robot-only demonstrations. This could significantly reduce the cost and effort associated with acquiring training data for generalist robots.

2. **Robust and Generalizable Robot Policies**: MoT-HRA's hierarchical VLA framework, which explicitly separates spatial grounding, human-motion intention modeling, and embodiment-specific action generation, enhances the robustness and generalization capabilities of robotic policies. By treating human demonstrations as structured evidence of manipulation intent rather than direct robot actions, the framework allows for more effective transfer of human priors. Experiments demonstrated improved motion plausibility in hand motion generation and better control robustness under distribution shifts in simulated and real-world manipulation tasks. This suggests that robots trained with MoT-HRA could perform more reliably in varied and novel environments, adapting to unseen object instances or visual conditions.

3. **Improved Understanding and Interpretability of Policies**: The decomposition of manipulation into distinct stages (where, how, what) provides a more structured and potentially interpretable approach to robot control. The latent human-intention state, derived from MANO-style hand motion, offers an explicit bridge that captures human motor preferences and temporal coordination. This structured representation could facilitate debugging, analysis, and refinement of robot behaviors.

4. **Advancement in VLA Model Design**: The concept of "knowledge insulation" via read-only key-value transfer is a notable architectural innovation. It enables downstream control modules to leverage human priors without destructive interference to upstream spatial and intention representations. This approach contributes to the broader design principles for building complex, multi-modal VLA models that can integrate heterogeneous data sources effectively.

5. **Path Towards Open-World Robotic Manipulation**: By enabling human videos to supervise robot policies without requiring robot actions in every training clip, MoT-HRA paves the way for scaling robot policies to a much broader range of tasks and environments encountered in open-world scenarios. This capability is critical for developing truly general-purpose robotic agents.

**Limitations and Future Work**: The authors acknowledge that MoT-HRA is currently limited by the quality and coverage of automatically curated human demonstrations, which may contain noise or imperfect action-language alignment. The evaluations, while representative, do not cover highly dynamic interactions, multi-object long-horizon planning, or vastly different embodiments. Future work includes improving data verification, expanding embodiment coverage, and incorporating failure detection for more reliable deployment in open-world settings. These areas suggest opportunities for further research to refine and expand the impact of intention-aware hierarchical learning.
