# Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton: Learning Compliant Whole-body Policies with Real-time Torque Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> The Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton (UME), developed by Ant Group and Stanford University, provides real-time joint torque feedback and whole-arm configuration capture during human teleoperation, facilitating the learning of compliant, whole-body, and bimanual policies for robots. This system achieved autonomous task success rates of up to 95% on challenging, contact-rich manipulation tasks, substantially outperforming baselines lacking torque data.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with active compliant behaviors in contact-rich or constrained environments due to policies primarily trained on visual/linguistic data, often overlooking force/torque feedback.
- Current robotic data collection methods for teleoperation, such as ALOHA and GELLO, typically only capture end-effector poses and lack real-time joint torque data or comprehensive whole-arm configuration control.
- Teleoperation often lacks haptic feedback, making it less intuitive and safe for operators to regulate interaction forces, hindering the collection of high-quality data for learning compliant robot behaviors.

## Method

- A Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton (**UME**) was developed to capture human demonstrations with real-time joint torque signals and full whole-arm configurations.
- The UME features a low-cost, lightweight mechanical design using coaxial actuators and ==quasi-direct-drive motors== for transparent haptic feedback, built primarily from 3D-printed PLA plastic.
- An accompanying universal retargeting algorithm decouples kinematic chains for stable control across various robot degrees of freedom, and an integrated IMU enables portable mobile manipulation.

## Results

- UME-trained autonomous policies achieved high success rates on challenging tasks: **90%** for Box Pushing, **85%** for Box Flipping, **95%** for GPU Picking, and **95%** for Fridge Drink Retrieval.
- Ablation studies showed that `No-torque` and `UMI` baselines performed poorly or failed completely (e.g., **0%** success for Box Flipping and GPU Picking with UMI), underscoring the criticality of torque feedback and whole-arm data.
- The system successfully demonstrated universal retargeting by controlling bimanual 6-DoF X-ARMs in the real world and 7-DoF Franka arms in simulation, showcasing its cross-embodiment generality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Real-time haptic torque feedback significantly enhances human operator intuition and safety during teleoperation, improving data collection efficiency by 3.3 times for complex, force-mediated tasks.
> - Policies trained with joint torque data, alongside visual and proprioceptive information, can learn active compliant behaviors necessary for robust force regulation and successful execution of contact-rich manipulation.
> - Capturing comprehensive whole-arm configurations during demonstrations is crucial for robots to learn effective collision avoidance and compliant sliding behaviors in tightly constrained environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is currently experiencing a shift from rigid, high-precision industrial applications to collaborative and household environments. This transition necessitates robots that can safely interact with humans and adapt to dynamic, unstructured surroundings, often involving physical contact. Active compliance, the ability to sense and adapt to physical interactions, is therefore becoming increasingly critical.

Existing research in robotics, particularly concerning vision-language-action (VLA) models and world models, has largely focused on visual and linguistic data, often neglecting force and torque feedback. This oversight results in a significant limitation: robots trained with such data struggle to learn active compliant behaviors essential for contact-rich tasks. Current data collection methods, such as those employing systems like ALOHA and GELLO, primarily focus on end-effector control and do not typically expose joint torque data to the operator or the learning algorithm. This absence of real-time haptic torque feedback reduces teleoperation intuitiveness and safety, and prevents the robot from learning how to effectively regulate interaction forces. Furthermore, these systems often lack direct control over the robot's whole-arm configuration, which is critical for maneuvering in tightly constrained spaces.

Another paradigm, hand-held data collection (e.g., UMI-style grippers), provides implicit haptic feedback from direct physical interaction. However, these systems usually record only end-effector poses, relying on inverse kinematics (IK) for collision avoidance during inference. This approach can be insufficient for cluttered or highly constrained environments and may offer limited global scene observations.

The Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton (UME) addresses these identified gaps by introducing a system designed to explicitly capture real-time joint torque data and whole-arm configurations during teleoperation, while simultaneously providing haptic feedback to the human operator. By doing so, UME aims to enable the learning of robust, active compliant, whole-body, and bimanual policies, particularly for tasks requiring deliberate contact and operation in confined spaces, thus contributing to the development of safer and more adaptable robots for household and collaborative settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the capabilities of robots operating in human environments by enabling them to perform compliant, whole-body manipulation. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives, driven by the current limitations in robotic data collection and control paradigms.

The key objectives are:

1. **To develop an upper-limb exoskeleton capable of providing real-time haptic torque feedback to the human operator.** The motivation here stems from the observation that human operators can more intuitively and safely teleoperate robots when they can "feel" the physical resistance encountered by the robot. This feedback is particularly crucial in force-mediated tasks, where sensing environmental contact and reaction forces allows the operator to regulate interaction forces effectively and avoid unsafe conditions, such as inadvertently overdriving a robot arm.
2. **To design a system that captures comprehensive whole-arm configurations and joint torque signals during teleoperation.** Current data collection pipelines often lack the ability to record joint-level torque information, which is essential for training robots to learn active compliant behaviors. By capturing both detailed arm kinematics and the forces experienced at the joints, the system aims to provide the rich dataset necessary for policies that can implicitly encode motion planning and adapt to contact-rich scenarios.
3. **To ensure the teleoperation system is universal, low-cost, lightweight, and portable.** The researchers aimed to create a widely accessible tool. Portability, facilitated by features like an embedded Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), is motivated by the need to enable teleoperation for mobile manipulation tasks, expanding the robot's operational range beyond a fixed base. Universal retargeting is motivated by the desire to control various robotic arms with different degrees of freedom (DoFs) using a single exoskeleton design.
4. **To enable the learning of bimanual, whole-body, and active compliant policies for complex manipulation tasks.** The ultimate motivation is to develop autonomous robot policies that can robustly handle tasks characterized by high force mediation, tight space constraints, visual occlusion, and long horizons. Humans naturally utilize contact and whole-arm coordination in such scenarios (e.g., retrieving an object from a cluttered refrigerator). The recorded data and haptic feedback are intended to allow robots to learn these human strategies for "deliberately utilizing contact" rather than strictly avoiding it, enhancing their dexterity and adaptability in real-world environments.

These objectives collectively address the challenge of bringing robots safely and effectively into human-centric spaces by improving how they perceive, interact with, and learn from physical contact.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton (UME) is designed with specific mechanical and algorithmic choices to achieve real-time haptic torque feedback, universal retargeting, and portability for mobile manipulation.

**UME Mechanical Design:**
The exoskeleton is configured to mirror the human arm’s kinematic structure (3-1-3 DoFs for shoulder, elbow, and wrist, respectively), prioritizing user comfort and intuitive control.
- **Coaxial Actuator Configuration:** The shoulder (J1, J2, J3) and wrist (J5, J6, J7) joints feature coaxial actuator arrangements, ensuring their axes intersect at the human shoulder and wrist ball joint centers, respectively. This design allows for a large range of motion without structural interference, particularly for the wrist. The elbow (J4) is a single-DoF hinge joint.
- **Actuators:** To ensure transparent haptic torque feedback, UME employs quasi-direct-drive motors with low reduction ratios (e.g., Damiao DM-J4340-2EC for the shoulder, DM-J4310-2EC for the elbow and wrist). These motors offer a balance of torque output, low weight, and high torque transparency compared to higher-ratio geared motors.
- **Materials and Cost:** The structural links of UME are primarily fabricated from PLA plastic using 3D printing. This choice contributes to the system's low weight (approximately 12kg) and low total material cost (approximately $1900), making it more accessible.

**Universal Retargeting Algorithm:**
The retargeting framework is robot-agnostic and designed for stability and intuitiveness. It decouples the kinematic chain of both the exoskeleton and the follower robot into three sub-manipulators: a virtual 3-DoF spherical shoulder, a 1-DoF elbow, and a virtual 3-DoF spherical wrist.
- **Position and Velocity Feedforward:**
 - For the shoulder and wrist, exoskeleton joint positions are converted to rotation matrices via forward kinematics. The corresponding target joint values for the follower robot are then solved using inverse kinematics for its respective sub-manipulator.
 - The elbow joint position is directly mirrored from the exoskeleton to the robot.
 - For velocities, the virtual angular velocity of the exoskeleton's spherical joints is computed using its Jacobian. This velocity is then mapped to commanded joint velocities for the follower robot using the pseudo-inverse of the follower's corresponding Jacobian.
 - This decoupling strategy is intended to mitigate kinematic singularities typically encountered in 6D end-effector task-space control, thereby enhancing tracking stability and control predictability.
- **Haptic Torque Feedback:**
 - The measured joint torques from the follower robot's shoulder are used to calculate the spatial rotational moment via the transpose of its dynamically consistent Jacobian inverse.
 - This moment is then mapped back to the exoskeleton's shoulder feedback torque using the transpose of the exoskeleton's Jacobian.
 - Elbow torque is directly mapped.
 - To maximize transparency, the exoskeleton's whole-arm commanded torque incorporates compensation for Coriolis, centrifugal, gravity, and friction forces.

**Portable Mobile Manipulation Teleoperation:**
- **IMU for Mobile Base Control:** A low-cost Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) is integrated into UME. It detects the exoskeleton's orientation, which is used for gravity compensation and linearly mapped to control the mobile base velocity vector (translational and rotational).
- **In-House Mobile Manipulator:** The mobile robot platform used for experiments is an upgraded version of Tidybot++, a dual-arm mobile manipulator. It features improved compliance and odometry through a lower reduction ratio and reduced backlash in its powered caster modules, maintaining holonomic kinematics.

**Learning Autonomous Whole-Body Active Compliant Policies:**
- **Learning Algorithm:** The Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) algorithm is utilized for learning policies from human demonstrations.
- **Torque Integration:** While ACT typically uses image and robot joint proprioception, UME extends this by processing torque data in the same manner as joint positions. These torque embeddings are appended to the image embeddings (from a ResNet18 backbone) before being fed into the transformer network.
- **Policy Output:** The transformer outputs desired joint positions. These desired positions are designed to implicitly encode compliance information, which then determines the robotic arm’s output torque, allowing for active compliant behaviors.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology includes evaluating UME's capabilities on four distinct, challenging tasks: box pushing, box flipping, GPU picking, and fridge drink retrieval. These tasks are chosen to specifically test the system's ability to handle force-mediated interactions, operate in tightly constrained spaces, and perform long-horizon mobile manipulation. The performance of UME is compared against two baselines: `No-torque` (policy trained without torque data) and `UMI` (policy trained only on end-effector 6D poses with IK for whole-arm control).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate UME's capability in collecting data for learning autonomous, compliant, bimanual, and whole-body robot policies across a variety of challenging tasks.

**Autonomous Policy Performance (Table 1):**
UME consistently achieved high success rates across the four evaluation tasks:
- **Box Pushing:** 0.90 (18 out of 20 trials).
- **Box Flipping:** 0.85 (17 out of 20 trials).
- **GPU Picking:** 0.95 (19 out of 20 trials).
- **Fridge Drink Retrieval:** 0.95 (19 out of 20 trials).

**Ablation Studies with Baselines:**

- **Box Pushing (Highly Force-Mediated, Mobile Manipulation):**
 - The `No-torque` baseline achieved a success rate of 0.50, frequently failing to push the box completely to close all gaps. This indicates that without torque feedback, the policy cannot reliably determine when sufficient pressure has been applied or when to stop, often stopping prematurely.
 - The `UMI` baseline performed at 0.40, exhibiting similar issues with premature stopping due to lack of torque information, in addition to suffering from collisions with the environment.
 - These results highlight the importance of real-time haptic feedback during human demonstration and torque information for policy learning in tasks requiring force regulation against visually occluded obstacles.

- **Box Flipping (Highly Force-Mediated, Space-Constrained):**
 - Both `No-torque` and `UMI` baselines achieved a 0 success rate. The policies were unable to distinguish between insufficient and sufficient pressing force against the box, a critical distinction for initiating the flipping motion. Failures included attempting to flip without enough friction or losing grip midway.
 - This task demonstrates the necessity of torque sensing for force regulation in dynamic contact-rich manipulation.

- **GPU Picking (Tightly Space-Constrained, Force-Mediated, Mobile Manipulation):**
 - The `No-torque` baseline learned to straighten the arm but occasionally applied excessive force to the desktop, hindering motion or causing surface scratching.
 - The `UMI` baseline achieved a 0 success rate, failing entirely due to collisions. Policies trained on end-effector poses struggled to learn whole-arm collision avoidance in the extremely constrained space (e.g., ~5mm clearance), often bending the arm and causing collisions (Fig. 4(c)).
 - UME, leveraging whole-arm configuration and torque feedback, learned to compliantly slide along the desktop while regulating contact force, successfully completing the task. This underscores the importance of whole-arm configuration capture and torque-mediated compliance for operating in tight spaces.

- **Fridge Drink Retrieval (Long-Horizon, Bimanual, Mobile Manipulation, Force-Mediated):**
 - The `No-torque` baseline achieved an overall success rate of 0.90, but occasionally failed to apply sufficient torque to open the heavy fridge door or experienced collisions.
 - The `UMI` baseline achieved a 0 success rate. While it sometimes opened the fridge door, it consistently failed to grasp and lift the drink due to collisions of the wrist-mounted camera or gripper with the drink holder (Fig. 4(d)).
 - UME's policy demonstrated robustness to external disturbances (e.g., drink removal during transport) and consistently performed all stages of this long-horizon, bimanual mobile manipulation task by coordinating base movement, arm control, and force regulation.

**Data Collection Throughput (Fig. 5):**
A user study on the box flipping task showed that real-time haptic torque feedback improved data collection efficiency.
- UME achieved 20 demonstrations per minute (DPM).
- UME without torque feedback achieved 6 DPM, indicating a 3.3x improvement with haptic feedback.
- Human demonstration, as an upper bound, achieved 28 DPM, with UME operating at 71% of human speed.

**Universal Teleoperation:**
The universal retargeting algorithm was demonstrated by successfully teleoperating bimanual 6-DoF X-ARMs in the real world for tasks like block transfer and placement. It also successfully controlled 7-DoF Franka arms in a MuJoCo simulation, illustrating its cross-embodiment generality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton (UME) represents a notable contribution to robotic manipulation by addressing critical challenges in data collection for compliant robot learning. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

1. **Enabling Robust Compliant Robot Learning:** UME provides a novel pipeline for acquiring comprehensive human demonstration data, including real-time joint torque signals and whole-arm configurations. This directly addresses the current scarcity of force and torque data, which has hindered the development of truly compliant robot policies. The learned policies, as demonstrated by high success rates in force-mediated and constrained tasks, indicate a pathway towards robots that can interact with their environment in a more adaptive and human-like manner.
2. **Enhancing Teleoperation Intuitiveness and Safety:** The integration of real-time haptic torque feedback significantly improves the operator's experience during teleoperation. By allowing humans to feel the forces encountered by the robot, UME makes complex, contact-rich tasks more intuitive to perform and safer, as operators can react appropriately to environmental resistance. This leads to more efficient data collection and potentially safer remote operation in hazardous environments.
3. **Advancing Whole-Body and Mobile Manipulation:** UME's ability to capture and retarget whole-arm configurations, combined with its portability and embedded IMU for mobile base control, enables the learning of complex whole-body and bimanual mobile manipulation policies. This expands the scope of tasks robots can perform, particularly long-horizon tasks requiring coordinated movement of the base and multiple manipulators in dynamic environments.
4. **Promoting Research Accessibility:** The low-cost and lightweight design of UME makes advanced teleoperation and data collection capabilities more accessible to a broader research community. This democratization of hardware could accelerate the development and evaluation of new compliant control strategies and learning algorithms across various robotic platforms.
5. **Foundation for Future Robotic Systems:** The principles demonstrated by UME—particularly the emphasis on transparent haptic feedback, comprehensive data capture, and universal retargeting—lay a foundation for the next generation of general-purpose robots. Such robots will be required to operate safely and effectively alongside humans in complex, unstructured domestic and collaborative settings, where sensing and reacting to physical contact is paramount.

While the current prototype utilizes PLA plastic and has fixed link dimensions, future work can leverage this foundation to develop more robust, lighter, and ergonomically adjustable versions. The successful teleoperation in simulation for Franka arms also suggests broad applicability across different robot models, with real-world validation expected to further solidify its impact. UME's capabilities have the potential to significantly push the boundaries of compliant robotics, making robots more adaptable and useful in everyday life.
