# Latent Diffeomorphic Co-Design of End-Effectors for Deformable and Fragile Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KTH Royal Institute of Technology developed a co-design framework that jointly optimizes robotic end-effector morphology and control strategies for manipulating deformable and fragile objects. This approach, using latent diffeomorphic shape parameterization and a stress-aware bi-level optimization, demonstrated up to a 33% higher success rate and 36% reduced object stress in simulation, alongside effective zero-shot transfer to real robots.

## Problem

- Manipulating deformable and fragile objects is challenging due to high-dimensional states, complex non-linear contact dynamics, and diverse material properties.
- Existing methods optimize end-effector hardware design or control strategies in isolation, leading to suboptimal performance and limiting adaptability.
- Ensuring object integrity (preventing damage or breakage) demands extremely gentle and precise robotic control, which traditional approaches struggle to provide.

## Method

- A bi-level co-design framework jointly optimizes end-effector morphology using Latent Diffeomorphic Co-Design (LDM) and an adaptive control strategy.
- LDM represents shapes via diffeomorphic deformations parameterized by Radial Basis Functions (RBFs), then uses PCA to create a compact, low-dimensional latent space for optimization.
- A stress-aware control policy is optimized in simulation using privileged feedback, then distilled into a 3D diffusion policy for zero-shot real-world deployment on point cloud observations.

## Results

- In simulation, the co-designed system achieved a 33% higher success rate and 36% reduced induced stress for large jelly cube grasping compared to a parallel-jaw gripper.
- Optimized end-effectors showed specialized morphologies, such as concave surfaces for robust pushing and extended shapes for scooping, outperforming baseline designs.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer was successfully demonstrated on a real robot, achieving high success rates (e.g., 9/9 grasping, 10/12 pushing) with minimal object damage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Co-design, integrating hardware and control optimization, effectively addresses the unique challenges of deformable and fragile object manipulation, surpassing isolated approaches.
> - Latent Diffeomorphic Co-Design (LDM) provides an expressive yet tractable method for exploring complex end-effector geometries while ensuring physical validity.
> - Privileged simulation feedback, particularly internal stress signals, is crucial for optimizing object integrity, and can be effectively transferred to real-world reactive controllers via policy distillation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of deformable and fragile objects (DFOM) stands as a persistent and fundamental challenge in robotics, crucial for applications spanning food handling, surgical assistance, and caregiving. These tasks are inherently difficult due to the high-dimensional states and complex, nonlinear contact dynamics of deformable objects, their large variations in physical properties (stiffness, fracture thresholds), and the stringent requirement for precise, gentle control to preserve object integrity.

Traditionally, approaches to DFOM have evolved along two separate, complementary trajectories:
1. **End-effector Hardware Design:** Researchers develop specialized grippers (rigid, soft, or hybrid) tailored for fragile objects. While often superior to generic grippers, these designs are typically handcrafted by domain experts and rarely optimized directly against empirical task performance. Control strategies for these custom grippers often remain simplistic.
2. **Control Strategy Development:** This direction assumes fixed, standard grippers and focuses on advanced control techniques, often leveraging additional sensing modalities (e.g., tactile, force sensors, or vision-based inference) to achieve gentle interaction. However, these methods can introduce hardware complexity, calibration burdens, and cost, while also being limited by the inherent constraints of a non-optimized gripper morphology.

The paper argues that this separation between hardware design and control optimization inherently limits achievable performance. It posits that a more principled approach, **robot co-design**—the joint optimization of robot morphology and control—offers a powerful mechanism to exploit their mutual dependence. While co-design has shown significant promise in rigid-body tasks (e.g., optimizing legged robots or rigid grippers), it has remained largely unexplored for the unique complexities of deformable and fragile object manipulation. This gap is precisely where the current work situates itself.

This paper distinguishes itself within the co-design landscape by:
- **Novel Problem Domain:** Being the *first* co-design framework explicitly targeting the challenges of DFOM, where object integrity (stress, deformation) is a primary concern.
- **Expressive Design Space:** Introducing Latent Diffeomorphic Co-Design (LDM) as a novel shape parameterization. Unlike prior works that use limited explicit templates, voxel grids, or low-dimensional parametric spaces, LDM provides an expressive yet tractable way to explore a rich continuum of end-effector geometries. This addresses the common limitation of low-dimensional design spaces in many co-design studies (often below 10-15 dimensions).
- **Leveraging Privileged Information:** Unlike sensing-centric DFOM approaches that rely on physical sensors or perception-based force inference, this method leverages *privileged stress signals* directly available in modern soft-body simulators. This allows for direct optimization against explicit object integrity objectives, which is a powerful supervisory signal.
- **Efficient Bi-level Optimization:** Employing a bi-level optimization scheme (design optimization in the outer loop, control optimization in the inner loop) that is robust and efficient. This contrasts with end-to-end reinforcement learning approaches for co-design, which often suffer from severe sample inefficiency when dealing with high-dimensional design and action spaces and stochastic physics simulations.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Integrating a privileged-to-pointcloud policy distillation scheme, enabling zero-shot deployment on real robots without requiring real-world training or on-finger tactile/force sensors, thereby bridging the sim-to-real gap effectively.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art by proposing a principled, integrated solution to a long-standing robotics problem, moving beyond the traditional hardware-control dichotomy to unlock new levels of performance and robustness for delicate manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties and limitations of current approaches to Deformable and Fragile Object Manipulation (DFOM). Despite the critical need for robotic solutions in food handling, surgery, and caregiving, DFOM remains a grand challenge due to:
- **High-Dimensional and Complex Dynamics:** Deformable objects exhibit high-dimensional state spaces and non-linear, often unpredictable contact dynamics, making precise control challenging.
- **Material Variability:** Large variations in physical properties (stiffness, fracture thresholds, surface friction) across different objects and environments hinder the generalization of robotic solutions.
- **Object Integrity Requirements:** Manipulating fragile objects demands extremely gentle, precise, and reactive control to prevent damage or breakage, which is a primary constraint.

Existing methods, which tend to optimize end-effector hardware design or manipulation control strategies in isolation, are inherently suboptimal. A hand-designed gripper, however specialized, cannot fully adapt to diverse object properties or task contexts without a co-optimized control strategy. Conversely, an advanced controller, however sophisticated, is constrained by the fixed morphology of its end-effector, limiting its ability to fundamentally improve contact geometry and force distribution.

This fundamental limitation motivates the paper's core objective: to develop a **co-design framework that jointly optimizes end-effector morphology and manipulation control strategies** specifically for deformable and fragile objects. The authors draw inspiration from natural evolution, where neural and bodily adaptations are interconnected (e.g., the co-evolution of human hand morphology and motor control enabling dexterity), to argue that such an integrated approach can substantially enhance robotic manipulation capabilities.

The key objectives of the research are threefold, aimed at realizing this overarching goal:

1. **To develop a novel shape parameterization for end-effector geometry optimization:** This objective is addressed by introducing **Latent DiffeoMorphism (LDM)**, which aims to enable expressive yet physically consistent (topology-preserving, no self-penetration) end-effector designs, while maintaining a low-dimensional and tractable optimization interface suitable for gradient-free methods.
2. **To establish a stress-aware bi-level co-design pipeline:** This objective focuses on integrating the optimized morphology with a manipulation control strategy. The pipeline leverages privileged simulator feedback, particularly soft-body stress signals, to jointly optimize both morphology and control, ensuring that task success is achieved while explicitly minimizing contact-induced damage to fragile objects.
3. **To devise a robust sim-to-real transfer strategy:** This objective aims to bridge the gap between simulation-based optimization and real-world deployment. It involves distilling the knowledge from the privileged simulation policy into a reactive controller that operates on non-privileged, real-world sensor data (specifically, point clouds), enabling zero-shot deployment on physical robots.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a unified framework that can automatically discover optimal hardware designs and corresponding control strategies, thereby enabling safe, gentle, and effective manipulation of a wide range of deformable and fragile objects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for co-designing end-effectors for deformable and fragile object manipulation is built upon a bi-level optimization problem, integrating a novel shape parameterization, a design-conditioned control strategy, and a sim-to-real transfer mechanism.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation

The co-design problem is formulated as a bi-level optimization:
- **Upper-Level (Design Optimization):** Find the optimal end-effector design $d^\star$ that maximizes an expected stress-aware task objective $J_\xi(d, \pi^\star_d)$.
- **Lower-Level (Control Optimization):** For any given design $d$, find the optimal manipulation strategy $\pi^\star_d$ that maximizes the expected task objective $J_\xi(d, \pi)$.
The objective $J$ inherently trades off task success with object integrity (minimizing internal stress), and expectations are taken over randomized task contexts $\xi$.

#### 3.2. B. Latent Diffeomorphic Shape Parameterization (LDM)

To enable expressive yet tractable end-effector geometry optimization, the authors introduce LDM:
1. **Diffeomorphic Shape Parameterization:** End-effector shapes are represented as smooth, invertible deformations (diffeomorphisms) of a common base shape. This guarantees topology preservation and prevents self-penetration during deformation. A stationary velocity field (SVF) $v(x)$ smoothly transports points from the base mesh to form the target shape.
2. **Velocity Field Parameterization:** The SVF is parameterized using Radial Basis Functions (RBFs). Each RBF kernel contributes four scalar parameters, resulting in a high-dimensional "raw" design space (e.g., 72 dimensions for $K=18$ RBFs).
3. **Latent Space Construction via PCA:** To reduce dimensionality for efficient optimization, a dataset of `N` curated finger designs (manual designs from Thingi10K, augmented via rotation, translation, scaling) is created. For each design, the corresponding diffeomorphic deformation parameters are fitted. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is then applied to this dataset to derive a compact, low-dimensional latent space (e.g., $M_{latent}=15$ dimensions), capturing the essential variations of end-effector morphology.
4. **Runtime Decoding and Shape Synthesis:** During optimization, a latent vector $z$ is sampled from this low-dimensional space, mapped back to the raw deformation parameters via inverse PCA, and then used to deform the base shape, generating a physically valid candidate design.

#### 3.3. C. Sim-to-Real End-Effector Co-Design Pipeline

The framework operates in a bi-level manner, with control optimization nested within design optimization.

#### 3.3.1. Design-Conditioned Control Strategy (Lower-Level)

For a given end-effector design $d$, a motion-adaptive control strategy $\pi^\star_d$ is computed. This is achieved not through end-to-end reinforcement learning (due to its sample inefficiency for high-dimensional design spaces) but through a model-based approach:
- **Pre-contact Configuration Optimization:** First, an optimal pre-contact end-effector configuration $p^\star_d$ (position, orientation, gripper opening) is found by maximizing a geometry-aware objective function $f(p; d, \xi)$. This function balances penetration avoidance, proximity to the target object, and task-specific geometric alignment criteria (e.g., antipodal contact for grasping). This non-convex problem is solved using CMA-ES. A two-stage evaluation strategy is employed for efficiency: initial screening using rigid-body surrogates, followed by soft-body simulation for promising candidates.
- **Adaptive Motion Primitives:** After reaching $p^\star_d$, the robot executes a sequence of closed-loop Cartesian motion primitives (e.g., approach, grip, lift for grasping; approach, push for pushing; approach, insert, lift for scooping). These primitives adapt online to the task context and *privileged simulator signals*, such as object internal stress and centroid motion, to trigger phase transitions and ensure gentle, reactive interaction.

#### 3.3.2. End-Effector Design Optimization (Upper-Level)

Given the design-conditioned control strategy $\pi^\star_d$, the optimal end-effector design $d^\star$ is identified:
- **Design Evaluation Score:** For each candidate design $d$, its performance is evaluated by executing $\pi^\star_d$ in multiple parallel simulation environments with randomized contexts $\xi$. A design score $J_e(d)$ is computed as a weighted sum of task progress ($q_{prog}$), binary task success ($q_{succ}$), and penalties for *mean stress ($\bar{\sigma}$)* and *maximum stress ($\sigma_{max}$)* experienced by the object. Stress metrics are derived from percentile values over all soft-body vertices and aggregated temporally. Stress penalties are only applied if the task is successful to prevent degenerate "do nothing" solutions.
- **Derivative-Free Design Optimization:** Since the objective $J(d)$ is non-differentiable and stochastic (due to physics rollouts), CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) is used as the derivative-free optimizer. CMA-ES iteratively samples designs, evaluates their scores, and updates a multivariate Gaussian distribution to bias sampling toward high-performing regions.

#### 3.4. D. Sim-to-Real Transfer via Policy Distillation

To enable zero-shot real-world deployment without physical sensors:
1. **Demonstration Collection:** After optimizing $d^\star$ and its privileged control policy $\pi^\star_{d,prv}$ in simulation, a large dataset (L=100 expert trajectories per task) of control actions and synchronized non-privileged observations (segmented object point cloud, centroid, robot proprioception) is collected by rolling out $\pi^\star_{d,prv}$.
2. **Student Policy Training:** A student 3D diffusion policy $\pi^\star_{d,pcd}$ is trained via imitation learning on this collected dataset. Diffusion policies are chosen for their strong performance with limited data and robustness to multimodal action distributions.
3. **Real-World Deployment:** The distilled student policy $\pi^\star_{d,pcd}$ is then deployed directly on a real robot using RGB-D point cloud observations, preserving the gentle manipulation behaviors learned in simulation.

This comprehensive methodology integrates advanced shape representation, efficient bi-level optimization, and robust sim-to-real transfer, explicitly addressing the unique challenges of DFOM by optimizing both the physical morphology and the adaptive control strategies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed co-design framework through extensive simulation and real-world experiments on challenging food manipulation tasks: jelly grasping, jelly pushing, and fillet scooping.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiment Results

The proposed method ("Ours") was compared against baselines: a standard parallel-jaw gripper ("PJ"), Bayesian Optimization for design search ("BO"), and end-to-end Reinforcement Learning for co-design ("RL").

1. **Co-Design Performance Across Tasks (Addressing RQ2 & RQ3):**
 - **Jelly Grasping (Table I):** "Ours" consistently achieved the best overall performance (highest score and success rate, lowest stress) across cylindrical, "onigiri"-shaped, and large cubic jelly objects.
 - For a large jelly cube (exceeding original gripper width), "Ours" achieved a 33% higher success rate and reduced induced stress by 36% compared to the PJ gripper, which struggled with oversized, fragile objects. The optimized design (Fig. 5) showed larger contact surfaces and outward-extending structures to improve grasp stability and distribute forces more evenly.
 - BO was competitive but less effective than CMA-ES in exploring the high-dimensional latent design space.
 - RL performed poorly, exhibiting very low success rates and excessively high stress, attributed to sample inefficiency in high-dimensional joint design-control space and challenges with reward engineering (e.g., reward hacking if stress penalties are too strong).
 - **Jelly Pushing (Table II):** "Ours" achieved a 6.4% higher success rate and 6.6% reduction in induced stress on average compared to BO. The optimized end-effector resembled a golf club head (Fig. 7), with a concave contact surface that partially constrains the object, improving stability and robustness during planar pushing. RL was again suboptimal, though less severely degraded than in grasping.
 - **Fillet Scooping (Table III):** "Ours" yielded higher overall scores and success rates compared to BO. Visualization of the design space (Fig. 6) revealed that optimal scooping designs tended to be longer and thinner, enabling them to slide underneath the fillet without collision. In some cases, "Ours" induced slightly higher stress, suggesting deep insertion might compress the fillet against the table, an area for future fine-grained control improvements.

2. **Design Space Comparison (Addressing RQ1):**
 - Comparing LDM against simpler, primitive shape spaces (cubic and spherical) on the onigiri grasping task (Table IV), LDM consistently outperformed them in both task success and stress reduction. This demonstrates that LDM enables the exploration of physically meaningful and task-effective end-effector designs that adapt contact geometry, which primitive shapes cannot achieve.

#### 4.2. B. Real-World Experiment Results (Addressing RQ4)

Real-world experiments were conducted on a UFactory xArm 7 robot with 3D-printed co-designed end-effectors, using an Intel RealSense LiDAR camera for point cloud observations.

- **Successful Zero-Shot Transfer:** The distilled 3D diffusion policy, trained purely in simulation, successfully transferred to the real robot for both jelly grasping and pushing tasks (Table V, Fig. 7, 13).
- **Improved Performance:** Co-designed end-effectors demonstrated superior performance compared to baselines. For grasping, the optimized design featured a curved, concave inner contact surface, improving form closure, grasp stability, and reducing stress concentration. For pushing, the golf-club-shaped head improved directional control and robustness.
- **Reduced Damage:** In real-world jelly grasping, "Ours" achieved 9/9 successes with only 1/9 instances of visible damage, whereas BO achieved 5/9 successes with 1/9 damage. For pushing, "Ours" achieved 10/12 successes with 0/12 damage, outperforming BO (9/12 successes with 0/12 damage). This validated the framework's ability to preserve object integrity in real-world settings.

In summary, the main findings robustly demonstrate that the proposed latent diffeomorphic co-design framework, leveraging privileged simulation feedback and policy distillation, successfully optimizes end-effector morphology and control for deformable and fragile objects, leading to superior performance, reduced damage, and effective sim-to-real transfer across various manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in robotic manipulation, particularly for challenging real-world tasks involving deformable and fragile objects. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Pioneering Co-Design for DFOM:** The most immediate impact is establishing the *first* comprehensive co-design framework specifically tailored for deformable and fragile object manipulation. This addresses a critical gap in the robotics literature, moving beyond the traditional isolated optimization of hardware or control to an integrated, synergistic approach. This opens new avenues for research into how morphology and intelligence can co-evolve in robotic systems.

2. **Bridging the Gap Between Hardware and Software:** The work strongly validates the principle of "morphological computation," demonstrating that intelligently designed physical structures can inherently simplify control challenges. By jointly optimizing morphology, the system discovers end-effector geometries that intrinsically facilitate gentle interaction and robust task execution, thereby shifting complexity from software (complex feedback control) to hardware. This leads to more efficient, reliable, and interpretable robotic systems.

3. **Enhanced Performance and Object Integrity:** The results unequivocally show that co-designed end-effectors, coupled with their adaptive control strategies, significantly outperform traditional baselines in terms of task success rate and, crucially, in reducing contact-induced stress and damage to fragile objects. This is a paramount concern in sensitive applications like food handling, surgical robotics, and assistive care.

4. **Novel and Tractable Design Space Exploration:** The introduction of Latent Diffeomorphic Co-Design (LDM) offers a powerful and flexible method for exploring a rich continuum of end-effector geometries. Its ability to ensure physical validity (topology preservation, no self-penetration) while maintaining a low-dimensional, optimizable latent space is a key methodological contribution that will likely inspire future work in robot design optimization.

5. **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed policy distillation scheme, converting a privileged simulation policy into a non-privileged point cloud-based real-world controller, is highly impactful. It enables zero-shot deployment on physical robots, drastically reducing the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection and training. This accelerates the deployment of advanced robotic solutions in practical settings.

6. **Potential for Real-World Applications:** The demonstrated success in manipulating objects like jelly and fish fillets has direct implications for industries requiring delicate handling, such as automated food processing, packaging, and preparation. In medical contexts, co-designed surgical tools could minimize tissue damage, and in caregiving, robots could interact more gently with vulnerable individuals.

7. **Future Research Directions:** The paper also lays the groundwork for future advancements, such as extending the framework to multi-task robot co-design (e.g., a single end-effector capable of both scooping and cutting), improving material modeling to further reduce sim-to-real discrepancies, and developing online adaptation mechanisms for real-time adjustments to unknown object properties.

In conclusion, this research not only presents a robust and effective framework for a challenging problem in robotics but also offers fundamental insights into the power of co-design. Its contributions are significant for both academic research in intelligent robotics and for the practical development of next-generation, highly capable robotic systems that can safely and dexterously interact with our fragile world.
