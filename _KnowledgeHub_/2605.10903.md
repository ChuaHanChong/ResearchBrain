# CapVector: Learning Transferable Capability Vectors in Parametric Space for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce CapVector, a method to extract and transfer general capabilities from advanced finetuning procedures into pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, enabling subsequent standard finetuning to achieve comparable performance with reduced computational overhead. The approach demonstrated strong out-of-distribution transferability and improved real-world robot task success rates across various VLA architectures and environments.

## Problem

- Pre-trained VLA models often lack strong generalization on complex downstream tasks, with standard supervised finetuning (SFT) proving insufficient for rapid adaptation.
- Advanced finetuning methods using auxiliary training objectives improve VLA model performance and training efficiency but introduce significant computational overhead.
- There is a trade-off between the performance benefits of advanced finetuning and the computational efficiency desired for practical VLA model deployment.

## Method

- The CapVector method extracts 'capability vectors' by computing the parameter difference between VLA models finetuned with standard SFT and auxiliary-objective SFT on the same multi-task datasets.
- These capability vectors are then merged with the original pre-trained model parameters, forming a 'capability-enhanced meta model' that serves as an improved initialization.
- A lightweight orthogonal regularization loss is applied during subsequent standard SFT on downstream tasks to preserve the transferred capabilities and prevent their degradation.

## Results

- CapVector achieved comparable or superior success rates to computationally intensive auxiliary-objective methods (e.g., Spatial Forcing) on LIBERO tasks, often requiring fewer training steps.
- Capability vectors demonstrated robust out-of-distribution transferability, improving base model performance on new environments like RoboTwin 2.0 (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT average success rate increased from 6.7% to 31.8%).
- The approach showed effective sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment generalization, yielding substantial improvements in real-world robot task success rates on UR3, ARX Lift 2, and AgileX Cobot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - General capabilities acquired during auxiliary-objective finetuning can be decoupled from task-specific learning and represented as transferable 'capability vectors' in the model's parametric space.
> - The quality and generalizability of extracted capability vectors are significantly influenced by the visual diversity of the capability extraction datasets, with higher diversity leading to better transferability.
> - Avoiding shortcut learning from task-relevant visual cues in extraction datasets is crucial for inducing meaningful and robust capability vectors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a prominent paradigm in robotic foundation model research. These models are designed to interpret multimodal sensory input and translate it into executable robotic control, thereby demonstrating abilities in language following and visual generalization. The typical training process for VLAs involves two stages: a computationally intensive pre-training phase on large-scale robotic datasets to learn fundamental input-to-action mappings, followed by a finetuning process to adapt the model to specific task structures.

However, existing literature indicates that pre-trained VLA models often do not exhibit strong generalization capabilities on complex downstream tasks. Standard supervised finetuning (SFT) with limited demonstrations frequently proves insufficient for rapid adaptation and achieving substantial performance improvements over training from scratch. To address this limitation, various advanced finetuning methods have been developed that incorporate auxiliary training objectives. These methods, such as Spatial Forcing or LaRA-VLA, aim to enhance specific foundational capabilities (e.g., spatial perception, multimodal reasoning) while simultaneously fitting task-specific action distributions. While auxiliary-objective SFT can significantly reduce training steps for convergence and improve downstream performance, they typically introduce considerable computational overhead due to additional modules and forward passes required for the auxiliary losses.

This research by Song et al. addresses the trade-off between the performance benefits of auxiliary-objective SFT and the computational efficiency of standard SFT. It positions itself within the landscape of model merging and parameter space manipulation, drawing inspiration from concepts like task vectors in large language models. The work seeks to extract the beneficial properties gained from computationally expensive auxiliary finetuning and imbue them directly into the pre-trained model, thereby enabling subsequent standard SFT to achieve comparable performance and efficiency without the recurring overhead. This approach aims to advance the development of more generalist VLA models that are both performant and computationally tractable for diverse downstream applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central challenge addressed by this research is the difficulty of effectively leveraging pre-trained VLA models to improve performance and reduce adaptation costs during standard supervised finetuning (SFT). While advanced finetuning methods with auxiliary training objectives can enhance performance and accelerate convergence, they incur significant computational overhead due to additional losses and processing requirements.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Problem Identification:** Pre-trained VLA models often lack the expected generalization capabilities for complex downstream tasks. Standard SFT is frequently insufficient for rapid adaptation and superior performance compared to training from scratch. Auxiliary-objective SFT methods exist that enhance foundational capabilities (e.g., spatial perception, multimodal reasoning) and improve performance/efficiency, but they introduce considerable computational overhead.
- **Core Research Question:** Can the beneficial properties (general capabilities) induced by carefully designed auxiliary-objective finetuning procedures be transferred into the pre-trained model itself? If so, could subsequent finetuning then rely solely on standard SFT to inherit these benefits without incurring additional computational overhead?
- **Primary Objective: Decoupling and Transferring Capabilities:** The authors hypothesize that the two gains from auxiliary-objective SFT—enhancing general capabilities and fitting task-specific action distributions—can be decoupled within the parameter space. Their main objective is to define and introduce the concept of a "capability vector" which represents the general capability gain in terms of model parameters. They aim to develop a method to extract these capability vectors.
- **Secondary Objective: Creating a Capability-Enhanced Meta Model:** Following the extraction, the goal is to merge these capability vectors with the original pre-trained model parameters to form a "capability-enhanced meta model." This meta model is intended to inherently possess the improved general capabilities, making it a better initialization for any subsequent finetuning tasks.
- **Tertiary Objective: Maintaining Capabilities During Downstream Finetuning:** A further objective is to ensure that these transferred capabilities are retained during downstream finetuning using standard SFT. This involves proposing a lightweight regularization strategy to prevent the degradation or "forgetting" of the injected capabilities as the model adapts to new tasks.
- **Empirical Validation and Understanding:** The research also aims to systematically investigate and empirically validate several aspects:
 - The effectiveness and efficiency of CapVector across various tasks, environments, and VLA architectures.
 - The versatility of CapVector in transferring diverse capabilities (e.g., spatial perception from Spatial Forcing, multimodal reasoning from LaRA-VLA).
 - The task-irrelevance and out-of-distribution transferability of the extracted capability vectors.
 - The underlying mechanisms determining the quality of the extracted capability vectors, specifically exploring the influence of visual characteristics of the capability extraction datasets.
 - The practical applicability and generalization of CapVector in real-world scenarios, including sim-to-real transfer and performance across different robot embodiments.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire to achieve the performance and efficiency benefits of advanced finetuning methods for VLA models without the associated computational burden, by conceptually and practically separating general capability enhancements from task-specific learning in the parameter space.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed CapVector method operates in two distinct stages: an initial "before training" phase for capability vector transfer, followed by a "during training" phase that incorporates a regularization strategy.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The method starts with a pre-trained VLA model $\theta_{pt}$ and a multi-task dataset $D_{ext}$ (referred to as capability extraction tasks). These tasks are not primarily for downstream performance but are designed to induce specific model capabilities through parameter variations during finetuning. The objective is to derive capability vectors $\gamma_{ao}$ and use them to construct a meta model $\theta_{meta} = \theta_{pt} + \alpha\gamma_{ao}$ that outperforms $\theta_{pt}$ when finetuned on any downstream task dataset $D_{down}$.

#### 3.2. Before Training: Capability Vectors Transferring

This stage focuses on extracting the capability vectors.
1. **Standard SFT:** The pre-trained model $\theta_{pt}$ is finetuned on the capability extraction dataset $D_{ext}$ using standard SFT, yielding a finetuned model $\theta_{ft}$. The parameter difference from the pre-trained model is denoted as $\Delta_{ft}$, such that $\theta_{ft} = \theta_{pt} + \Delta_{ft}$.
2. **Auxiliary-Objective SFT:** Concurrently, the pre-trained model $\theta_{pt}$ is finetuned on the same $D_{ext}$ using an auxiliary-objective SFT method (e.g., Spatial Forcing, LaRA-VLA). This process results in model $\theta_{ao}$. The parameter difference for this model is $\Delta_{ao}$. The authors decompose $\Delta_{ao}$ into two components: $\delta_{ao}$ representing vectors for task-specific action learning, and $\gamma_{ao}$ representing the capability vectors acquired from the auxiliary objective. Thus, $\theta_{ao} = \theta_{pt} + \Delta_{ao} = \theta_{pt} + \delta_{ao} + \gamma_{ao}$.
3. **Capability Vector Extraction:** A core assumption of the method is that when the finetuning settings are consistent between standard SFT and auxiliary-objective SFT, the task-relevant vectors can be considered approximately equivalent, i.e., $\Delta_{ft} \approx \delta_{ao}$. Under this assumption, the capability vector $\gamma_{ao}$ can be extracted through simple parameter arithmetic: $\gamma_{ao} = \theta_{ao} - \theta_{ft}$. This subtraction effectively isolates the parameter changes primarily attributable to the auxiliary objective, which are interpreted as enhancements in general capabilities.
4. **Meta Model Formation:** The extracted capability vectors $\gamma_{ao}$ are then merged with the original pre-trained parameters $\theta_{pt}$ to create the capability-enhanced meta model $\theta_{meta} = \theta_{pt} + \alpha\gamma_{ao}$, where $\alpha$ is a trainable or predefined vector weight that controls the influence of the capability vectors. This $\theta_{meta}$ serves as an improved initialization for subsequent finetuning on any new downstream tasks.

#### 3.3. During Training: Regularization in Orthogonal Subspaces

After transferring capabilities to $\theta_{meta}$, the challenge is to prevent their degradation during standard SFT on new downstream tasks.
1. **Orthogonal Regularization:** The authors introduce a lightweight orthogonal regularization loss, $L_{orth}$, during the downstream finetuning of $\theta_{meta}$. This loss aims to maintain orthogonality between the capability vectors $\gamma_{ao}$ and the new parameter changes $\Delta'_{ft}$ that occur during finetuning on $D_{down}$ (where $\theta'_{ft} = \theta_{meta} + \Delta'_{ft}$). The rationale is that by keeping these vectors orthogonal, interference is prevented, preserving the pre-injected capabilities.
2. **Loss Definition:** The orthogonal regularization loss is defined as: $L_{orth}(\gamma_{ao}, \Delta'_{ft}) = \sum_p \sum_{i,j} |\gamma_{ao}(p)_{ij} \Delta'_{ft}(p)_{ij}|$, where $p$ denotes a parameter in the capability vectors and task vectors, and $(i,j)$ refers to elements within a matrix.
3. **Total Training Loss:** The overall training loss for downstream finetuning becomes $L = L_{action} + \lambda L_{orth}$, where $L_{action}$ is the standard action loss and $\lambda$ is a weight for the orthogonality loss. The authors state that this orthogonal loss introduces negligible computational overhead. For Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) tuning, $L_{orth}$ is computed only on matrix A, which represents the update direction.

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

The methodology is evaluated across various settings:
- **Simulated Environments:** LIBERO (a benchmark for lifelong robot learning with Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites) and RoboTwin 2.0 (a bimanual manipulation benchmark).
- **Base VLA Models:** OpenVLA-OFT (LoRA tuning), StarVLA (full tuning), and $\pi_{0.5}$ (full tuning), representing different architectures (autoregressive and flow-matching).
- **Auxiliary-objective SFT Methods:** Spatial Forcing (enhancing spatial perception) and LaRA-VLA (enhancing multimodal reasoning).
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success rates across multiple tasks and seeds.
- **Real-world Deployment:** Experiments on a UR3 industrial robot and external collaborators' platforms (ARX Lift 2 and AgileX Cobot) to assess sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings addressing five key research questions.

#### 4.1. RQ1: In-distribution Effectiveness

- **CapVector's Efficiency and Effectiveness:** Experiments on the LIBERO benchmark, using Spatial Forcing as the auxiliary objective, demonstrated that CapVector achieves comparable or superior success rates relative to Spatial Forcing across various training steps (5k, 1 Epoch, 8 Epochs, 150k Steps) and tasks (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long). This indicated that capability vectors implicitly represent the extra capabilities and that parameter merging successfully transfers them. CapVector also significantly outperformed OpenVLA-OFT (standard SFT) with only 5k training steps, suggesting it inherits the training efficiency of auxiliary-objective methods.
- **Criticality of Orthogonal Loss:** Without the orthogonal regularization loss, CapVector's performance, while initially strong, degraded over abundant training steps (e.g., 150k steps), failing to match Spatial Forcing. This suggests that pre-injected capabilities are updated and reduced during regular finetuning. With the orthogonal loss, this degradation was largely mitigated, leading to consistent performance improvements over the baseline without it, and often surpassing Spatial Forcing even after extensive training. An ablation study indicated optimal performance with an orthogonal regularization weight $\lambda = 1e-4$.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Out-of-distribution (OOD) Effectiveness & Generalization

- **Task-Irrelevance and OOD Transferability:** The capability vectors demonstrated out-of-distribution transferability. When capability vectors ($\gamma_{ao}$) were obtained from LIBERO and applied to RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, CapVector consistently outperformed base models (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT improved from 6.7% to 31.8% average success rate) and achieved performance comparable to Spatial Forcing, which was finetuned directly on RoboTwin 2.0. Similarly, OOD transfer was validated when capabilities extracted from RoboTwin 2.0 transferred to LIBERO-Long tasks. These results support the notion that capability vectors are task-agnostic and facilitate generalized model performance.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Versatility Study

- **Versatility Across Models and Auxiliary Objectives:** CapVector proved effective across different pre-trained VLA architectures (OpenVLA-OFT, StarVLA, $\pi_{0.5}$) and diverse auxiliary-objective SFT methods (Spatial Forcing, LaRA-VLA). It successfully extracted geometric comprehension from Spatial Forcing and multimodal chain-of-thought reasoning abilities from LaRA-VLA. For instance, with StarVLA as the backbone and LaRA-VLA as the auxiliary method, CapVector achieved a 97.1% average success rate on LIBERO, surpassing standard StarVLA (94.5%) and performing comparably to the full LaRA-VLA method (97.9%), all without LaRA-VLA's computational overhead.
- **Application to Flow-Matching Architectures:** For VLA models with flow-matching architectures (e.g., StarVLA, $\pi_{0.5}$), CapVector consistently improved success rates. Merging both Vision-Language Model (VLM) parameters and action expert parameters generally yielded better performance than merging only VLM parameters.

#### 4.4. RQ4: Determinants of Capability Vector Quality

- **Impact of Diverse Task-Irrelevant Visual Features:** Datasets with higher visual diversity, characterized by varied backgrounds and objects (e.g., RoboTwin 2.0 with randomized backgrounds), led to higher-quality capability vectors. When D_ext had randomized backgrounds compared to clean backgrounds, it resulted in significantly higher success rates on OOD tasks (LIBERO-Long). This suggests that richer visual diversity fosters more robust and generalizable spatial understanding capabilities that are effectively transferred.
- **Degradation from Task-Relevant Visual Cues and Shortcut Learning:** Datasets with high data disparity (heterogeneity across task-specific datasets, such as LIBERO-90) but similar data diversity (pairs per task) resulted in lower-quality capability vectors. For instance, on OOD RoboTwin tasks, CapVector using capabilities from LIBERO-Spatial (low disparity) performed better than from LIBERO-Long (medium disparity), which in turn performed better than from LIBERO-90 (high disparity). The authors hypothesize that task-specific backgrounds and objects in high-disparity datasets can induce spurious correlations, leading the model to prioritize simpler, high-variance patterns (shortcut learning) over generalizable features, thereby degrading the quality of the capability vectors. Avoiding shortcut learning is considered crucial for inducing meaningful capability vectors.

#### 4.5. RQ5: Real-world Performance & Practicality

- **Sim-to-Real Transferability:** Capability vectors extracted from simulated environments (LIBERO-Spatial) demonstrated effective generalization to the physical world. When these vectors were applied to real-world industrial tasks on a UR3 robot, CapVector yielded substantial improvements over standard baselines and, in some cases, even surpassed Spatial Forcing directly applied in the real world. This finding indicates that the spatial perception capabilities in the vectors are environment-agnostic.
- **Robustness and Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** CapVector demonstrated out-of-the-box generalization across different robot embodiments. Capability vectors (from LIBERO-Spatial) consistently improved performance on complex tasks for ARX Lift 2 (dual-arm system) and AgileX Cobot (teleoperation-ready dual-arm system), with notable improvement on long-horizon precise manipulation tasks (e.g., from 0.36 to 0.53 success rate for transferring test tubes). This suggests CapVector as a general strategy for improving pre-trained models and finetuning processes in diverse real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces "CapVector," a novel approach for enhancing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, with several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Novelty in Capability Transfer:** The work defines and proposes the concept of "capability vectors" as a representation of general capabilities acquired during finetuning with auxiliary objectives. The method of extracting these vectors through parameter arithmetic between models finetuned with different strategies, followed by merging them into a pre-trained model, represents a novel contribution to the field of VLA model adaptation.
- **Computational Efficiency and Practicality:** CapVector offers a method to achieve the enhanced performance and training efficiency typically associated with auxiliary-objective SFT, but with the simplicity and minimal computational overhead of standard SFT. This addresses a critical limitation of existing advanced finetuning techniques, which often incur significant additional computational costs. By separating capability acquisition from task-specific finetuning, it makes advanced VLA capabilities more accessible and cost-effective for practical deployment.
- **Enhanced Generalizability and Transferability:** The extensive experimental validation demonstrates that the extracted capability vectors are effective, versatile, and generalizable. They show strong performance across diverse VLA architectures, various auxiliary-objective methods, different simulated environments, and crucially, exhibit robust sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment generalization in real-world robotic tasks. This broad applicability suggests CapVector can be a foundational strategy for improving VLA models.
- **Insights into Model Learning Mechanisms:** The investigation into the determinants of capability vector quality, particularly the findings regarding data diversity, data disparity, and shortcut learning, provides valuable insights into how VLA models acquire and generalize capabilities. This understanding can guide future data collection strategies and finetuning methodologies to produce more robust and transferable model enhancements.
- **Facilitating Robot Learning Research:** By providing a more efficient pathway to imbue VLA models with critical capabilities like spatial perception and multimodal reasoning, CapVector can accelerate research and development in robotic learning. It offers a framework for transferring beneficial knowledge between models and tasks without needing to re-implement complex auxiliary training regimes repeatedly.

In conclusion, CapVector presents a promising strategy for developing more capable, efficient, and broadly applicable VLA models. Its ability to extract and transfer foundational capabilities addresses a key challenge in robotic foundation model development, potentially leading to more robust and adaptable robots in various real-world scenarios.
