# Solving Deep Reinforcement Learning Tasks with Evolution Strategies and Linear Policy Networks

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from Leiden University evaluated Evolution Strategies (ES) against gradient-based Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) using linear policy networks across various tasks. The work shows ES successfully optimize linear policies for many RL benchmarks, often outperforming DRL methods when both are restricted to linear architectures, and achieving performance comparable to deep DRL on select complex environments.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) often requires complex algorithms, extensive training durations, and large neural network architectures.
- Reproducibility of DRL results is a challenge, frequently influenced by random seeds and hyperparameter choices.
- There is a question whether the complexity commonly associated with current DRL solutions is always necessary for established benchmark tasks.

## Method

- Benchmarked three ==Evolution Strategies== (**CSA-ES**, **sep-CMA-ES**, **CMA-ES**) against prominent gradient-based DRL algorithms (**DQN**, **PPO**, **SAC**) and ==Augmented Random Search (ARS)==.
- Optimized ==policy networks== consisting of a single ==linear layer== for ES, ARS, and constrained linear DRL versions across classical control, Box2D, MuJoCo, and Atari environments.
- Employed game Random Access Memory (RAM) state as input for Atari tasks to evaluate ==linear policies== without complex visual processing.

## Results

- ES and ARS consistently found optimal linear policies for classical control tasks (e.g., CartPole, Acrobot), often outperforming deep DRL, which struggled with linear policies.
- For Atari games using RAM input, ES methods frequently achieved higher scores than linear DQN and, in some cases (e.g., Atlantis, Boxing, SpaceInvaders), matched or exceeded the performance of deep pixel-based DQN.
- Linear ES algorithms consistently outperformed their linear gradient-based counterparts across various MuJoCo tasks, with CMA-ES finding linear policies for Humanoid-v4 that were competitive with deep SAC.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evolution Strategies (ES) can effectively optimize linear policy networks, frequently outperforming gradient-based DRL when DRL is similarly constrained to linear architectures.
> - The complexity of deep neural networks commonly used in DRL might be unnecessary for many benchmark tasks, as simpler linear policies often suffice.
> - ES offer advantages such as algorithmic simplicity, inherent parallelizability, and a lower propensity to converge to local optima compared to gradient-based DRL methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated considerable success in developing agents capable of complex behaviors across various domains, including challenging tasks like Atari games and robotics. Despite these achievements, DRL methodologies often involve significant algorithmic complexity, extended training durations, and large neural network architectures. Furthermore, concerns regarding the reproducibility of DRL results, often linked to random seeds and hyperparameter choices, have been raised in the literature. This context sets the stage for exploring alternative optimization paradigms.

Evolution Strategies (ES), a family of black-box optimization algorithms within Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs), represent one such alternative. ES are employed for direct policy search through neuroevolution, optimizing neural network weights to learn control policies. Historically, ES have been applied to neural network optimization, though often considered less efficient than gradient-based methods for supervised learning. However, in reinforcement learning, the balance between exploration and exploitation inherent in gradient-based DRL can lead to prolonged training. ES offer advantages such as easier parallelization, scalability, and a lower propensity to converge to local optima due to their global search characteristics. They also impose fewer assumptions on the optimization problem structure.

Previous research has explored the efficacy of simpler policy representations, such as linear policies, in solving continuous control tasks, exemplified by Augmented Random Search (ARS). Studies have also indicated that by decoupling vision from control in environments like Atari, significantly smaller neural networks can achieve competitive performance. This current work extends these explorations by systematically benchmarking different ES variants against prominent gradient-based DRL methods, with a particular focus on the performance of linear policy networks. The research aims to evaluate whether the complexity commonly associated with DRL is always necessary for benchmark tasks and to identify scenarios where ES, especially with simpler architectures, might offer a competitive or superior alternative.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this study is to investigate the comparative performance of Evolution Strategies (ES) against gradient-based Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) methods in optimizing reinforcement learning policies. A central focus is placed on assessing the effectiveness of these algorithms when utilizing neural networks that consist of a single linear layer, thus acting as low-complexity controllers.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several observations within the contemporary DRL landscape:
- **Complexity and Training Demands of DRL:** While gradient-based DRL has achieved high-profile successes, it often necessitates complex algorithms, extensive training times, and deep neural network architectures with a large number of parameters. This complexity can hinder implementation, understanding, and tuning.
- **Reproducibility Concerns:** The reliability and reproducibility of DRL results have been identified as challenges, often influenced by factors such as random seeds and hyperparameter sensitivity.
- **Adequacy of Benchmarks:** Many DRL methods are benchmarked on widely used tasks from OpenAI Gym (e.g., MuJoCo, Atari games). The authors hypothesize that these benchmarks may not always demand the level of complexity inherent in current DRL solutions, suggesting that simpler, more parsimonious policies might suffice.
- **Exploration of ES as an Alternative:** Evolution Strategies offer a distinct approach to optimization. As black-box optimizers, they directly search the policy parameter space, are inherently parallelizable, and are less susceptible to local optima compared to local search gradient-based methods. They also do not require differentiability or specific structural assumptions about the environment.
- **Potential of Simpler Policy Representations:** Building on prior work that demonstrated the efficacy of simpler policy structures (e.g., linear policies in ARS or reduced networks for Atari when vision is separated), the study aims to determine if ES can effectively discover these simpler, yet potent, linear policies where gradient-based DRL methods might struggle.

Specifically, the study outlines several key contributions:
- **Demonstrating ES Effectiveness with Linear Policies:** To show that ES can successfully optimize linear policies for many RL benchmark tasks, contrasting this with the observation that gradient-based methods frequently require much larger networks for similar performance. This aims to reinforce the idea that many benchmarks may be simpler than commonly assumed.
- **Benchmarking Advanced ES Variants:** To introduce and evaluate three advanced ES algorithms (CSA-ES, sep-CMA-ES, CMA-ES) that have not been extensively explored in DRL benchmarks.
- **Comparative Performance against ARS:** To demonstrate that ES can achieve superior performance and sample efficiency compared to Augmented Random Search (ARS) across various tasks, highlighting their robustness in training linear policies.
- **Challenging the Notion of ES Limitations:** To present evidence that ES can effectively tackle higher-complexity MuJoCo tasks, sometimes outperforming gradient-based DRL methods, particularly with comparable network architectures. This further suggests that conventional DRL solutions might be unnecessarily complex for these benchmarks.
- **Highlighting Algorithmic Simplicity and Efficiency:** To emphasize that ES are algorithmically simpler, require smaller network architectures, and are easier to implement and tune, while still being competitive.
- **Investigating ES Self-Adaptation:** To explore whether complex self-adaptation techniques within ES (like full covariance matrix adaptation) are genuinely necessary for single-layer neuroevolution in these contexts.
- **Atari with RAM Input:** To specifically assess if ES can find successful linear policies for Atari games by directly utilizing the game's Random Access Memory (RAM) state as input, potentially outperforming Deep Q-Learning in this context.

These objectives collectively aim to re-evaluate the utility of ES and linear policy networks within the DRL landscape, advocating for potentially simpler, more efficient solutions for a range of tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study adopted a comparative experimental methodology, benchmarking three Evolution Strategies (ES) against three prominent gradient-based Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms and Augmented Random Search (ARS).

**A. Algorithms Compared:**
- **Gradient-Based DRL:** Deep Q-learning (DQN), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). Brief descriptions of their core update mechanisms (e.g., DQN's Q-value update with replay buffer, PPO's clipped objective, SAC's entropy-regularized objective with twin Q-networks) are provided.
- **Evolution Strategies (ES):**
 - **CSA-ES (Cumulative Step-size Adaptation Evolution Strategy):** Adapts only the global step size, resulting in isotropic mutations.
 - **sep-CMA-ES (Separable Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy):** Adapts the diagonal entries of the covariance matrix, allowing for arbitrary scaling parallel to coordinate axes.
 - **CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy):** Adapts the full covariance matrix, enabling arbitrary rotations of the mutation distribution.
 - All ES methods optimize neural network parameters (θ) for a policy function (π) by maximizing the cumulative return (G) over a fixed number of timesteps. They follow a generic ES algorithm involving sampling offspring from a normal distribution, evaluating fitness, selecting the best, and adapting the distribution's mean (m), step size (σ), and covariance matrix (C).
- **Augmented Random Search (ARS):**
 - A gradient-free method that directly optimizes policy parameters by sampling perturbations (δ) and estimating a gradient using a weighted average of observed cumulative rewards, scaled by their standard deviation.
 - Two versions were evaluated: ARS-V1 and ARS-V2 (which includes state normalization). Both use an enhancement of selecting top-performing directions.

**B. Network Architectures:**
- **Linear Policy Networks:** For all ES, ARS, and designated "linear" versions of DRL methods (DQN*, PPO*, SAC*), policies consisted of a single linear layer mapping observations directly to actions. Activation functions were `argmax` for discrete action spaces and `tanh` for continuous action spaces. This constitutes a fully connected shallow network without hidden layers. The number of trainable weights for these linear networks varied by environment (e.g., 8 for CartPole-v1, 6392 for Humanoid-v4, up to 2304 for Atari-v5).
- **Deep Policy Networks:** For the standard gradient-based DRL methods (DQN, PPO, SAC), network architectures based on their original studies or established libraries (CleanRL for DQN) were employed. These typically involve multiple hidden layers. ES and ARS were not used to train these deep architectures, serving instead as a benchmark for DRL's conventional usage.

**C. Experimental Setup:**
- **Environments:** Experiments were conducted across a diverse set of reinforcement learning environments from the Gymnasium API:
 - **Classic Control:** CartPole-v1, Acrobot-v1, Pendulum-v1.
 - **Box2D Simulations:** LunarLander-v2, BipedalWalker-v3.
 - **MuJoCo Simulated Robotics:** Swimmer-v4, Hopper-v4, HalfCheetah-v4, Walker2d-v4, Ant-v4, Humanoid-v4.
 - **Atari Learning Environment (ALE):** Atlantis-v5, BeamRider-v5, Boxing-v5, CrazyClimber-v5, Enduro-v5, Pong-v5, Qbert-v5, Seaquest-v5, SpaceInvaders-v5.
- **Trials and Reproducibility:** Five independent trial runs were performed for each algorithm-environment combination, each with a different random seed, to assess stability.
- **Action Space Compatibility:** DQN was used only for discrete action spaces. SAC was used only for continuous action spaces. PPO, ES, and ARS were applied to both discrete and continuous action spaces.
- **Training Timesteps:** Maximum training timesteps were set for each environment to ensure a fair comparison. For classic control, 5 · 10^5 timesteps (2 · 10^6 for BipedalWalker). For MuJoCo, timesteps ranged from 5 · 10^5 to 1 · 10^7. For Atari, ES were trained for 20,000 episodes (max 270,000 timesteps/episode), while DRL methods ran for 2 · 10^7 timesteps.
- **State Normalization:** For ES, a rolling mean and variance of observations were used to normalize state inputs to have zero mean and unit variance.
- **MuJoCo Reward Modification:** For ES, the standard reward function in MuJoCo locomotion tasks was modified. Instead of rewarding merely staying upright, the stability bonus was subtracted, and only forward locomotion was rewarded. This modification was adopted from previous ARS studies to prevent ES from getting stuck in local optima where agents remain stationary but upright.
- **Atari Input Processing:** For Atari games, ES agents were trained on the 128 bytes of Random Access Memory (RAM) state, rather than raw pixel input. This significantly reduced input dimensionality, enabling the use of linear policies and separating the control task from the computer vision aspect. Frame skipping was fixed to 4, and sticky actions were disabled.
- **Hyperparameters:** Specific hyperparameters for each algorithm and environment were either based on original studies, established libraries (CleanRL, Stable Baselines3), or empirically determined (e.g., initial step size for ES). Detailed hyperparameter tables are provided in the Appendix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the comparative performance of Evolution Strategies (ES) and gradient-based DRL methods, particularly with linear policy networks.

**A. Classical RL Environments:**
- For **CartPole-v1** and **Acrobot-v1**, ES methods and ARS consistently outperformed deep gradient-based DRL, often finding optimal linear policies in the initial iterations or with significantly fewer timesteps (e.g., a few thousand for ES vs. tens of thousands for DRL). Gradient-based DRL methods struggled to find effective *linear* policies for these tasks.
- In **LunarLander-v2**, ES required approximately 2 · 10^5 timesteps to solve the environment with a linear policy, while gradient-based methods failed to find a good linear policy within the allowed timesteps. Deep DRL eventually caught up but required more than 5 · 10^5 timesteps. ARS performed better than linear DRL but was less sample-efficient than ES.
- For **BipedalWalker-v3**, ES, SAC, and PPO found good policies, with DRL methods generally requiring fewer timesteps. ARS-V1 struggled, but ARS-V2 with state normalization showed improved performance.
- **Pendulum-v1** was challenging for most methods, with SAC being the only algorithm to approach an optimal reward within the given timesteps.

**B. MuJoCo Simulated Robotics:**
- For **Swimmer-v4**, ES policies were notably more effective at finding solutions compared to ARS and both linear and deep gradient-based methods. ARS-V2 struggled in this environment, while ARS-V1 performed better but slower than ES. Gradient-based DRL methods did not find good *linear* policies.
- In **HalfCheetah-v4**, SAC significantly outperformed all other methods, including ES, PPO, and ARS, with its deep architecture. However, ES still outperformed PPO and ARS. Critically, none of the gradient-based methods found effective *linear* policies for HalfCheetah and Swimmer.
- As network complexity (number of weights) increased in tasks like **Hopper-v4**, **Walker2d-v4**, and **Humanoid-v4**, deep gradient-based DRL methods (especially SAC) generally showed superior performance.
- Nevertheless, ES demonstrated the ability to find good linear policies for most MuJoCo environments. For **Ant-v4**, linear PPO surprisingly found an effective policy that surpassed its own deeper counterpart.
- For the most complex MuJoCo task, **Humanoid-v4**, CMA-ES found linear policies in several trials that were competitive with, or even slightly higher in episodic return than, those found by the best deep gradient-based method (SAC).
- A key observation was that linear ES algorithms consistently outperformed their linear gradient-based counterparts across various MuJoCo environments.

**C. Atari Learning Environment (RAM input):**
- When training agents using the controller's RAM state as observations, ES and ARS generally outperformed linear DQN for most Atari games.
- In **Atlantis-v5** and **Boxing-v5**, an ES method (sep-CMA for Atlantis, CMA-ES for Boxing) achieved the highest average scores across trials, surpassing human performance and the reported scores of pixel-based DQN. The best ES policy often matched or exceeded DQN's performance.
- For **SpaceInvaders-v5**, CMA-ES achieved an average score almost identical to deep, pixel-based DQN, despite using a linear policy with significantly fewer weights (768 vs. ~1.5 million). The best-found ES policy also reached 2635 compared to DQN's 1976.
- ARS-V1 achieved the highest scores in **Enduro-v5** and **BeamRider-v5**.
- In most other Atari games (e.g., CrazyClimber, Pong, Qbert, Seaquest), deep DQN (trained on RAM) still yielded higher scores than the RAM-trained ES, but the linear ES policies were often competitive.
- Gradient-based methods, when restricted to linear policies on RAM input, largely failed to learn effective policies for Atari, with the exception of CrazyClimber where linear DQN showed comparable performance to ES.

**D. Comparison with ARS:**
- ES generally achieved higher performance compared to ARS across most evaluated games, often converging more quickly to effective linear policies and demonstrating greater robustness.

**E. ES Adaptation Mechanisms:**
- The results suggested that for single-layer neuroevolution, advanced self-adaptation techniques that adapt the full covariance matrix (like in CMA-ES) were often not strictly required. Step-size adaptation (as in CSA-ES) appeared sufficient for many problems, indicating that relative scaling and rotation of search coordinates might be less critical for linear policies.

In summary, the study found that ES, particularly with linear policy networks, could effectively solve many RL benchmark tasks, often outperforming gradient-based DRL methods, especially when DRL was constrained to similarly simple linear architectures. For certain high-complexity tasks, deep DRL methods maintained an advantage, but even there, competitive linear policies could sometimes be found by ES.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this study carry several significant implications for the field of reinforcement learning and neural network optimization:

**A. Re-evaluation of DRL Complexity and Benchmarks:**
The research challenges the common assumption that deep and complex neural network architectures, coupled with intricate gradient-based DRL algorithms, are universally necessary for achieving high performance on established benchmarks. The demonstration that Evolution Strategies (ES) can find highly effective linear policies for many classic control, MuJoCo, and Atari tasks suggests that these benchmarks might be "easier" to solve than previously believed, or that current DRL solutions are often over-engineered. This could prompt a re-evaluation of benchmark design and the criteria for assessing algorithmic complexity.

**B. Validation of Simpler Policy Networks:**
The study provides strong evidence for the viability and effectiveness of linear policy networks (single-layer neural networks) across a broad spectrum of reinforcement learning tasks. This directly counters the prevailing trend towards increasingly deep and parameter-heavy models. Simpler policies offer advantages such as enhanced interpretability, reduced computational cost during inference, and lower energy consumption, which are crucial for real-world applications and resource-constrained environments.

**C. Advocating for Evolution Strategies in RL:**
The work positions ES as a competitive and often superior alternative to gradient-based DRL, particularly for training simpler policies. Key advantages highlighted include:
- **Algorithmic Simplicity:** ES algorithms are generally simpler to implement and understand compared to complex DRL methods (e.g., DQN, PPO, SAC), which often involve multiple interacting components (replay buffers, target networks, clipped objectives, entropy regularization).
- **Parallelizability:** ES are inherently and trivially parallelizable, which can drastically reduce wall-clock training time, even if they require more environmental interactions in terms of total timesteps for certain complex tasks.
- **Global Search Capability:** Unlike gradient-based methods that are essentially local search algorithms and can get trapped in local optima, ES perform a global search, which can be advantageous in multimodal or deceptive search landscapes. This explains their ability to find optimal linear policies that gradient-based methods, even with exploration heuristics, may miss.
- **Fewer Hyperparameters:** ES often require fewer hyperparameters, making them easier to tune.

**D. Implications for DRL Research:**
The observation that gradient-based DRL methods struggle to find optimal linear policies, even for simple tasks like CartPole, but excel with deeper architectures, suggests an intriguing research direction. It implies that the implicit inductive biases of deep networks or the specific dynamics of gradient descent may favor complex solutions over simpler ones, even when simpler solutions exist. This could lead to investigations into why deeper architectures are "easier" to train with gradients and how gradient-based methods could be adapted to effectively discover simple, robust policies.

**E. Energy Efficiency and Sustainability:**
As computational demands for DRL models continue to rise, the need for energy-efficient solutions becomes critical. The success of linear policies, particularly when optimized by ES, points towards a more sustainable approach to AI development. Smaller networks require less memory, computation, and thus less energy for both training and deployment.

**F. Future Research Directions:**
The study opens several avenues for future work, including:
- Exploring a wider array of classical ES variants and multimodal optimization strategies to consistently find these effective linear policies.
- Investigating the impact of step-size adaptation methods in ES when applied to networks with one or more hidden layers.
- Extending the application of linear networks and ES to even more complex Atari games or other domains by leveraging techniques like world models or autoencoders to reduce state dimensionality.

In conclusion, this research provides a significant counter-narrative to the prevailing DRL paradigm, demonstrating that effective and robust solutions can often be achieved with greater algorithmic simplicity and smaller policy networks, particularly when employing Evolution Strategies. This work advocates for a renewed focus on fundamental optimization principles and parsimonious model design in the pursuit of more efficient, interpretable, and reproducible reinforcement learning agents.
