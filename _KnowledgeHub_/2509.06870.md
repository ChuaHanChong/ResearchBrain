# The Majority is not always right: RL training for solution aggregation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at FAIR at Meta introduce AggLM, a method that fine-tunes a language model using Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to synthesize a single solution from multiple LLM-generated candidates. AggLM consistently surpasses majority voting and reward-model-based selection on math reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating improved accuracy, token efficiency, and generalization across diverse solution models.

## Problem

- Standard majority voting methods for LLM-generated solutions often fail when the correct answer is a minority view among candidates, leading to overlooked valuable insights.
- Existing model-based selection approaches (e.g., reward models) only pick the best candidate and cannot synthesize a novel, correct solution from partially accurate or complementary components.
- Fixed heuristics for aggregating solutions lack the adaptive reasoning required to intelligently reconcile and combine diverse outputs from LLMs.

## Method

- ==AggLM (Aggregation Language Model)== is trained using Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to take a problem and multiple candidate solutions as input, then output a single, synthesized solution.
- A carefully ==balanced training dataset== is constructed by mixing 'hard' examples (where the majority candidate is incorrect) with 'easy' examples (where it's correct), enabling AggLM to learn both selection and synthesis skills.
- AggLM is optimized using ==Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== with a binary reward signal for exact match to the ground-truth solution.

## Results

- AggLM-1.7B consistently outperforms majority voting and reward-model baselines by **3 to 7** percentage points on all four MathArena benchmarks.
- The method demonstrates strong generalization, maintaining top performance when aggregating solutions from a stronger Qwen3-8B model or from non-thinking mode solutions.
- AggLM achieves higher accuracy with superior token efficiency, using roughly **one-third** fewer tokens per inference compared to baselines relying on generating a larger number of samples for majority voting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aggregation can be learned as an explicit reasoning skill through RLVR, moving beyond fixed heuristics to intelligently review, reconcile, and synthesize information from multiple sources.
> - Balancing easy and hard examples in the training data is crucial for the aggregation model to effectively learn to recover minority-but-correct answers while also handling straightforward cases.
> - The learned aggregation skill generalizes robustly across different base models (weaker or stronger) and solution generation modes, indicating broad applicability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has witnessed a rapid surge in capabilities, particularly in their ability to tackle complex reasoning tasks such as mathematics, code generation, and scientific problem-solving. A central paradigm for improving LLM performance on these challenging tasks involves "scaling up test-time compute" – generating multiple independent solutions or intermediate thoughts and then selecting or aggregating among them. This strategy trades additional inference-time computation for higher accuracy, leading to state-of-the-art results in various domains.

Existing approaches predominantly fall into two categories:
1. **Rule-Based Voting**: Methods like Self-Consistency decoding (Wang et al., 2023) generate multiple chain-of-thought trajectories and select the most frequent answer (majority voting). While effective, this approach can fail when the correct answer is a minority view among the generated solutions, which happens when the model assigns low probability to the correct answer due to inherent modeling errors or biases.
2. **Model-Based Selection and Aggregation**: More advanced methods use a separate "reward model" or "verifier" to score candidate solutions, then select the top-scoring one (Liu et al., 2024). Some also prompt the LLM itself to compare and consolidate solutions (Chen et al., 2024). These approaches try to incorporate a learned notion of quality but can sometimes introduce new errors or lack the ability to synthesize novel correct solutions from partially correct components.

This paper, with its proposed **AggLM** method, directly addresses the limitations of these existing paradigms. It challenges the assumption that the majority is always right or that a simple reward model is sufficient for optimal solution aggregation. AggLM introduces a novel approach by treating aggregation itself as a **learned reasoning skill**, rather than a fixed heuristic or a selection problem. This positions the work at the forefront of research into making LLMs more sophisticated in their problem-solving and self-correction capabilities. It extends the idea of using LLMs for "meta-reasoning" – reasoning about the outputs of other reasoning processes – and uses reinforcement learning to fine-tune this meta-reasoning process for optimal performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that can more effectively aggregate multiple independently generated solutions from LLMs to produce a superior final answer, especially for challenging reasoning tasks. The authors identify several key motivations for pursuing this objective:

1. **Overcoming Limitations of Majority Voting**: Standard majority voting methods often fail when the correct solution is a "minority" among the generated candidates. LLMs can sometimes produce correct, albeit less frequent, answers, and simple voting schemes overlook these valuable insights, leading to error amplification instead of correction.
2. **Beyond Simple Selection**: Reward models are designed to *select* the best candidate from a set. However, individual candidate solutions, even if incorrect overall, might contain partially correct thought traces or valuable intermediate steps. Current methods do not fully exploit this "partial correctness" to synthesize a *new*, fully correct solution that might not have appeared verbatim in the original set.
3. **Treating Aggregation as a Learned Skill**: Instead of relying on predefined heuristics or simple ranking, the authors are motivated to teach an LLM to explicitly *reason* about multiple solutions. This involves reviewing, reconciling, correcting mistakes, filling gaps, and combining complementary ideas to synthesize a final, robust answer. This shift from a fixed aggregation strategy to a learned reasoning skill is central to their motivation.
4. **Enhancing Robustness and Generalization**: The goal is to create an aggregator that is robust to varying quality of input solutions (e.g., from weaker or stronger base models, or different generation modes) and can generalize effectively across different problems and solution distributions.
5. **Improving Token Efficiency**: Generating a very large number of solutions for robust majority voting can be computationally expensive. The authors aim for an aggregation method that achieves high accuracy with fewer tokens, thus offering a more cost-effective test-time scaling strategy.

In essence, the motivation is to unlock a higher level of meta-reasoning in LLMs, allowing them to intelligently "critique" and "synthesize" information from multiple sources, leading to a more reliable and accurate problem-solving system.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method is **AggLM (Aggregation Language Model)**, which learns to synthesize a final solution from a set of candidate solutions using **Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The approach distinguishes between two models:
- **Solution Model ($p_\theta(y|x)$)**: An off-the-shelf LLM that generates $m$ candidate solutions ($y_{1:m}$) for a given problem $x$. In main experiments, this model is fixed.
- **Aggregation Model ($p_\phi(\tilde{y}|x, y_{1:m})$)**: AggLM, which takes the problem $x$ and the set of $m$ candidate solutions $y_{1:m}$ as input, and outputs a single aggregated solution $\tilde{y}$. The parameters $\phi$ are trained.

#### 3.2. Training Data Construction

The training process leverages a collection of problems with ground-truth solutions, $D = \{(x, y^\star)\}$. For each problem, the solution model samples $s \cdot m$ solutions, which are then grouped into $s$ sets of $m$ solutions each. This creates an aggregation-training corpus $D' = \{x, y_{1:m}, y^\star\}_{s \cdot n}$.

A critical innovation is the **careful balancing of easy and hard training examples**. An example set $y_{1:m}$ is considered "hard" if the majority answer among the candidates is incorrect, and "easy" if it's correct. A purely easy dataset might under-train the model's ability to recover minority-but-correct answers, while a purely hard dataset makes rewards sparse. To address this, the final training mixture is constructed by including *all* hard examples and mixing in a specific percentage ($p\%$, empirically set to 50%) of easy examples. This ensures the model learns both to identify correct answers even when they are not popular and to synthesize solutions from diverse, partially correct inputs.

#### 3.3. Training Process

AggLM is optimized using **Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** (Shao et al., 2024). For each training example, AggLM generates $\tilde{y}$, and a binary reward $r(\tilde{y}) = 1$ is assigned if $\tilde{y}$ exactly matches the ground-truth solution $y^\star$, and $0$ otherwise. This verifiable reward signal directly trains AggLM to produce correct answers. The training aims to teach two complementary skills:
- **Selection**: Identifying and adopting a correct candidate if it already exists in the set.
- **Synthesis**: Detecting mistakes, filling gaps, and combining partial ideas across candidates to produce a new, correct solution that wasn't verbatim in the input.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Base Model**: AggLM-1.7B is initialized from Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al., 2025).
- **Training Data**: DeepScaler (Luo et al., 2025), a collection of approximately 40,000 math problems.
- **Solution Sampling**: 128 independent solutions sampled at temperature 1.5 from Qwen3-1.7B (thinking mode), divided into 16 sets of 8 solutions ($s=16, m=8$).
- **Prompting**: A specific prompt template (Figure 2 in the paper) instructs the LLM to review, reconcile, and synthesize a final solution from the provided attempts.
- **Evaluation**: Four math competition datasets from MathArena (Balunović et al., 2025): AIME24, AIME25, HMMT24, and HMMT25. A robust protocol involving 128 samples per problem (partitioned into 16 sets of 8) and averaging pass@1 across sets is used.
- **Baselines**: Majority voting (Self-Consistency), Best-of-N and Weighted Majority voting using state-of-the-art reward models (AceMath-7B and AceMath-72B), and Prompted Aggregation (Qwen3-1.7B without fine-tuning).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

AggLM consistently demonstrates superior performance and robustness across various evaluation scenarios.

#### 4.1. Performance on In-Distribution Solutions

When aggregating solutions from Qwen3-1.7B (the same solution model used for training data), AggLM-1.7B significantly outperforms all baselines across all four MathArena benchmarks (Table 1). It shows consistent gains of 3 to 7 percentage points over majority voting and the zero-shot prompted aggregation baseline. Reward-model selection (Best-of-N and Weighted Majority with AceMath-7B/72B) generally performs worse than standard majority voting, indicating that learned generative aggregation is more effective at surfacing correct minority solutions than simple frequency or quality scoring.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Stronger Models

AggLM-1.7B, despite being trained solely on solutions from Qwen3-1.7B, generalizes effectively to solutions generated by a stronger Qwen3-8B model (Table 2). It remains the top performer, consistently outperforming majority voting, reward-model selection, and prompted aggregation. This indicates that the learned aggregation skill is robust even when faced with higher-quality or different distributions of input solutions than those seen during training.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Non-Thinking Solutions

Similarly, AggLM-1.7B generalizes well to solutions from Qwen3-1.7B in non-thinking mode (Table 3), which typically produces lower-quality and noisier outputs. In this lower-signal regime, reward models show some improvement over majority voting, suggesting their utility increases when base model outputs are weak. However, AggLM-1.7B still achieves the best overall performance, highlighting its ability to synthesize and correct candidates rather than merely selecting among them.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies and Analysis

- **Scaling via Number of Solutions (k)**: AggLM-1.7B, trained at $k=8$, generalizes well to both smaller and larger $k$ (Figure 3), with performance improving as $k$ increases. Its performance curve rises more steeply than majority voting, demonstrating better scaling. Notably, AggLM-1.7B with 8 solutions often outperforms majority voting with 16 solutions, indicating superior token efficiency.
- **Effect of Majority Answer Size**: AggLM-1.7B's gains are most substantial when the majority answer size is small (Figure 4), meaning solutions are more diverse or uncertain, and correct answers are likely to be in minority modes. In cases where the majority answer is already frequent and correct, AggLM-1.7B performs on par with majority voting, confirming its ability to benefit from careful reasoning in challenging cases.
- **Ablating Data Mixtures (Easy%)**: Balancing easy and hard training examples is crucial (Table 4). Training on hard-only sets or using all available (largely easy) data yields suboptimal performance. An "easy%" of 5-50% (relative to hard examples) consistently enhances accuracy, demonstrating that the model needs to learn from both types of scenarios to effectively recover minority-correct answers and handle straightforward cases.
- **Ablating Number of Solution Sets (s)**: Increasing the number of solution sets ($s$) per problem for training offers only marginal improvements (Table 5). This suggests that while diversity in training combinations can help slightly, significant gains are not dependent on an extremely high $s$, allowing for a more economical training budget.
- **Aggregation vs. Extra Data**: The gains observed are attributed to the learned aggregation skill, not merely providing more training data (Table 6). Fine-tuning the solution model on the same dataset used for the aggregator yields small or negative improvements, remaining far below AggLM's performance. This confirms that explicitly learning to aggregate via RLVR and balanced data mixtures is the key.
- **Token Efficiency**: Aggregator models use significantly fewer tokens per inference (roughly one-third) compared to solution models generating full solutions (Table 7). This makes AggLM a more token-efficient strategy for achieving performance boosts than simply increasing the number of samples for majority voting.
- **Same Model for Solution & Aggregation**: Multitask training (where a single LLM learns both solution generation and aggregation) yields performance comparable to separate models (Table 6). This suggests that aggregation can be natively integrated into LLM post-training pipelines, enabling a single unified model to achieve these test-time scaling gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AggLM represents a significant advancement in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models. Its impact is multifaceted:

1. **Paradigm Shift in Aggregation**: By demonstrating that aggregation can be learned as an explicit reasoning skill through RLVR, this work shifts the paradigm from heuristic-based or simple selection methods to a more intelligent, adaptive approach. This opens new avenues for improving LLMs' meta-cognitive abilities.
2. **Increased Robustness and Accuracy**: AggLM's ability to recover correct answers even when they are in the minority, or to synthesize novel solutions from partially correct components, makes LLMs significantly more robust and accurate on challenging reasoning tasks. This is particularly valuable in domains like mathematics and code generation where precision is paramount.
3. **Enhanced Generalization**: The method's effectiveness across different base models (weaker, stronger) and generation modes (thinking, non-thinking) indicates a high degree of generalization. This means AggLM is not just a point solution but a broadly applicable technique for improving LLM outputs.
4. **Improved Efficiency**: Achieving higher accuracy with substantially fewer tokens than relying on a large number of samples for majority voting makes AggLM a more computationally and cost-efficient strategy for deploying high-performance LLMs. This addresses practical concerns about the inference costs of increasingly powerful models.
5. **Foundation for Future LLM Development**: The findings suggest that aggregation could be "natively" incorporated into the post-training or instruction-tuning phases of LLMs. This could lead to future LLMs being inherently better at self-correction and synthesizing complex solutions, potentially reducing the need for external aggregation mechanisms.
6. **Broader Applications**: While demonstrated on math problems, the principle of learning to review, reconcile, and synthesize from multiple outputs has potential applications across diverse domains where LLMs are used for complex tasks, including scientific discovery, legal analysis, and creative writing, where multiple drafts or perspectives might need to be consolidated.
7. **Guidance for RL Training**: The ablation study on balanced training mixtures provides crucial insights for RL-based fine-tuning of LLMs, highlighting the importance of strategically curating training data to maximize skill acquisition, especially for tasks requiring subtle discernment.

In conclusion, AggLM pushes the boundaries of how LLMs leverage test-time compute, moving towards a more intelligent and flexible aggregation mechanism. This work lays important groundwork for developing more reliable, efficient, and truly "reasoning-capable" AI systems.
