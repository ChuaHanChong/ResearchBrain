# Winoground: Probing Vision and Language Models for Visio-Linguistic Compositionality

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced Winoground, a meticulously hand-curated dataset and task designed to evaluate visio-linguistic compositional reasoning in vision and language models. The evaluation revealed that state-of-the-art models perform near random chance on this task (often below 20% group score), significantly lagging human performance (over 85% group score).

## Problem

- State-of-the-art vision and language models exhibit impressive performance but their genuine understanding of compositional reasoning remains poorly understood.
- It is unclear if these models are sensitive to subtle, semantically crucial word order differences in language when grounded in a visual context.
- Existing multimodal stress tests do not specifically isolate visio-linguistic compositionality by controlling for lexical content while varying word order.

## Method

- Introduced **Winoground**, a novel task and dataset featuring ==two images and two captions==, where captions share ==identical words but different order==, uniquely corresponding to their respective images.
- The dataset was meticulously ==hand-curated by expert annotators== to ensure high quality, challenging examples, and includes ==fine-grained linguistic and visual tags==.
- Evaluation metrics (==Text, Image, Group Scores==) were designed to rigorously assess model ability to match correct image-caption pairs and distinguish between semantically similar foils.

## Results

- All tested state-of-the-art V&L models performed poorly on Winoground, achieving group scores often below random chance (e.g., best CLIP **17.5%**, FLAVA **19.5%**) compared to humans at **85.5%**.
- Models struggled significantly with image retrieval (avg **~20%** image score) and complex linguistic structures, showing a negative correlation between performance and caption length/complexity.
- Models consistently failed on 'series' examples, where images are visually very similar, suggesting their visual encoders cannot discriminate subtle but crucial differences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current vision and language models severely lack visio-linguistic compositional reasoning, performing far below human levels even with vast pretraining data.
> - Models struggle with discriminating between visually similar images and with processing linguistically complex sentences, indicating a reliance on superficial cues.
> - The Winoground design effectively exposes that current metrics and training paradigms may not adequately foster true compositional understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the broader context of understanding the capabilities and limitations of large, pre-trained vision and language (V&L) transformer models. While these models have demonstrated impressive performance across a multitude of multimodal tasks, their underlying mechanisms and true understanding of complex phenomena, such as compositional reasoning, remain "poorly understood."

**Addressing a Core Challenge: Compositionality:** The central challenge addressed by Winoground is *visio-linguistic compositional reasoning*. This refers to a model's ability to interpret how the arrangement or relationships between elements in language (word order, syntax) affect the meaning in a visual context, and vice-versa. Humans effortlessly distinguish between "the tree is in the shopping cart" and "the shopping cart is in the tree," even though the constituent words are identical. The paper posits that current models might be remarkably insensitive to such critical word order differences, similar to observations in purely linguistic contexts.

**Inspiration from the Winograd Schema Challenge:** A key innovation of Winoground is its direct inspiration from the "Winograd Schema Challenge" (WSC) in NLP. The WSC presents two minimally differing sentences where a subtle change in wording dramatically alters the interpretation of an ambiguous pronoun, requiring commonsense reasoning. Winoground translates this concept to the multimodal domain: it presents two captions with *identical words* but different ordering, each uniquely corresponding to one of two images. This ingenious design specifically isolates the challenge of compositional understanding by controlling for lexical content and forcing models to rely on structural relationships.

**Distinguishing from Existing Stress Tests and Probing Tasks:** The paper thoroughly reviews existing "visio-linguistic stress tests" and "probing tasks," highlighting how Winoground fills a crucial gap:
- **Multimodal Stress Tests:** Previous works have explored understanding implausible scenes, exploiting priors, single word mismatches, hate speech detection, or distracting models with visual/textual similarity. However, none of these hold the *set of words constant* in the captions while varying their order. This key distinction prevents models from relying on simple bag-of-words representations or co-occurrence statistics, thereby forcing them to engage with compositional structure.
- **NLP Probing Tasks:** Research in NLP has shown that transformers can be surprisingly insensitive to word order, often performing well based on high-level word co-occurrence statistics. Winoground extends this inquiry to the multimodal space, questioning if this insensitivity carries over when grounding language in vision.

By providing a highly targeted, expertly-curated dataset, Winoground aims to serve as a critical "probing task" that can effectively expose fundamental limitations in V&L models' understanding of how language components combine to form meaning grounded in visual scenes. Its introduction marks a significant step towards developing more robust and truly "understanding" multimodal AI systems, moving beyond superficial pattern matching.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the Winoground paper stems from a recognized gap in the current understanding of sophisticated vision and language (V&L) models. Despite their impressive performance on a wide array of multimodal tasks, the extent to which these models possess genuine compositional reasoning abilities remains largely unknown and, as the paper argues, "poorly understood."

The core problem highlighted is the disparity between human and machine comprehension of subtle but semantically crucial differences in visio-linguistic expressions. For humans, sentences like "the tree is in the shopping cart" versus "the shopping cart is in the tree" evoke distinct visual representations, even though they share the exact same words. Prior work in NLP has shown that transformers can be remarkably insensitive to word order, suggesting a potential reliance on bag-of-words semantics rather than deep structural understanding. The authors question whether this insensitivity extends to the multimodal domain, where visual grounding should ideally enforce a stronger grasp of compositional meaning.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of the paper are:

1. **Introduce a Novel Task:** To propose Winoground, a new evaluation task specifically designed to measure visio-linguistic compositional reasoning. The task's unique design, featuring two images and two captions where the captions use an identical set of words but in a different order, is central to isolating this specific capability.
2. **Create a High-Quality Dataset:** To develop the Winoground dataset, meticulously hand-curated by expert annotators, ensuring the fidelity and challenging nature of the examples. This dataset is equipped with a rich set of fine-grained linguistic and visual tags to facilitate detailed analysis of model performance across different types of compositional phenomena.
3. **Probe State-of-the-Art Models:** To apply the Winoground task and dataset to a diverse range of leading V&L models (both transformer-based and RNN-based) to systematically evaluate their compositional reasoning capabilities.
4. **Uncover Model Shortcomings:** To reveal whether current state-of-the-art models truly understand visio-linguistic compositionality or merely exploit superficial cues. The explicit goal is to identify that these models "are not as skilled at visio-linguistic compositional reasoning as we might have hoped."
5. **Provide Insights for Future Work:** To perform extensive analysis of model failures, providing insights that can guide future research efforts toward mitigating these observed shortcomings and developing more robust and truly understanding multimodal AI systems.
6. **Establish a Useful Evaluation Set:** To establish Winoground as a valuable benchmark that can drive further progress and advance the state of the art in the field of vision and language modeling.

In essence, the motivation is to rigorously test a fundamental aspect of intelligence – the ability to derive meaning from how components combine – in multimodal AI, and to provide the community with a diagnostic tool to overcome existing limitations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers around the design and curation of the Winoground dataset and the subsequent rigorous evaluation of various V&L models using a set of carefully defined metrics.

#### 3.1. Winoground Dataset Construction

The core of the methodology is the **Winoground schema**. An example in the dataset consists of two image-caption pairs, (C0, I0) and (C1, I1), adhering to two crucial conditions:
1. **(C0, I0) and (C1, I1) are preferred by annotators over (C1, I0) and (C0, I1):** This ensures a clear ground truth matching.
2. **C0 and C1 have the same words and/or morphemes, but the order differs:** This is the defining characteristic, forcing models to attend to word order and compositional structure rather than just lexical content.

The dataset was **hand-curated by four expert annotators** with extensive experience in vision and language research and computational linguistics. This expert curation ensures high quality and intentionality in example design, prioritizing challenging and nuanced cases over sheer volume. Annotators used the Getty Images API to find suitable images and jointly created captions to fit the schema. The dataset comprises 400 examples, totaling 800 unique images and 800 unique captions, creating 1600 image-text pairs (correct and incorrect pairings).

A significant feature of Winoground is its **rich set of fine-grained tags**, designed to enable detailed analysis of model performance across different linguistic and visual phenomena:

- **Linguistic Tags (Swap-Dependent):** These categorize the type of word/phrase swap that differentiates the two captions:
 - **Object Swaps:** Reordering of noun phrases referring to objects (e.g., "a mug in some grass" vs. "some grass in a mug").
 - **Relation Swaps:** Reordering of verbs, adjectives, prepositions, adverbs that define relationships (e.g., "a person sits and a dog stands" vs. "a person stands and a dog sits").
 - **Both Swaps:** Involving changes in both relations and objects, or a single swap changing parts of speech (e.g., "it's a truck fire" vs. "it's a fire truck").
- **Linguistic Tags (Swap-Independent):** These characterize the overall complexity of the captions, irrespective of the specific swap:
 - **1 Main Predicate:** Simpler sentences.
 - **2 Main Predicates:** More complex sentences, often longer.
- **Visual Reasoning Tags (Non-mutually Exclusive):** These relate to the images and specific visual phenomena:
 - **Pragmatics:** Examples requiring non-literal interpretation or sensitivity to typical attachment preferences (e.g., idiomatic expressions).
 - **Symbolic:** Examples where symbolic depictions (like a child's drawing) must be understood.
 - **Series:** Images from the same photo series on Getty, implying high visual similarity (same people, background, lighting), posing a challenge for fine-grained visual discrimination.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Metrics

Performance on Winoground is measured using three distinct metrics, designed to assess different facets of visio-linguistic reasoning:

1. **Text Score:** Measures a model's ability to select the correct caption given an image. For a given example (C0, I0, C1, I1), it checks if the model scores s(C0, I0) > s(C1, I0) AND s(C1, I1) > s(C0, I1).
2. **Image Score:** Measures a model's ability to select the correct image given a caption. It checks if s(C0, I0) > s(C0, I1) AND s(C1, I1) > s(C1, I0).
3. **Group Score:** This is the most stringent metric, requiring both the text score and image score conditions to be met simultaneously for an example to be considered correct. Inspired by analysis of the Winograd Schema Challenge, it aims to prevent overestimation of performance by evaluating the twin sentences as a set.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

The evaluation included a diverse range of state-of-the-art V&L models:

- **Vision & Language Transformers:** CLIP, FLAVA, LXMERT, UniT, UNITER, ViLLA, VinVL, ViLT, VisualBERT, and ViLBERT. The paper details their varying architectures (single-stream vs. dual-stream encoders), image embedding techniques (Faster R-CNN region features, Vision Transformers), and pretraining objectives (masked language modeling, masked region modeling, image-text matching). Models were selected if they could produce an image-text similarity score.
- **Multimodal RNNs:** VSE++ and VSRN. These were included to determine if the observed limitations are specific to transformer architectures, and their hard negative-mining loss functions were noted as a potentially relevant design choice.

A **human baseline** was established using Amazon Mechanical Turk crowd workers. Unlike the expert annotators who curated the twin examples, crowd workers were shown one image and one caption at a time and asked a binary "Does the caption match the image?" question. This provided a conservative human performance benchmark for direct comparison with models. Rigorous quality control measures, including an onboarding test and approval rate requirements, were implemented for crowd worker participation.

This multi-faceted methodology, combining expert curation, targeted evaluation metrics, a diverse set of models, and a human baseline, ensures a comprehensive and insightful analysis of visio-linguistic compositional reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The results from the Winoground evaluation highlight a significant deficiency in the compositional reasoning abilities of current state-of-the-art vision and language models, performing dramatically worse than humans.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Compared to Humans (Table 3)

- **Striking Performance Gap:** All tested models struggle severely across all metrics, often performing close to or even below random chance. In stark contrast, human performance (MTurk crowdworkers) consistently remains very high.
- **Text Score:** The best-performing models (UNITER, ViLLA, VinVL, ViLT, FLAVA, CLIP) achieve text scores in the range of 30-38%, barely above the 25% random chance baseline. Humans score a remarkable 89.5%.
- **Image Score:** Models perform even worse on the image score, with the highest-performing model (FLAVA_ITM) reaching only 20.5%, significantly below the human score of 88.5%. This consistent pattern of lower image retrieval performance compared to caption retrieval for models is noted as an area requiring further investigation.
- **Group Score:** This most stringent metric reveals the models' profound limitations. All models perform *below random chance* (16.67%), indicating they frequently fail to correctly match *both* image-caption pairs within a Winoground example simultaneously. Humans, conversely, maintain an impressive 85.5% group score.

#### 4.2. Results by Tags (Tables 4 & 5)

The fine-grained tagging provides deeper insights into specific failure modes:

- **Linguistic Tags (Swap-Dependent):**
 - Humans excel across all linguistic swap types, performing best on object swaps (92.2% text score) and slightly lower on relation (89.27%) and 'both' (76.92%) categories.
 - Models show a reverse trend: they perform relatively "better" (though still poorly overall) on examples with simpler, shorter sentences, often involving morpheme-level swaps or part-of-speech changes (e.g., the 'both' tag, where CLIP achieves an 80.77% text score, comparable to humans). This suggests models might rely on simpler lexical differences or statistical patterns rather than deep compositional understanding. Conversely, models perform worse on complex relation swaps.
- **Linguistic Tags (Swap-Independent - Predicate Count):**
 - Humans perform better on examples with two main predicates (longer, more complex sentences) (95.37% text score) than one main predicate (87.33%).
 - Models show the opposite pattern, performing worse on examples with more main predicates, further indicating a struggle with linguistic complexity.
- **Visual Reasoning Tags:**
 - **Symbolic:** Both models and humans perform relatively well here, though models are still significantly worse than humans.
 - **Pragmatics:** Human performance is comparatively lower here (58.82% text score), which the authors attribute to the human evaluation task design (binary choice for single pairs) rather than a direct comparison of twin sentences, possibly missing subtle pragmatic preferences.
 - **Series:** Models perform worst on the 'series' tag, where images are visually very similar (from the same photo series). Many models achieve a 0% group score, suggesting they cannot discriminate between highly similar images and resort to arbitrary choices or unimodal priors.

#### 4.3. Discussion and Analysis of Performance Factors

The analysis section explores potential reasons for the observed performance differences:

- **Encoder Capabilities:** Models that perform marginally above random chance (primarily UNITER, ViLLA, VinVL, ViLT, FLAVA, CLIP) tend to have **richer image features** (e.g., large-scale pretraining, specialized object detectors, or Vision Transformer approaches). However, even these models fall far short of human performance.
- **Common Failure Modes:** The consistent failure on the 'series' tag indicates that V&L models' visual encoders might be **too weak to discriminate between subtle visual differences**, causing them to fall back on unimodal (text-only) priors and fail to make appropriate image-caption pairings.
- **Complicated Captions and Perplexity:**
 - There is a significant **negative correlation between caption length and model performance** for the best models, implying they struggle with longer, more complex sentences, despite humans performing *better* on such examples.
 - A weak negative correlation exists between model scores and **caption perplexity** (from GPT2), suggesting models perform slightly better on less perplexing (simpler) captions.
- **Architecture and Attention Type:** No clear performance advantage was observed for either single-stream or dual-stream architectures, nor for specific attention mechanisms like co-attention, when comparing models trained on similar scales of data. The strong performance of CLIP and FLAVA is attributed more to their significantly larger pretraining datasets than their specific architecture.
- **Multimodal Pretraining Dataset Size:** A **highly significant positive correlation** was found between the size of the multimodal pretraining dataset (excluding CLIP and FLAVA as outliers due to their extreme scale) and the models' text, image, and group scores. This suggests that data scale remains a crucial factor, though even massive datasets don't confer human-level compositional understanding on Winoground.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that current V&L models lack robust visio-linguistic compositional reasoning. They struggle with word order sensitivity, complex linguistic structures, and fine-grained visual discrimination, often defaulting to superficial cues or unimodal priors, especially when image differences are subtle.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Winoground holds significant implications for the field of vision and language modeling, acting as a critical diagnostic tool and a catalyst for future research.

**1. Unveiling a Fundamental Shortcoming:**
The most profound significance of Winoground is its unequivocal demonstration that state-of-the-art vision and language models severely lack visio-linguistic compositional reasoning. Despite their impressive performance on many tasks, models consistently perform at or below random chance on Winoground, revealing a crucial blind spot in their "understanding." This highlights that current metrics and training paradigms might not adequately capture or foster true compositional understanding, instead rewarding models that exploit superficial correlations or statistical patterns. The paper effectively punctures the illusion of deeper comprehension, forcing the community to confront this limitation.

**2. Providing a Targeted and Rigorous Benchmark:**
Winoground provides the first dataset and task specifically designed to isolate and evaluate visio-linguistic compositionality by keeping word content constant while manipulating word order. This unique design distinguishes it from other stress tests and benchmarks, which often vary lexical items or focus on other types of reasoning. As a carefully hand-curated dataset by expert annotators, it offers high-quality, nuanced examples that truly test the models' ability to parse and ground structural meaning. It is poised to become a standard, go-to benchmark for researchers aiming to improve compositional reasoning in multimodal AI.

**3. Guiding Future Research Directions:**
The detailed analysis of model performance across various linguistic and visual tags, as well as architectural considerations, provides concrete insights for mitigating current shortcomings:
- **Enhanced Image Encoding:** The consistent failure on visually similar "series" examples suggests a need for more robust visual encoders capable of discerning subtle differences crucial for compositional meaning.
- **Improved Linguistic Processing and Fusion:** Models struggle with longer, more complex captions and relation-based swaps. This points to a need for V&L architectures that are more sensitive to fine-grained linguistic structure, semantic roles (e.g., agent vs. patient), and how these roles interact with visual elements.
- **Better Pretraining Objectives:** The paper notes that current pretraining objectives may not sufficiently emphasize "similar but wrong" image-text pairs. Future work could explore loss functions or training strategies that explicitly push models to distinguish between closely related, yet semantically distinct, multimodal inputs (e.g., sophisticated hard negative mining).
- **Data Scale and Quality:** While massive datasets like CLIP's show some correlation with improved performance, Winoground demonstrates that sheer scale alone is not a panacea for compositional understanding. This suggests a need for not just more data, but also data with richer compositional annotations or derived through processes that implicitly encourage such learning.
- **Rethinking Architectures:** The analysis did not find a clear architectural winner (single-stream vs. dual-stream), implying that fundamental understanding issues may transcend current architectural choices, prompting a need for more innovative designs specifically tailored for compositional reasoning.

**4. Fostering More Robust and Trustworthy AI:**
Understanding compositional meaning is fundamental to commonsense reasoning and robust AI. Models that cannot differentiate "a dog biting a person" from "a person biting a dog" are prone to critical failures in real-world applications like autonomous systems, content moderation, or human-AI interaction. By exposing these limitations, Winoground contributes to the development of more reliable, interpretable, and truly intelligent multimodal AI systems.

**5. Ethical Considerations in Dataset Development:**
The paper's discussion of ethical considerations, particularly regarding gender-underspecified terms and efforts to encourage diverse representations in images, sets a good example for responsible AI research. While acknowledging limitations (English-only, expert curation size limits), it emphasizes the importance of thoughtful annotation practices in multimodal datasets to avoid perpetuating biases.

In conclusion, Winoground is a significant contribution that redefines the bar for evaluating visio-linguistic intelligence in AI models. It forces the community to move beyond superficial performance metrics towards a deeper understanding of what these models truly comprehend, paving the way for the next generation of more capable and reliable multimodal AI.
