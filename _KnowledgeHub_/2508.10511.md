# KDPE: A Kernel Density Estimation Strategy for Diffusion Policy Trajectory Selection

> [!summary] Summary
> The KDPE method from Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia and the University of Genoa improves Diffusion Policy (DP) by statistically filtering generated trajectories at inference time using Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) with a manifold-aware kernel, selecting the most probable action. This approach yielded higher success rates on various simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks while maintaining low computational overhead.

## Problem

- Diffusion Policy (DP)'s stochastic denoising process can generate a diverse set of action trajectories, some of which may be suboptimal or outside the distribution of the training data.
- As a supervised learning method, DP is susceptible to learning and reproducing outlier behaviors present in demonstration datasets, potentially leading to inefficient or unsafe robot actions.
- Existing DP implementations lack a robust, statistically grounded mechanism to select the most representative or optimal trajectory from multiple generated options during inference.

## Method

- **KDPE** generates a population of N independent action trajectories from ==Diffusion Policy== for a given observation, each initialized with different random noise.
- Apply ==Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)== to the 'last actions' of these generated trajectories, utilizing a specialized ==manifold-aware kernel== designed for mixed-manifold robot actions (Euclidean for position/gripper, SO(3) for orientation).
- Select the trajectory whose 'last action' exhibits the highest estimated probability density within the sampled population, thereby filtering out less probable or outlier behaviors.

## Results

- **KDPE** improved the average success rate of CNN-based Diffusion Policy by **3.3%** and Transformer-based Diffusion Policy by **1.9%** across various simulated tasks from RoboMimic and MimicGen benchmarks.
- On real-world robot manipulation tasks, KDPE consistently achieved higher success rates (e.g., **96%** vs. **90%** for PickPlush, **70%** vs. **60%** for CoffeeMaking) and resulted in qualitatively smoother robot movements compared to baseline DP.
- The method introduces a low computational overhead, adding approximately **16ms** to the inference time for generating and selecting from 100 trajectories, enabling real-time closed-loop robot control at practical frequencies (approx. **10.75 Hz**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Statistically filtering Diffusion Policy's stochastic outputs at inference time using density estimation can significantly enhance policy robustness and performance without requiring additional training.
> - Accurate density estimation for robot actions requires a manifold-aware kernel that appropriately handles Euclidean (position, gripper) and non-Euclidean (orientation on SO(3)) components.
> - Focusing density estimation on the 'last action' of short-horizon trajectories provides an effective and computationally efficient way to identify representative behaviors, circumventing the curse of dimensionality faced by full trajectory density estimation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a challenge in robot policy learning, specifically within the domain of behavior cloning (BC). Behavior cloning aims to enable robots to learn tasks by imitating human demonstrations. A long-standing difficulty in this field has been the effective capture and reproduction of multimodal behaviors present in training data, where a single observation might correspond to multiple valid actions or trajectories.

Recent advancements in generative models, particularly Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), have offered a promising direction for behavior cloning. Diffusion Policy (DP) has emerged as a state-of-the-art approach that leverages diffusion models to generate robot action trajectories. DP's ability to model conditional action distributions allows it to represent the multimodality inherent in complex manipulation tasks.

However, the authors identify two primary limitations of Diffusion Policy that can impact its reliability during robot execution. First, the stochastic nature of the denoising process during inference can lead to the generation of a diverse set of trajectories, some of which may be suboptimal, erratic, or lie outside the distribution of the original training data. Second, as a supervised learning method, DP is susceptible to learning and reproducing outliers present in the demonstration dataset, potentially leading the robot into undesirable or unsafe states.

Existing research has explored various strategies to mitigate these issues. Some approaches focus on scaling DP with large datasets or combining it with classical behavior cloning algorithms like DAgger to reduce compounding errors. Other works, such as Sentinel and RoboMD, concentrate on failure detection and mitigation, often identifying issues after the robot has entered an out-of-distribution state. V-GPS attempts to improve policies at inference time by using a value function, which necessitates additional reinforcement learning training and reward function design. In broader machine learning contexts, particularly in Natural Language Processing and text-to-image generation, "best-of-N" sampling or inference-time scaling methods have been investigated to select optimal outputs from multiple generated samples.

KDPE distinguishes itself within this landscape by proposing an inference-time solution that statistically filters the trajectories generated by Diffusion Policy. Unlike methods that require additional training, detect failures reactively, or rely on external evaluators, KDPE proactively selects trajectories based on their estimated probability density within the learned distribution. This statistical approach, applied at test time with low computational overhead, aims to enhance the robustness and performance of Diffusion Policy in visuomotor control tasks, positioning it as a refinement strategy that can be broadly applicable to probabilistic generative policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and reliability of robot policies learned through Diffusion Policy (DP) by addressing its inherent limitations related to trajectory selection.

The motivation for this work stems from two specific drawbacks identified in Diffusion Policy:

1. **Stochasticity of the Denoising Process:** During policy execution, DP generates action trajectories by denoising randomly sampled points. While this stochasticity is crucial for capturing multimodal behaviors, it can also lead to variability in the generated trajectories. Given the same environmental observation, different initial noise samples can result in substantially different action sequences. Some of these generated trajectories might deviate significantly from the "intended" or most probable behaviors learned from the training data, potentially causing the robot to perform actions that are inefficient, unsafe, or lead it into states outside the distribution of successful demonstrations. The authors highlight that this can be problematic if the sampled trajectory is an outlier with respect to the modes most represented in the training data.

2. **Susceptibility to Data Outliers:** As a supervised learning algorithm, Diffusion Policy trains directly on a dataset of demonstrations. If this dataset contains noisy, suboptimal, or erroneous trajectories (outliers), the policy may learn to reproduce these undesirable behaviors. During inference, if the stochastic denoising process generates a trajectory that aligns with such learned outliers, the robot's performance and reliability can be compromised. This issue is particularly pronounced when dealing with datasets collected by non-proficient operators or those with inherent variability.

To overcome these limitations, the authors propose KDPE (Kernel Density Estimation Policy Trajectory Selection). The key objectives driving the development of KDPE are:

- **Filter Outlier Trajectories:** To develop a strategy capable of identifying and discarding potentially harmful or unrepresentative trajectories generated by Diffusion Policy. This filtering should ensure that the robot executes actions that are more consistent with the dominant modes of the learned behavior distribution.
- **Maintain Low Computational Overhead:** To ensure that the proposed filtering mechanism operates efficiently at inference time, making it suitable for real-time, closed-loop robot control tasks without significantly impacting the control frequency.
- **Develop a Manifold-Aware Kernel:** To accurately model the probability density function (PDF) for complex robot actions, which typically comprise end-effector Cartesian position (Euclidean), orientation (non-Euclidean, specifically SO(3)), and gripper state (Euclidean). This requires a specialized kernel function that can appropriately handle data residing on different manifolds.
- **Empirically Validate Performance:** To rigorously evaluate KDPE against standard Diffusion Policy on a diverse set of simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks, including scenarios designed to test robustness to visual perturbations. The aim is to demonstrate an improvement in average success rate and qualitative behavior.

In essence, the motivation is to make Diffusion Policy more reliable and performant in practical robotic applications by introducing an intelligent, statistically grounded selection mechanism for its generated action trajectories, without adding complexity to the training process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, KDPE, is a strategy to select robust and representative action trajectories from the output of Diffusion Policy (DP) by employing Kernel Density Estimation (KDE). This is achieved through a combination of multi-hypothesis sampling from the diffusion process and a manifold-sensitive kernel for density estimation.

**Diffusion Policy (DP) Foundation:**
KDPE operates on top of Diffusion Policy, which itself adapts Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) for visuomotor behavior cloning. Given an environment observation, DP generates a robot action trajectory $A \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$ (where $T$ is the number of time-steps and $D$ is the action dimensionality) through an iterative denoising process. This process starts with randomly sampled Gaussian noise and progressively refines it by subtracting a learned gradient field, governed by a noise prediction network $\epsilon_{\theta}$. The training of $\epsilon_{\theta}$ minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted noise and actual noise added to ground-truth trajectories. DP's inherent stochasticity allows it to model multimodal distributions in the training data, but it can also produce varied outputs for the same observation.

**KDPE Strategy:**
1. **Multi-Hypothesis Trajectory Generation:** For a given current observation $o_t$, KDPE first generates a set of $N$ independent and identically distributed action trajectories $\{A_i\}_{i=1}^N$. This is accomplished by running $N$ parallel denoising processes within DP, each initialized with a different random noise sample but conditioned on the same observation $o_t$. This effectively creates a population of potential robot action trajectories.

2. **Density Estimation on Last Actions:** Instead of analyzing the entire trajectory, KDPE focuses on the "last action" of each generated trajectory, denoted as $a_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$. This is because the execution horizon $T$ is typically short in receding horizon control, and the final state of the planned trajectory often represents the policy's ultimate objective or desired end-effector configuration within that horizon. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) is then applied to this population of $N$ last actions $\{a_i\}$ to estimate the underlying Probability Density Function (PDF).

3. **Manifold-Aware Kernel Function:** A critical component of KDPE is the design of a kernel function $k(a_i, a_j)$ that can appropriately handle the mixed-manifold nature of robot actions. A robot action $a$ is composed of:
 - **End-effector Cartesian position ($t$):** A 3D Euclidean vector.
 - **End-effector orientation ($R$):** A rotation matrix in $SO(3)$, which is a non-Euclidean manifold.
 - **Gripper aperture ($g$):** A 1D Euclidean scalar.

 The paper proposes a modification of an existing probability distribution for poses to include the gripper state. The kernel function is defined as:
 $k(a_i, a_j) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^D |H|}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{ij}^\top H^{-1} \Delta_{ij} \right)$
 where $a_i = [t_i; R_i; g_i]$ and $a_j = [t_j; R_j; g_j]$.
 The difference vector $\Delta_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is defined as:
 $\Delta_{ij} = [t_i - t_j; \log(R_j^\top R_i)^\vee; g_i - g_j]$
 - For position ($t$) and gripper aperture ($g$), standard Euclidean differences are used.
 - For rotations ($R$), the Lie group logarithm map $\log: SO(3) \to \mathfrak{so}(3)$ is applied to the relative rotation $R_j^\top R_i$, followed by the vee operator $(\cdot)^\vee: \mathfrak{so}(3) \to \mathbb{R}^3$. This computes the axis-angle representation of the minimal rotation from $R_j$ to $R_i$, providing a Euclidean representation of the geodesic distance on $SO(3)$.
 The covariance matrix $H$ is diagonal, $H = \text{diag}(\sigma_{pos}^2 I_3, \sigma_{rot}^2 I_3, \sigma_{grip}^2 I_1)$, with bandwidths $\sigma_{pos}, \sigma_{rot}, \sigma_{grip}$ empirically tuned for each component.

4. **Density Estimation and Trajectory Selection:** The probability density of a generic action $\tilde{a}$ is estimated as the average of kernel values over the $N$ sampled actions:
 $\rho(\tilde{a}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N k(\tilde{a}, a_i)$
 For each of the $N$ final actions $a_i$, its density $\rho_i = \rho(a_i)$ is computed. KDPE then selects the trajectory $A_i$ that corresponds to the action $a_i$ with the highest density $\rho_i$. This strategy aims to select the trajectory that is most representative of the prominent modes learned by the Diffusion Policy, effectively filtering out outliers.

**Comparative Methods:**
- **Diffusion Policy (DP):** Serves as the baseline, selecting one trajectory uniformly at random from the $N$ generated.
- **KDPE-OOD:** A variant that selects the trajectory corresponding to the *minimum* density, intended to demonstrate the effect of outlier selection.
- **Tr-KDPE (Trajectory-KDPE):** An extension that attempts to model the PDF of *entire trajectories* using conditional KDE and a first-order Markovian assumption. This approach aims to consider the entire sequence of actions rather than just the final one (details in Appendix B).

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated extensively in both simulated and real-world environments.
- **Simulations:** Tasks from RoboMimic (Lift, Can, Square, ToolHang) and MimicGen (Coffee, Stack, Assembly) benchmarks are used. Experiments are conducted with both CNN-based and Transformer-based DP backbones. Robustness to visual domain shift is tested by modifying object colors. Each setting involves 100 environment resets and 100 trajectories generated per inference step. Non-deterministic methods are evaluated over three runs.
- **Real Robot:** Experiments are conducted on a Franka Emika Panda manipulator with a Robotiq 2f-85 gripper. Tasks include PickPlush (and PickSponge variant), CubeSort (multimodal), and CoffeeMaking (fine-grained, long-horizon). Datasets are collected via VR teleoperation.
- **Computational Overhead:** Inference times are measured on an NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU to quantify the additional cost of KDPE.
- **Hyperparameters:** Key hyperparameters, including learning rate, diffusion inference steps, population size ($N=100$), execution horizon ($T=8$), action dimensionality ($D=10$), and the KDE bandwidths for position, rotation, and gripper, are specified.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of KDPE demonstrates consistent performance improvements over Diffusion Policy (DP) across various simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks.

**Simulated Benchmark Tasks (RoboMimic and MimicGen):**
- **Overall Performance:** KDPE generally outperforms DP on both CNN-based (DP-C vs. KDPE-C) and Transformer-based (DP-T vs. KDPE-T) architectures. KDPE-C achieved the highest average success rate of 88.2% across all tasks.
- **Performance Gain:** KDPE-C improved DP-C's average success rate by 3.3%, and KDPE-T improved DP-T by 1.9%. Excluding the "Lift" task, which achieved 100% success for most methods, these improvements were 4.2% and 2.4%, respectively.
- **Task-Specific Improvements:** KDPE showed more pronounced improvements on tasks requiring higher precision, such as "ToolHang," and on tasks where the policy was trained with lower-quality demonstration data ("mh" datasets). This suggests that KDPE's filtering mechanism is particularly effective in scenarios where outlier trajectories have a greater negative impact or where the base policy is more prone to generating them due to noisy training.
- **Outlier Rejection Validation:** The KDPE-OOD variant, which selected trajectories with minimum density (outliers), experienced significant performance drops, particularly on tasks where KDPE performed best. This result supports the hypothesis that filtering out low-density trajectories is beneficial for task success.
- **Trajectory-based KDE (Tr-KDPE):** Tr-KDPE, which attempted to model the density of entire trajectories, yielded performance similar to or slightly lower than the baseline DP. The authors attribute this to the "curse of dimensionality," as modeling high-dimensional trajectory PDFs accurately requires a substantially larger sample size, which was not computationally feasible for the given population size.

**Analysis Under Visual Environment Perturbations:**
- **Robustness:** KDPE maintained its performance advantage over DP when subjected to visual domain shifts, specifically object color changes. The success rate improvements were similar to those observed in the unperturbed setting (e.g., KDPE-C improved DP-C's average by a similar margin). This indicates that KDPE's ability to filter out trajectory outliers is robust even in visually perturbed and potentially noisier environments.

**Real Robot Experiments:**
- **Consistent Outperformance:** KDPE consistently achieved higher success rates than DP on all three real-world tasks: PickPlush, PickSponge, CubeSort, and CoffeeMaking.
 - **PickPlush/PickSponge:** KDPE-C achieved 96% and 90% success rates, respectively, compared to DP-C's 90% and 88%. In cases where DP failed, KDPE's selection was observed to be critical for task completion.
 - **CubeSort:** KDPE-C achieved 44% success, a 3% improvement over DP-C's 41%.
 - **CoffeeMaking:** KDPE-C completed 70% of episodes successfully, while DP-C completed 60%.
- **Smoother Robot Behavior:** Qualitatively, the authors noted that KDPE resulted in smoother end-effector and gripper aperture movements. This suggests that KDPE contributes to choosing trajectories that represent task modalities more consistently, leading to more fluid and predictable robot operations.

**Inference Time Analysis:**
- **Low Computational Overhead:** KDPE introduces a relatively low computational overhead. For CNN-based models on the real-world experiment machine (NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU):
 - DP-C (single trajectory generation): 77ms (approx. 12.99 Hz).
 - Generating 100 trajectories: an additional 13ms.
 - Performing KDE on these 100 trajectories: an additional 3ms.
 - Total for KDPE-C: 93ms (approx. 10.75 Hz).
- The effective overhead of KDPE-C over the baseline DP-C (generating a single trajectory) is approximately 16ms, or a reduction in control frequency of about 2.24 Hz. This suggests that KDPE is computationally efficient enough for real-time closed-loop control tasks.
- Tr-KDPE, however, had a higher overhead, requiring 30ms for KDE, resulting in a total inference time of approximately 120ms (approx. 8.33 Hz), further supporting the challenge of high-dimensional density estimation.

In summary, the results indicate that KDPE effectively addresses the identified limitations of Diffusion Policy by improving success rates, enhancing robustness to perturbations, and yielding smoother robot behavior, all while maintaining a practical inference-time computational cost.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of KDPE represents a notable advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly for policies based on generative models like Diffusion Policy. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Reliability and Robustness of Generative Policies:** KDPE directly addresses critical limitations of Diffusion Policy: the stochastic generation of potentially suboptimal or out-of-distribution trajectories and the susceptibility to learning data outliers. By statistically filtering these trajectories at inference time, KDPE enhances the reliability and robustness of robot policies. This is crucial for deploying advanced learning-based control systems in real-world, safety-critical environments where unpredictable or erratic behavior is unacceptable.

2. **Improved Performance in Challenging Scenarios:** The demonstrated performance gains in simulated tasks (especially those requiring precision or learned from lower-quality data) and the consistent outperformance in real-world robot experiments highlight KDPE's practical utility. The improved success rates on complex, multimodal, and long-horizon tasks, coupled with qualitatively smoother robot movements, indicate that KDPE enables robots to execute tasks more effectively and consistently.

3. **Generalizable Inference-Time Improvement Strategy:** The core concept of KDPE – sampling multiple trajectories and selecting the most representative one via Kernel Density Estimation – is not inherently tied to Diffusion Policy. The authors suggest its applicability to other probabilistic generative policies, such as flow matching models, or even more generalist robot policies. This potential for broad applicability could extend the benefits of enhanced reliability and robustness across a wider range of modern robot learning architectures.

4. **Novel Manifold-Aware Density Estimation for Robotics:** The introduction of a specialized manifold-aware kernel capable of accurately modeling probability density for combined end-effector position (Euclidean), orientation (SO(3)), and gripper state (Euclidean) is a technical contribution. This kernel allows for more precise statistical characterization of robot actions, which is valuable for various tasks in robot control and perception beyond trajectory selection.

5. **Practical and Low-Overhead Solution:** KDPE operates entirely at inference time and introduces a relatively low computational overhead. This makes it a practical solution for integration into existing robot control loops without requiring extensive retraining of the base policy or significant additional hardware resources. Its "plug-and-play" nature as a policy post-processing step reduces deployment complexity compared to methods involving additional training or complex value function learning.

6. **Guidance for Future Research in Diffusion Models:** The work opens avenues for future research, such as using KDPE's density estimates not just for selection but also to directly guide the denoising process of diffusion models, potentially leading to even more robust trajectory generation. It also highlights the challenges of extending such statistical filtering to higher-dimensional problems (e.g., bimanual or dexterous manipulation), pointing towards ongoing research needs in overcoming the curse of dimensionality for kernel-based methods in complex action spaces.

In summary, KDPE offers a statistically grounded, efficient, and effective method to improve the operational consistency and performance of diffusion-based robot policies. Its contributions stand to make robot learning systems more dependable, thereby facilitating their transition from research labs to diverse real-world applications.
