# Experiential Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Experiential Reinforcement Learning (ERL) from Shi et al. introduces a novel training paradigm that augments standard reinforcement learning with an explicit experience reflection consolidation loop. This approach enables large language models to self-improve by transforming environmental feedback into structured behavioral revisions and internalizing these lessons for efficient, durable performance gains at inference time, demonstrating up to an 81% reward increase in complex sparse-reward tasks like Sokoban.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) struggle to learn effectively as decision-making agents in environments characterized by sparse and delayed feedback.
- Current reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms for LLMs compel models to implicitly infer how observed failures should translate into behavioral changes, leading to unstable and sample-inefficient learning.
- Traditional RL treats environmental feedback primarily as a scalar optimization signal, failing to leverage LLMs' explicit reflective capacity for systematic behavioral revision during learning.

## Method

- ERL introduces an explicit ==experience–reflection–consolidation loop==, triggered only when initial attempts fall below a reward threshold, to transform raw feedback into structured behavioral revisions.
- The model generates a ==self-reflection== after an initial failure, then produces a refined second attempt, with both the reflection and the second attempt contributing to RL updates.
- Successful reflection-guided improvements are internalized into the base policy via ==selective distillation==, ensuring durable behavioral enhancements without requiring the explicit reflection step at inference time.

## Results

- ERL consistently outperformed standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) across all six evaluated settings, including sparse-reward control environments (FrozenLake, Sokoban) and agentic reasoning (HotpotQA).
- Achieved substantial performance gains in complex, sparse-reward tasks, such as an **81%** increase in Qwen3-4B-Instruct's reward on Sokoban (from **0.06** to **0.87**) and a **70%** increase on FrozenLake for Olmo3-7B-Instruct.
- Demonstrated improved learning efficiency and stability, reaching higher reward levels earlier in training than RLVR, with ablation studies confirming the critical contributions of structured reflection and cross-episode memory.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - ERL bridges the gap between LLM self-reflection capabilities and practical RL training by embedding an explicit revision process within each trajectory, making learning more efficient and stable.
> - The internalization mechanism is crucial, as it distills reflection-driven improvements directly into the base policy, allowing enhanced behaviors to persist without additional inference-time overhead.
> - A gated mechanism selectively triggers reflection only on suboptimal attempts, conserving computational resources and focusing learning on critical failure cases, thereby concentrating exploration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned at the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL) and large language models (LLMs), specifically addressing the challenges of enabling LLMs to learn effectively as decision-making agents in interactive environments. The paper identifies two key areas where it contributes and distinguishes itself: "Reinforcement Learning for LLMs" and "Learning from Experience."

**Reinforcement Learning for LLMs:** The paper acknowledges the foundational role of RL in improving LLMs, starting with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (e.g., Ouyang et al., 2022; Christiano et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024) which aligns models with human preferences. It then points to more recent advancements where RL enhances mathematical reasoning through verifiable or programmatic rewards (e.g., OpenAI et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2026), and where LLMs are treated as policies interacting with external environments for multi-step problem-solving (agentic LLMs, e.g., Yao et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026).

ERL differentiates itself from these existing approaches by fundamentally altering how feedback is processed. Traditional RL for LLMs primarily treats environmental feedback as a scalar optimization signal, requiring the model to *implicitly* infer how observed failures should translate into behavioral changes. This implicit inference can be unstable and sample-inefficient, especially with sparse or delayed rewards. ERL, in contrast, introduces an *explicit* experience-reflection-consolidation loop that transforms raw feedback into *structured behavioral revision* within the same learning episode, rather than solely relying on global, delayed reward signals.

**Learning from Experience:** The paper aligns with a growing sentiment in AI research that future scaling regimes will rely on agents generating richer data through interaction, emphasizing "learning from experience" over static human corpora (Silver & Sutton, 2025). In classic RL, techniques like Hindsight Experience Replay (Andrychowicz et al., 2018) addressed sparse rewards by relabeling goals. For LLM-agents, prior work by Zhang et al. (2025) explored training agents on their own interaction traces, including self-reflection for learning from suboptimal actions. Furthermore, inference-time reflection methods (e.g., Zelikman et al., 2022; Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023) have shown LLMs' capacity to critique and revise their outputs.

ERL builds upon these ideas but makes a crucial distinction: while inference-time reflection improves behavior at runtime, it typically requires the reflection mechanism to be active during deployment, incurring additional inference cost. Concurrent research (Hübatter et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026) also explores integrating feedback-conditioned improvement into training by distilling a teacher policy. ERL shares this goal but uniquely emphasizes self-reflection as an *intermediate reasoning step embedded within the RL trajectory*. The key innovation lies in its *internalization mechanism*, which consolidates reflection-driven improvements into the base policy, allowing these enhanced behaviors to persist *without requiring explicit reflection at inference time*. This addresses a major practical limitation of prior reflection-based approaches.

In essence, ERL bridges the gap between the demonstrated power of LLM self-reflection and the practical challenges of integrating it into efficient, deployable RL training pipelines. It represents a significant step towards enabling LLM agents to learn more robustly and efficiently from their own interactions by explicitly converting failures into actionable, internalized knowledge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind Experiential Reinforcement Learning stems from the inherent difficulties large language models face when learning as decision-making agents in environments characterized by sparse and delayed feedback. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in agentic roles that require sequential actions, observation, and adaptation, they encounter real-world challenges where immediate, dense reward signals are rare. In such scenarios, current reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms compel models to implicitly infer the causal links between observed failures and necessary behavioral adjustments. This implicit learning process is often:
- **Unstable:** Policy updates can oscillate or diverge due to imprecise credit assignment.
- **Sample-inefficient:** Models require a vast number of interactions to discover effective corrective strategies through undirected trial-and-error exploration.
- **Challenging in multi-step tasks:** In agentic reasoning tasks involving multiple steps, small errors can compound, further obscuring credit assignment and making implicit learning even harder.

Drawing inspiration from human cognition, the authors highlight "experiential learning" as a robust mechanism for adaptation, where individuals engage in a cycle of experience, reflection, conceptualization, and experimentation. Humans, after observing an outcome, reflect on what happened, formulate revised internal models, and apply these revisions in subsequent attempts. While LLMs have demonstrated reflection-like capabilities at inference time (e.g., Chain-of-Thought, Self-Refine), standard RL training typically reduces complex feedback to a scalar optimization signal, failing to leverage this explicit reflective capacity for systematic behavioral revision during learning.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of ERL are:
1. **Introduce an explicit experience–reflection–consolidation loop:** To transform raw environmental feedback into structured, actionable behavioral revisions within the reinforcement learning process.
2. **Improve exploration and stabilize optimization:** By providing a mechanism for immediate, targeted self-correction, ERL aims to guide the model's exploration more efficiently and make the learning process more stable.
3. **Preserve gains at deployment without additional inference cost:** Through an "internalization" mechanism, ERL seeks to embed the learned corrective behaviors directly into the base policy, ensuring that the improvements are durable and accessible during inference without requiring the reflection step at runtime. This is crucial for practical deployability.
4. **Enhance learning efficiency and final performance:** Ultimately, the goal is to consistently achieve higher learning efficiency and stronger final policy performance across diverse agentic tasks compared to standard RL baselines.

In summary, ERL aims to imbue LLM agents with a more human-like capacity for experiential learning, allowing them to systematically learn from their mistakes and consolidate these lessons into robust, deployable policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Experiential Reinforcement Learning (ERL) introduces a novel training paradigm that augments standard reinforcement learning with an explicit experience–reflection–consolidation loop. This framework enables a language model to iteratively improve its behavior through self-generated feedback and subsequent internalization.

The core training loop of ERL, as illustrated in Algorithm 2 (Full Version) and Figure 3, involves three main phases within each episode:

1. **First Attempt and Initial RL Update:**
 - Given an input task `x`, the language model `π_θ` generates an initial response `y^(1)`.
 - This attempt is evaluated by the environment, yielding textual feedback `f^(1)` and a scalar reward `r^(1)`.
 - A standard reinforcement learning update (`L_policy(θ)`) is applied to `π_θ` based on this first attempt.

2. **Gated Reflection and Refined Second Attempt:**
 - **Gated Mechanism:** Crucially, a reflection-and-retry phase is triggered *only if* the first attempt's reward `r^(1)` falls below a predefined threshold `τ` (e.g., `r^(1) < 1.0` for sparse reward settings). This selective gating mechanism conserves computational resources, focuses learning on failure cases, and helps stabilize training by avoiding unnecessary reflection on already successful trajectories.
 - **Self-Reflection:** If triggered, the model `π_θ` generates a *reflection* `Δ`. This reflection is a structured verbal critique or self-guidance, conditioned on the original task `x`, the first attempt `y^(1)`, its feedback `f^(1)`, reward `r^(1)`, and an optional `cross-episode reflection memory m`. The memory `m` stores successful corrective patterns from previous episodes, acting as a contextual prior to stabilize reflection generation and encourage the reuse of effective strategies. The prompt templates (Tables 4 and 5) show how these reflections are guided (e.g., acting as a "chief scientific strategist" to distill a "Master Rulebook" for game rules and strategy).
 - **Second Attempt:** Conditioned on the original task `x` and the generated reflection `Δ`, the model `π_θ` then produces a refined, second attempt `y^(2)`.
 - **Second Attempt Evaluation and Reward:** This `y^(2)` is also evaluated by the environment, yielding `(f^(2), r^(2))`. The reflection `Δ` itself is assigned a reward `˜r = r^(2)`, incentivizing reflections that lead to improved downstream performance.
 - **Reflection Memory Update:** If the second attempt's reward `r^(2)` exceeds the threshold `τ`, the successful reflection `Δ` is stored in the cross-episode memory `m`, allowing corrective knowledge to accumulate over training.
 - **RL Update on Second Attempt and Reflection:** Another RL update (`L_policy(θ)`) is performed, optimizing `π_θ` based on the reflection `Δ` and the second attempt `y^(2)`.

3. **Internalization via Selective Distillation:**
 - To ensure that the improvements discovered through reflection are *durable* and available at inference time *without* requiring the explicit reflection step, ERL introduces an internalization phase.
 - If the second attempt `y^(2)` was successful (`r^(2) > 0`), the model `π_θ` is trained via supervised distillation (`L_distill(θ)`) to reproduce `y^(2)` *directly from the original input `x`*, *without* the reflection context `Δ`. This process effectively distills the knowledge gained from the reflection-guided refinement into the base policy, allowing it to generate the improved behavior in a zero-shot setting.

**Comparison to Standard RLVR:**
Standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) directly optimizes a policy from scalar outcome signals, requiring the model to implicitly infer corrective structure through undirected exploration. ERL augments this by embedding an explicit, internal revision process within each trajectory, converting feedback into immediate, structured guidance. This shift reduces reliance on blind trial-and-error, making learning more efficient and stable while remaining compatible with underlying RLVR objectives.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Tasks:** ERL was evaluated on sparse-reward control environments (FrozenLake, Sokoban) and an agentic reasoning benchmark (HotpotQA). A key aspect of these environments was the lack of explicit rules or environment dynamics, forcing the agent to infer task structure purely through interaction.
- **Models:** Olmo-3-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, representing different scales of LLMs.
- **Optimizer:** GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) was used for all policy gradient optimizations.
- **Compute Equivalence:** To ensure a fair comparison, ERL's rollouts per task were scaled to match the total training compute budget of RLVR.
- **Ablation Studies:** Variants without cross-episode memory ("ERL w/o Mem.") and without structured reflection ("ERL w/o Refl.") were used to isolate the contributions of key components.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Experiential Reinforcement Learning (ERL) across sparse-reward control environments (FrozenLake, Sokoban) and agentic reasoning benchmarks (HotpotQA) yielded several significant findings:

1. **Consistent Performance Superiority:** ERL consistently outperformed standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) across all six evaluated settings (three tasks with two different model backbones).
 - **Substantial Gains in Control Environments:** ERL demonstrated remarkable improvements in complex, sparse-reward environments. In **Sokoban**, Qwen3-4B-Instruct's reward increased from 0.06 to 0.87 (+81%), and Olmo3-7B-Instruct from 0.04 to 0.20. In **FrozenLake**, the Qwen model improved from 0.86 to 0.94 (+9%), and Olmo from 0.39 to 0.66 (+70%). These large gains underscore ERL's effectiveness when tasks demand long-horizon planning, recovery from errors, and the inference of unknown environment dynamics through interaction.
 - **Reliable Gains in Reasoning Tasks:** For **HotpotQA**, ERL achieved smaller but consistent improvements, with Qwen improving from 0.45 to 0.56 (+24%) and Olmo from 0.47 to 0.50 (+6%). The authors suggest this difference in magnitude is due to HotpotQA having a more homogeneous interaction pattern and denser feedback, where RLVR already performs relatively well.

2. **Improved Learning Efficiency and Stabilization:**
 - ERL consistently reached higher reward levels earlier in training and maintained a persistent performance margin over RLVR when plotted against wall-clock training time. This "acceleration" was particularly evident in FrozenLake and Sokoban, where ERL rapidly converged to high-reward behavior while RLVR progressed more gradually.
 - These dynamics indicate that ERL's reflection mechanism introduces an intermediate corrective signal that reshapes and concentrates exploration. By self-critiquing and refining actions within an episode, the model focuses its learning updates on trajectories that are already partially aligned with the objective, thereby reducing inefficient exploration and stabilizing the overall optimization process.

3. **Mechanistic Role of Reflection:**
 - Analysis of training reward trajectories showed that "post-reflection" attempts within ERL consistently achieved higher rewards than both "pre-reflection" attempts and RLVR.
 - This finding directly demonstrates the immediate, within-episode corrective power of self-reflection. The model's ability to analyze feedback from its first attempt and generate a structured revision leads to an improved second attempt, indicating that reflection provides actionable corrections that are reinforced and contribute to both short-term performance gains and longer-term policy improvement.

4. **Ablation Study: Contributions of Memory and Reflection:**
 - **Criticality of Structured Reflection:** The "no-reflection" variant, where structured self-reflection was replaced with raw first-attempt context and a generic retry instruction, showed the largest performance degradation across most tasks and models. This confirms that the explicit, structured nature of the reflection is crucial for ERL's benefits, not merely the act of re-attempting a task.
 - **Value of Cross-Episode Memory:** The "no-memory" variant, which disabled the storage and reuse of successful reflections across episodes, generally performed worse than full ERL, often slowing convergence. This highlights the importance of the cross-episode memory in accumulating and propagating effective corrective patterns, enabling cumulative refinement.
 - **Memory Caveat:** In one specific setting (Olmo3-7B-Instruct on Sokoban), the no-memory variant slightly outperformed full ERL. This suggests a potential scenario where a model with limited self-reflective ability, operating in a complex environment, might propagate inaccurate reflections through memory, hindering rather than helping. However, the overall trend across tasks and models strongly supports the combined benefit of structured reflection and persistent memory.

In essence, ERL’s design, incorporating explicit reflection and memory with an internalization mechanism, proved to be a powerful approach for improving LLM agents’ learning efficiency, final performance, and ability to handle complex, sparse-reward environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Experiential Reinforcement Learning (ERL) represents a significant advancement in the field of large language model (LLM) agents, offering a robust and practical mechanism for self-improvement that addresses core limitations of traditional reinforcement learning. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Enhanced Learning from Sparse and Delayed Feedback:** ERL fundamentally changes how LLMs learn from environmental feedback. By embedding an explicit experience–reflection–consolidation loop, it transforms sparse and delayed scalar rewards into structured, actionable behavioral revisions. This is crucial for real-world agentic applications where dense feedback is often unavailable, enabling LLMs to learn more effectively in complex, open-ended environments that mimic human-like learning scenarios.

2. **Durable Behavioral Improvement without Inference-Time Overhead:** A cornerstone of ERL's design is the internalization mechanism, which distills reflection-guided improvements directly into the base policy. This means that the enhanced behaviors, learned through self-critique and revision, become an intrinsic part of the model and can be executed at deployment time without requiring the explicit reflection step. This solves a major practical challenge faced by previous inference-time reflection methods, which often incur significant computational overhead at runtime. ERL makes self-improvement directly deployable and efficient.

3. **Improved Exploration Efficiency and Training Stability:** The ability to generate and leverage self-reflection within an episode allows ERL to focus exploration on promising trajectories and address specific failure modes immediately. This targeted correction reduces the need for undirected trial-and-error, leading to significantly improved sample efficiency and more stable optimization, especially in challenging environments where models must infer latent dynamics or plan over long horizons. This could accelerate the development and deployment of intelligent agents in domains like robotics, game playing, and complex decision-making systems.

4. **Advancing Agentic AI Capabilities:** ERL pushes the boundaries of agentic LLMs by moving beyond simply mimicking human actions or optimizing against scalar rewards. It endows agents with a form of meta-learning or self-awareness, allowing them to critically evaluate their own performance, reason about why they succeeded or failed, and systematically adjust their strategies. This self-improvement capability is a hallmark of truly intelligent agents and is essential for building autonomous systems that can continually adapt and improve from their own interactions in dynamic environments. The cross-episode memory further enables the accumulation of corrective knowledge, leading to more robust and generalized policies over time.

5. **New Research Directions in LLM Fine-tuning and Cognitive Architectures:** ERL opens up new avenues for research into LLM fine-tuning techniques, particularly concerning the integration of explicit reasoning and self-correction into training pipelines. It also encourages further exploration of cognitive architectures for AI, drawing inspiration from human experiential learning. Future work might explore more sophisticated memory systems, advanced on-policy distillation techniques (as mentioned in the paper), and how to generalize ERL to even more diverse and challenging interactive tasks, potentially enabling LLMs to acquire complex skills and knowledge with unprecedented autonomy.

In conclusion, ERL offers a compelling paradigm shift towards language models that are fundamentally grounded in rich experiential learning. By transforming feedback into structured, internalized behavioral improvements, this work paves the way for a new generation of LLM agents capable of robust, efficient, and continuous self-improvement.
