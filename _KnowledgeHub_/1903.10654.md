# Failure-Scenario Maker for Rule-Based Agent using Multi-agent Adversarial Reinforcement Learning and its Application to Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> FAILMAKER-ADVRL, a multi-agent adversarial reinforcement learning framework, generates non-trivial failure scenarios for rule-based agents in multi-agent environments, achieving high player failure rates (e.g., 84.5% in autonomous driving with N=2 NPCs) while maintaining natural adversarial behavior (low NPC failure rates, e.g., 3.3%). It integrates personal rewards for natural NPC behavior with adversarial objectives, along with techniques for credit assignment and balanced learning from rare failures.

## Problem

- Identifying non-trivial failure scenarios for rule-based agents in complex, safety-critical multi-agent environments (e.g., autonomous driving) is challenging.
- Traditional adversarial methods often produce trivial or unnatural failures, struggle with sparse rewards and credit assignment in multi-agent settings, and are inefficient.
- Adversarial training for robust rule-based agents is hindered by imbalanced experience data, as actual failures are often rare.

## Method

- The FAILMAKER-ADVRL algorithm uses a Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) framework where adversarial NPCs learn to cause failures for a rule-based player.
- It combines personal rewards (for natural NPC behavior) with adversarial rewards (for causing player failure) to generate realistic and informative scenarios.
- Contributor Identification (CI) and Adversarial Reward Allocation (AdvRA) are employed to assign credit and distribute sparse adversarial rewards specifically to contributing NPCs and relevant state-action pairs within failure episodes. Prioritized Sampling by Replay Buffer Partition (PS-RBP) addresses imbalanced data by selectively sampling from success and failure experiences.

## Results

- FAILMAKER-ADVRL achieved high player failure rates, reaching 99.2% in a multi-agent particle environment (N=3 NPCs) and 84.5% in an autonomous driving simulator (N=2 NPCs).
- Crucially, the method maintained very low NPC failure rates (e.g., 1.2% in particle, 3.3% in autonomous driving), demonstrating that generated failure scenarios are natural and useful, unlike pure adversarial baselines which resulted in over 98% NPC failures.
- Each proposed component (Adversarial Reward Allocation, Contributor Identification, and Prioritized Sampling by Replay Buffer Partition) progressively improved the player's failure rate and contributed to the overall effectiveness and learning efficiency of the system.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating personal rewards alongside adversarial objectives for NPCs is crucial for generating 'natural' and useful failure scenarios, preventing trivial collisions by the adversaries themselves.
> - Explicit mechanisms for credit assignment (CI) and fine-grained reward allocation (AdvRA) are necessary for effective learning of adversarial policies in multi-agent settings with sparse failure signals.
> - Dynamically balancing the experience replay buffer with prioritized sampling of failure events (PS-RBP) significantly improves the ability of adversarial agents to learn robust policies, even when failures are rare.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper situates itself within the intersection of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), adversarial learning, and the verification of safety-critical software, particularly in the domain of autonomous systems.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in complex tasks such as robotics and game playing. However, a significant portion of practical, safety-critical applications, including autonomous driving algorithms, continue to rely on rule-based systems. This reliance is often due to requirements for explainability, regulatory compliance, or compatibility with existing infrastructure. For these rule-based systems to be deployed reliably, it is essential to identify and mitigate as many failure scenarios as possible during their development. As these systems grow in complexity, especially in multi-agent environments where interactions between entities are numerous and dynamic, the manual or systematic identification of failure cases becomes increasingly challenging.

Traditional adversarial learning, as seen in computer vision for crafting adversarial images to test classifiers or in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for robust model training, focuses on generating data that causes a target system to fail. In the context of RL, this often translates to training an adversary to destabilize a protagonist agent. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) further extends this by considering interactions among multiple learning agents, which can be cooperative, competitive, or mixed. Existing MARL approaches like Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) address scenarios where agents learn to maximize collective or individual rewards in complex interactive settings.

However, several challenges arise when attempting to apply adversarial MARL to identify failure scenarios for *rule-based* agents. Firstly, a naive adversarial approach often generates trivial or unnatural failure cases (e.g., multiple agents intentionally colliding with the tested system), which are of limited practical use for improving robust algorithms. Secondly, when a failure occurs in a multi-agent setting, attributing the failure to specific adversarial agents or their actions (the credit assignment problem) is not straightforward. Thirdly, the reward signal for a rule-based agent's failure is typically sparse and binary (fail/succeed), making it difficult for adversarial agents to learn effectively. Finally, if the rule-based agent rarely fails, the adversarial agents are trained on an imbalanced dataset predominantly composed of success scenarios, hindering their ability to develop effective adversarial policies.

Previous work on testing autonomous driving systems primarily focused on generating test cases related to image recognition, rather than decision-making logic in multi-agent interactive contexts. Similarly, credit assignment methods in MARL, such as difference rewards, usually assume that all agents are reinforcement learning agents. This paper aims to address these identified gaps by proposing a method for efficiently generating non-trivial failure scenarios for rule-based agents in multi-agent environments, specifically tackling the issues of unnatural adversarial behavior, sparse reward allocation, and imbalanced experience data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for efficiently identifying failure scenarios for rule-based agents operating within multi-agent environments. This method aims to train adversarial agents using multi-agent reinforcement learning such that the tested rule-based agent experiences failures.

The motivation for this work stems from the critical need for robust and reliable decision-making algorithms in safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving and flight control. In these domains, a malfunction can have severe consequences, necessitating the identification and rectification of as many potential failure cases as possible during the development phase. While reinforcement learning has advanced significantly, many practical applications continue to utilize rule-based algorithms due to factors like explainability and compatibility requirements. Therefore, a method is required to systematically stress-test these rule-based algorithms.

The authors identify four specific problems that hinder the effective generation of useful failure scenarios in such a multi-agent, adversarial setting:

1. **Trivial and Obvious Failures:** Purely adversarial training often leads to adversarial agents (Non-Player Characters, or NPCs) intentionally causing collisions in an unrealistic manner. Such obvious failures are not productive for improving the tested rule-based algorithm, which typically needs to handle subtle or complex edge cases.
2. **Credit Assignment in Multi-Agent Failures:** In multi-agent interactions, when the rule-based agent fails, it is often unclear which specific adversarial NPCs contributed to the failure. For efficient and stable learning of adversarial policies, rewards should be assigned specifically to the contributing NPCs.
3. **Sparse Adversarial Rewards:** From the perspective of the adversarial NPCs, the reward for the player's failure is highly sparse, typically a binary signal (e.g., "1" for failure, "0" for success) obtained only at the end of an episode. This sparsity makes it difficult for NPCs to learn effectively.
4. **Imbalanced Experience Data:** If the rule-based agent is designed to be robust, it will rarely fail. This results in an imbalanced experience replay buffer for the NPCs, predominantly filled with scenarios where the player succeeded. Training with such imbalanced data can prevent NPCs from acquiring effective adversarial policies.

By addressing these challenges, the research seeks to enable the generation of "natural" and informative failure scenarios that can genuinely assist in enhancing the robustness of rule-based algorithms in safety-critical, multi-agent contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, FAILMAKER-ADVRL, integrates multi-agent adversarial reinforcement learning with specific techniques to address the identified challenges. It operates within a multi-agent framework where a single rule-based agent (the "player") is tested against one or more adversarial reinforcement learning agents (Non-Player Characters, or "NPCs"). The underlying reinforcement learning framework is Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG), which supports decentralized actors and a centralized critic capable of observing all agents' states and actions.

The FAILMAKER-ADVRL algorithm consists of three main components:

1. **Adversarial Learning with Personal Reward:**
 To prevent adversarial NPCs from generating trivial or unnatural failure scenarios (e.g., direct, intentional collisions), the reward function for each NPC $i$ is defined to include both a personal reward ($\hat{r}_t^i$) and an adversarial reward ($\bar{r}_t^i$). The total reward is $r_t^i = \hat{r}_t^i + \alpha \cdot \bar{r}_t^i$, where $\alpha$ is a scaling factor. The personal reward incentivizes NPCs to behave naturally (e.g., reaching a goal safely, avoiding self-collision), while the adversarial reward incentivizes them to cause the player's failure. This combination encourages NPCs to create challenging yet realistic situations where the player fails, rather than simply forcing a collision at all costs.

2. **Contributor Identification (CI) and Adversarial Reward Allocation (AdvRA):**
 These two techniques address the challenges of credit assignment and sparse adversarial rewards.
 - **Contributor Identification (CI):** When the player fails in an episode, CI is performed to determine which NPCs were primarily responsible. This involves re-running the recorded failed episode with subsets of NPCs. NPCs are classified into "classes" (e.g., Class 1 can cause failure alone, Class 2 can cause failure with another Class 2 contributor) by progressively removing or isolating them. This process identifies a set of "contributing NPCs" (C) for a given failure, ensuring that adversarial rewards are only given to relevant agents. The number of re-simulations required is combinatorial ($N \choose k$), so the maximum class $K$ is typically kept small.
 - **Adversarial Reward Allocation (AdvRA):** Once contributors are identified, AdvRA distributes the sparse, episodic adversarial reward to specific state-action pairs within the failed episode. Instead of a single binary reward at the episode's end, a continuous "contribution function" $g_t^i(x, a_i)$ is used to measure an NPC's contribution at each timestep. For instance, this function can be based on the inverse of the distance between the player and NPC $i$, indicating that actions taken when close to the player are more contributory. The adversarial reward $\bar{r}_t^i$ is then allocated proportionally to this function, giving higher rewards to actions and states that directly precede or facilitate the failure. The biggest contributor gets a normalized reward of 1, and other contributors receive a scaled reward based on their class and contribution function values.

3. **Prioritized Sampling by Replay Buffer Partition (PS-RBP):**
 To combat the problem of imbalanced experience data, PS-RBP partitions the replay buffer into two distinct parts: $D^+$ for experiences where the player succeeded, and $D^-$ for experiences where the player failed. During NPC training, samples are drawn independently from both buffers. A dynamic coefficient $\eta(e)$ determines the proportion of samples taken from $D^+$ versus $D^-$ (e.g., $\eta(e)S$ samples from $D^+$ and $(1-\eta(e))S$ samples from $D^-$ for a mini-batch of size $S$). This allows the training process to selectively prioritize samples from failure scenarios, even if they are rare, thereby mitigating the impact of imbalanced data.

The overall algorithm proceeds iteratively: agents execute actions based on their policies, experiences are stored temporally, and at the end of an episode, they are moved to either $D^+$ or $D^-$ (with CI and AdvRA applied if it's a failure episode). Finally, NPCs are trained using mini-batches sampled via PS-RBP, with the MADDPG algorithm updating their actors and critics. The critic, consistent with MADDPG, has access to the observations and actions of the player and all NPCs.

**Experimental Setup:**
The effectiveness of FAILMAKER-ADVRL was evaluated in two environments:
1. **Simple Multi-agent Particle Environment:** A 2D simulation where a rule-based player attempts to reach a goal while avoiding NPCs. NPCs are incentivized to make the player collide with an agent or a wall, while also trying to reach their own goals naturally.
2. **Autonomous Driving Simulator:** Using Microsoft AirSim, a 3D environment featuring a rule-based player car (controlled by PD feedback to follow a reference trajectory and avoid obstacles) and multiple adversarial RL-based NPC cars. NPCs aim to cause the player to have an accident or significantly delay its arrival, while also trying to follow their own reference trajectories at a good speed and avoid self-collision.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
The proposed method was compared against several baselines:
- **Good Agent:** NPCs only optimize their personal reward (no adversarial objective).
- **Attacker:** NPCs only optimize the adversarial reward (no personal reward).
- **P-Adv:** Adversaries with personal reward, but no CI, AdvRA, or PS-RBP.
- **P-Adv AdvRA:** P-Adv with Adversarial Reward Allocation.
- **P-Adv AdvRA CI:** P-Adv with both AdvRA and Contributor Identification.
- **P-Adv PS-RBP:** P-Adv with Prioritized Sampling by Replay Buffer Partition.

**Metrics:**
Performance was measured by:
1. **Player's Failure Rate:** Percentage of episodes where the rule-based player failed (indicating adversarial effectiveness).
2. **NPCs' Failure Rate:** Percentage of episodes where an NPC collided or failed (indicating naturalness/usefulness of generated scenarios, as low NPC failure implies more realistic adversarial behavior).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of FAILMAKER-ADVRL in generating failure scenarios for rule-based agents while maintaining natural behavior for the adversarial NPCs.

**Simple Multi-agent Particle Environment (N=1 and N=3 NPCs):**
- **Player Failure Rate:** FAILMAKER-ADVRL achieved high player failure rates, specifically 95.6% for N=1 and 99.2% for N=3. These rates were comparable to, or slightly lower than, the "Attacker" baseline (98.9% for N=1, 100.0% for N=3), which represents a pure adversarial approach without constraints on naturalness.
- **NPCs' Failure Rate (Naturalness):** Crucially, FAILMAKER-ADVRL maintained very low NPC failure rates, approximately 1.0% for N=1 and an average of 1.2% for N=3. This contrasted sharply with the "Attacker" baseline, where NPC failure rates were extremely high (98.0% for N=1, and over 98% for each NPC in the N=3 case), indicating that pure adversarial agents cause failures by also failing themselves, which is typically undesirable for generating useful test cases.
- **Component Contribution:** The individual components of FAILMAKER-ADVRL (AdvRA, CI, PS-RBP) progressively improved the player's failure rate compared to the "P-Adv" baseline (which had 8.6% for N=1 and 12.9% for N=3). "P-Adv AdvRA" showed a significant increase (60.8% for N=1), and "P-Adv AdvRA CI" further improved this (79.6% for N=1). This indicates that each proposed mechanism contributed to the method's overall effectiveness.
- **Learning Efficiency:** FAILMAKER-ADVRL and the "Attacker" baseline demonstrated stable and efficient learning, reaching high player failure rates relatively quickly. Baselines like "P-Adv AdvRA" and "P-Adv AdvRA CI" exhibited slower convergence.

**Autonomous Driving Simulator (N=1 and N=2 NPCs):**
- **Player Failure Rate:** In the more complex autonomous driving environment, FAILMAKER-ADVRL again achieved substantial player failure rates: 78.1% for N=1 and 84.5% for N=2. These figures were close to the "Attacker" baseline (98.0% for N=1, 100.0% for N=2).
- **NPCs' Failure Rate (Naturalness):** Similar to the simple environment, FAILMAKER-ADVRL maintained low NPC failure rates (2.8% for N=1, and an average of 3.3% for N=2). In contrast, the "Attacker" baseline again showed very high NPC failure rates (98.0% for N=1, and over 98% for each NPC in the N=2 case), reinforcing the finding that pure adversarial strategies result in trivial and unrealistic scenarios.
- **Qualitative Observation:** An example illustrated a successful scenario where an adversarial NPC induced the player car to collide with a rock, without the NPC itself colliding with the player or any obstacles. This highlights the ability of FAILMAKER-ADVRL to generate meaningful and non-trivial failure conditions where the rule-based agent fails due to complex interactions rather than forced collisions.

Overall, the results consistently indicate that FAILMAKER-ADVRL effectively trains adversarial NPCs to generate a high percentage of player failures. Simultaneously, by incorporating personal rewards and specific credit assignment mechanisms, the method ensures that these generated failure scenarios are natural and useful, as evidenced by the low failure rates of the adversarial NPCs themselves, which distinguishes it from purely adversarial approaches. The prioritized sampling technique also contributed to the efficiency and stability of learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FAILMAKER-ADVRL algorithm addresses a significant challenge in the development and validation of safety-critical systems that rely on rule-based decision-making, particularly in complex multi-agent environments. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Robustness of Autonomous Systems:** By systematically and efficiently generating non-trivial failure scenarios for rule-based agents, this method directly contributes to improving the safety and robustness of systems like autonomous vehicles and flight algorithms. Identifying weaknesses in decision-making logic through realistic adversarial interactions allows developers to refine and harden their algorithms before deployment, potentially preventing catastrophic failures in real-world applications.

2. **Bridging the Gap for Rule-Based Systems:** While much research in reinforcement learning focuses on end-to-end learning agents, a substantial portion of deployed safety-critical software remains rule-based due to needs for explainability, certification, or legacy compatibility. This work provides a valuable tool specifically tailored for testing and improving these prevalent rule-based systems, rather than exclusively targeting learning agents.

3. **Methodological Advancements in Adversarial MARL:** The paper introduces novel techniques—Contributor Identification (CI), Adversarial Reward Allocation (AdvRA), and Prioritized Sampling by Replay Buffer Partition (PS-RBP)—that tackle common limitations in adversarial multi-agent reinforcement learning. These include:
 - **Mitigating Trivial Adversaries:** The integration of personal rewards with adversarial objectives is an effective mechanism to ensure that adversarial behaviors are natural and informative, rather than simplistic or unrealistic.
 - **Solving Sparse Credit Assignment:** CI and AdvRA offer a structured approach to attribute blame and distribute rewards effectively in multi-agent failure scenarios, which is crucial for stable and efficient learning of adversarial policies.
 - **Handling Imbalanced Data:** PS-RBP provides a robust strategy for learning from rare failure events, enabling adversarial agents to specialize in uncovering vulnerabilities even when successes are overwhelmingly more common. These individual components are generalizable and could potentially be applied to other adversarial MARL problems beyond rule-based agents.

4. **Efficiency in Testing and Development:** The ability to automatically generate diverse and challenging failure scenarios can significantly accelerate the testing and iteration cycles for complex software. Manual identification of such scenarios is resource-intensive and often incomplete, whereas an automated, intelligent system can explore a wider range of possibilities, leading to more comprehensive testing. The demonstrated efficiency in both simple and autonomous driving environments suggests practical utility.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. It can be extended to more complex and realistic environments incorporating elements like pedestrians, traffic signs, and dynamic environment configurations. Furthermore, integrating the decision-making adversarial scenarios with computer vision aspects, such as generating adversarially challenging visual inputs, could lead to more holistic and comprehensive testing of autonomous systems.

In summary, FAILMAKER-ADVRL provides a practical and effective framework for enhancing the reliability of safety-critical rule-based systems. By systematically identifying and characterizing failures, it contributes directly to the engineering of safer and more robust autonomous technologies.
