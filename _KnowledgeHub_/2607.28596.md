# FA-RDP: A Frequency-Adaptive Reactive Diffusion Policy for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Noematrix Ltd. introduce FA-RDP, a frequency-adaptive reactive diffusion policy that resolves the conflict between action multimodality and real-time reactive control in contact-rich robotic manipulation tasks. The policy dynamically switches between low-frequency, multi-step planning for pre-contact exploration and high-frequency, one-step control for post-contact reactivity, achieving an average success rate of 81.7% across three manipulation tasks, outperforming prior diffusion-based methods by up to 46.7 percentage points.

## Problem

- Contact-rich robot manipulation requires both diverse action choices before contact (multimodality) and rapid, precise reactions after contact (high-frequency control).
- Existing end-to-end diffusion policies struggle with a fundamental trade-off: low-frequency multi-step sampling preserves multimodality but causes latency, while high-frequency one-step sampling improves reactivity but sacrifices pre-contact diversity and action smoothness.
- Limitations in prior methods include information loss in hierarchical designs, slow update rates from multi-step denoising in end-to-end policies, and instability in distilling noise-like targets for one-step prediction.

## Method

- A ==multi-frequency visual-force Transformer== with ==frequency-adaptive positional encoding== uses a single shared backbone to predict action chunks at both low (10 Hz) and high (30 Hz) frequencies.
- A learned ==multimodality indicator== dynamically selects the inference frequency and sampling strategy based on the perceived ambiguity of the action space, switching from multi-step low-frequency for exploration to one-step high-frequency for reactive control.
- **Manifold Consistency Distillation (MCD)** reparameterizes the high-frequency denoising model to directly predict actions on the robot ==action manifold==, enabling stable one-step, high-frequency control through combined ==consistency== and ==sample regression losses==.

## Results

- FA-RDP achieved an average success rate of **81.7%** across three real-world contact-rich tasks (Box Flipping, Switch Toggling, Button Pressing), surpassing Diffusion Policy (**10.0%**), RDP (**35.0%**), and ImplicitRDP (**51.7%**).
- The policy successfully preserved all four pre-contact approach modes, unlike a fixed high-frequency distilled policy which collapsed to one dominant mode, demonstrating effective multimodal behavior.
- The multimodality indicator dynamically guided frequency selection, improving success rates by **20 percentage points** compared to a fixed high-frequency policy, and Manifold Consistency Distillation enabled a **61.7%** success rate for one-step high-frequency control, significantly outperforming other distillation methods (**1.7-20.0%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adapting the policy's inference frequency based on the manipulation phase allows simultaneous preservation of pre-contact action multimodality and achievement of real-time post-contact reactivity.
> - A learned multimodality indicator, trained on empirical action residuals, provides an effective, context-aware mechanism for switching between exploration-focused and reaction-focused control strategies.
> - Distilling a diffusion policy by directly targeting action chunks on the robot action manifold, rather than noise components, significantly improves the stability and performance of one-step, high-frequency control for contact-rich tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot manipulation, particularly in contact-rich scenarios, presents a challenge due to the requirement for diverse action choices before contact and rapid, precise reactions after contact is established. Existing robot learning paradigms address these aspects with varying degrees of success, but often encounter tradeoffs.

**Vision-only policies**, such as Diffusion Policy, ACT, and 3D Diffusion Policy, primarily rely on visual input. While effective for many tasks, they lack immediate force feedback, which limits their reactivity in dynamic contact situations. The actions predicted by these policies are typically executed in an open-loop manner within each chunk, meaning they cannot adapt to real-time force signals, potentially leading to errors like force spikes, slippage, or loss of contact.

To mitigate these limitations, a segment of **robot learning research integrates force or tactile input**. Methods like DexForce and 3D-ViTac condition action prediction on contact signals. Other works focus on learning force-aware representations, and some explore scaling tactile and video-tactile data for more generalized policies. While these methods acknowledge the importance of contact signals, they often still involve open-loop action execution within predicted chunks, thereby maintaining a delay in real-time force reaction. More direct approaches to high-frequency force reaction include relying on separate low-level force controllers, using hierarchical slow-fast designs like RDP (which can suffer from information loss between policy layers due to compressed latent interfaces), or employing end-to-end learning of visual and force modalities such as ImplicitRDP. However, ImplicitRDP, despite its end-to-end nature, is limited by its multi-step denoising process, which restricts the closed-loop inference frequency.

**Multi-frequency policy learning** is another related area. Prior work has investigated the tradeoff between low-frequency inference (supporting longer-horizon and multimodal behavior) and high-frequency inference (improving force reactivity but increasing sampling cost and training difficulty). Approaches include hierarchical multi-frequency action chunking (HiPolicy), high-frequency latent chunk learning, adaptive replanning based on denoising variance (DVAC), and separating low-frequency world planning from high-frequency action execution (AHA-WAM). ManipForce utilizes frequency-aware representations to fuse asynchronous observations. These methods are typically motivated by hierarchical control, latent action learning, adaptive replanning, or asynchronous sensing.

Finally, **robot policy distillation** aims to accelerate inference. Techniques like progressive distillation, Consistency Models, and Consistency Policy reduce the number of sampling steps, often to one or a few. Flow-based policies pursue similar low-latency goals through different parameterizations. A common challenge in these distillation methods, particularly for robot action prediction, is the difficulty in distilling models that predict noise-like targets (epsilon, score, or velocity).

FA-RDP positions itself by addressing the fundamental compromise within end-to-end visual-force diffusion policies: how to achieve high-frequency closed-loop responses while simultaneously preserving pre-contact multimodality. It proposes a phase-dependent adaptation of inference frequency, guided by a multimodality indicator, and introduces a novel manifold consistency distillation method to stabilize one-step high-frequency action prediction, directly tackling the limitations of existing end-to-end policies and distillation techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work stems from a fundamental challenge in contact-rich robotic manipulation: the dual requirement for action multimodality and reactive control, which are dominant at different stages of a single manipulation episode.

**Before contact**, the robot often faces an underconstrained environment where multiple approach trajectories or contact modes might be equally valid. For instance, when approaching an object, there could be several collision-free paths. In such scenarios, a robust policy needs to preserve diverse action modes to allow for flexibility and robustness to minor environmental variations. If a policy commits too early to a single mode, it might fail if that mode becomes infeasible or suboptimal.

**After contact**, the dynamics change significantly. Geometric constraints, friction, and force limits narrow the solution space, requiring the robot to maintain contact, regulate applied forces, and respond rapidly to sensory feedback (specifically, measured wrench data). Small pose errors can quickly escalate into force spikes, slippage, or loss of contact if the policy cannot update its actions quickly enough.

**The core problem identified** is that standard diffusion policies, such as Diffusion Policy, typically operate with a fixed inference frequency and a fixed number of sampling steps throughout an entire episode. This design leads to a compromise:
- **Low-frequency, multi-step sampling:** While effective at preserving diverse pre-contact multimodal action distributions and generating smoother trajectories, it introduces latency. This makes it slow to react to real-time force feedback once contact is established, leading to poor post-contact control.
- **High-frequency sampling:** This improves reactivity by allowing for quicker action updates in response to force feedback. However, applying a one-step or few-step sampler throughout the episode can cause the policy to collapse distinct pre-contact modes, sacrificing the necessary multimodality. It can also degrade action smoothness, potentially leading to contact loss or violation of force constraints.

**This fundamental tradeoff motivates the central question of this research:** "Can an end-to-end visual-force diffusion policy achieve high-frequency closed-loop response while preserving pre-contact multimodality?"

To address this question, the key objectives of FA-RDP are:
1. **Develop a mechanism for frequency-adaptive inference:** The policy should dynamically adjust its inference frequency and sampling strategy based on the current manipulation phase (e.g., pre-contact vs. post-contact).
2. **Preserve multimodal action distributions before contact:** Ensure that the policy can explore and leverage diverse valid trajectories when the action space is ambiguous.
3. **Enable rapid, high-frequency closed-loop control after contact:** Facilitate swift responses to force feedback for stable and compliant interaction with objects under physical constraints.
4. **Overcome limitations of existing methods:** This includes addressing the information loss in hierarchical designs (like RDP), the slow update rates of end-to-end multi-step policies (like ImplicitRDP), and the difficulty of stably distilling noise-like targets in existing distillation techniques.

By resolving this tradeoff, FA-RDP aims to improve the overall robustness and success rate of robotic policies in complex contact-rich manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FA-RDP is a frequency-adaptive reactive visual-force diffusion policy designed to manage the phase-dependent requirements of contact-rich manipulation. It integrates three main components: a multi-frequency visual-force Transformer, a learned multimodality indicator for frequency selection, and Manifold Consistency Distillation (MCD) for one-step high-frequency action prediction.

**A. Multi-Frequency Visual-Force Transformer**
FA-RDP employs a single, shared visual-force Transformer backbone capable of predicting action chunks at both low and high frequencies. This shared architecture eliminates the need for separate models for different frequencies.
- **Frequency-Adaptive Positional Encoding:** The key design feature is how the network handles different frequencies. Instead of embedding the frequency mode as an explicit token, it is used to select temporal indices on a shared temporal grid. For the high-frequency mode (e.g., 30 Hz), consecutive positions are used, while for the low-frequency mode (e.g., 10 Hz), sparse positions are selected on the same underlying clock. This allows weight sharing across frequencies while maintaining the distinct temporal layout for each mode.
- **Training Objective:** The shared backbone is trained in a multi-stage process. In Stage 1, it is trained with a joint multi-frequency diffusion objective (L_MF = L_l_epsilon + L_h_epsilon). The network is parameterized to predict the clean action chunk (ˆA_ν), but the training objective uses epsilon supervision, a common practice in diffusion models. A causal action-force mask is incorporated to prevent future contact leakage, ensuring closed-loop updates.
- **Temporal Resolution:** The policy uses a low-frequency mode at 10 Hz with an action chunk length (H_ℓ) of 16 steps and a high-frequency mode at 30 Hz with H_h = 48 steps. Both modes cover the same 1.6-second prediction horizon, but with different action densities. Camera observations are processed at 10 Hz, and force/torque data at 30 Hz.

**B. Multimodality-Based Frequency Selection**
A learned multimodality indicator determines which sampling mode (low-frequency or high-frequency) to use during inference. This indicator dynamically assesses the degree of action ambiguity rather than relying on predefined phase labels.
- **Indicator Training (Stage 2):** An independent calibration set is used to train a Transformer indicator head. The Stage 1 visual-force policy is frozen during this stage. For each calibration example, the low-frequency policy is sampled multiple times (N=8) with different initial diffusion noise, and an empirical action residual `e(o_n)` is computed (average squared difference between sampled actions and the demonstration). The indicator head maps slow observation tokens to a scalar logit `s(o_n)`. It is trained with a confidence-weighted regression loss (L_MM) that penalizes errors based on the predicted confidence and regularizes the confidence itself, following the VGGT approach.
- **Inference Time Selection:** During execution, FA-RDP computes the indicator value `q` from the current slow observation tokens. If `q` is above a fixed threshold `τ`, the high-frequency distilled sampler is selected. Otherwise, the low-frequency multi-step diffusion sampler is used. This mechanism allows for adaptive switching between strategies based on the estimated multimodality.

**C. Manifold Consistency Distillation (MCD) for One-Step Control**
The high-frequency sampler, even after selection, would typically require multiple denoising steps, which limits real-time closed-loop control. MCD is introduced to convert this into an efficient one-step sampler.
- **Reparameterization for Action Manifold:** Unlike conventional distillation methods that often target epsilon, score, or velocity, MCD reparameterizes the denoising model to directly predict action chunks on the robot action manifold. This simplification makes the target more "robot-action-like" rather than "noise-like," thereby reducing the learning difficulty for the student network and improving stability. The fixed DDPM conversion (Eq. 2) maintains residual supervision.
- **Distillation Objective (Stage 3):** The student network is trained using a composite loss function (L_distill). This loss combines:
 1. **Manifold Consistency Distillation (MCD) Term (λ_MCD):** This term aligns the student's predicted action chunk with a stop-gradient target prediction from an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) teacher network. The teacher prediction is based on a less noisy state (A_h_r, r), effectively enforcing consistency in the action space between noisy and less-noisy states.
 2. **Sample Regression Loss (SRL) Term (λ_SRL):** This term regularizes the student's prediction by keeping it close to the demonstrated high-frequency action chunk (A_h). This helps stabilize the one-step prediction and ensures it remains relevant to the target behavior.
- **Inference Procedure:** The final inference procedure (Algorithm 1) follows a consistent reactive diffusion mechanism. At the beginning of an action chunk, the slow context and initial noise are cached. The multimodality indicator selects the frequency. At each control step within the chunk, wrench tokens are refreshed. If the low-frequency mode is selected, multi-step DDIM (with η=0) is performed. If the high-frequency mode is selected, the distilled model directly performs a one-step action prediction. The newest valid action is then executed.

**D. Implementation Details**
- **Force Compensation:** A 100 Hz command layer adjusts policy outputs by adding a linear compensation term based on measured external force (`p_cmd = p_policy - λf_ext`) before sending targets to the robot's Cartesian motion-force interface.
- **Hardware:** Flexiv Rizon 4R arm for teleoperation (demonstration) and a Rizon 4s follower arm for task execution. Observations from a wrist-mounted iPhone fisheye camera and a fixed third-view USB camera are used.
- **Latency:** Policy inference latency is below 30 ms for high-frequency and 50 ms for low-frequency execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FA-RDP was conducted on three real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks: Dual Box Flipping, Dual Switch Toggling, and Dual Button Pressing. Each task involved multiple pre-contact approaches and required reactive force control after contact. Experiments aimed to answer four key questions.

**1. Comparison with Baselines (Q1):**
FA-RDP demonstrated superior performance compared to several baselines.
- **FA-RDP (Ours)** achieved an average success rate of 81.7% (14/20 Box, 18/20 Button, 17/20 Switch).
- **Diffusion Policy (DP)**, a vision-only approach, had the lowest average success rate at 10.0%, primarily failing due to contact loss. This was attributed to its open-loop nature and inability to use immediate force feedback.
- **Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP)**, a hierarchical visual-force policy, achieved 35.0% success. Its failures were characterized by incorrect contact points, suggesting spatial information loss from its compressed slow-policy interface.
- **ImplicitRDP**, a fixed-frequency end-to-end visual-force diffusion policy, reached 51.7% success. Similar to DP, it struggled with contact loss, but this was attributed to its multi-step denoising limiting its closed-loop update frequency.
- **Regression Policy w/ Force**, a non-diffusion visual-force regression approach, had an average success rate of 20.0%. This baseline often failed by knocking down front blocks before target approach, indicating an inability to preserve multimodal pre-contact approaches (due to averaging under an MSE objective).
FA-RDP's success was attributed to its ability to separate pre-contact multimodal exploration (using the low-frequency sampler) from post-contact reactive control (using the high-frequency distilled sampler).

**2. Multimodal Preservation (Q2):**
An analysis of pre-contact trajectory distributions (right, middle-right, middle-left, left modes) across 80 trials per task indicated that:
- **FA-RDP and ImplicitRDP** successfully covered all four approach modes, demonstrating preservation of multimodal capabilities.
- The **high-frequency distilled alone policy** (without multimodality guidance) collapsed to one dominant approach mode, confirming that fixed high-frequency policies can sacrifice pre-contact diversity.
This finding supports FA-RDP's design, showing that it maintains multimodality before contact.

**3. Indicator Accuracy (Q3):**
The effectiveness of the multimodality indicator in guiding frequency selection was evaluated by comparing FA-RDP with the high-frequency distilled alone policy.
- The **high-frequency distilled alone policy** (always using the distilled high-frequency mode) achieved an average success rate of 61.7%.
- **FA-RDP**, with its indicator-guided switching, improved this to 81.7%.
Analysis of the indicator value and force curves showed that the indicator remained low before contact and during initial target approach, consistent with phases requiring multimodal exploration and smooth motion. It subsequently rose with the force response after contact, signaling the transition to phases demanding reactive force control. This dynamic switching, therefore, contributed to a 20 percentage point improvement in average success rate.

**4. Distillation Method Comparison (Q4):**
The efficacy of Manifold Consistency Distillation (MCD) was assessed against other distillation methods in the high-frequency mode (without multimodality-based selection).
- The **high-frequency alone policy** (original multi-step policy forced to one-step sampling) achieved 20.0% success, indicating that simply reducing sampling steps without distillation leads to poor accuracy.
- **MeanFlow Policy** and **Consistency Policy** both achieved a low average success rate of 1.7%. These methods, while designed for one-step generation, did not perform effectively in this contact-rich manipulation context.
- The **high-frequency distilled alone policy** (using MCD) achieved 61.7% average success. This result indicates that MCD, by reparameterizing the network to predict actions on the robot action manifold with SRL regularization, significantly improves the stability and accuracy of one-step high-frequency control compared to other distillation approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FA-RDP addresses a central challenge in robotic manipulation by effectively managing the phase-dependent requirements of contact-rich tasks: preserving pre-contact action multimodality while enabling rapid, reactive control post-contact. This work offers several contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Resolution of a Fundamental Tradeoff:** The primary significance lies in providing a solution to the inherent compromise between action diversity and reactive control in diffusion-based robot policies. By dynamically adapting the inference frequency and sampling strategy, FA-RDP achieves both capabilities within a single episode, leading to higher task success rates.
2. **Novel Frequency-Adaptive Architecture:** The introduction of a shared multi-frequency visual-force Transformer with frequency-adaptive positional encoding allows a single network to operate efficiently across different temporal resolutions. This design promotes weight sharing and reduces the need for multiple specialized models.
3. **Learned Multimodality-Guided Control:** The development of a learned multimodality indicator that dynamically selects the appropriate control strategy (low-frequency diffusion for exploration, high-frequency distilled for reaction) represents a novel approach to adaptive policy execution. This method moves beyond static phase segmentation or heuristic switching, offering a more nuanced and context-aware control paradigm.
4. **Manifold Consistency Distillation (MCD):** MCD provides a stable and effective method for distilling diffusion policies into one-step controllers for robotic manipulation. By reparameterizing the network to predict directly on the robot action manifold and incorporating sample regression loss, MCD overcomes some of the difficulties associated with distilling noise-like targets, which has implications for accelerating other diffusion-based robot policies.
5. **Improved Performance in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The experimental results demonstrate that FA-RDP significantly outperforms various baselines on real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks. This improved performance can lead to more robust and reliable robotic systems capable of handling complex interactions with their environment.
6. **Broader Applicability:** The concept of phase-adaptive control, guided by a learned indicator of task ambiguity or state, has potential applications beyond contact-rich manipulation. It could be extended to other complex robotic tasks that involve distinct stages with varying control requirements (e.g., navigation, dexterous manipulation, assembly).

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as reliance on visual-force input, single-task policies, and a multi-stage training procedure, FA-RDP lays groundwork for future research in adaptive and efficient robot policy learning. Its methodological innovations contribute to advancing the capabilities of robots in challenging interaction scenarios.
