# Action Robust Reinforcement Learning and Applications in Continuous Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Technion Institute of Technology introduce and formalize two criteria for action robustness, Probabilistic Action Robust MDP (PR-MDP) and Noisy Action Robust MDP (NR-MDP), to train reinforcement learning agents in continuous control environments. Their Deep Reinforcement Learning algorithms, based on DDPG, produce policies robust to abrupt forces and mass uncertainty, often achieving higher rewards than baselines even in the absence of perturbations across various MuJoCo tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies often fail to generalize when deployed in real-world environments due to unmodeled perturbations or uncertainties.
- Existing Robust Markov Decision Process (RMDP) approaches primarily focus on uncertainty in transition probabilities, which is difficult to define for complex systems and challenging to extend to Deep RL.
- Empirical Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) methods for robustness often lack theoretical guarantees and require extensive simulator control.

## Method

- The paper formalizes action uncertainty using two criteria: Probabilistic Robust MDP (PR-MDP) for abrupt adversarial actions and Noisy Robust MDP (NR-MDP) for continuous adversarial perturbations.
- Policy Iteration schemes are developed for both criteria in the tabular setting, with convergence guarantees provided for PR-MDP.
- These robust criteria are integrated into a Deep ==Reinforcement Learning== framework, Action-Robust DDPG (AR-DDPG), enabling robust policy learning in continuous control tasks using actor-critic networks for both agent and adversary.

## Results

- Both NR-MDP and PR-MDP variants of AR-DDPG generally outperformed the DDPG baseline in most MuJoCo continuous control domains, demonstrating improved robustness and higher average rewards even without perturbations.
- PR-MDP showed more stable performance and an implicit ability to facilitate exploration, while NR-MDP was more sensitive to hyperparameter tuning.
- The proposed methods successfully generated policies robust to abrupt forces and mass uncertainty, with hyperparameters tuned on one domain often transferring effectively to others.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling uncertainty directly through actions offers a more intuitive and practically accessible approach to robustness than modeling uncertainties in transition dynamics.
> - Training with action-robust criteria, particularly PR-MDP, acts as an implicit regularizer, leading to policies that perform well even without explicit perturbations.
> - While PR-MDP often results in deterministic optimal policies with strong duality in theory, empirical DRL implementations can diverge, highlighting challenges with function approximation in adversarial settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in various applications, including real-world deployment. However, a limitation of conventional RL is the assumption that training and testing environments are identical, which can lead to poor generalization when small perturbations occur. For instance, in robotics, changes in parameters like mass or friction can significantly affect policy performance, and in autonomous vehicles, varying conditions such as traction or road state pose similar challenges.

The broader field of Robust Markov Decision Processes (RMDPs) addresses this issue by formulating problems as max-min optimization over a set of possible model parameters, known as an uncertainty set. Pioneering work by Nilim & El Ghaoui (2005), Iyengar (2005), and Wiesemann et al. (2013) has explored robustness to uncertainty in transition probabilities, primarily in the tabular case. Mannor et al. (2012) extended these to non-rectangular uncertainty sets. However, these traditional RMDP approaches face challenges in practical application: the precise definition and acquisition of uncertainty sets for transition probabilities can be difficult, and their extension to non-linear function approximation schemes, such as deep neural networks used in Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), is not straightforward. Recent empirical efforts in DRL (Pinto et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2018) have attempted to tackle robustness, often requiring simulator control and lacking theoretical guarantees.

This paper proposes an alternative approach to robustness by focusing on *action uncertainty*, rather than uncertainty in transition dynamics. This aligns with a subset of robust control literature that investigates control policies robust to deterministic perturbations, often solved via methods like linear or quadratic programming. The authors frame action robustness as a two-player zero-sum game between an agent and an adversary, distinguishing their work by establishing existence and duality properties, including the nature of optimal policies (deterministic vs. stochastic).

Furthermore, the work relates to Robust Supervised Learning, particularly the concept of adversarial examples and methods like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). Similar to how GANs can generate perturbations to cause misclassification in supervised learning, this paper's adversarial framework aims to learn policies that perform well despite an adversary introducing action disturbances. However, the authors note that DRL-based adversarial training methods often lack convergence proofs and are primarily empirical heuristics.

By formalizing action-robustness and developing DRL algorithms with some theoretical underpinnings (for the probabilistic case), this research contributes to bridging the gap between theoretical robust control and practical DRL, offering a distinct and potentially more accessible way to achieve robustness in real-world continuous control applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to formalize and investigate two novel criteria for robustness to action uncertainty within the Reinforcement Learning framework and to develop scalable algorithms for these criteria, particularly for continuous control tasks using Deep Reinforcement Learning.

The motivation behind this work stems from several challenges observed in the deployment of standard RL policies in real-world scenarios:

1. **Lack of Generalization under Perturbations:** Traditional RL algorithms assume that the training and test environments are identical. In reality, systems are subject to various disturbances and uncertainties, such as changes in physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction), external forces, or sensor noise. Policies trained without considering these perturbations often perform poorly or fail when faced with minor deviations from the training environment. For example, a robot trained for manipulation might encounter unexpected forces or slight changes in its own mass, which could destabilize a non-robust policy.

2. **Limitations of Existing Robust RL Approaches:** Current Robust MDP formulations primarily focus on uncertainty in transition probabilities. This approach presents two practical difficulties:
 - **Defining Uncertainty Sets:** It is often challenging to precisely define the set of possible transition models (uncertainty set) that adequately covers real-world variations.
 - **Scalability to DRL:** Extending these transition-based robust methods to high-dimensional, non-linear function approximation schemes like neural networks in DRL remains an open problem. Empirical DRL methods attempting robustness often lack theoretical guarantees.

3. **Natural Representation of Uncertainty via Actions:** The authors propose that action uncertainty offers a natural and intuitively relatable way to model real-world disturbances. They identify two distinct scenarios:
 - **Probabilistic Action Uncertainty (PR-MDP):** This models situations where, with a certain probability $\alpha$, the agent's intended action is replaced by a different, potentially adversarial, action. This can represent abrupt external interruptions, such as a sudden push on a robot.
 - **Noisy Action Uncertainty (NR-MDP):** This considers scenarios where an adversary adds a perturbation directly to the agent's chosen action, resulting in the executed action being a mixture of the agent's and adversary's actions (e.g., $(1-\alpha)a + \alpha\bar{a}$). This can model constant interfering forces or uncertainties in system parameters like mass, where a heavier robot might be perceived as an adversary constantly applying force in the opposite direction.

4. **Hypothesis of Implicit Regularization:** Beyond merely achieving robustness, the authors hypothesize that training with action-robust criteria might also improve a policy's performance even in the absence of perturbations. This suggests that robust training could act as a form of implicit regularization, leading to more generalized and stable policies.

By addressing these motivations, the paper aims to provide a more accessible, theoretically grounded (for PR-MDP), and empirically effective framework for training robust policies that can better withstand real-world uncertainties in continuous control tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on formalizing two types of action robustness as zero-sum Markov Games (MGs) and developing Policy Iteration (PI) schemes for their solution, which are then adapted for Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in continuous control environments.

#### 3.1. Formalization of Action Robustness

The work introduces two distinct criteria for action robustness, both framed as zero-sum games between an agent (maximizer) and an adversary (minimizer):

- **Probabilistic Action Robust MDP (PR-MDP):**
 - **Definition:** Given an agent's policy $\pi$ and an adversary's policy $\bar{\pi}$, the joint action $a_t$ at time $t$ is drawn from a mixture distribution: with probability $(1-\alpha)$, the action is from $\pi(s_t)$, and with probability $\alpha$, it is from $\bar{\pi}(s_t)$. The parameter $\alpha \in$ controls the adversary's influence.
 - **Objective:** The agent aims to maximize the expected return, considering the worst-case scenario where an adversary selects the action $\bar{\pi}$ to minimize this return. This is expressed as a max-min optimization problem: $\max_{\pi} \min_{\bar{\pi}} E[\sum \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)]$.
 - **Properties:** The paper establishes that for PR-MDPs, an optimal policy for the agent exists which is stationary and deterministic (Proposition 1). Strong duality also holds, meaning $\max_{\pi} \min_{\bar{\pi}} = \min_{\bar{\pi}} \max_{\pi}$. The PR-MDP implicitly defines a convex and rectangular uncertainty set over models, relating it to traditional RMDPs.

- **Noisy Action Robust MDP (NR-MDP):**
 - **Definition:** In this criterion, the action performed on the system is a convex combination of the agent's intended action $a$ and the adversary's action $\bar{a}$: $(1-\alpha)a + \alpha\bar{a}$. This assumes a continuous action space where blended actions are valid.
 - **Objective:** Similar to PR-MDP, the agent maximizes its return against an adversary minimizing it: $\max_{\pi} \min_{\bar{\pi}} E[\sum \gamma^t r(s_t, (1-\alpha)a_t + \alpha\bar{a}_t)]$.
 - **Properties:** A key distinction is that, generally, for NR-MDPs, the optimal agent policy may be stochastic, not necessarily deterministic (Proposition 4). Furthermore, strong duality does not always hold on the class of deterministic policies.

#### 3.2. Policy Iteration Schemes (Tabular Case)

For the tabular setting, the paper proposes two Policy Iteration (PI) algorithms:

- **Probabilistic Robust PI (PR-PI, Algorithm 1):** A two-player PI scheme adapted for PR-MDPs. It iteratively: (i) calculates the optimal counter-strategy for the agent given a fixed adversary, and (ii) updates the adversary's policy using a 1-step greedy approach with respect to the agent and adversary mixture policy.
- **Soft Probabilistic Robust PI (Soft PR-PI, Algorithm 2):** A generalization of PR-PI where the adversary's policy is updated using a Frank-Wolfe update, which is a gradient-based method. This "soft" update is hypothesized to be less sensitive to errors. Theorem 3 proves the convergence of Soft PR-PI to the unique Nash-Equilibrium value, contracting with a rate of $(1 - \eta + \gamma\eta)$.

For NR-MDPs, a standard two-player zero-sum PI algorithm (similar to Algorithm 3 and 4 in the appendix) would require solving a 1-step min-max problem for the adversary update, which is computationally more demanding than the single-agent problem in PR-PI.

#### 3.3. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) Generalization

The theoretical PI schemes are adapted to DRL for continuous control using a variant of Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG), termed **Action-Robust DDPG (AR-DDPG, Algorithm 5)**.

- **Network Architecture:** Two deterministic policy networks are trained: an actor $\mu_\theta(s)$ for the agent and an adversary $\bar{\mu}_{\bar{\theta}}(s)$ for the opponent. A critic network $Q(s, a; \phi)$ estimates the Q-function for the *joint policy*.
- **Joint Policy Formulation for DRL:**
 - **PR-MDP:** The joint policy is approximated as a mixture of Dirac delta functions: $\pi_{mix_{P,\alpha}}(u|s; \theta, \bar{\theta}) = (1-\alpha)\delta(u-\mu_\theta(s)) + \alpha\delta(u-\bar{\mu}_{\bar{\theta}}(s))$.
 - **NR-MDP:** The joint policy is a single Dirac delta at the mixed action: $\pi_{mix_{N,\alpha}}(u|s; \theta, \bar{\theta}) = \delta(u-((1-\alpha)\mu_\theta(s) + \alpha\bar{\mu}_{\bar{\theta}}(s)))$.
- **Policy Gradient Updates:** Proposition 5 generalizes the DPG theorem to derive gradient updates for both actor and adversary parameters ($\theta$ and $\bar{\theta}$) for both PR-MDP and NR-MDP. The gradients are weighted by $(1-\alpha)$ for the actor and $\alpha$ for the adversary, and are taken with respect to the Q-value of the joint policy.
- **Training Procedure:** The AR-DDPG algorithm trains both networks using gradient descent. To manage the two-player game, the actor is updated for $N$ gradient steps, followed by a single adversary step. The critic is updated based on the target Q-values derived from the respective joint policies.
- **Exploration:** Three exploration schemes are considered: noiseless (on-policy), Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) noise, and parameter space noise.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Extensive experiments are conducted on various continuous control tasks in the MuJoCo physics engine (e.g., Hopper-v2, Walker2d, Humanoid, InvertedPendulum, Swimmer, HalfCheetah, Ant).
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Policies are evaluated for robustness against two types of perturbations: abrupt forces (simulating PR-MDP scenarios) and mass uncertainty (simulating NR-MDP scenarios). Performance is also assessed in the absence of perturbations to check for potential implicit regularization.
- **Hyperparameter Ablation:** A detailed study on Hopper-v2 investigates the effects of $\alpha$ (0.01-0.2), actor update steps N (2, 5, 10, 20), and exploration schemes.
- **"Diving Deeper" Analysis:** Additional experiments explore (i) "Off-Policy Action Robustness" (training robustly but collecting data without adversarial intervention) and (ii) the impact of the MaxMin vs. MinMax update order (i.e., agent updates first vs. adversary updates first) on empirical performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides insights into the performance and characteristics of the proposed action-robust reinforcement learning methods.

#### 4.1. Hyperparameter Ablation on Hopper-v2

- **PR-MDP Stability:** The Probabilistic Action Robust MDP (PR-MDP) criterion exhibited a more stable relationship between the uncertainty parameter $\alpha$ and performance. An optimal $\alpha$ range of $[0.1, 0.15]$ was observed, with performance deteriorating outside this range.
- **NR-MDP Sensitivity:** The Noisy Action Robust MDP (NR-MDP) criterion showed more erratic behavior, with performance being highly sensitive to subtle changes in hyperparameters. A clear correlation between $\alpha$ and performance was not consistently observed.
- **Implicit Exploration in PR-MDP:** Notably, for PR-MDP, even without explicit exploration noise (e.g., "No Noise" exploration scheme), the approach often outperformed the DDPG baseline. This suggests that the adversary's actions inherently introduce sufficient exploration or act as a form of regularization.
- **Optimal Hyperparameter Selection:** Based on Hopper-v2 experiments, the PR-MDP variant using parameter space noise with $\alpha=0.1$ and an actor-to-adversary update ratio of 10:1 was chosen. For NR-MDP, OU noise with $\alpha=0.1$ and a 1:1 update ratio was selected, despite its higher sensitivity.

#### 4.2. Performance across Various MuJoCo Domains

- **General Outperformance:** In most evaluated MuJoCo domains, both the NR-MDP and PR-MDP approaches outperformed the DDPG baseline, not only in terms of robustness to perturbations (abrupt noise and mass uncertainty) but also in terms of average reward in the absence of perturbations. This provides empirical evidence for the implicit regularization hypothesis.
- **Domain-Specific Differences:** While PR-MDP performed strongly on Hopper-v2, its superiority was not universal. Performance differences between PR-MDP and NR-MDP varied across domains, indicating that the suitability of each criterion may depend on the specific task dynamics.
- **Transferability of Hyperparameters:** The hyperparameters tuned on Hopper-v2 generally transferred well to other domains, resulting in improved performance without extensive re-tuning, which suggests a degree of robustness in the method's configuration.
- **Observed Failures:** The InvertedPendulum domain presented an exception, where PR-MDP performed worse than the baseline. Possible explanations include: (i) non-optimal hyperparameter transfer for this specific domain, and (ii) the task's sensitivity to strong perturbations (a large $\alpha$ value), where a powerful adversary might prevent the agent from ever successfully solving the balancing task.

#### 4.3. "Diving Deeper" Analysis

- **Off-Policy Action Robustness:**
 - For **NR-MDP**, the core operator (the mechanism of mixing agent and adversary actions) itself appeared to be the primary driver of performance improvement, with adversarial exploration contributing only a small increase in stability. This suggests the regularization effect is inherent to the action mixing formulation.
 - For **PR-MDP**, both the adversarial exploration and the specific operator were found to be necessary to achieve the observed performance gains, indicating a more intertwined relationship between exploration strategy and the robustness mechanism.

- **MaxMin vs. MinMax Update Order:**
 - Theoretically, for PR-MDP, strong duality suggests that solving for MaxMin (agent maximizes against adversary minimizing) should yield the same value as MinMax (adversary minimizes against agent maximizing).
 - Experimentally, however, a "stronger" adversary (by increasing its update frequency relative to the actor, effectively prioritizing the MinMax objective) resulted in performance degradation. This empirical contradiction of theoretical strong duality in the deep learning context is attributed to factors like the fixed number of training steps (fewer actor updates) or the potential for faster convergence to sub-optimal solutions due to function approximation errors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents two distinct and practically motivated criteria for action robustness in reinforcement learning, contributing to the field in several significant ways:

1. **Addressing a Gap in Robust RL:** By formalizing Probabilistic Action Robust MDP (PR-MDP) and Noisy Action Robust MDP (NR-MDP), the paper offers a novel perspective on robustness, complementing existing work that primarily focuses on uncertainty in transition probabilities. This is particularly relevant given the difficulties in defining and utilizing uncertainty sets for transitions in complex DRL environments.

2. **Increased Real-World Applicability:** The proposed criteria are directly correlated to common real-world uncertainties. PR-MDP (adversarial action taken with probability $\alpha$) can model abrupt external forces or sudden system failures, while NR-MDP (adversary adds a perturbation to the agent's action) can model constant disturbances, such as changes in mass or friction. This direct mapping makes the approach highly relevant for applications in robotics, autonomous systems, and other control problems.

3. **Generalization to Deep Reinforcement Learning:** The successful adaptation of these criteria to Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) via Action-Robust DDPG (AR-DDPG) is a key contribution. This provides a scalable methodology for training robust policies in high-dimensional continuous control tasks, moving beyond the limitations of tabular or linear function approximation methods.

4. **Implicit Regularization and Improved Performance:** A notable empirical finding is that training with action-robust criteria not only yields robust policies but also often improves performance even in the absence of adversarial perturbations. This suggests that action-robustness acts as a form of implicit regularization, leading to more generalized and stable policies. This dual benefit could encourage its adoption even when explicit robustness is not the sole objective.

5. **Simplified Uncertainty Specification:** Unlike many robust control or RMDP approaches that require complex definitions of model uncertainty sets or distributions over perturbations, this method primarily relies on a single scalar parameter, $\alpha$ (or potentially a state-dependent $\alpha(s)$). This simplicity in problem formulation can ease implementation and reduce the burden on algorithm designers.

6. **Theoretical Foundations with Empirical Discrepancies:** The paper provides theoretical guarantees for PR-MDP, including the existence of deterministic optimal policies and convergence properties for the Soft PR-PI algorithm in the tabular case. However, it also highlights instances where empirical DRL results diverge from theoretical predictions (e.g., the MaxMin vs. MinMax game for PR-MDP), signaling important avenues for future theoretical work to better understand DRL's behavior in adversarial settings.

In summary, this work provides a practical and effective framework for enhancing the robustness and generalization capabilities of DRL agents in continuous control. Its simplicity, demonstrated performance benefits (even without perturbations), and direct relevance to real-world challenges position it as a valuable approach for developing more reliable autonomous systems.
