# TIP: Token Importance in On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Princeton University and collaborators introduced a two-axis taxonomy for token importance in on-policy knowledge distillation, identifying previously overlooked "overconfident but wrong" error tokens and developing a parameter-free selection score to address this. This method achieved up to 58% peak memory savings and consistently improved student model performance in mathematical reasoning and agentic planning tasks.

## Problem

- Standard on-policy distillation applies supervision to all tokens, which can be computationally inefficient due to redundant or low-value signals.
- Existing token selection methods, often relying solely on student entropy, overlook a critical category of "confident but wrong" errors where the student is certain but incorrect.
- A comprehensive framework was lacking to systematically analyze different types of informative tokens based on student confidence and teacher-student agreement.

## Method

- A ==two-axis taxonomy== **TIP** categorizes tokens based on ==student entropy== and ==teacher-student divergence== into four quadrants.
- Theoretically proved a ==blind spot== for entropy-only methods, showing their inability to identify ==confident but wrong (Q3)== tokens.
- A ==parameter-free Soft-OR score== combines student entropy and teacher-student divergence, recovering the Q3 blind spot for comprehensive token selection.

## Results

- Entropy-based token selection at **50% retention** matched or exceeded baseline on math reasoning while reducing peak memory by up to **47%** (peak savings up to **58%**).
- Training on **<10%** of "overconfident" (Q3) tokens nearly matched or surpassed the all-token baseline accuracy, confirming their dense corrective signal.
- Soft-OR score outperformed entropy-only methods: **79.1%** vs **78.6%** for 50% retention on MATH-500 with Qwen3, with larger gains under aggressive retention.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Token importance in on-policy distillation can be robustly classified by considering both student uncertainty (entropy) and disagreement with the teacher (divergence).
> - "Overconfident and wrong" (Q3) tokens, where the student exhibits low entropy but high divergence, contain crucial corrective learning signals that are missed by entropy-only approaches.
> - Focusing training on a carefully selected subset of tokens based on a combined score (Soft-OR) can lead to both significant memory savings and improved student model performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Knowledge distillation (KD) is a technique that involves transferring capabilities from a larger, more powerful "teacher" model to a smaller "student" model by training the student on the teacher's output distributions. This process is recognized as a key factor in the development of efficient, smaller models that retain significant capacity.

This research specifically focuses on **on-policy knowledge distillation (OPD)**. Unlike classical off-policy KD, where students are trained on teacher-generated sequences, OPD allows the student to generate its own responses (rollouts) and receive token-by-token supervision from the teacher. This approach addresses the train-test distribution mismatch that can occur in off-policy methods. The token-level nature of OPD inherently raises the question of which specific tokens within a student-generated sequence are most informative for learning.

Prior work has explored various forms of importance sampling and curriculum learning, primarily at the example or sequence level. These include methods that weight or order samples by difficulty, or select mini-batch elements based on gradient norms or learned weights. In the context of sequence generation, some approaches select entire rollouts to train on but treat all tokens within those responses uniformly.

More granularly, token-level importance has been studied in related fields like Reinforcement Learning (RL), where high-entropy "forking tokens" have been identified as drivers of gradient signal, and overconfident errors as critical failure modes. In distillation, several methods have attempted token-level interventions:
- AdaSwitch switches between teacher and student guidance based on divergence.
- Entropy-Aware OPD adapts the loss based on teacher entropy.
- SelecTKD uses a propose-and-verify mechanism where the teacher verifies student-proposed tokens.
- LeaF uses gradient-guided comparison for token pruning.
- AdaKD combines a divergence-based token selector (LATF) with token-level temperature scaling.
- EntroDrop drops low-entropy tokens during pretraining to improve generalization.

This paper differentiates itself by proposing a comprehensive taxonomy for token importance in OPD. It moves beyond single-axis views (like entropy-only or divergence-only) to integrate two key metrics, student entropy and teacher-student divergence, into a unified framework. While existing works may touch upon aspects of uncertainty or disagreement, this work provides a theoretical basis for why both are necessary and identifies a specific "blind spot" in common entropy-only approaches, an area often overlooked but containing critical learning signals. The authors explicitly compare their approach to AdaKD's LATF module, arguing that their combined, type-aware selection is structurally distinct and more efficient under constrained budgets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of this research is to identify **which tokens carry the most useful learning signal in on-policy knowledge distillation (OPD)**. This central question is motivated by several factors:

1. **Efficiency and Resource Constraints:** Training large language models (LLMs) via distillation, especially in an on-policy setting where the student generates its own rollouts, is computationally intensive and memory-demanding. If not all tokens contribute equally to effective learning, identifying and prioritizing the most informative ones could significantly reduce training costs (e.g., peak GPU memory, computation time) without sacrificing, or even improving, student performance. The abstract highlights empirical results of significant memory savings, directly addressing this motivation.

2. **Improving Learning Effectiveness:** The implicit assumption in standard OPD, where all tokens are weighted equally, may lead to inefficient gradient updates. Training on "solved" or redundant tokens might dilute the learning signal from more critical positions. By focusing the student's learning on tokens that offer the densest or most critical corrective signals, the quality and speed of knowledge transfer from teacher to student could be enhanced. The paper seeks to demonstrate that selective training can not only match but also surpass all-token training performance.

3. **Addressing Incomplete Views of Token Importance:** The paper observes that existing heuristics or models of token importance, particularly those based solely on student entropy (uncertainty), are structurally incomplete. The authors hypothesize that entropy alone fails to distinguish between "confident and correct" tokens (where no learning is needed) and "confident and wrong" tokens (where strong correction is required despite low student uncertainty). This unaddressed "blind spot" motivates the development of a more nuanced understanding of token importance.

4. **Developing a Principled Framework:** The work aims to organize the understanding of token importance into a comprehensive and theoretically grounded taxonomy. By introducing "TIP" (Token Importance in on-Policy distillation), a two-axis framework combining student entropy and teacher-student divergence, the authors intend to provide a clearer conceptual map for researchers and practitioners, explaining *why* certain tokens are important and *how* different types of tokens contribute to learning. This framework is designed to be parameter-free and leverage information already computed during standard OPD loss calculation, ensuring practical applicability.

In summary, the motivation is to make OPD more efficient and effective by providing a principled, two-dimensional understanding of token importance, which can then be leveraged to design improved token selection strategies that overcome the limitations of simpler heuristics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this paper is structured around a novel two-axis taxonomy, theoretical analysis, and an empirical validation using this framework for token selection.

#### 3.1. Two-Axis Taxonomy (TIP)

The core of the approach is the "Token Importance in on-Policy distillation" (TIP) taxonomy, which classifies each token position based on two quantities already computed during standard OPD training:
- **Student Entropy ($h_t$):** Normalized Shannon entropy of the student's predicted token distribution at position $t$. High $h_t$ indicates student uncertainty; low $h_t$ indicates student confidence.
- **Teacher-Student Divergence ($\delta_t$):** The KL-divergence $D_{KL}(P_S(\cdot|c_t) \parallel P_T(\cdot|c_t))$ between the student's and teacher's token distributions at position $t$. High $\delta_t$ indicates strong disagreement between student and teacher.

Crossing these two axes defines four quadrants:
- **Q1 (High entropy, high divergence):** Student is uncertain and disagrees with the teacher. Characterized as having the "densest signal," requiring error correction or consolidation of fragile knowledge.
- **Q2 (High entropy, low divergence):** Student is uncertain but agrees with the teacher. Represents stabilization signal for underconfident predictions.
- **Q3 (Low entropy, high divergence):** Student is confident but disagrees with the teacher. This is the "overconfident errors" region, a crucial blind spot for entropy-only methods, requiring systematic bias correction.
- **Q4 (Low entropy, low divergence):** Student is confident and agrees with the teacher. These are "solved tokens" with negligible learning signal.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Analysis

The paper presents three theoretical arguments to support the taxonomy:
1. **Oracle Token Weight (Proposition 1):** By approximating the expected loss reduction after one gradient step, the authors derive an oracle token weight ($w_t^*$) that minimizes this bound. This weight is proportional to the alignment between the per-token gradient and the overall loss gradient, inversely proportional to the gradient's squared norm. This analysis suggests that Q1, Q2, and Q3 tokens should receive positive oracle weight due to their corrective signal, while Q4 tokens should receive near-zero weight.
2. **Signal-to-Curvature View:** A local second-order approximation of the per-token loss is presented, where optimal weight depends on the ratio of gradient magnitude squared to Hessian curvature ($||g_t||^2 / (g_t^T H_t g_t)$). This explains why high-entropy tokens are often important (useful signal, higher curvature), but also why Q3 tokens can be important (large signal despite low curvature due to confident but wrong predictions).
3. **Blind Spot of Entropy-Only Scores (Proposition 2):** The paper formally proves that any non-decreasing score based solely on student entropy (e.g., $f(h_t) = h_t$) will assign near-zero weight to Q3 tokens, thereby failing to distinguish between "confident and correct" (Q4) and "confident and wrong" (Q3). This structural blindness motivates the need for divergence in the scoring function.
4. **Soft-OR Score (Remark 1):** To address the blind spot, a parameter-free Soft-OR score is proposed: $s_t = \hat{h}_t + \hat{\delta}_t - \hat{h}_t \cdot \hat{\delta}_t$, where $\hat{h}_t$ and $\hat{\delta}_t$ are min-max normalized student entropy and divergence. This score is designed to be non-zero if either entropy or divergence is non-zero, effectively recovering Q3 tokens while still suppressing Q4 tokens and preserving high-entropy coverage.

#### 3.3. Type-Aware Token Selection Method

Based on the Soft-OR score, the proposed method selects tokens as follows:
1. For each token in a student rollout, calculate $h_t$ and $\delta_t$.
2. Clip the top 2% of $h_t$ values within each batch to suppress outliers.
3. Min-max normalize $h_t$ and $\delta_t$ to obtain $\hat{h}_t, \hat{\delta}_t \in$.
4. Compute the Soft-OR score $s_t$.
5. Select the top-k tokens (based on a retention ratio $\rho$) with the highest $s_t$ values.
6. The training loss $L_{TIP}$ is then computed as the average KL-divergence over the selected tokens.

The method incurs minimal computational overhead, involving only clipping, min-max normalization, and a top-k sort, which is negligible compared to the forward and backward passes.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted across:
- **Model Pairs:** Three teacher-student pairs for mathematical reasoning (Qwen3-8B→4B, Llama-70B→8B, Qwen2.5-14B→1.5B) and one for agentic planning (Qwen3-{14B, 32B}→1.7B).
- **Datasets:** Mathematical reasoning tasks (DAPO for training, MATH-500 and AIME 2024/2025 for evaluation) and agentic planning tasks (DeepPlanning for training and evaluation).
- **Training Details:** AdamW optimizer, cosine learning rate schedule, reverse KL loss on student-generated rollouts.

For the specific experiment isolating Q3 tokens, a "Q3 score" $w_{Q3,t} = \delta_{fwd,t} \cdot (1 - \hat{h}_t)$ was used for ranking, where $\delta_{fwd,t}$ is forward KL-divergence $D_{KL}(P_T \parallel P_S)$ and $(1 - \hat{h}_t)$ represents student confidence. The authors justify using forward KL for ranking in this specific diagnostic experiment because it directly penalizes missing teacher mass, providing a sharper signal for student overconfidence, while the training loss remained the standard reverse KL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments systematically validate the theoretical predictions of the TIP taxonomy across diverse models and tasks.

#### 4.1. High-Entropy Tokens (Q1/Q2)

- **Prediction:** Q1/Q2 tokens, where the student is uncertain, carry substantial learning signal, while Q4 (solved) tokens are negligible. Removing Q4 should improve efficiency.
- **Results (Table 3, Appendix Figure 3):** Retaining 50% of tokens based on entropy-based sampling consistently matched or outperformed the all-token baseline across most mathematical reasoning benchmarks. For instance, Qwen3-8B→4B improved from 76.7% to 78.6% on MATH-500, and Llama-70B→8B improved from 71.0% to 74.0%. This was achieved with significant memory savings, cutting end-to-end peak training memory by up to 47% (e.g., Qwen3 from 72.0GB to 38.1GB at 50% retention). This confirms that a large portion of low-entropy tokens (Q4) are indeed redundant. However, aggressive retention (20%) often led to performance drops, hinting at useful signal being missed in the discarded low-entropy regions.

#### 4.2. Overconfident Tokens (Q3)

- **Prediction:** Entropy-only selection is structurally blind to Q3 tokens (low student entropy, high teacher-student divergence) which, despite their low entropy, carry critical corrective signal (Proposition 2).
- **Results (Table 4):** Training exclusively on Q3 tokens (selected using a confidence-weighted forward KL divergence) with a retention rate as low as 10% nearly matched the full-token baseline across all model pairs for mathematical reasoning. For example, Qwen3-8B→4B achieved 76.1% on MATH-500 with 10% Q3 tokens, compared to 76.7% for the 100% baseline. This demonstrates that Q3 tokens, which entropy-only rules would largely ignore, contain concentrated and valuable corrective signals.
- **Q3 vs. "Large Divergence" (Appendix Table 8):** A detailed ablation comparing Q3-only selection to divergence-only selection (LATF-style) further underscored the importance of the low-entropy conjunct for efficiency. At an equal budget of 10% tokens, Q3-only consistently outperformed divergence-only selection across all benchmarks. Divergence-only selection only caught up when given 5 times more tokens (50% retention), indicating that the combination of low entropy and high divergence is crucial for efficient token selection under tight budgets.

#### 4.3. Type-Aware Selection (TIP / Soft-OR)

- **Prediction:** The Soft-OR score, by combining entropy and divergence, should recover the Q3 blind spot while preserving coverage of Q1/Q2, thereby outperforming entropy-only selection (Remark 1).
- **Results (Table 5, Figure 1):** The Soft-OR score consistently improved over entropy-only selection on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, especially at higher retention ratios (50% and 20%). For instance, on MATH-500 with Qwen3-8B→4B, Soft-OR 50% achieved 79.1% compared to entropy-only 50%'s 78.6% and the baseline's 76.7%. For AIME'25 with Llama-70B→8B, Soft-OR 50% reached 11.5% versus entropy-only 50%'s 7.5% and baseline's 4.9%. The Soft-OR selection also remained competitive on agentic planning tasks.
- **Top vs. Bottom Tokens (Table 6):** Training on the bottom 50% of tokens (primarily Q4) by Soft-OR score yielded substantially lower accuracy compared to the top 50%, confirming that the lowest-scoring tokens carry negligible learning signal.
- **Teacher Entropy (Appendix B.1):** Teacher entropy was found to be near-zero and largely uninformative for token selection, reinforcing that student state and student-teacher disagreement are the critical axes.

#### 4.4. Beyond Mathematical Reasoning: Agentic Planning

- **Results (Table 7, Appendix Figure 4):** The findings generalized to the DeepPlanning benchmark for long-horizon agentic planning. Entropy-based selection at 50% retention matched or exceeded full-token OPD. Notably, training on only 20% of Q3 (overconfident) tokens surpassed full-token OPD for both 14B and 32B teachers (e.g., 12.6% vs. 11.7% for 14B teacher). This suggests that correcting overconfident errors is particularly impactful in tasks where a single confident misstep can invalidate an entire multi-step plan.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Examples (Appendix B.5)

Five qualitative examples illustrate the different quadrants, demonstrating:
- Q3 tokens where the student is extremely confident but fundamentally wrong (e.g., repeating a generic variable instead of substituting a concrete value, arithmetic errors, confident logical missteps), and where entropy-only rules would fail.
- Q1 tokens where the student is uncertain, and the teacher provides a clear corrective signal (e.g., suggesting a more precise reasoning step or correct mathematical symbol).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a refined understanding of token importance in on-policy knowledge distillation (OPD), offering both theoretical insights and practical advancements.

1. **Conceptual Framework:** The introduction of TIP, a two-axis taxonomy based on student entropy and teacher-student divergence, establishes a more comprehensive framework for analyzing learning signals in OPD. This framework elucidates why existing entropy-only methods are partially effective but structurally incomplete, particularly in their inability to detect "overconfident errors" (Q3). This conceptual clarity can guide future research in designing more sophisticated distillation strategies.

2. **Improved Efficiency and Resource Management:** By demonstrating that a significant portion of tokens (primarily Q4, "solved" tokens) contribute negligibly to learning, and that selective training can match or exceed full-token performance, the work offers substantial practical benefits. The reported memory savings (up to 47% at 50% retention, 58% at more aggressive retention) directly address the critical resource constraints associated with distilling large language models, making OPD more accessible and cost-effective.

3. **Enhanced Student Performance:** The consistent observation that type-aware selection (Soft-OR) and even Q3-only training can surpass full-token OPD highlights the potential for better learning outcomes. By focusing the training signal on the most informative tokens (Q1, Q2, and especially Q3), the student model can learn more effectively, avoiding dilution of gradients from irrelevant or redundant data points. The strong performance of Q3-only training on agentic planning tasks, where overconfident errors have significant downstream consequences, underscores the critical value of correcting such specific token types.

4. **Broader Applicability:** The authors suggest that this two-axis framing could be applicable to other settings involving on-policy, token-level supervision, such as process reward fine-tuning and speculative decoding. This indicates the potential for the TIP taxonomy and derived selection methods to influence a wider range of language model training and deployment paradigms.

5. **Direct Comparison and Advancement of Prior Work:** By explicitly comparing its approach with existing methods, such as AdaKD's divergence-only token selection, the paper clarifies the unique contribution of combining student uncertainty and disagreement. This distinction demonstrates that a nuanced, two-dimensional view offers superior efficiency under constrained conditions, advancing the state-of-the-art in token-level distillation.

A stated limitation is the scope of models (up to 70B teacher) and rollout lengths (up to 16K tokens); the persistence of these findings at trillion-parameter scales or with extremely long agentic tool-calling pipelines remains an open question for future investigation.
