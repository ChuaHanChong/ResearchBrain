# Humanoid Robot Acrobatics Utilizing Complete Articulated Rigid Body Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a control architecture enabling humanoid robots to perform highly dynamic acrobatic motions like jumps and flips by explicitly utilizing complete articulated rigid body dynamics throughout planning and control. The framework, which avoids model approximations, demonstrates successful execution of complex maneuvers in simulation with accurate trajectory tracking, robust constraint handling, and precise terminal state control.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots with high degrees of freedom face computational intractability when planning highly dynamic, acrobatic motions using their complete equations of motion.
- Current methods often rely on model simplifications (e.g., constant inertia, linearized dynamics) or restrict robots to quasi-static motions, leading to degraded performance in real-world dynamic tasks.
- Integrating robust constraint handling (joint limits, collision avoidance) into highly dynamic motion planning for complex robots remains challenging without sacrificing performance.

## Method

- A control architecture combining trajectory optimization and operational-space whole-body control is developed to explicitly use complete articulated rigid body dynamics.
- The '==Strangely Supported Deformable Potato==' (SSDP) model serves as a novel abstraction for trajectory optimization, simplifying the problem while retaining critical dynamic properties like adjustable rotational inertia.
- Acrobatic motions are decomposed into launch, flight, and landing phases, each optimized with a quadratic program (QP) and controlled by a multi-layered, prioritized whole-body controller.

## Results

- The simulated humanoid robot successfully executed various acrobatic maneuvers, including box jumps (up and down), a 360° front flip, and a 180° twisting jump.
- The system demonstrated accurate tracking of planned center-of-mass (COM) position, velocity, and angular momentum trajectories across all phases, maintaining contact stability and precise control of torso orientation via inertia shaping during flight.
- Robustness analysis showed precise touchdown orientation control (mean error **0.094**°, standard deviation **0.020**°) across randomly sampled targets, alongside effective avoidance of joint limits and self/external collisions during aggressive movements like the 360° front flip.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex acrobatic motions are achievable in high-DOF humanoids by planning and controlling directly on complete articulated rigid body dynamics, avoiding performance-degrading approximations.
> - Model abstraction, such as the SSDP, can effectively bridge the gap between high-level trajectory planning and low-level whole-body control, enabling efficient optimization for dynamic tasks.
> - Hierarchical whole-body control allows for robust simultaneous management of primary tasks (e.g., COM trajectory, angular momentum), high-priority constraints (e.g., collision avoidance, joint limits), and lower-priority posture regulation during aggressive maneuvers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of executing highly dynamic locomotion, such as jumping, hopping, flipping, and spinning, is a long-standing objective in robotics research. This capability is considered fundamental for enabling humanoid robots to operate effectively in complex human environments, as these dynamic motions often generalize simpler maneuvers like walking and balancing.

Prior research in humanoid locomotion has explored various foundational methods. The Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) method and its extensions have been significant for stable walking. The Spring-Loaded Inverted Pendulum (SLIP) model offers insights into dynamic locomotion. Early work also investigated angular momentum-based walking controllers. A seminal branch of research by Marc Raibert on hopping and legged systems led to the development of highly capable robotic systems by Boston Dynamics, although their specific implementation details often remain proprietary.

More recent advancements towards jumping and highly dynamic motions include the use of centroidal angular momentum control with inverse dynamics for vertical jumps, simplified centroidal dynamics models combined with full kinematics for acrobatic motion planning, dynamic planning and control frameworks for jumping that approximate legs as cuboids, and models using a single rigid body for the robot's base with point masses for legs to demonstrate twisting and forward jumps. Additionally, variable inertia model predictive control has been explored for fast bipedal maneuvers using pre-trained neural networks for inertia tensor approximation.

A central challenge in this field stems from the high degree-of-freedom (DOF) count of humanoid robots, which leads to exponentially scaling complexities in computing explicit equations of motion. This complexity makes trajectory optimization based on these complete dynamics intractable, especially for real-time applications like Model Predictive Control (MPC). Current workarounds typically involve:
a) Limiting robots to quasi-static motions where fast dynamics can be neglected.
b) Employing model simplifications and approximations (e.g., potato models with constant inertial properties) to enable faster optimization, which often results in degraded performance on the physical system due to inaccuracies.
c) Designing robots with specific compositions (e.g., lightweight limbs, heavy torsos) to better match simplified models.

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a control architecture that explicitly utilizes the unabbreviated equations of motion of the articulated rigid body model throughout planning and control. It aims to achieve highly dynamic, acrobatic motions without relying on model approximations or restricting the robot to quasi-static behaviors, thereby contributing to the development of more robust and capable humanoid systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable humanoid robots to perform highly dynamic, acrobatic motions – such as jumps, flips, and twists – by utilizing a control architecture that accounts for the complete articulated rigid body dynamics. This work aims to overcome the performance degradation and computational intractability associated with conventional model approximations and simplifications.

The motivation for pursuing this objective is multi-faceted:
1. **Deployment in Human Environments**: Humanoid robots are intended for operation in environments designed for humans. These environments necessitate the ability to execute a wide range of motions, including highly dynamic maneuvers, which often represent a generalization of simpler locomotion tasks like walking, running, and balancing. Achieving human-level acrobatics would significantly broaden the accessibility of diverse environments for robots.
2. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Approaches**: Current methodologies often face trade-offs between computational feasibility and dynamic accuracy. Linearization methods and model approximations typically used in trajectory optimization fail to produce optimal or reliable performance on the true robotic system due to their inherent inaccuracies. Planning on explicit, non-linear equations of motion for high-DOF humanoids is generally computationally intractable. This research is motivated by the need for a solution that avoids these compromises.
3. **Enhancing Robotic Capabilities**: By developing a control architecture that operates on the complete underlying physics, the work seeks to endow humanoid robots with a more fundamental and robust ability to interact with their environment dynamically. This includes controlling complex behaviors such as constraint handling (joint limits, collision avoidance) and posture regulation, all while executing aggressive, non-static movements.
4. **Advancing Control and Planning Frameworks**: The paper aims to demonstrate a novel integration of trajectory optimization and whole-body control, intermediated by a specific model abstraction, that allows for precise planning and execution of complex, dynamic tasks. This approach is intended to serve as a foundation for future advancements in dynamic robot control.

In summary, the core motivation is to achieve advanced acrobatic capabilities in humanoid robots by developing a robust and accurate control framework that operates on the complete dynamics, without performance-degrading approximations, thereby expanding the potential applications and versatility of these robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented involves a control architecture comprising trajectory optimization and whole-body control, facilitated by a specific model abstraction. This architecture is designed to enable acrobatic maneuvers while adhering to the complete articulated rigid body dynamics without approximations.

#### 3.1. Underlying Models and Control Principles

- **Articulated Rigid Body Dynamics**: The robot is modeled as an articulated rigid body dynamical system with `n` generalized (minimal) coordinates, including both active and passive degrees of freedom (e.g., six free-floating coordinates for the torso). The equations of motion are expressed as `M(q) ¨q + b(q, ˙q) + g(q) = Γ + J⊤s Fs`.
- **Operational-Space Based Whole-Body Control (OPWBC)**: A multi-layered, prioritized control framework is utilized. Tasks, constraints, and posture behaviors are defined in their respective operational spaces.
 - **Task Dynamics**: The dynamic behavior of a task (associated with Jacobian `Jt`) is derived as `Λt ˙ϑt + μt + pt = J⊤t Γ = Ft`. The control force `Ft` for the task applies to a decoupled unit-mass system.
 - **Control Torque Decomposition**: The control torque `Γ` is decomposed into contributions from task, posture, and constraints using dynamically consistent null-space projections. This ensures lower-priority behaviors are consistent with higher-priority ones.
 - **Prioritization**: Constraints (`Fc`) are given the highest priority, followed by the main task (`Ft|c`), and then posture control (`Fp|t|c`). This creates a hierarchical control structure: `Γ = J⊤c Fc + J⊤t|c Ft|c + J⊤p|t|c Fp|t|c`.
 - **Underactuation**: The system's underactuation (due to the free-floating base) is managed by mapping extended control torques to actuated joint torques using a selection matrix `U`.

#### 3.2. Centroidal Quantities

The centroidal model represents the entire robot's inertial properties as a single rigid body (a centroid) with equivalent inertial quantities at a given configuration. Key centroidal quantities used include:
- **Centroidal Mass (`m_centroid`)**: Sum of all body masses.
- **Rotational Inertia Tensor (`⁰I_centroid`)**: Sum of individual body inertias transformed to the centroidal frame.
- **Linear Momentum (`h_lin`)**: `m_centroid * v_com`.
- **Angular Momentum (`h_ang`)**: Related to generalized velocities `˙q` by the Centroidal Momentum Matrix `H_ang` (`h_ang = H_ang ˙q`).

#### 3.3. Planning and Trajectory Optimization

Trajectory optimization is crucial for highly dynamic motions over an extended time horizon. The approach introduces a novel model abstraction:

- **Strangely Supported Deformable Potato (SSDP) Model**: This abstraction serves as an intermediary between high-level planning and low-level control. It models the robot as a single rigid body (a "deformable potato") with constant total mass but *adjustable* rotational inertia. "Strangely supported" refers to the abstract, massless support geometries (e.g., feet) that apply external forces without representing the robot's detailed kinematics. This abstraction significantly reduces the dimensionality of the optimization problem while retaining critical dynamic properties.
- **Jump Phases**: Acrobatic motions are divided into three phases:
 1. **Launch**: From initialization to takeoff. External contact forces are applied to accelerate the robot's center-of-mass (COM) and angular momentum to desired takeoff states.
 2. **Flight**: From takeoff to touchdown. No external forces act on the robot; the COM follows a ballistic trajectory, and total angular momentum is conserved. The robot's rotational velocity can be modulated by shaping its inertia.
 3. **Landing**: From touchdown to rest. External contact forces dissipate kinetic and potential energies.

- **Launch Trajectory Optimization**: A quadratic program (QP) is formulated to minimize the sum square norm of contact forces. Constraints include:
 - Discretized dynamics of COM and angular momentum.
 - Boundary conditions for COM position/velocity and angular momentum at start and takeoff.
 - Contact friction limits (`PF_foot[k] ≼ 0`).
 - Center-of-Pressure (CoP) location within the support zone (`A_support p[k] ≼ b_support`).
 - COM path constraints.
 Takeoff velocity is determined using a ballistic target solution with a pre-defined launch angle.

- **Flight Trajectory Optimization**: This phase leverages the conservation of angular momentum (`h = I_centroid ω = const.`) and the ability to shape the robot's inertia. The QP aims to achieve a desired orientation at touchdown by minimizing changes in inertia. Constraints include:
 - Kinematic integration of orientation and angular velocity.
 - Boundary conditions for orientation and angular velocity at takeoff and touchdown.
 - Angular momentum conservation.
 - Inertia ('path') constraints (`I_min ≼ I[k] ≼ I_max`).
 Crucially, the whole-body controller must ensure that angular momentum contributions from actuated joints (`h_act`) vanish to allow the `h = I_centroid ω` relationship to hold for the torso's rotation.

- **Landing Trajectory Optimization**: Similar to the launch phase, this QP focuses on dissipating kinetic and potential energies back to a stable steady-state while respecting contact constraints.

#### 3.4. Whole-Body Controller Implementation

The controller operates on a rigid multi-body dynamical system with a free-floating base (6-DOF minimal coordinates for torso, `q_joints` for actuated joints). It uses distinct dynamics model abstractions for each task, which are projections of the complete model into lower-dimensional task-spaces.

- **Launch Controller**:
 - **Task**: Control of the robot's center-of-mass (`ϑ_com`) and total angular momentum (`ϑ_h`).
 - **Constraints (Highest Priority)**: Joint limits (`ϑ_joint_limit`), external collision avoidance (`ϑ_collision`), and self-collision avoidance (`ϑ_self_collision`). Repulsive forces are computed using a Firas function.
 - **Posture (Lowest Priority)**: Torso orientation and limb posture (`ϑ_torso_orientation`, `ϑ_limb_posture`).
 The unified Jacobian `J_launch_⊗` is computed, and consistent with support contact, `J_⊗|s` and `F_launch_⊗|s` are determined to compute `Γ_launch`.

- **Flight Controller**:
 - **Task**: Inertia shaping (`ϑ_I`), ensuring actuated joint contributions to angular momentum are controlled (`ϑ_h_act`), and preparing the landing foot's linear position and orientation (`ϑ_foot`). `J_I` (inertia Jacobian) ensures off-diagonal terms are considered to avoid undesired rotations. `J_h_act` is crucial for eliminating active joint contributions to total angular momentum.
 - **Constraints**: Joint limits and collision avoidance.
 - **Posture**: Limb posture (`ϑ_limb_posture`).
 There are no support Jacobians as the robot is in the air.

- **Landing Controller**: Similar to the launch controller, it focuses on dissipating energy and maintaining contact stability, enforcing the optimal trajectories planned during optimization.

#### 3.5. Simulation Environment

Simulations are conducted in Mujoco (version 3.2) with a 1ms integration time step. The robot model has 18 hinge joints plus a free-floating base, totaling 24 DOF. Each link is modeled as a rigid cuboid. IPOPT (version 3.14) is used as the underlying solver for trajectory optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of the proposed control architecture was evaluated through various acrobatic maneuvers in simulation, highlighting different aspects of the controller's performance.

#### 4.1. Box Jump (Up and Down)

- **Maneuver Execution**: The robot successfully executed box jump-up and jump-down maneuvers, transitioning from a single-leg balance to a jump and landing on an elevated platform or the ground.
- **Trajectory Tracking (Launch)**: During the launch phase, the controller accurately tracked the desired center-of-mass (COM) position, velocity, and angular momentum trajectories (Figure 6). The resultant foot-ground contact forces were maintained within the static friction limits, and the center-of-pressure (CoP) remained within the foot support zone, ensuring balance and preventing slippage (Figure 7).
- **Flight Control (Inertia Shaping)**: The flight phase demonstrated the importance of inertia shaping. The robot modulated its rotational inertia (e.g., through limb extension) to achieve the desired torso orientation at touchdown (Figure 8). Controlling the actuated joints' contributions to angular momentum was critical to ensure that inertia shaping primarily influenced the torso's rotation.
- **Trajectory Tracking (Landing)**: The landing controller effectively dissipated the robot's kinetic and potential energies. COM position converged to a stable resting state within support boundaries, and COM velocity and angular momentum were successfully brought to zero. The planned and simulated resultant contact force trajectories, including magnitude, direction, and CoP, showed close alignment (Figures 9 and 10).

#### 4.2. Robustness Analysis

- **Arbitrary Targets**: Fifty jumping simulations were performed to randomly sampled target COM positions (`x_touchdown_com ∈ [1.2m, 2m]`) and torso orientations (`q_touchdown_torso_y ∈ [10°, 40°]`).
- **Performance**: The robot generally achieved the desired landing configurations. A slight bias in COM position was observed (mean error 0.888cm, standard deviation 0.288cm), which is attributed to unmodeled contact dynamics during launch. Touchdown orientation was precisely accomplished (mean error 0.094°, standard deviation 0.020°), primarily due to the controllability afforded by inertia shaping during flight (Figure 12).

#### 4.3. 360° Front Flip and Constraints

- **Complex Maneuver**: The robot successfully performed a complete 360° front flip.
- **Constraint Avoidance**: A key finding was the controller's ability to avoid joint limits and both self-collisions (e.g., ankle-torso) and external collisions (e.g., knee-floor) *while* simultaneously tracking the abstract COM and momentum trajectories planned by the optimizer. The Firas function effectively generated repulsive forces when distances approached activation thresholds (Figure 14). This demonstrates that the high number of degrees-of-freedom in the humanoid model is beneficial for resolving task conflicts and maintaining safety.
- **Launch Planning**: The optimizer's ability to plan an initial lowering of the COM before acceleration and a coordinated angular momentum ramp ensured that ground contact forces remained within the support zone during the complex launch phase (Figure 15).
- **Inertia Shaping for Rotation Control**: During the flight phase of the front flip, the robot launched with excess angular momentum. The flight controller correctly elevated the system's inertia (by spreading limbs) to slow down the angular velocity, enabling the robot to land at the desired orientation while avoiding joint limits (Figure 16).

#### 4.4. 180° Twisting Jump - Posture Control

- **Maneuver**: The robot executed a 180° twisting jump about the global z-axis.
- **Posture Goal Achievement**: This experiment highlighted the role of posture control in achieving a desired final robot configuration. At touchdown, the torso was initially angled, but the posture controller successfully transitioned it into the desired upright and axis-aligned state at termination (Figure 18). This demonstrates the controller's ability to manage remaining null-space after higher-priority tasks, ensuring stable and aesthetically desired final states.

**Overall Findings**: The simulations consistently demonstrated that the proposed control architecture effectively enables humanoid robots to perform a variety of highly dynamic, acrobatic motions. Crucially, this was achieved without approximating the articulated rigid body dynamics, validating the approach's ability to leverage complete physics for accurate trajectory tracking, robust constraint handling, and precise terminal state control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics by addressing the fundamental challenge of enabling highly dynamic, acrobatic motions without relying on simplified dynamic models. The proposed control architecture offers several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Advancement in Dynamic Control**: The work demonstrates that it is feasible to plan and control complex acrobatic maneuvers for high-DOF humanoid robots using their complete articulated rigid body dynamics. This moves beyond common limitations of model approximations (e.g., constant inertia centroidal models, linearizations) which often lead to degraded performance in real-world applications. By operating with full physical awareness, the system can potentially achieve higher fidelity and robustness in dynamic tasks.
2. **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Versatility**: Endowing humanoid robots with acrobatic capabilities, as demonstrated, significantly expands their operational envelope. Such robots could potentially navigate highly unstructured or complex environments that require dynamic re-orientation, jumping over obstacles, or rapidly changing their base of support. This ability is a generalization of more basic locomotion skills and moves robots closer to human-level agility.
3. **Improved Safety and Reliability**: The integration of constraint handling (joint limits, self-collision, and environmental collision avoidance) directly within the whole-body controller is critical. This ensures that even during aggressive, high-speed maneuvers planned at an abstract level, the robot maintains physical integrity and avoids damaging itself or its surroundings. This is a crucial step towards deploying dynamic humanoids safely in real-world settings.
4. **Foundation for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning and Optimization**: By demonstrating a robust model-based approach that can explicitly handle complete dynamics and complex constraints, this work provides a strong foundation for future research. It could inform the design of more accurate and efficient sampling-based planners, or serve as a robust baseline for reinforcement learning techniques that aim to synthesize complex behaviors.
5. **Generalizability Across Robot Platforms**: As a model-based approach, the architecture is designed to generalize to various robot types with distinct kinematics and mass properties. This suggests that the principles demonstrated for the simulated humanoid could be adapted to other legged robots or complex multi-body systems, potentially accelerating development across different robotic platforms.
6. **Future Research Directions**: The paper outlines immediate extensions, such as integrating the trajectory optimizer into a Model Predictive Control (MPC) architecture. This would offer additional robustness by continually re-planning trajectories based on real-time feedback, further enhancing the system's adaptability to disturbances and unmodeled dynamics. Additionally, improvements in contact dynamics modeling and support for multiple contact surfaces (e.g., bipedal landing) are natural next steps that could further broaden the applicability of the framework.

In conclusion, this research marks a step forward in robustly enabling complex, highly dynamic behaviors in humanoid robots. By reconciling the need for computationally tractable planning with the requirement for physical accuracy, it contributes to the development of more capable, versatile, and safer robotic systems poised for deployment in human-centric environments.
