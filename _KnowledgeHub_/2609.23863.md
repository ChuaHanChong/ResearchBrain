# Grounded Action Model: 3D Grounding as a Foundation for Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Grounded Action Model (GAM) builds a robot policy on a frozen promptable 3D grounding backbone (WildDet3D) instead of language or video backbones. Language, point, or box prompts become object-centric image tokens and metric point-cloud detection tokens, fused with state history in a flow-matching MM-DiT action head. GAM reaches 55.3% on RoboTwin 2.0, 47.6% under randomization, and 61% on LIBERO-PRO.

## Problem

- VLA and WAM backbones are pretrained on language or video generation objectives that never require metric 3D grounding of task objects, leaving it to be learned implicitly from demonstrations.
- Whole-scene policies latch onto visual correlations in limited demonstrations and collapse under background, distractor, or appearance changes; FastWAM falls from 77.8% to 1.9% under randomization.
- Existing VLAs memorize training-layout trajectories rather than grounding instructions, scoring mostly near zero on LIBERO-PRO when targets are relocated or a different object is newly designated.

## Method

- Promptable 3D grounding foundation: a frozen **WildDet3D** backbone resolves language (via a Flan-T5 span-tagging head), 2D points, or 2D boxes into per-object 2D boxes, metric 3D boxes, depth maps, and features.
- **Object-centric observation**: image tokens keep only grid cells overlapping task-object boxes or the URDF-projected robot arm, while detection tokens encode cropped object point clouds with ==octant pooling==, center, extents, and rotation.
- **Multi-stream MM-DiT action head**: 12 blocks with per-stream weights fuse image, detection, and state-history tokens, inject language through ==adaptive layer norm==, and ==flow-match== joint-position chunks; a **Molmo2** planner drives it via point prompts.

## Results

- RoboTwin 2.0 (50 tasks, clean-scene training only): **55.3%** average versus **52.0%** Spatial Forcing and **45.0%** single-task π0.5; **47.6%** under randomization versus **30.4%** next best, retaining **76%** of clean performance.
- LIBERO-PRO: **0.61** average over 16 perturbations versus **0.53** for π0.5, reaching **0.88** on Spatial-Task and **0.60** on Spatial-Pos where best baselines reach **0.01** and **0.26**; trails on object-appearance/size perturbations.
- Real robots under shift: GAM keeps **17/20** on bimanual YAM versus π0.5's **4/20**, and **49.8%** Franka OOD step completion with Molmo2 versus **24.0%**; ablating object-centric filtering drops success **46.8%** to **10.3%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Backbone pretraining objective matters: a foundation trained for promptable metric 3D grounding can beat language- or video-generation backbones for manipulation, especially under scene shift.
> - 3D input alone does not buy robustness; selecting what enters the observation does. DP3's whole-scene point clouds collapse under randomization, while task-object filtering preserves performance.
> - Explicit target prompts decouple what to act on from how to act, enabling planner composition and new-target generalization, but grounding errors propagate unrecovered and filtering can hide unselected obstacles.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot policy can fail when a target object moves to a new location or when distractors change—not because the policy misunderstood the instruction, but because it associated learned actions with memorized positions and appearances rather than with the selected object's current geometry. GAM addresses this by making grounding explicit: language, a 2D point, or a 2D box first selects the task objects; a frozen 3D model then supplies their visual features, 3D locations, sizes, and orientations; and an action head uses those grounded observations to predict joint-position action chunks.

Left: the same manipulation specified by a 2D box, text, and a point. Center: those prompts processed through a 3D grounding model into image and detection tokens. Right: in-domain and out-of-domain scenes with oriented 3D boxes around selected objects.

### 2. Grounding before acting

For each selected object, the frozen 3D grounding model WildDet3D returns a 2D box, an oriented metric 3D box, a dense metric depth map, and visual features. These enter the action policy as two complementary streams. **Image tokens** retain pooled visual-feature cells overlapping selected-object boxes or the robot-arm silhouette, zeroing the rest, so distractor appearance is suppressed while the object–gripper relationship remains. **Detection tokens** back-project depth into the robot-base frame, crop 512 points inside each selected 3D box, and encode point-cloud shape, center, extent, orientation, and normalized 2D box. A state-history token records current and previous joint positions, and a multi-stream transformer combines all streams with language to predict a chunk of absolute joint-position targets plus gripper commands. At deployment, a high-level Molmo2 planner can select a new object at each subtask while GAM's low-level action policy remains unchanged.

Text, 2D boxes, and tracked points enter the frozen 3D grounding model; image and detection adapters create separate token streams; state history and language join them in condition fusion and MM-DiT; the output is an action chunk. The diagram describes the proposed computation, not measured performance.

Fixed-backbone observation ablations on 10 RoboTwin tasks separate the contributions of these streams. The full image-plus-detection model averages 46.8%; removing image masking gives 22.3%, removing point cropping gives 12.8%, and removing both gives 10.3%. Using only detection tokens averages 16.0%, and using only image tokens averages 20.3%. The evidence supports two claims together: excluding irrelevant scene content matters, and appearance plus metric geometry are complementary. It does not prove that grounding is the only causal factor in every benchmark.

### 3. The gain under spatial change

RoboTwin 2.0 reports success as the fraction of rollouts completing the task, averaged across 50 tasks under a clean "Easy" setting and a randomized "Hard" setting. GAM is trained only on 50 clean-scene demonstrations per task. GAM reaches 47.62% Hard success and 62.96% Easy success, for a 55.29% average; π0.5 reaches 25.90% Hard and 44.95% average. Leaderboard entries include both single-task and co-trained systems, so this is not a universal matched-training comparison. Spatial Forcing—which aligns VLA visual embeddings with a pretrained 3D model rather than making explicit detections the policy's input—reaches 26.70% Hard success. The margin is largest in the Hard setting, which the authors interpret as arising from task-object filtering plus metric localization; the comparison establishes correlation with the design, not that distractor filtering is the sole cause.

On LIBERO-PRO, GAM scores 0.61 versus 0.53 for π0.5. The strongest evidence for the mechanism is in Pos (relocation) and Task (new target designation), where GAM reaches 0.60/0.47 on Spatial/Object Pos and 0.88/0.50 on Spatial/Object Task. The authors note the exception of Goal-Pos, where π0.5 leads 0.38 to 0.19. GAM trails on Obj (appearance or size change), especially Goal-Obj (0.69) and LIBERO-10-Obj (0.50), because unseen object sizes can require unseen grasp adjustments.

On the bimanual YAM under visual shift, GAM achieves 17/20 successes versus 4/20 for π0.5, with retention rates of 89% and 21%. On the Franka with a Molmo2 planner selecting targets through points, GAM averages 64.7% in-distribution and 49.8% out-of-distribution step completion, versus 42.4%/24.0% for π0.5. These results demonstrate planner composition under the tested shifts, not autonomous long-horizon reasoning by GAM alone.

Franka step-completion rates (completed steps divided by total steps) across four tasks and an average, comparing in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions. GAM with Molmo2 planner is highest in every shown group.

When the selected object moves or is re-designated, GAM changes the policy's object-centered observation rather than requiring a memorized scene-to-action mapping. Grounding errors propagate into actions, and filtering to selected objects can hide relevant context such as unselected obstacles, so robustness is demonstrated under the tested shifts, not guaranteed universally.
