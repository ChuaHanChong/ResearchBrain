# Exploring Advanced LLM Multi-Agent Systems Based on Blackboard Architecture

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Chinese Academy of Sciences propose LbMAS, a Large Language Model multi-agent system built on the classical blackboard architecture. This system enables dynamic agent collaboration and shared information access, achieving superior performance on complex reasoning and mathematical tasks while significantly reducing token consumption by avoiding pre-training requirements.

## Problem

- Existing LLM multi-agent systems often use fixed architectures, limiting their generality and adaptability to diverse tasks.
- Dynamic LLM multi-agent systems frequently require a costly pre-optimization or supervised training phase to define their workflows, restricting real-time adaptation and generalizability.
- Many LLM multi-agent systems struggle with complex, ill-structured problems due to insufficient shared information and rigid collaboration mechanisms.

## Method

- Proposing the ==blackboard-based LLM multi-agent system (bMAS)== framework, with LbMAS as its first implementation, which centralizes information sharing and communication through a shared 'blackboard'.
- Developing an LLM-based ==Control Unit== that dynamically selects suitable agents based on the query and current blackboard content, enabling flexible and adaptive collaboration.
- Incorporating a mix of dynamically generated ==expert agents== and predefined operational agents (e.g., Decider, Planner, Critic, Conflict-Resolver, Cleaner) to facilitate iterative problem-solving and self-correction on the blackboard.

## Results

- LbMAS achieved the best average performance across six diverse benchmarks (MMLU, ARC-Challenge, GPQA-Diamond, BBH-dateunderstand, MATH, GSM8K), outperforming Chain-of-Thought methods by an average of **4.33%** and static Multi-Agent Systems by **5.02%**.
- On the MATH benchmark, LbMAS incurred the second lowest total token cost (**4,721,489** tokens) while achieving the best performance (**72.60%**), demonstrating significant token efficiency compared to training-based autonomous MASs.
- Ablation studies confirmed the crucial role of the LLM-based control unit in token efficiency and the necessity of active blackboard content management (cleaning) for maintaining performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Revitalizing the classical blackboard architecture effectively enables LLM-based multi-agent systems to achieve dynamic, adaptive collaboration.
> - The blackboard serves as a highly efficient shared context mechanism, allowing agents to dynamically adjust their contributions and significantly reducing token consumption compared to other dynamic multi-agent systems.
> - Integrating an LLM-based control unit facilitates autonomous agent selection and execution, leading to robust performance without the need for pre-training or fixed workflows.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research on Large Language Model (LLM) Multi-Agent Systems (MASs) is a rapidly evolving field, building upon the impressive capabilities of individual LLMs in tasks such as planning, reasoning, and code generation. The broader landscape can be generally categorized by the sophistication of collaboration mechanisms among agents.

Initially, LLM-based MASs often adopted **fixed architectures**. These systems relied on pre-defined agent roles and communication protocols, which, while effective for specific tasks, inherently lacked generality. Their manual construction made them cumbersome to adapt to diverse problem domains. Examples of such static MASs include systems employing debate mechanisms (e.g., Chateval, Exchange-of-Thought, MultiPersona) where agents engage in structured argumentation to refine solutions.

More recently, the field has seen the emergence of **dynamic or autonomous MASs**. These systems aim to configure their structures and communication strategies adaptively, often based on task requirements or environmental feedback. Approaches like DyLAN, GPTSwarm, ADAS, AFlow, and MaAS fall into this category. While more flexible than fixed architectures, many dynamic MASs typically involve a two-step process: an initial, often time-consuming, supervised training or search phase to optimize the multi-agent workflow, followed by execution. This dependence on prior training data and a simplified search space limits their on-the-fly adaptability and generalizability, particularly when well-defined structures or training samples are unavailable for novel, complex problems.

The work by Han and Zhang positions itself as a significant advancement by reintroducing the **blackboard architecture** into this LLM-based MAS landscape. The blackboard architecture, originally proposed in the 1980s (e.g., Nii, 1986; Hayes-Roth, 1985), is a decentralized problem-solving paradigm. It emulates human experts collaborating around a shared workspace (the "blackboard"), where each expert contributes their knowledge incrementally until a solution is reached. This classical AI concept is inherently designed for complex, ill-structured problems requiring heterogeneous knowledge sources and dynamic control.

By incorporating the blackboard architecture, this paper addresses the limitations of both fixed and existing dynamic MASs. Unlike fixed systems, it offers inherent flexibility. Critically, unlike many contemporary dynamic MASs, it bypasses the need for an explicit, pre-solving optimization or training step. Instead, it enables a **truly dynamic adaptation of collaboration mechanisms** in real-time, based on the evolving state of the shared information on the blackboard. This allows for a more autonomous and adaptive problem-solving process, effectively reviving a powerful, albeit older, AI paradigm with the robust capabilities of modern LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **propose and implement a novel LLM multi-agent system (MAS) built upon the classical blackboard architecture**, thereby addressing limitations observed in existing LLM-based MAS paradigms.

The core motivations driving this work are:

- **Enhancing Information Sharing and Collaboration:** Traditional LLM-based MASs often involve explicit message passing or limited shared memory. The authors aim to enable agents with diverse roles to **share all information and messages** throughout the entire problem-solving process via a central, accessible blackboard. This shared context is intended to foster more comprehensive collaboration and consistency.
- **Achieving Dynamic Agent Selection and Execution:** Current MASs, particularly static ones, rely on pre-defined roles and fixed interaction patterns, lacking generality. Even dynamic MASs often involve costly pre-optimization steps. The authors seek a system where agents are **selected dynamically based on the current content of the blackboard**, allowing for flexible and adaptive collaboration. The iterative selection and execution process continues until a consensus or solution is achieved on the blackboard.
- **Improving Generality and Adaptability for Complex Problems:** Many real-world problems are ill-structured, without clear workflows or pre-definable collaboration mechanisms. The research is motivated by the need for a MAS that can **enable complex and dynamic problem-solving where well-defined structures or workflows are unavailable**. The blackboard architecture, historically robust for such domains, is posited as a natural fit when augmented with LLMs.
- **Optimizing Resource (Token) Consumption:** Efficient use of LLM tokens is crucial for practical MAS deployments. The proposed system aims to be **token-economical** by centralizing context on the blackboard, potentially reducing redundant information in individual agent prompts and allowing for more substantive discussions within token constraints.
- **Revitalizing a Classic AI Paradigm:** A significant underlying motivation is to demonstrate how the power of modern LLMs can **revive and facilitate the development of agentic systems** by reintroducing the time-tested blackboard architecture, proving its continued relevance in the era of large language models.

In essence, the authors seek to build a more autonomous, flexible, and efficient multi-agent system that can dynamically adapt its collaboration strategy to solve a wide array of problems, particularly those lacking clear, pre-defined solution paths.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose the **blackboard-based LLM multi-agent system (bMAS)** as a general framework and present **LbMAS** as its first concrete implementation. The core methodology revolves around three main components interacting through an iterative cycle, supplemented by an agent generation module.

#### 3.1. Core Components of bMAS

1. **Blackboard:** This is the central, shared information repository, analogous to a public workspace. It is functionally divided into:
 - **Public Space:** Stores all dialogues and knowledge accessible to every agent. This serves as the sole communication medium, effectively replacing individual agent memory modules. Agents read the entire blackboard content as context for their LLM prompts.
 - **Private Spaces:** Designed for specific, focused interactions, such as debates or self-reflection among a subset of agents (e.g., for conflict resolution or detailed verification).
2. **Agent Group:** A collection of LLM-based agents, each with predefined or dynamically generated roles, capabilities, and actions.
3. **Control Unit (ConU):** An LLM-based component responsible for orchestrating the MAS. It takes the query and the current blackboard content as input and dynamically selects suitable agents from the agent group to act in the next round of problem-solving.

#### 3.2. LbMAS Implementation and Workflow

LbMAS extends the bMAS framework with a specific workflow:

1. **Agent Generation:** For a given query, an "Agent Generating Agent (AG)" (itself an LLM) is used to randomly generate *n* expert prompts (identity `E_i` and expertise description `D_i`) from domains relevant to the query. Each newly generated expert agent is assigned a base LLM randomly chosen from a pre-defined set of diverse LLMs (specifically, Llama-3.1-70b-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-72b-Instruct in experiments) to leverage model diversity.
2. **Predefined Agents:** In addition to dynamically generated experts, LbMAS includes a set of predefined, constant agents with specific operational roles:
 - **Decider:** Assesses if the blackboard contains enough information for a final solution. If so, it provides the solution and stops the cycle.
 - **Planner:** Formulates plans to solve the query, potentially decomposing complex problems into smaller tasks.
 - **Critic:** Identifies errors or hallucinations in messages on the blackboard, prompting relevant agents to revise their outputs.
 - **Conflict-Resolver:** Detects contradictions among messages, identifies conflicting agents, and initiates discussions (potentially in private spaces) to resolve them.
 - **Cleaner:** Manages blackboard content by detecting and removing useless or redundant messages to maintain quality and reduce token consumption.
3. **Blackboard Cycle:** This is the iterative core process:
 - The Control Unit selects relevant agents based on the query, current blackboard content, and agent abilities.
 - Each selected agent reads the *entire* blackboard content as its prompt and writes its LLM-generated output back to the blackboard.
 - This cycle repeats, building incrementally on the shared knowledge.
4. **Stopping Condition:** The cycle continues until either:
 - A maximum number of rounds (set to 4 in experiments) is reached.
 - The Decider agent determines a final solution.
5. **Solution Extraction:** If the Decider doesn't provide a solution, or the maximum rounds are reached, all relevant agents are prompted to provide an answer based on the final blackboard content. A majority vote mechanism is then employed, where the answer with the highest cumulative similarity to other agents' answers is chosen as the final solution.

**Key Design Choices:**

- **Blackboard as Sole Communication Medium:** Agents communicate exclusively through the blackboard, eliminating the need for individual agent memory modules and simplifying consistency management while potentially reducing prompt length.
- **LLM-based Control Unit:** The control unit itself is an LLM, providing flexibility in dynamic agent selection.
- **Diverse Base LLMs:** Randomly selecting from a set of LLMs for agents leverages model diversity, compensating for individual model limitations.

The overall approach emphasizes flexibility, autonomy, and dynamic adaptation, aiming to create a highly generalized problem-solving framework without the need for pre-training or fixed workflows.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Han and Zhang demonstrate the competitive performance and cost-effectiveness of LbMAS across a range of tasks.

#### 4.1. Performance Analysis

- **Overall Superiority:** LbMAS achieved the best average performance across six diverse benchmarks: MMLU (knowledge), ARC-Challenge, GPQA-Diamond, BBH-dateunderstand (reasoning), MATH, and GSM8K (mathematical). It outperformed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) methods by an average of 4.33% and static Multi-Agent Systems (MASs) by 5.02%.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - **Single-agent (Vanilla, CoT):** LbMAS significantly surpassed single LLM agents and even CoT methods, highlighting the benefits of multi-agent collaboration.
 - **Static MASs (Chateval, Exchange-of-Thought, MultiPersona):** LbMAS consistently outperformed these systems, demonstrating the advantage of its dynamic, blackboard-based collaboration. For instance, on GPQA-Diamond, LbMAS achieved 54.04% compared to Chateval's 51.26% and MultiPersona's 44.44%.
 - **Autonomous MASs (GPTSwarm, AFlow, MaAS):** While specific performance numbers for these baselines are only provided in the cost analysis table for MATH, LbMAS generally showed strong or superior performance. For MATH, LbMAS (72.60%) outperformed GPTSwarm (67.25%), AFlow (69.25%), and MaAS (70.77%).
- **Decision-Making Mechanisms:** Both the dedicated "Decider" agent and the "Majority Vote" mechanism for solution extraction yielded similar performances, indicating that the multi-agent discussion often converges towards a consensus. The consensus rates were notably high for MMLU (89.8%), moderate for GPQA (52.5%), and lower for MATH (29.4%), suggesting varying degrees of difficulty in reaching full agreement across problem types.
- **Impact of LLM Diversity:** Experiments using a single base LLM (Llama or Qwen) showed that LbMAS's performance is sensitive to the underlying model's capabilities. LbMAS with Qwen as the sole base model outperformed CoT on MATH and GSM8K, while LbMAS with Llama performed worse. This underscores the benefit of using diverse LLMs within the agent group, as implemented in the main LbMAS setup, to mitigate individual model weaknesses.
- **Unique Problem Solving:** LbMAS uniquely solved the highest number of problems across MMLU, GPQA, and MATH datasets, indicating its ability to tackle specific complex cases that other methods struggle with.

#### 4.2. Cost Analysis (Token Consumption)

- **Token Efficiency:** LbMAS demonstrated remarkable token efficiency, especially when compared to autonomous MAS baselines. On the MATH benchmark, LbMAS incurred the second lowest total token cost (4,721,489 tokens) while achieving the best performance (72.60%). This is significantly lower than autonomous MASs like AFlow (16,697,032 tokens) and MaAS (13,012,658 tokens), which require substantial training tokens for workflow optimization.
- **Advantage over Training-Based Systems:** The finding that LbMAS is cost-effective is attributed to its dynamic adaptation strategy, which avoids the costly supervised training step required by many autonomous MASs.

#### 4.3. Framework Study

- **Round Analysis:** The average number of blackboard cycle rounds varied by dataset difficulty: MMLU (easy) averaged 2.88 rounds, GPQA (medium) 3.05 rounds, and MATH (hard) 3.29 rounds. This indicates that LbMAS dynamically adjusts the number of interaction rounds based on problem complexity, stopping early for simpler problems and engaging in more extensive discussions for harder ones.
- **Ablation Study:**
 - **Without Control Unit:** Removing the control unit drastically increased token cost (e.g., from 4.7M to 13.8M tokens on MATH) with only a slight performance perturbation. This highlights the control unit's crucial role in token efficiency by judiciously selecting agents, preventing all agents from responding in every round, while also confirming a degree of inherent autonomy in LLM agents.
 - **Without Message Removal (Cleaner):** If the cleaner agent merely marked redundant messages instead of removing them, performance declined on three datasets. This confirms the necessity of active blackboard content management (cleaning) for maintaining effective communication and overall system performance.

In summary, LbMAS presents a compelling case for the blackboard architecture's revival in the LLM era. It demonstrates a superior balance of performance and token efficiency, particularly for complex reasoning and mathematical tasks, by enabling highly dynamic and autonomous multi-agent collaboration without the pre-computation overhead of many state-of-the-art dynamic MASs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research by Han and Zhang holds significant implications for the development of advanced LLM multi-agent systems and the broader field of artificial intelligence.

#### 5.1. Significance of the Research

- **Revitalization of Classical AI Architectures:** The most immediate significance is the successful integration and demonstration of the blackboard architecture with modern Large Language Models. This work effectively bridges symbolic AI (blackboard systems) with connectionist AI (LLMs), showcasing how well-established architectural patterns can be leveraged to enhance the capabilities of contemporary models. It suggests that valuable insights from classical AI can provide robust frameworks for current challenges, particularly in managing complex interactions in LLM agents.
- **True Dynamic Adaptability:** Unlike many existing dynamic MASs that rely on a pre-optimization or training phase to define workflows, LbMAS offers "on-the-fly" adaptation of collaboration mechanisms. The control unit dynamically selects agents based on the evolving state of the blackboard, allowing for a genuinely agile and context-aware problem-solving process. This reduces the dependency on large datasets for training specific workflows, making the system more generally applicable.
- **Enhanced Generality for Ill-Structured Problems:** The blackboard architecture was originally designed for complex, ill-structured problem domains where explicit algorithms or fixed workflows are not available. By successfully porting this capability to LLM MASs, LbMAS significantly enhances their potential for tackling real-world challenges that lack pre-defined solution paths, a key limitation of many current AI systems.
- **Improved Token Efficiency:** The intelligent management of shared context on the blackboard, coupled with the LLM-based control unit's selective agent activation, leads to substantial token savings compared to other dynamic MASs. This is crucial for the practical deployment of LLM-based systems, as token consumption directly translates to computational cost and inference latency.
- **Increased Agent Autonomy and Scalability:** The framework fosters a high degree of autonomy in agent interaction, with agents contributing to the blackboard independently and the system dynamically adjusting. The modular nature of agents and the blackboard also suggests high scalability, making it easier to integrate new agents or functionalities as needed.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Foundation for Self-Organizing MASs:** LbMAS lays groundwork for truly automated and self-organizing multi-agent systems. By dynamically adapting collaboration and managing information flow, it moves closer to systems that can autonomously tackle complex tasks without extensive human oversight or pre-configuration.
- **Complex Problem Solving in Diverse Domains:** The framework's ability to handle ill-structured problems opens avenues for application in areas where traditional rule-based or fixed-workflow systems struggle. This could include complex scientific discovery, medical diagnosis, strategic planning, or even creative content generation, where diverse 'expert' LLMs can collectively converge on solutions.
- **Catalyst for Future Research:** This work is likely to inspire further research into hybrid AI architectures, combining the strengths of symbolic reasoning, knowledge representation (via the blackboard), and the pattern recognition/generative capabilities of LLMs. Future work might explore more sophisticated control units, richer blackboard structures (e.g., semantic blackboards), and more advanced agent generation and management.
- **More Accessible Dynamic MAS Development:** By reducing the need for costly pre-training and workflow optimization, LbMAS could democratize the development and deployment of dynamic multi-agent systems, making them accessible to a wider range of researchers and practitioners who might not have access to vast training data or computational resources.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions (as acknowledged by authors)

The authors candidly acknowledge several limitations in their initial implementation, which also point to rich avenues for future research:

- **Limited Predefined Agent Types and Benchmarks:** The current LbMAS has a relatively small set of predefined agents (e.g., lacking robust tool-use or code-writing capabilities) and was evaluated on a finite set of academic benchmarks. Expanding agent roles and testing on a wider array of real-world, more complex tasks would further validate the framework's potential.
- **Simple Agent Generation Module:** The agent generation module is basic, primarily focusing on role prompts and random LLM selection. Future work could incorporate more sophisticated techniques for optimizing agent prompts, roles, and even the selection of base LLMs (e.g., using approaches like MASRouter).
- **Inadequate Exploration of Blackboard Features:** While the blackboard serves as a shared memory, a deeper comparative analysis with individual agent memory modules and more nuanced explorations of private vs. public spaces, and different ways to manage blackboard content, could yield further insights into optimal information management for LLM MASs.

Despite these limitations, the paper makes a compelling case for the blackboard architecture as a powerful paradigm for building advanced, flexible, and token-efficient LLM multi-agent systems, paving the way for truly autonomous and self-organizing AI agents.
