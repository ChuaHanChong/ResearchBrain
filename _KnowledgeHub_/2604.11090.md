# Simulator Adaptation for Sim-to-Real Learning of Legged Locomotion via Proprioceptive Distribution Matching

> [!summary] Summary
> Oregon State University researchers developed a practical simulator adaptation framework for legged robots by employing proprioceptive distribution matching with a 1D marginal approximation of the Wasserstein distance. This approach significantly reduced locomotion drift by up to 80% on a Unitree Go2 for challenging tasks using less than five minutes of hardware data, without relying on motion capture systems.

## Problem

- The sim-to-real gap often degrades the performance of locomotion policies trained in simulation when deployed on physical legged robots.
- Existing methods for quantifying this gap, such as time-aligned trajectory matching, demand motion capture and privileged sensing, which are impractical for dynamic, unstable legged robots.
- Dynamics Randomization (DR) only addresses robustness to the sim-to-real gap and is limited in the types of dynamics it can represent, rather than actively improving simulator fidelity.

## Method

- The framework iteratively adapts a parameterized simulator by collecting proprioceptive observations and actions from physical robot rollouts and optimizing simulator parameters.
- A novel sim-to-real cost function quantifies discrepancies by comparing distributions of proprioceptive observations and actions using a 1D marginal approximation of the Wasserstein distance, eliminating the need for time alignment or external sensing.
- Simulator dynamics are modified using either static physics parameters, a learned state-dependent action-delta model, or a residual actuator model, with optimization performed by CMA-ES.

## Results

- The method reduced locomotion drift by up to 80% on a Unitree Go2 for challenging tasks like bipedal walking and spring-joint scenarios, utilizing less than five minutes of hardware data.
- In sim-to-sim experiments, the Wasserstein-based cost effectively recovered ground-truth parameters and demonstrated superior robustness to noise, outperforming L2-based proprioceptive matching (e.g., 18.7% mean error vs. 86.3% at high noise).
- Using expressive residual actuator models with the proposed cost function, the framework successfully adapted the simulator to complex, non-parametric dynamics, matching privileged state-matching baselines without motion capture.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A distributional metric comparing proprioceptive observations and actions, specifically the 1D marginal Wasserstein distance, effectively quantifies the sim-to-real gap without motion capture or time-aligned trajectories.
> - This distributional metric demonstrates superior robustness to real-world noise for parameter identification compared to L2-based proprioceptive matching.
> - Expressive learned models, such as residual actuator networks, are essential for adapting simulators to complex, non-parametric dynamics shifts, whereas static parameter adjustments are inadequate for such changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Sim-to-real transfer is a prominent challenge in robotics learning, particularly for complex systems like legged robots, where policies trained in simulation often exhibit degraded performance on physical hardware due to dynamics discrepancies.

Historically, Dynamics Randomization (DR) has been a prevalent approach to bridge this gap. DR involves randomizing simulator parameters during training to encourage policy robustness to varied dynamics. However, DR is typically limited to static model parameters, offers a trade-off between robustness, performance, and sampling efficiency, and fundamentally aims to make policies robust *to* the sim-to-real gap rather than actively *closing* it. As tasks become more complex, the limitations of DR become more pronounced, highlighting the need for methods that incorporate real-world feedback to adapt the simulator itself.

Quantifying the sim-to-real gap is central to such adaptation. Traditional system identification methods often rely on executing known command sequences in both simulation and hardware, followed by a direct, time-aligned comparison of state trajectories, typically using Mean Squared Error (MSE). This approach faces significant practical challenges for legged robots, which are often unstable and highly sensitive to initial conditions. Small discrepancies can rapidly lead to divergent trajectories, and real-world stochasticity can further complicate direct comparisons. Furthermore, accurate state matching often necessitates full ground-truth state measurements, including base pose and velocity, typically obtained via motion capture systems. Such infrastructure requirements are often impractical or unavailable, and the sensing process itself can introduce noise.

Related works in sim-to-real transfer can be broadly categorized into online adaptation and offline finetuning. Online adaptation methods update the policy at test time by estimating real-world properties, often by conditioning the policy on dynamics parameters or latent encodings. While successful, these methods do not modify the policy itself, limiting its robustness to the dynamics encountered during training. Offline finetuning methods, in contrast, aim to improve simulator fidelity using hardware data. These include:
- **System Identification (System ID):** Tuning model parameters (e.g., masses, frictions) to match trajectories, often requiring known actions and specialized sensing.
- **Learned State-Dependent Dynamics Models:** Using neural networks to approximate complex actuator dynamics, which often requires specialized sensing like torque or contact measurements.
- **Learned "Delta Actions":** Modifying the policy's action through a learned correction, which typically relies on time-aligned full-state tracking errors and may require motion-capture data.
- **"Delta Dynamics" Models:** Adding residuals directly to the simulator's next-state predictions for broader corrections.
- **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs):** Approaches like SimGAN train a discriminator to classify simulation vs. hardware tuples, using the discriminator's output as a reward. However, GANs can be prone to instability and hyperparameter sensitivity.
- **Wasserstein Distance Applications:** Some prior work has utilized Wasserstein Distance for measuring sim-to-real gaps, similar to the approach presented in this paper, but often in conjunction with other metrics or with computationally more expensive multi-dimensional computations.

The present work positions itself by addressing the practical limitations of prior sim-to-real gap quantification methods, particularly for legged robots, by proposing an alternative that avoids motion capture and time-aligned trajectory matching, using a computationally efficient distributional metric.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a practical and effective method for adapting robot simulators to better match hardware behavior, thereby improving the sim-to-real transfer of learned legged locomotion policies.

The motivation stems from several limitations observed in existing sim-to-real techniques:
- **Impracticality of Current Gap Quantification:** Existing methods for quantifying the sim-to-real gap, such as precise time-aligned trajectory matching, often necessitate motion capture, privileged sensing, and carefully controlled initial conditions. These requirements are difficult to meet for complex, unstable systems like legged robots and are often unavailable to many users. The sensitivity of legged robots to initial conditions and real-world stochasticity further renders direct trajectory comparisons ambiguous over longer horizons.
- **Limitations of Dynamics Randomization:** While dynamics randomization (DR) enhances policy robustness, it does not fundamentally resolve the underlying simulator discrepancies. DR is limited in the types of dynamics it can represent and introduces trade-offs in robustness, performance, and sampling efficiency.
- **Need for Sim-to-Real Feedback:** To move beyond merely making policies robust to the sim-to-real gap, there is a need for techniques that can incorporate real-world feedback to iteratively refine and adapt the simulator's dynamics model.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the paper are:
- **Introduce a Novel Gap Metric:** Propose a practical alternative for quantifying the sim-to-real discrepancy based on proprioceptive distribution matching. This metric should compare hardware and simulation rollouts as distributions of joint observations and actions, thereby eliminating the need for time alignment or external sensing.
- **Utilize Metric as Black-Box Objective:** Employ this distributional metric as a black-box objective function for optimizing simulator dynamics.
- **Explore Simulator Modification Functions:** Investigate the effectiveness of various simulator adaptation functions, ranging from simple static parameter identification to more expressive models like action-delta models and residual actuator models.
- **Evaluate Against Baselines:** Conduct extensive sim-to-sim ablations to compare the proposed approach's parameter recovery and policy-performance gains against established privileged state-matching baselines.
- **Demonstrate Real-World Efficacy:** Validate the method on physical hardware, demonstrating substantial drift reduction in challenging locomotion tasks using minimal hardware data, without requiring motion capture.

Ultimately, the goal is to provide a practical and effective route for simulator adaptation that can enhance the real-world transfer and performance of learned legged locomotion policies by creating a simulator that more accurately reflects hardware behavior in a task-targeted manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed sim-to-real framework adapts a parameterized simulator, denoted as `M(θ)`, such that a policy learned within it behaves consistently across both simulation and hardware. The process involves defining a Markov Decision Process (MDP) `M(θ)` where `θ` encompasses physics simulator parameters and any additional parameters from the chosen simulator adaptation approach.

The overall methodology comprises the following steps:
1. **Initial Policy Training:** A locomotion policy `π_0` is initially trained using reinforcement learning (PPO) in a nominal simulator `M(θ_0)`.
2. **Trajectory Collection:**
 - `N` trajectories `D_hw` are collected by executing `π_0` on the physical robot. These trajectories consist solely of proprioceptive observations (`o_t`, e.g., joint positions, velocities) and policy actions (`a_t`), omitting any privileged information like base pose.
 - Corresponding simulated trajectories `D_sim` are generated by executing `π_0` in the current simulator `M(θ)`.
3. **Sim-to-Real Discrepancy Quantification:** A cost function `C_S2R(D_sim, D_hw)` is used to measure the behavioral differences between the simulated and hardware trajectories.
4. **Simulator Adaptation:** The simulator parameters `θ` are optimized to minimize this cost, yielding updated parameters `θ_1 = arg min_θ C_S2R(D(π_0, θ), D_hw)`.
5. **Policy Finetuning:** The original policy `π_0` is then re-trained (finetuned) in the updated simulator `M(θ_1)` to obtain an improved controller `π_1`. This entire process can be iterated.

The methodology is characterized by three core components:

**A. A Distributional Sim-to-Real Cost Function (Wass)**

Instead of comparing time-aligned trajectories, which are prone to rapid divergence in complex systems, the method treats each trajectory as a collection of samples drawn from the stationary distribution induced by the policy. This approach removes the need for synchronized initial states and time-aligned rollouts, providing a smoother objective for optimization.
- **Wasserstein Distance:** The Wasserstein distance is selected as the metric due to its non-parametric nature and properties as a proper metric.
- **1D Marginal Approximation:** To address the computational expense of multi-dimensional Wasserstein distance, the method approximates it by averaging 1D Wasserstein distances computed independently along each dimension of the proprioceptive observations and actions. This approximation is motivated by computational efficiency, sample efficiency, and the observation that simulator modifications often influence marginal distributions more significantly than inter-joint correlations.
- **Feature Selection:** The distributions compared are over joint positions, joint velocities, and policy actions. Joint velocities provide higher-order and time-dependent information, while policy actions reveal motor/actuator effects. Accelerations are excluded due to noise.
- **Normalization:** All feature values are normalized to `[-1, 1]` based on hardware data, with the normalization parameters fixed throughout adaptation.

**B. Simulator Modification Functions**

The paper investigates three types of parameterized functions to modify the simulator's transition dynamics:
1. **Static Simulator Parameters (FricArm):** This involves adjusting fixed physics parameters such as joint friction and armature coefficients. The simulator's transition function `T_θ` is replaced with `T_hat_θ`, where `hat_θ` represents the optimized static parameters. This is a low-dimensional modification space.
2. **Action–Delta Model (ActionDelta):** This model augments the nominal policy by learning a state-dependent neural network `Δ_a(s_t | hat_θ)` that outputs a residual action. This residual is added to the policy's output, modifying the desired joint targets fed to the PD controller. The `Δ_a` network is a 2-layer feedforward network that processes a three-step history of joint position and velocity, operating at the policy rate (50Hz). Each joint has its own network.
3. **Residual Actuator Model (ResidAct):** This model introduces a state-dependent neural network `Δ_τ(s_t | hat_θ)` that outputs a residual torque, which is directly added to the output of the nominal PD controller. This allows for correcting unmodeled motor dynamics, torque saturation, or complex frictional behavior at the simulation rate (1KHz), offering greater expressivity. Similar to ActionDelta, it uses a 2-layer feedforward network with a three-step history for each joint.

**C. Optimization**

Given that evaluating the cost function `C_S2R` requires running the simulator for full trajectories, gradient-based optimization is impractical unless the simulator is differentiable. Therefore, the method employs a sampling-based black-box optimization algorithm:
- **Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES):** CMA-ES is used for its suitability in handling non-smooth and noisy objective landscapes, which are characteristic of sim-to-real system identification problems.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated using a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot within the IsaacLab simulator. Policies are trained for velocity-commanded locomotion using PPO, with initial dynamics randomization. Three source-target scenarios are considered:
- **Model Parameter Shift (Sim-to-Sim):** An idealized scenario where known joint friction and armature parameters are perturbed to create a target, allowing for evaluation of parameter recovery.
- **Spring Joint (Sim-to-Sim and Sim-to-Real):** A virtual or physical spring is added to a joint to introduce complex, non-parametric dynamics changes.
- **Bipedal Walking (Sim-to-Real):** A challenging task where the Go2 walks on its hind legs, representing a significant dynamics shift from its nominal quadrupedal configuration.

For comparative analysis, three sim-to-real cost functions are evaluated:
- **MatchS(H):** An idealized, privileged baseline using full state information, time-aligned trajectories, and simulator resets every `H` steps (L2 error on joint/base positions/velocities).
- **MatchO(∞):** Compares proprioceptive observations directly using L2 discrepancy, without privileged state or resets, susceptible to trajectory divergence.
- **Wass:** The proposed 1D marginal approximation of Wasserstein distance on proprioceptive observations, requiring no time alignment, resets, or privileged state.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across sim-to-sim and sim-to-real experiments, evaluating the proposed Wasserstein-based approach against baselines and across different simulator modification functions.

**Sim-to-Sim Experiments:**

- **Parameter Identification (Ideal Conditions):** In the Model Parameter Shift scenario with no noise, all tested cost functions—Wass, MatchS(1), MatchS(20), and MatchO(∞)—successfully identified the ground-truth joint friction and armature parameters with negligible numerical error. This indicated that the proposed distributional Wasserstein cost is as effective as state-matching metrics in quantifying the sim-to-sim gap under ideal circumstances.
- **Parameter Identification (Noisy Conditions):** Under conditions emulating real-world hardware (Gaussian torque noise and temporal shifts), the Wasserstein (Wass) cost demonstrated superior robustness for parameter identification. It significantly outperformed MatchO(∞) (L2 proprioceptive matching) across all noise levels, with its advantage increasing with noise magnitude. For instance, at the highest noise level (σ=5.0 N·m), Wass achieved a mean parameter error of 18.7%, compared to MatchO(∞)'s 86.3%. While MatchS(∞) (privileged state matching) performed slightly better at low noise, Wass proved competitive at the smallest noise level and superior at higher noise levels. These results highlight Wass as a robust and practical alternative to motion-capture-based methods for parameter identification in legged systems, even in the presence of noise.
- **Sim-to-Sim Finetuning (Model Parameter Shift):**
 - When using the **FricArm** (static parameter) modification, policies finetuned using parameters identified by all cost functions achieved performance comparable to a policy directly finetuned on the true target model. This was expected, given the accurate parameter recovery in this scenario.
 - However, when employing the more expressive **ActionDelta** and **ResidAct** models, finetuning performance was substantially worse, often no better than the original policy. This was attributed to the higher dimensionality of these models, making optimization challenging and prone to local minima. Notably, MatchS(1) (1-step horizon state matching) performed poorly with these models, indicating that short-horizon resets may provide insufficient dynamical information for effective learning with expressive networks, potentially leading to out-of-distribution behaviors during longer rollouts.
- **Sim-to-Sim Finetuning (Spring Joint):** This scenario involved a non-parametric dynamics change.
 - The **FricArm** modification catastrophically failed, producing policies significantly worse than the baseline, as static parameters could not capture the complex, non-linear effects of the spring.
 - In contrast, the **ActionDelta** and **ResidAct** models successfully learned useful modifications, leading to consistent performance improvements over the baseline policy. The Wasserstein-based models (with ActionDelta/ResidAct) matched the performance of MatchS(20) variants, effectively closing the sim-to-sim gap to near ground-truth performance without requiring privileged base-state information or time-aligned trajectory resets.

**Sim-to-Real Experiments:**

For real-world validation, 64 hardware trajectories (under five minutes of data) were collected. Due to the absence of a motion-capture system, only the proposed Wasserstein metric was applicable, and the Residual Actuator Model (ResidAct) was chosen as the simulator modification function based on sim-to-sim results. Performance was evaluated via manual measurements of drift during constant-velocity trials.

- **Spring Joint:** In this scenario, a physical spring was attached to the Go2's rear-right calf joint. The original policy exhibited over 2 meters of drift and complete failure for some sideways walking commands. The policy finetuned using the ResidAct model and the Wass cost function significantly reduced drift to approximately 0.4 meters (around 20% tracking error in the worst case). This demonstrates the learned residual model's accuracy in representing hardware dynamics, enabling effective finetuning and showing that the distributional cost function works without initial state alignment or controlled resets.
- **Bipedal Walking:** For the challenging bipedal locomotion task, the policy trained in the nominal simulator experienced substantial displacement error on real hardware, especially during sideways walking (-y direction), showing a 1.2-meter forward drift. After training a residual actuator model with the Wass cost and finetuning, drift was substantially reduced across command directions, with reductions up to 50% in the -y case. This indicates that the Wasserstein-based ResidAct model produced a simulator that more accurately reflected the complex bipedal hardware dynamics, leading to meaningful performance gains.

**Challenges of Humanoid Application (Agility Robotics Digit):**

The researchers attempted to apply the framework to the Agility Robotics Digit humanoid but encountered limitations. The baseline Digit policy failed outright for backward walking (robot consistently fell). As the collected hardware data contained no valid backward walking examples, the sim-to-real pipeline could not identify modifications to reproduce this failure in simulation. This highlighted an inherent limitation: the approach implicitly assumes that the initial sim-to-real transfer is sufficient to generate informative hardware trajectories within the relevant state space. If failure states dominate or occur in unrepresented regions, the method struggles, potentially leading to degenerate optimizations that match failure distributions trivially without physically plausible dynamics. Chaotic and non-deterministic nature of falls further complicates reliable distribution matching.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a significant advancement in the field of sim-to-real transfer for legged robots by providing a highly practical and effective simulator adaptation framework.

The primary significance lies in its **practicality and reduced infrastructure requirements**. By employing proprioceptive distribution matching with a 1D marginal approximation of the Wasserstein distance, the method eliminates the need for expensive and often unavailable equipment like motion capture systems. It also bypasses the complexities of time-aligned trajectory matching, privileged sensing, and precisely controlled initial conditions, which are major hurdles for many robotics researchers and practitioners, particularly for dynamic and unstable legged systems. This democratizes access to robust sim-to-real techniques.

Furthermore, the framework demonstrates **high efficiency in data utilization**, achieving substantial performance improvements and drift reduction with less than five minutes of hardware data. This minimal data requirement significantly accelerates the adaptation cycle and reduces the burden of real-world experimentation.

The method exhibits **robustness to noisy data**, as evidenced by its superior performance over L2-based proprioceptive matching in sim-to-sim parameter identification under increasing noise levels. This robustness is crucial for real-world applications where sensor noise, unmodeled effects, and timing inconsistencies are prevalent.

The work's **generality** is another key strength. It supports various types of simulator modification functions, from simple static parameter adjustments to more expressive learned models like action-delta and residual actuator networks. This flexibility allows for tailoring the adaptation complexity to the specific nature of the sim-to-real gap, ranging from minor parameter miscalibrations to complex unmodeled dynamics. The demonstration on challenging tasks like two-legged walking on a quadruped highlights its applicability to diverse and complex behaviors.

The potential impact of this research is substantial:
- **Accelerated Robotics Development:** By simplifying and streamlining the sim-to-real transfer process, the framework can significantly accelerate the development and deployment of high-performance controllers for legged robots.
- **Enabling Complex Behaviors:** A more accurate simulator allows policies to be trained for increasingly complex and agile behaviors that would otherwise be difficult or impossible to realize on physical hardware due to persistent sim-to-real discrepancies.
- **Broader Accessibility:** Reducing the reliance on specialized hardware and tedious manual tuning makes advanced robotic learning techniques more accessible to a wider range of researchers and developers.

Future work directions identified by the authors include expanding the framework to learn **stochastic dynamics**. Instead of fixed modifications, learning a distribution of model parameters or having residual models output torque distributions could create more robust and realistic simulators, better reflecting the inherent stochasticity of real-world dynamics (e.g., motor temperature variations). Additionally, addressing the identified limitation concerning **catastrophic failure modes** (as seen with the Digit humanoid) will be crucial, requiring principled data collection strategies that can capture the causal structure of failures without falling into degenerate optimization outcomes.
