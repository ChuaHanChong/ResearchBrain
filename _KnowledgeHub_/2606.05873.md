# LadderMan: Learning Humanoid Perceptive Ladder Climbing

> [!summary] Summary
> LadderMan introduces a unified depth-based visuomotor system enabling the Unitree G1 humanoid robot to robustly climb diverse ladders and perform stable on-ladder manipulation via zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The system achieves a climbing speed of approximately 3.4 seconds per rung, comparable to human performance.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with robust ladder climbing due to challenges in precise whole-body coordination on sparse, discontinuous rungs and poor generalization across diverse ladder geometries.
- Sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich tasks like ladder climbing is difficult due to discrepancies in depth perception, sensor noise, and robot dynamics.
- Maintaining stability during on-ladder manipulation is challenging, as general teleoperation policies often lead to balance loss in highly constrained environments.

## Method

- A two-stage learning pipeline, starting with hybrid ==motion tracking== to learn diverse state-based expert policies from a single reference motion, then distilling them into a unified visuomotor policy using hybrid DAgger and reinforcement learning.
- Bridging the sim-to-real perception gap by employing a Vision Foundation Model (Fast-FoundationStereo) for depth processing and introducing Rung-Focused Masking (RFM) to focus perception on ladder rungs.
- A dual-agent learning framework for on-ladder manipulation, decoupling a lower-body stabilization agent from an upper-body manipulation agent to maintain balance while performing teleoperated tasks.

## Results

- The Unitree G1 humanoid robot demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, robustly climbing diverse real-world ladders with varying geometries and performing stable on-ladder manipulation tasks via teleoperation.
- LadderMan achieved over **95%** success in simulation across a range of ladder configurations, significantly outperforming a motion tracking baseline that peaked at **49%** success, and a climbing speed of 3.4 seconds per rung, comparable to human performance.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the hybrid DAgger + RL approach, the Vision Foundation Model for depth processing, and Rung-Focused Masking were crucial for achieving robust sim-to-real transfer and high success rates (e.g., 9/10 on Ladder A compared to 0/10-3/10 without these components).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex, diverse locomotion skills can be learned from a single reference motion by employing hybrid motion tracking with asymmetric rewards, allowing flexible adaptation of certain body parts.
> - Vision Foundation Models combined with targeted masking effectively bridge the sim-to-real perception gap for contact-rich tasks on sparse structures like ladders, reducing the need for extensive manual depth randomization.
> - A dual-agent learning framework, which decouples lower-body stabilization from upper-body manipulation, enables stable and robust whole-body manipulation on inherently unstable platforms like ladders.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are being developed for operation in environments designed for humans, such as industrial settings, construction sites, warehouses, and maintenance scenarios. A critical capability for such deployment is the ability to navigate challenging terrains and perform tasks that require complex whole-body coordination. Ladder climbing represents a significant challenge within this domain due to several factors: the sparse, discontinuous, and narrow nature of footholds and handholds; the demand for precise and sustained multi-limb coordination; and the sensitivity to minor perception or control inaccuracies which can lead to system failure.

Prior research in robot ladder climbing has largely relied on model-based control approaches. These methods, documented in works such as those by Yoneda et al. (2008) and Luo et al. (2014), typically necessitate accurate environmental models, task-specific tuning, and sometimes specialized hardware. Consequently, these approaches often result in slow climbing speeds, limited robustness, and poor generalization to varying ladder configurations. Furthermore, they generally do not extend to on-ladder manipulation tasks.

More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has shown promise for complex locomotion tasks. While quadrupedal robots have demonstrated ladder climbing capabilities using end-to-end policies (e.g., Vogel et al., 2025), these often rely on privileged information like motion capture data and known ladder geometry, may require hardware modifications, and do not directly incorporate perception into the control loop. Humanoid ladder climbing introduces additional complexities due to heterogeneous limbs, inherently less stable support polygons, and the necessity for coordinated whole-body contacts.

Perceptive locomotion for legged robots, utilizing exteroceptive sensors like LiDAR and depth cameras, is an active area. Early methods often convert raw sensor data into terrain representations (e.g., elevation maps) for foothold selection. However, these can be limited by calibration requirements, state estimation accuracy, and latency, and may struggle to precisely represent thin structures such as ladder rungs. More contemporary research has moved towards end-to-end visuomotor policies that directly process depth images for locomotion. These have enabled impressive performance on terrains like stairs and stepping stones. However, when applied to humanoids, these methods often produce lower-body-dominated behaviors due to reliance on reward shaping, thereby not fully exploiting the whole-body coordination essential for complex tasks. Concurrent work on perceptive whole-body humanoid locomotion has explored dynamic interactions like obstacle climbing and vaulting, which primarily focus on agility. Ladder climbing, in contrast, requires precise, highly constrained multi-contact coordination on sparse structures, demanding high accuracy in both perception and control.

This work addresses the gap in enabling humanoid robots to perform robust, perceptive, and generalized whole-body ladder climbing, integrated with on-ladder manipulation, without requiring hardware modifications. It aims to develop a unified visuomotor system that can operate across diverse ladder geometries and support stable interaction tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a visuomotor system that enables humanoid robots to robustly climb diverse ladders and perform on-ladder manipulation tasks while maintaining stable balance, without requiring additional hardware modifications.

This objective is motivated by several inherent challenges associated with humanoid operation on ladders:

1. **Precise Whole-Body Coordination**: Ladder climbing necessitates highly precise and synchronized movements of both upper and lower body limbs. Hand-foot contacts are tightly coupled, and maintaining balance on sparse, discontinuous rungs requires a delicate interplay of forces and positions across the entire robot body. Small deviations in contact or posture can lead to instability and failure.

2. **Generalization Across Diverse Ladder Geometries**: Real-world ladders exhibit significant variations in physical parameters such as inclination angle, rung spacing, rung dimensions (length, width, shape), and material. A practical climbing policy must generalize robustly across these diverse configurations without requiring specific retraining or extensive tuning for each new ladder. Current approaches often lack this generalization capability, being limited to configurations seen during training or specific design.

3. **Sensitivity to Perception and Control Errors in Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Deploying learned policies from simulation to the real world ("sim-to-real transfer") is challenging, particularly for tasks involving fine-grained interaction with thin and sparse structures like ladder rungs. Discrepancies between simulated and real-world depth perception, sensor noise, and inaccuracies in robot dynamics can accumulate rapidly, leading to catastrophic failures during contact-rich tasks.

4. **Stable On-Ladder Manipulation**: Once a humanoid robot has climbed a ladder, it must be able to perform manipulation tasks (e.g., adjusting objects, tightening components) while maintaining its precarious balance. Directly applying general-purpose teleoperation policies in such constrained and unstable environments often leads to loss of balance or inefficient task execution, as the policy struggles to simultaneously stabilize the robot and track manipulation targets.

To address these motivations, the LadderMan system proposes specific solutions:
- A scalable two-stage learning pipeline to acquire a unified depth-based visuomotor climbing policy.
- The use of vision foundation models to mitigate the sim-to-real gap in depth perception, reducing reliance on manual depth randomization.
- A dual-agent learning framework to enable stable whole-body manipulation on ladders by decoupling lower-body stabilization from upper-body manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LadderMan is structured as a unified system comprising four main components designed to address the challenges of perceptive humanoid ladder climbing and on-ladder manipulation.

#### 3.1. Learning Multiple Expert Policies from a Single Reference Motion

The first stage focuses on learning state-based expert policies (π_expert_ϕ,z) for various ladder configurations (ϕ: inclination, z: rung spacing). This is challenging because reward shaping in RL often leads to lower-body-dominated behaviors for humanoids, and standard motion tracking requires extensive motion datasets for diverse ladder geometries, which are difficult to acquire.

- **Hybrid Motion Tracking**: The core of this stage is a novel hybrid motion tracking approach that combines relaxed upper-body tracking with task-driven adaptation, using a single reference motion.
 - **Reward Function**: It employs an asymmetric tracking reward (Eq. 1) that relaxes tracking constraints for upper-body keypoints (K_u) while enforcing faithful reproduction for lower-body keypoints (K_l). This allows the upper body to adapt hand contact locations. Additionally, ladder-centric contact rewards (r_contact) guide end-effector placement to target rung positions (P) derived from the ladder geometry and a manually annotated reference contact indicator (ˆc). A task reward (r_task) encourages the root pose (R) to track a target root pose (G).
 - **Observation Space**: This includes standard motion tracking observations (proprioception, reference motion, privileged robot states) augmented with relative poses between the humanoid pelvis and the ladder.
 - **Termination and Domain Randomization**: Episodes terminate if root or body pose errors exceed thresholds, or if required ladder contacts are lost for over 30 consecutive frames. Domain randomization is applied to physical parameters, initial states, and local perturbations to ladder configurations (inclination, rung spacing, rung geometry, friction) to enhance robustness.
- **Training Details**: Expert policies are trained using PPO in IsaacSim with 4096 parallel environments on an NVIDIA L40S GPU for 8,000 epochs.

#### 3.2. Unified Climbing Policy via Hybrid Imitation and Reinforcement Learning

The second stage distills the multiple state-based expert policies into a single visuomotor policy (π_visual) that can generalize across diverse ladders.

- **Hybrid DAgger + RL**: A combination of DAgger-style imitation learning and reinforcement learning is used. The unified policy is optimized using a loss function (Eq. 3) that includes a PPO objective (L_RL) with the same rewards as expert policies, and a KL divergence term (D_KL) that encourages imitation of expert behaviors. The weight (λ) of the KL term is gradually annealed to transition from imitation-driven initialization to task-driven optimization.
- **Observation and Depth Rendering**: The unified policy receives proprioception, depth observations (60x80 resolution), and a binary climbing direction command (up/down). Depth images are rendered using NVIDIA Warp for high throughput.
- **Depth Randomization**: Lightweight depth randomization, including Gaussian noise (N(0, 0.005)) and pixel dropout (0.05 probability), is applied. Depth values are clipped to [0.1m, 2m]. Camera extrinsics are also randomized to account for real-world mounting variations.

#### 3.3. Bridging Sim-to-Real Perception Gap with Vision Foundation Model

Addressing the discrepancy between simulated and real-world depth observations is critical, especially for sparse ladder structures.

- **Unstructured Noise Reduction**: Instead of extensive depth randomization, a pretrained vision foundation model (VFM), Fast-FoundationStereo, is used to process real-world stereo grayscale images into cleaner, geometrically consistent depth observations. This reduces reliance on manual tuning of randomization hyperparameters.
- **Structured Perception Gap Mitigation (Rung-Focused Masking - RFM)**: To improve robustness against ladder-specific geometric variations (e.g., handrails), rung-focused masking is introduced. During training, non-rung regions in depth images are masked with a probability of 0.1, encouraging the policy to prioritize task-relevant structures.
- **High-Fidelity Collision Geometry**: The paper emphasizes the importance of accurate collision geometry for sim-to-real transfer in contact-rich tasks. Simplified collision models, observed in other projects, were found to cause discrepancies. LadderMan adopts and refines high-fidelity collision geometry, especially for shins, hands, and feet, to ensure precise interaction simulation.

#### 3.4. On-Ladder Manipulation via Dual-Agent Learning

Building on the climbing capability, a separate manipulation policy (π_manip) is trained to allow stable on-ladder manipulation.

- **Dual-Agent Formulation**: This approach decouples the task into two agents: a lower-body agent (π_l) responsible for maintaining stable balance on the ladder, and an upper-body agent (π_u) for tracking teleoperated manipulation targets. Each agent has separate actor and critic networks.
- **Observation, Action, and Reward**: Both agents receive whole-body proprioception. The upper-body agent additionally receives teleoperated upper-body motion targets (arm, wrist, waist poses), which are sampled from the AMASS dataset during training or generated via Inverse Kinematics (IK) from a VR headset during deployment. The final action is a concatenation of outputs from both agents. The upper-body agent is trained with an upper-body tracking reward, while the lower-body agent uses contact and stabilization rewards. Upper-body collisions with the ladder are disabled in simulation during training to enlarge the manipulation workspace.
- **Teleoperation System**: A PICO 4 Ultra VR headset provides whole-body motion streaming for teleoperation, using a head-mounted display, handheld controllers, and ankle-mounted trackers, without external motion capture.

**4.5. Hardware and Software**: All policies are trained in IsaacSim and deployed on a 1.3m, 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Perception is handled by an onboard Intel RealSense D435i camera, and policy inference by an onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin at 50 Hz control frequency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates LadderMan through extensive simulation and real-world experiments, addressing four key questions.

#### 4.1. Robust Climbing and Manipulation Across Diverse Ladders (Q1)

- **Real-World Climbing**: LadderMan successfully demonstrates zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, robustly climbing both upward and downward on a diverse set of real-world ladders (A, B, C) within approximately 20 seconds. These ladders varied in inclination (64.8° to 69.2°), rung spacing (23.1cm to 25.5cm), and material. The policy maintained stable whole-body coordination and reliable multi-contact transitions despite these variations. The reference motion was collected on Ladder A, yet the policy generalized to B and C.
- **On-Ladder Manipulation**: Building on the climbing capability, the humanoid robot performed various manipulation tasks, including adjusting paintings, replacing light bulbs, and box handover, while maintaining stable balance on the ladder. These tasks were controlled via teleoperation using a PICO 4 Ultra VR headset.
- **Simulation Generalization**: In simulation, LadderMan demonstrated strong generalization across a grid of ladder configurations, achieving over 95% success rate for inclination angles between 55° and 65° and rung spacings between 24cm and 28cm. For more extreme configurations at the boundary of the evaluation space, the policy maintained an average success rate of approximately 57%. In contrast, a blind motion tracking baseline achieved a peak success rate of only 49% near the reference configuration and failed rapidly as ladder geometry deviated.

#### 4.2. Dual-Agent Policy Improves Manipulation Stability (Q2)

- A comparison between LadderMan's dual-agent manipulation policy and a state-of-the-art whole-body teleoperation policy, TWIST2, revealed that TWIST2 frequently lost balance when performing on-ladder tasks. LadderMan's dual-agent policy, by decoupling lower-body stabilization from upper-body manipulation, maintained stable balance throughout the manipulation tasks.

#### 4.3. Hybrid Motion Tracking Enables Expert Policy Learning from Single Reference (Q3)

- **Ablation Study**: An ablation study on expert policy learning methods demonstrated that standard DeepMimic-style motion tracking failed to learn stable climbing policies on ladder configurations significantly different from the reference motion. In contrast, LadderMan's hybrid motion tracking successfully learned climbing behaviors across various ladder geometries (smaller, identical, larger) using only a single reference motion.
- **Component Importance**: The study indicated that both the contact tracking reward and the task rewards within the hybrid motion tracking framework were critical, as their removal degraded learning performance.

#### 4.4. Key Components for Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer (Q4)

- **Real-World Ablation**: A real-world ablation study on critical system components for sim-to-real transfer showed that:
 - **Hybrid DAgger and RL**: The incorporation of reinforcement learning alongside DAgger substantially improved policy robustness and recovery behaviors, leading to significantly higher real-world success rates compared to pure DAgger. For instance, on Ladder A, the full policy achieved 9/10 successes, while without RL it achieved only 2/10.
 - **Vision Foundation Model (VFM)**: The use of VFM-based depth perception contributed significantly to sim-to-real robustness and generalization. Without VFM, success rates dropped markedly (e.g., 9/10 to 3/10 on Ladder A). The VFM produced cleaner and more geometrically consistent depth observations, reducing sensor artifacts.
 - **Rung-Focused Masking (RFM)**: This component was found to be crucial for sim-to-real perception robustness and generalization. Masking task-irrelevant ladder structures improved perception consistency and significantly reduced failures caused by unseen ladder rail geometries not accurately modeled in simulation (e.g., 9/10 to 0/10 on Ladder A).

#### 4.5. Human Comparison

- LadderMan achieved an average climbing speed of approximately 3.4 seconds per rung, which is comparable to human subjects who averaged about 3.2 seconds per rung. This indicates that the learned policy demonstrates practical climbing efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a unified system, LadderMan, that addresses the long-standing challenge of humanoid ladder climbing and on-ladder manipulation. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Enabling Humanoid Operation in Human-Centered Environments**: By demonstrating robust ladder climbing and manipulation, LadderMan moves humanoid robots closer to practical deployment in industrial settings such as construction, warehousing, and maintenance, where ladders are common. This expands the operational envelope of humanoid robots beyond flat ground or stairs.
- **Robust and Generalizable Locomotion**: The system enables a Unitree G1 humanoid to robustly climb diverse ladders (varying in inclination, rung spacing, and geometry) using a single perceptive policy. This addresses a major limitation of prior model-based and less generalized learning approaches.
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The achievement of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer without hardware modifications is a notable practical advancement. This capability is facilitated by strategic techniques for bridging the perception gap, such as leveraging vision foundation models and rung-focused masking, which can reduce the arduous process of manual tuning and extensive domain randomization for real-world deployment.
- **Innovative Learning Pipeline**: The scalable two-stage learning pipeline, particularly the hybrid motion tracking method, is a significant methodological contribution. Learning multiple climbing experts from a *single* reference motion, rather than requiring large, diverse motion datasets, offers a more efficient and practical approach to skill acquisition for complex whole-body tasks. The subsequent distillation via hybrid imitation and reinforcement learning provides a robust method for policy generalization.
- **Stable On-Ladder Loco-Manipulation**: The introduction of a dual-agent learning framework for on-ladder manipulation is important. It effectively decouples stabilization from manipulation, allowing the robot to maintain balance while performing interaction tasks. This provides a solution for integrating manipulation capabilities into complex locomotion behaviors on unstable platforms.
- **Contribution to Sim-to-Real Methodologies**: The explicit identification and mitigation of both unstructured (sensor noise) and structured (ladder geometry variations) perception gaps, alongside the emphasis on high-fidelity collision geometry, offer valuable insights for future research in robot learning and sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich tasks.
- **Open-Source Contribution**: The stated intention to open-source all training and inference code, along with deployable models, will contribute to the broader robotics research community, enabling further development and benchmarking.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite these advancements, the paper acknowledges certain limitations. The climbing policy is primarily evaluated on inclined ladders up to 75 degrees and does not yet extend to vertical ladders, which would require different stability margins and potentially distinct climbing strategies. Furthermore, the manipulation capabilities are currently constrained by the absence of dexterous end-effectors, suggesting that integration with more capable hands could expand the range of practical real-world applications. These limitations represent clear avenues for future research and development to further enhance the capabilities of humanoid robots.
