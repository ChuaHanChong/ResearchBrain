# Rank-GRPO: Training LLM-based Conversational Recommender Systems with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> ConvRec-R1 is an end-to-end framework for training LLM-based conversational recommender systems, addressing issues like catalog unawareness and degraded ranking quality. It integrates a data-driven supervised fine-tuning stage with a specialized reinforcement learning algorithm, Rank-GRPO, enabling smaller LLMs to match or exceed the performance of larger proprietary models.

## Problem

- LLM-based conversational recommender systems often generate out-of-catalog or non-existent items and fail to adhere to specific output formats.
- The quality of recommended lists generated by LLMs tends to degrade towards the end, a problem particularly noticeable with smaller models.
- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) methods designed for LLM alignment, like GRPO, are misaligned for tasks with rank-style outputs, leading to non-causal credit assignment and unstable policy optimization.

## Method

- The ==ConvRec==-R1 framework utilizes a two-stage approach: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) followed by Reinforcement Learning-based Alignment (RL).
- A "Remap–Reflect–Adjust" pipeline constructs the SFT dataset, distilling knowledge from a teacher LLM to ground the student LLM in the item catalog and desired output formats.
- **Rank-GRPO**, a novel RL algorithm, refines the LLM's ranking ability by applying rank-level importance ratios and advantages with causally masked returns, treating each rank as the distinct action unit.

## Results

- The SFT stage improved NDCG@20 by over 30 times for a **0.5B** model and achieved over **99%** in-catalog recommendations across all tested LLM backbones.
- Rank-GRPO demonstrated faster convergence and consistently higher NDCG scores, especially at higher ranks (e.g., NDCG@20), outperforming vanilla GRPO, GSPO, and DPO-based methods.
- ConvRec-R1 with a Qwen2.5-**0.5B**-Instruct backbone outperformed GPT-4o-mini, and a Llama-3.2-**3B**-Instruct backbone surpassed GPT-4o and CRAG in Recall and NDCG@20 on the REDDIT-V2 dataset.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A multi-step data distillation pipeline effectively grounds LLMs in specific item catalogs and output structures, serving as a robust warm-start for subsequent reinforcement learning.
> - Aligning reinforcement learning policy updates with the rank-structure of outputs, rather than token-level or sequence-level, provides more stable optimization and better credit assignment for generative ranking tasks.
> - Smaller open-source LLMs (0.5B-3B parameters), when trained with this structured SFT and rank-aware RL, can achieve performance comparable to or superior to larger, proprietary LLMs for conversational recommendation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges within the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM)-based conversational recommender systems (CRS). Traditional recommender systems primarily focus on passive prediction of user behavior. In contrast, CRS aim to provide interactive dialogue, allowing users to express nuanced preferences and receive tailored recommendations. LLMs are central to this shift due to their capabilities in understanding natural language and generating responses.

Prior LLM-based CRS approaches either embed item IDs into the LLM vocabulary or represent items in natural language. The latter approach is gaining prominence for its flexibility and preservation of LLM's language capabilities. However, several limitations restrict the practical deployment of LLM-based CRS: LLMs often lack awareness of specific item catalogs, leading to the generation of out-of-catalog or non-existent items; they may fail to adhere to predefined output formats; and the quality of their generated recommendation lists typically degrades towards the end. These issues are particularly pronounced in smaller LLMs, which are often preferred in industrial settings for efficiency.

Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Reward (RLVR) has emerged as a method for aligning LLMs by introducing structured rewards. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is a notable RLVR algorithm. However, existing GRPO-style methods, which typically perform token-wise policy updates based on sequence-level rewards, are considered misaligned for tasks with rank-style outputs like recommendation. This misalignment leads to non-causal credit assignment and unstable policy optimization. This paper positions itself as an advancement in overcoming these specific alignment challenges for LLM-based CRS, particularly for small-to-medium sized LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end training framework, named ConvRec-R1, for LLM-based conversational recommender systems that explicitly addresses the limitations of pretrained LLMs and the challenges of applying existing reinforcement learning techniques to ranking tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical issues observed in the application of LLMs to conversational recommendation:

- **Catalog Unawareness**: Pretrained LLMs, trained on general web data, do not inherently possess knowledge of specific item catalogs relevant to a particular platform (e.g., a movie streaming service's available titles). This often results in LLMs generating recommendations for items that are out-of-catalog (OOC) or entirely non-existent, diminishing their practical utility.
- **Output Format Inconsistencies**: LLMs frequently fail to adhere to precise output formats required for recommendations (e.g., movie title with release year). Such inconsistencies complicate item matching and integration with downstream systems.
- **Degraded Ranking Quality**: The quality of recommended items generated by LLMs tends to deteriorate significantly towards the end of a list. This issue is attributed to the scarcity of high-quality, ranking-style data during LLM pretraining. These problems are exacerbated when utilizing smaller LLMs, which are frequently adopted in practice for their efficiency and lower latency.

Beyond these LLM-specific limitations, the authors identify two fundamental challenges in applying reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) to LLM-based CRS:

- **Lack of High-Quality Demonstrations for Warm-Starting RL**: RLVR methods, particularly those involving self-exploration, typically benefit from a warm-start via behavioral cloning using high-quality human demonstrations. However, obtaining large-scale, catalog-grounded, and well-ranked human-annotated recommendation lists is impractical due to the difficulty for annotators to recall vast, dynamic catalogs and the influence of subjective biases. This necessitates an automated approach to generate such demonstrations.
- **Misalignment of Existing RL Algorithms for Rank-Style Outputs**: Current RLVR algorithms, such as GRPO, generally perform token-wise policy updates based on sequence-level rewards (e.g., overall NDCG of a list). This approach is considered misaligned for ranking tasks because sequence-level rewards are too coarse to distinguish the contribution of individual items at different ranks, while token-level updates are overly fine-grained for items represented by multiple tokens. This mismatch can lead to non-causal credit assignment, inappropriate importance weighting, and unstable policy optimization.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes a two-stage framework: a data-driven supervised fine-tuning stage to ground LLMs in the catalog and output format, and a novel reinforcement learning algorithm specifically designed for rank-structured outputs to optimize recommendation quality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ConvRec-R1 framework operates in two distinct stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning-based Alignment (RL).

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

Conversational recommendation is formulated as a sequential decision problem. Given a dialogue `x`, the objective is to train a policy `π_θ(y|x)` (parameterized by a target LLM) to generate a ranked list of `N` items `y = (y^(1)..., y^(N))`. Each item `y^(k)` is a natural language sequence of tokens, and ideally, all items should belong to a predefined catalog `C`. The LLM generates the sequence `y` auto-regressively at the token level, with items separated by a delimiter.

#### 3.2. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The first stage, SFT, aims to provide the LLM-based CRS policy with foundational knowledge regarding the item catalog, required output formats, and initial ranking capabilities. This is achieved by constructing a behavioral cloning dataset `D_SFT` through a lightweight distillation pipeline called **Remap–Reflect–Adjust**. This pipeline leverages zero-shot recommendations from a powerful, non-deployable teacher LLM (e.g., GPT-4o) to create high-quality, catalog-grounded demonstrations.

- **Teacher-Generated Preliminary Demonstrations**: For each training dialogue `x_SFT_i`, the teacher LLM `Θ` generates a preliminary list of `N` candidate items, `y_SFT_i,raw`. These raw recommendations benefit from the teacher's advanced knowledge but may contain out-of-catalog (OOC) items, formatting inconsistencies, or the teacher's internal biases.

- **The Remap–Reflect–Adjust Pipeline**: This three-step pipeline refines `y_SFT_i,raw` into `y_SFT_i` by calculating a final score `s_final` for all catalog items, which is then ranked.
 - **(i) Remap**: This step grounds the teacher's recommendations into the target catalog space `C`. An initial score `s_remap` for each item in `C` is computed. This involves:
 - Generating comprehensive metadata (keywords, plot summaries) for items in both the teacher's space `C_Θ` and target catalog `C`, using the teacher LLM and Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) for unknown items.
 - Calculating item-item semantic similarity `S_item-item` between `C_Θ` and `C` using sentence embeddings.
 - Calculating conversation-item similarity `s_conv-item` between the dialogue `x_SFT_i` and catalog items.
 - Aggregating these signals: `s_remap = p ⋅ (S_item-item + I_ic) + λ ⋅ s_conv-item`, where `p` represents positional scores (e.g., `1/√k` for rank `k`), `I_ic` indicates direct catalog matches, and `λ` is a weighting hyperparameter.
 - **(ii) Reflect**: This step refines the ranking quality by leveraging the teacher LLM as a judge. The top `N_r` candidates (e.g., 100) from `s_remap` are presented to the teacher LLM, which rates their contextual suitability for `x_SFT_i` on a numerical scale. These ratings form `r_reflect`, which is then scaled by `γ` and added to `s_remap` to produce `s_reflect = s_remap + γ ⋅ r_reflect`.
 - **(iii) Adjust**: This final step corrects for residual popularity biases. Item-specific multiplicative (`w`) and additive (`b`) biases are learned to align `s_reflect` with the empirical distribution of groundtruth items in the training data, resulting in `s_final = w ⊙ s_reflect + b`. The final demonstration `y_SFT_i` consists of the top-N items ranked by `s_final`.
The SFT stage then minimizes the negative log-likelihood of these constructed demonstrations.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning-Based Alignment (RL)

Following SFT, the LLM policy `π_θ` is further aligned using reinforcement learning to optimize directly against structured rewards derived from user feedback (`y_gt_i` representing groundtruth positive items). The training data `D_RL` consists of conversations `x_RL_i` and corresponding groundtruth items.

- **Vanilla GRPO (Baseline)**: The paper first describes GRPO, which generates a group of `G` responses `{y_i}` from an old policy `π_θ_old`. A sequence-level reward `r(x, y_i)` (e.g., DCG@N) is calculated for each response. Advantages `Â_i` are estimated based on relative rewards within the group. Policy updates are performed by maximizing an objective that clips the token-level importance ratio `w_i,t(θ)` and applies the sequence-level advantage `Â_i` to token-level gradients.
 - **Limitations**: Gradient analysis reveals two misalignments for ranking tasks: (1) Sequence-level rewards are uniformly assigned to every token, leading to non-causal credit assignment (tokens of later items receive credit from earlier ranks). (2) The token-level importance ratio is too fine-grained and mismatched with sequence-level advantages. Methods like GSPO attempt to use sequence-level importance weights but still suffer from the coarseness of sequence-level rewards for ranking.

- **Rank-GRPO**: To address these misalignments, Rank-GRPO treats each rank `k` as the distinct action unit for advantage estimation and importance reweighting.
 - **Objective**: The objective `J_Rank-GRPO(θ)` sums over ranks and groups, using rank-level importance ratios `w_i,k(θ)` and advantages `Â_i,k`.
 - **Rank-Level Importance Ratio**: `w_i,k(θ) = ¯π_θ(y^(k)_i|x) / ¯π_θ_old(y^(k)_i|x)`. Here, `¯π_θ(y^(k)_i|x)` is defined as the geometric mean of the token probabilities for the item at rank `k`. This normalization helps stabilize importance weights across items of varying token lengths.
 - **Rank-Wise Advantage Estimation**: The advantage `Â_i,k` is derived from a rank-level return `r(x, y^(k)_i)`. The gradient analysis confirms that Rank-GRPO assigns a rank-specific advantage `Â_i,k` to all tokens of the `k`-th item, and both importance weight and gradient operate at the rank level, improving stability and task alignment.

- **Reward Shaping**: Rank-GRPO employs specific reward shaping strategies to prevent non-causal credit assignment:
 - **DCG@k:N**: Instead of sequence-level DCG@N, the rank-level return `r(x, y^(k)_i)` is defined as `DCG@k:N = Σ_{j=k to N} rel_j / log_2(j+1)`. This masks out "non-causal" rewards from previous ranks, ensuring an item receives credit only for its own contribution and subsequent influence.
 - **Exponential Decay (exp_∞)**: An alternative return `r_exp_Γ(x, y^(k)_i) = Σ_{j=k to N} rel_j Γ^(j-k)` is introduced. When `Γ = ∞`, only the current item's relevance `rel_k` is credited, indicating the highest causal alignment.
 - **Penalties**: Penalties `ϵ_o < 0` are applied for instruction-following failures, specifically for incomplete lists (fewer than N items) and overflow items (more than N items), to discourage under- or over-generation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted primarily on the REDDIT-V2 dataset, a large public benchmark for CRS, using Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct as backbone LLMs. Additional experiments were performed on the REDIAL dataset.

#### 4.1. Evaluation of the SFT Stage

- **Training Dynamics**: Training loss decreases, while validation loss plateaus, indicating the challenge of learning long, structured lists via pure behavioral cloning.
- **Catalog Grounding and Format Adherence**: The Remap–Reflect–Adjust pipeline effectively grounds the models. The proportion of in-catalog recommendations rapidly exceeds 99% across all backbone LLMs after SFT.
- **Ranking Ability Improvement**: SFT significantly improves the ranked list generation. NDCG@20 increased over 30× for the 0.5B model, 3× for the 1B model, and 1.5× for the 3B model compared to the initial zero-shot performance. This establishes SFT as a crucial warm-start for RL by providing catalog awareness, proper formatting, and initial ranking ability.

#### 4.2. Evaluation of the RL Stage

- **Reward Dynamics**: During Rank-GRPO training, rewards increase monotonically across ranks in both on-policy and off-policy settings, suggesting a comprehensive improvement across the recommendation list.
- **Convergence and NDCG Improvement**: Rank-GRPO demonstrated faster convergence and achieved higher NDCG scores on the validation set compared to vanilla GRPO and GSPO, with more substantial improvements observed at larger `k` values (e.g., NDCG@20). This indicates that Rank-GRPO maintains recommendation quality effectively towards the tail of the list, where standard methods typically degrade.
- **Reward Shaping Impact (Rank-GRPO exp_∞)**: The exponential decay variant (Rank-GRPO exp_∞), particularly when `Γ = ∞` (crediting only current item relevance), showed significant performance improvement at higher ranks. This observation suggests that the RL alignment stage of Rank-GRPO (exp_∞) operates in a manner resembling a retrieval and rerank strategy, where relevant items are initially included and then moved to higher ranks based on learned rewards. For lower ranks (e.g., 15, 20), rewards increased then decreased, while for higher ranks (1, 2, 5), rewards increased monotonically.
- **Off-Policy Performance**: In off-policy settings (where trajectories are reused), Rank-GRPO consistently outperformed GRPO and GSPO, although overall performance lagged behind on-policy due to the higher variance introduced by importance weighting.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Baselines

ConvRec-R1 demonstrated performance improvements over traditional CRS methods, prompting-based LLMs, and other LLM post-training approaches:

- **Contribution of Two Stages**: Both the SFT and RL stages were found to contribute substantially. SFT provides a strong baseline by grounding recommendations and ensuring correct formatting. RL further enhances performance by aligning generation with rank-wise rewards, particularly at higher ranks.
- **Outperforming Proprietary LLMs**: ConvRec-R1 with the Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct backbone substantially outperformed GPT-4o-mini. The Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct backbone performed comparably to GPT-4o, and the Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct backbone surpassed GPT-4o and CRAG (a GPT-4o-based system) in Recall and NDCG@20. This indicates that properly aligned smaller open-source LLMs can match or exceed larger proprietary models.
- **Comparison with DPO-based Methods**: Rank-GRPO consistently outperformed DPO, S-DPO, and SPRec (DPO variants) across all backbone sizes. This highlights Rank-GRPO's advantage in directly generating ranked lists, contrasting with DPO methods that primarily focus on candidate selection and require post-hoc ranking.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

- **Impact of Pipeline Components**: Removing the `remap` step significantly harmed performance by weakening catalog grounding. Removing the `reflect` step degraded the quality of the learned ranking, particularly at larger `k`. The full Remap–Reflect–Adjust pipeline was crucial for optimal performance, confirming the contribution of each component.
- **Necessity of SFT**: Removing the entire SFT stage resulted in a substantial performance drop (R1-zero baseline), underscoring SFT's role in providing strong catalog awareness and preliminary generative ranking ability to warm-start RL.

#### 4.5. Additional Analysis

- **Catalog Memorization in RL**: During RL training, both GRPO and Rank-GRPO showed a gradual drift away from the catalog. However, this drift was slower for Rank-GRPO, attributed to its rank-level return explicitly assigning zero reward to out-of-catalog items.
- **Sensitivity to Reward Shaping Hyperparameter**: Rank-GRPO (exp_∞) showed robustness to the exact magnitude of the instruction-following penalty `ϵ_o`. While some penalty was necessary to correct list length failures, performance did not fluctuate significantly across different non-zero penalty values.
- **Generalization to Larger Models (REDIAL)**: Experiments with the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbone on the smaller REDIAL dataset also showed consistent trends, with SFT providing strong improvements and Rank-GRPO further boosting ranking ability. The Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct + Rank-GRPO (exp_∞) model slightly outperformed its Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct counterpart, suggesting scalability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ConvRec-R1 framework presents a methodological advancement for training LLM-based conversational recommender systems. Its significance and potential impact include:

- **Addressing Core LLM-CRS Challenges**: The framework directly tackles persistent issues in LLM-based CRS, such as the generation of out-of-catalog items, output format non-conformance, and the degradation of recommendation quality in longer lists. By providing a structured approach to instill catalog awareness and proper formatting (SFT) and then refining ranking quality (RL), it enhances the reliability and usability of LLM-powered recommenders.
- **Enabling Smaller, Efficient LLMs**: The research demonstrates that small-to-medium sized open-source LLMs (0.5B-3B parameters), when aligned with ConvRec-R1, can achieve or surpass the performance of much larger, proprietary black-box models like GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini. This has substantial implications for the practical deployment of CRS by reducing computational costs, latency, and reliance on expensive API services.
- **Principled RL for Ranking Tasks**: The introduction of Rank-GRPO offers a principled solution for applying reinforcement learning to tasks with rank-structured outputs. By redefining the action unit to the rank level, introducing rank-level importance ratios, and employing causal reward masking, it resolves the fundamental misalignment issues of traditional GRPO-style algorithms. This innovation provides a more stable and effective optimization method for generative models producing ordered lists.
- **Broader Applicability**: While primarily demonstrated in conversational recommender systems, the Rank-GRPO methodology extends beyond this domain. It shows promise for a variety of other tasks that involve generating ranked or sequential outputs, such as search result ranking, document summarization, information retrieval, or any sequential decision-making problem where reward signals have a rank-dependent structure.
- **Facilitating Future Research**: The release of code and datasets can serve as a benchmark and foundation for further research into LLM alignment techniques, particularly those addressing structured generative outputs and the challenges of deploying LLMs in real-world interactive systems.
