# Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces randomization-based sanity checks to rigorously evaluate saliency map methods, revealing that some popular techniques like Guided Backpropagation are invariant to model parameter and data label randomization, essentially functioning as input feature detectors rather than true explanations of a model's learned behavior. The work establishes a methodology for assessing explanation faithfulness, showing that methods such as Gradients and GradCAM generally pass these tests while others do not.

## Problem

- The field of explainable AI, particularly for saliency methods, lacked rigorous and objective methodologies for evaluating the quality and faithfulness of explanations, often relying on subjective visual assessment.
- Many saliency methods had emerged, but their actual scope, reliability, and whether they truly reflected a model's learned decision-making or merely highlighted input features remained unproven.
- Visually plausible saliency maps could be deceptive, leading practitioners to trust explanations that did not accurately represent the model's internal workings or the data-generating process.

## Method

- The authors proposed a principled methodology using two ==randomization tests== to evaluate saliency methods' sensitivity to key aspects of the machine learning pipeline: model parameters and data labels.
- A **Model Parameter Randomization Test** was introduced, which progressively re-initializes model weights (==cascading== or independently) to assess if a saliency map genuinely reflects learned parameters or inherent input/architecture properties.
- A **Data Randomization Test** was implemented, comparing saliency maps from a truly trained model with one trained on ==randomly permuted labels== to determine if the explanation is faithful to learned input-label mappings or just memorized noise.

## Results

- Guided Backpropagation and Guided GradCAM demonstrably failed the proposed sanity checks, exhibiting remarkable insensitivity to both model parameter randomization and training on random labels.
- Saliency methods including Gradients, SmoothGrad, and GradCAM generally passed the sanity checks, showing sensitivity to changes in learned model parameters and data-label relationships.
- Theoretical analysis for simple models confirmed that saliency methods can inherently produce edge-like features, supporting the empirical observation that some methods resemble classical edge detectors and respond to low-level input features over learned semantics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Solely relying on visual assessment of saliency maps is insufficient and often misleading, as maps can maintain structural similarity to the input even when the model's learned parameters or semantic meaning are destroyed.
> - Popular saliency methods such as Guided Backpropagation and Guided GradCAM are surprisingly invariant to randomization of higher-layer model parameters and data labels, suggesting they primarily capture low-level input features.
> - Some 'explanation' methods may function more as sophisticated input feature detectors, akin to classical edge detectors, rather than providing insights faithful to the specific decision-making process learned by the model from the data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits Into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps" is situated at a critical juncture in the field of artificial intelligence, specifically within the burgeoning domain of explainable AI (XAI) and interpretable machine learning (IML). As machine learning models, particularly deep neural networks, have grown dramatically in complexity and impact across various sensitive applications (e.g., healthcare, finance, autonomous driving), the demand for understanding *why* these models make certain predictions has intensified.

Saliency methods, which aim to highlight features in an input that are most relevant to a model's prediction, emerged as a popular class of tools to address this demand. They promise to offer insights for tasks such as model debugging, satisfying regulatory requirements (like GDPR's "right to explanation"), identifying biases, and building user trust. However, the proliferation of numerous saliency methods, often guided by their visual appeal, has created a significant methodological challenge: the lack of principled guidelines for assessing their actual scope and quality. Prior to this work, evaluating these methods often relied on subjective visual assessment, perturbation-based metrics, or object localization tasks, which the authors argue are insufficient or can be misleading.

This paper directly addresses this critical gap by proposing a rigorous, actionable methodology for evaluating explanation approaches. It challenges the prevailing practice of solely relying on visual plausibility, which it demonstrates can be deceiving. By introducing "sanity checks" based on statistical randomization tests, the authors advocate for a more scientific and objective approach to XAI. This work fundamentally questions the faithfulness of certain popular saliency methods, revealing that some might merely highlight intrinsic input features (like edges) rather than truly explaining the model's learned decision-making process or the underlying data distribution.

The paper connects with existing research in several ways:
- **Related Saliency Methods:** It evaluates a broad range of established saliency techniques (e.g., Gradients, Guided Backpropagation, GradCAM, Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad), putting them under scrutiny.
- **Fragility and Vulnerability:** It builds upon previous work that exposed the fragility or unreliability of saliency methods (e.g., Ghorbani et al., Kindermans et al., Nie et al.) by providing a framework to *detect* such fundamental weaknesses, rather than just demonstrate them.
- **Generalization and Memorization:** The data randomization test is inspired by Zhang et al.'s work on understanding deep learning's generalization capabilities, adapting the concept of training on random labels to evaluate explanation methods.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** The analytical interpretation for linear and simple convolutional models attempts to provide theoretical underpinnings for the observed empirical behaviors, drawing parallels to earlier work on understanding network representations (e.g., Saxe et al., Ulyanov et al.).

By advocating for a shift from qualitative, impressionistic evaluation to quantitative, principle-based assessment, this paper contributes significantly to maturing the field of explainable AI. It pushes the community to develop and deploy methods that are not just visually appealing but are genuinely faithful to the models they purport to explain and the data they learned from. This work is pivotal for establishing trustworthy and reliable AI systems, thereby influencing how future XAI methods will be designed, validated, and ultimately adopted.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps" stems from the increasing reliance on complex machine learning models, particularly deep neural networks, and the corresponding critical need for explanation methods. While saliency maps have emerged as a popular tool for this purpose, a significant methodological void exists regarding their evaluation. The authors highlight that the current reliance on visual assessment of saliency maps can be profoundly misleading, leading practitioners to trust explanations that may not accurately reflect the model's internal workings or the data-generating process.

The key objectives of this research are multi-faceted:

1. **Develop a Principled Evaluation Methodology:** The overarching goal is to propose an actionable, principled methodology to rigorously evaluate the scope and quality of explanation methods, moving beyond subjective visual appeal. This methodology aims to determine what kinds of explanations a given method can and cannot reliably provide.
2. **Introduce Concrete Sanity Checks:** To instantiate this methodology, the paper proposes two specific, easy-to-implement randomization tests:
 - **Model Parameter Randomization Test:** To assess an explanation method's sensitivity to the *learned parameters* of a model. The objective is to determine if the saliency map genuinely reflects what the model has learned, or if it primarily captures properties inherent to the input or the network architecture itself, irrespective of training.
 - **Data Randomization Test:** To evaluate an explanation method's sensitivity to the *relationship between instances and labels* encoded in the training data. The aim is to check if the explanation is faithful to the meaningful correlations learned from the data, or if it persists even when the model has merely memorized random associations.
3. **Empirically Evaluate Existing Saliency Methods:** To apply the proposed sanity checks comprehensively to several widely used saliency methods across various model architectures (Inception v3, CNN, MLP) and datasets (ImageNet, MNIST, Fashion MNIST). This objective seeks to expose the limitations of current practices and identify which methods truly pass these fundamental tests.
4. **Uncover Fundamental Invariances:** Through these experiments, a key objective is to discover that some popular saliency methods are surprisingly *invariant* to significant transformations—specifically, randomization of model parameters and data labels. This finding would imply that such methods are inadequate for tasks that require explanations faithful to the model or the data.
5. **Provide Theoretical Interpretation:** To interpret the empirical findings through theoretical analysis, examining simple models (linear models, 1-layer convolutional networks) to understand *why* certain saliency methods behave as observed, particularly their resemblance to low-level image processing techniques like edge detection.
6. **Advocate for Rigorous XAI:** Ultimately, the motivation is to guide researchers and practitioners toward a more rigorous and scientifically sound approach to designing, evaluating, and deploying explainable AI systems, ensuring that explanations are genuinely informative and trustworthy for critical tasks such as model debugging, bias detection, and compliance.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need to bring scientific rigor to the evaluation of XAI methods, ensuring that "explanations" are truly reflective of a model's learned behavior rather than merely visually convincing artifacts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in proposing and implementing an actionable methodology based on randomization tests to evaluate the adequacy and faithfulness of saliency methods. The central idea is to compare the output of a saliency method under normal conditions (a trained model on real data) with its output under artificially randomized conditions, thereby probing its sensitivity to crucial aspects of the machine learning pipeline.

The methodology comprises two main types of sanity checks:

1. **Model Parameter Randomization Test:**
 - **Purpose:** To assess whether a saliency method is sensitive to the *learned weights and biases* of a neural network. If a method truly explains a model's learned behavior, its output should change substantially when the model's parameters are randomized, effectively destroying what the model has learned.
 - **Procedure:**
 - **Cascading Randomization:** This involves progressively re-initializing the weights of a model, starting from the top (output) layer and moving downwards towards the input layer, block by block. For each step, saliency maps are generated and compared to those from the fully trained model. This reveals at what depth a method loses its dependence on learned parameters.
 - **Independent Randomization:** In this variation, individual layers or blocks are randomized one at a time, while all other layers retain their original, trained weights. This helps isolate the dependence of explanations on specific layers.
 - **Evaluation:** The similarity between saliency maps from the trained model and those from randomized models is assessed using both visual inspection and quantitative metrics.

2. **Data Randomization Test:**
 - **Purpose:** To evaluate whether a saliency method is sensitive to the *meaningful relationship between input instances and their labels* that the model learned from the training data. A model trained on randomly permuted labels is forced to memorize noise rather than generalize from underlying patterns, achieving high training accuracy but random test accuracy. If an explanation method is faithful to the learned input-label mapping, its output should differ significantly between a model trained on true labels and one trained on random labels.
 - **Procedure:** A model with the same architecture is trained on a version of the dataset where all training labels have been randomly shuffled. This model is trained until it achieves high training accuracy (e.g., >95%), demonstrating its capacity to fit random data. Saliency maps are then generated for test inputs using both the truly trained model and the randomly-labeled-trained model, and compared.

**Saliency Methods Evaluated:**
The study applies these tests to a diverse set of commonly used saliency methods for deep neural networks, including:
- Gradient (Simonyan et al.)
- Gradient Input (Shrikumar et al.)
- Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al.)
- Guided Backpropagation (Springenberg et al.)
- GradCAM (Selvaraju et al.)
- Guided GradCAM (combination of GradCAM and Guided Backpropagation)
- SmoothGrad (Smilkov et al.)
- VarGrad (variance analog of SmoothGrad)

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Datasets:** ImageNet (for object recognition), MNIST (for handwritten digit classification), and Fashion MNIST (for clothing item classification).
- **Model Architectures:** Inception v3 (a large, pre-trained convolutional neural network for ImageNet), a custom Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for MNIST/Fashion MNIST, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) for MNIST/Fashion MNIST.
- **Weight Initialization:** For randomization, truncated normal distributions (and uniform distribution for sanity checks) were used for re-initializing weights.

**Visualization and Similarity Metrics:**
The comparison of saliency maps relies on both qualitative and quantitative assessments:
- **Visualization:** Saliency maps are visualized in two ways:
 - Absolute-value (ABS): Absolute values of normalized maps.
 - Diverging visualization: Original map values, using different colors for positive and negative importance.
- **Quantitative Metrics:**
 - Spearman rank correlation (with and without absolute values)
 - Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)
 - Pearson correlation of Histogram of Gradients (HOGs)

**Theoretical Analysis:**
The paper complements its empirical findings with theoretical analyses for simplified models:
- **Linear Model:** To demonstrate how basic gradient methods should behave.
- **1-Layer Sum-Pooling Convolutional Network:** To show how saliency methods applied to even simple convolutional structures can inherently produce edge-like features, irrespective of learned semantics.

**Analogy with Edge Detectors:**
A crucial part of the approach is drawing an analogy between the outputs of certain saliency methods and classical edge detectors. This comparison highlights that some "explanations" might simply be rediscovering low-level image features without any dependence on the high-level concepts learned by the model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation using the proposed randomization tests yielded several striking and counter-intuitive findings, fundamentally challenging the common perceptions about certain saliency methods:

1. **Visual Assessment is Insufficient and Misleading:** A consistent and critical finding across all experiments is that solely relying on visual inspection of saliency maps can be highly deceptive. Maps that appear visually plausible or informative (e.g., highlighting object contours) can, in fact, be largely independent of the model's learned parameters or the true input-label relationships. This "visual perception versus ranking dichotomy" demonstrates that a method's output might maintain structural similarity to the input even when its semantic meaning or reliance on learned features has been completely destroyed.

2. **Model Parameter Randomization Test Reveals Invariances:**
 - **Gradients, SmoothGrad, and GradCAM:** These methods generally showed sensitivity to model parameter randomization. Their saliency maps changed substantially as model weights were randomized, particularly in higher layers, indicating that they genuinely depend on what the model has learned. GradCAM's sensitivity was observed if the randomization occurred downstream of its last convolutional layer.
 - **Guided Backpropagation (GBP) and Guided GradCAM:** These methods were found to be remarkably *invariant* to randomization of higher-layer weights. Their saliency maps remained visually and quantitatively similar to those produced by the fully trained model, even when most of the network's learned parameters were destroyed. Changes were only observed when very low-level (input-proximal) convolutional weights were randomized, but even then, the masks remained dominated by the input's inherent structure. This implies that GBP and Guided GradCAM are largely insensitive to the high-level semantic features learned by the model.
 - **Gradient Input and Integrated Gradients:** While these methods showed some visual changes with randomization (especially in the sign of attributions), the underlying input structure remained clearly prevalent in their masks, reinforcing the idea of input dominance over learned features in certain contexts.

3. **Data Randomization Test Confirms Insensitivity:**
 - **Gradients, SmoothGrad, and GradCAM:** These methods demonstrated substantial changes when applied to models trained on randomly permuted labels, reflecting their sensitivity to the meaningful input-label relationships encoded in the original data.
 - **Guided Backpropagation (GBP) and Guided GradCAM:** Even when the model was trained on random labels (and thus learned no meaningful patterns), GBP and Guided GradCAM still produced saliency maps that, upon naive visual inspection, highlighted "plausible" portions of the input. Quantitatively, their similarity to maps from the truly trained model remained surprisingly high, indicating that they do not primarily reflect the *learned relationships* between inputs and outputs, but rather inherent input characteristics.
 - **Gradient Input and Integrated Gradients:** Similar to the model parameter test, these methods also showed that despite visual changes (e.g., sign of attributions), the fundamental input structure persisted, suggesting that they are not fully reflecting the learned data patterns either.

4. **Theoretical Interpretation and Edge Detector Analogy:**
 - **Linear Model:** For a simple linear model, basic gradients correctly returned the model weights, demonstrating faithful explanation. Integrated Gradients reduced to `(x - x_bar) * w/2`.
 - **1-Layer Convolutional Network:** Analysis showed that for a simple 1-layer ReLU convolutional network with sum-pooling, saliency methods can inherently behave like *edge detectors*. The convolutional structure itself, even with random weights, can produce activations and gradients that highlight boundaries and sharp transitions in the input image.
 - **Edge Detector Comparison:** The paper provides compelling visual evidence comparing saliency maps from methods like Guided Backpropagation to outputs of classical, untrained edge detectors. The striking visual similarity strongly suggests that these saliency methods are primarily responding to low-level image features (edges, textures) that are present in the input *regardless* of what the deep network has learned or how it makes its prediction. This means they often act as unsupervised image processing techniques rather than faithful model explanations.

In summary, the key finding is that while some methods (Gradients, SmoothGrad, GradCAM) pass the proposed sanity checks, indicating faithfulness to learned model parameters and data relationships, others, most notably Guided Backpropagation and its variants (e.g., Guided GradCAM), fundamentally fail these tests. Their insensitivity suggests they primarily capture low-level input features and are therefore inadequate for tasks requiring an explanation of the *model's specific learned decision process* or the *data's underlying structure*.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps" carry significant implications for the field of explainable AI (XAI) and machine learning more broadly, potentially reshaping how interpretability methods are developed, evaluated, and utilized.

1. **Elevating Rigor in XAI Evaluation:** The most immediate impact is the call for a more scientific and rigorous approach to evaluating interpretability methods. By demonstrating that subjective visual appeal can be misleading, the paper provides a concrete methodology—the randomization tests—that shifts the focus from "does it look good?" to "does it truly explain what the model learned?" This will undoubtedly influence the design and validation of future XAI techniques, pushing researchers to include these sanity checks as a fundamental part of their evaluation pipelines.

2. **Guiding Practitioners and Researchers:** The paper offers invaluable guidance to both researchers developing new explanation methods and practitioners seeking to apply them. It provides a clear, actionable framework for assessing the suitability of a given method for a specific task. For instance, if a task requires understanding *why* a model made a specific prediction based on its learned parameters (e.g., debugging, identifying bias), methods that fail the model parameter randomization test (like Guided Backpropagation) would be deemed inadequate. This helps prevent the misinterpretation and misuse of saliency maps, which could otherwise lead to erroneous conclusions about model behavior.

3. **Challenging Established Methods:** The paper directly challenges the utility of several widely adopted saliency methods, particularly Guided Backpropagation and Guided GradCAM, for tasks requiring faithfulness to the learned model or data. By revealing their invariance to model parameter and data label randomization, it suggests that these methods may often function more as sophisticated edge detectors or input feature highlight tools rather than genuine explanations of a model's high-level reasoning. This finding necessitates a critical re-evaluation of past research that relied on these methods for interpreting complex model decisions.

4. **Promoting Trustworthy and Responsible AI:** For AI systems to be deployed responsibly, especially in high-stakes domains, their explanations must be trustworthy. This work contributes to building more reliable XAI by identifying methods that can genuinely reveal model pathologies, biases, or unexpected behaviors. If an explanation method is not sensitive to how the model was trained or what it learned, it cannot effectively contribute to debugging, auditing, or ensuring fairness, thus undermining efforts towards responsible AI development.

5. **Redefining "Explanation":** The research prompts a deeper philosophical and practical discussion about what constitutes a "model explanation." It argues that an explanation must inherently depend on the specific function the model has learned and the data it learned from. Methods that merely highlight salient input features, independent of the learned function, are fundamentally different from those that reveal the model's internal reasoning process. This distinction is crucial for advancing the theoretical foundations of XAI.

6. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The paper explicitly states that its work is a "stepping stone towards a more rigorous evaluation." It encourages the exploration of other invariances and transformations to further refine interpretability assessment. This opens avenues for future research into robustness, causality, and the interaction between model architecture, training data, and explanation fidelity.

In essence, "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps" serves as a critical checkpoint in the rapid expansion of XAI. It provides a necessary dose of skepticism and a robust toolset to ensure that the pursuit of interpretability leads to genuine understanding and not merely to visually pleasing, yet misleading, artifacts. Its impact will be felt in the improved scientific rigor of XAI research, more informed deployment of interpretable models, and ultimately, greater trust in complex AI systems.
