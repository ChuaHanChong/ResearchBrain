# Belief-Aware Influence and Trust (BAIT): Shaping Human Belief During Repeated Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> The Belief-Aware Influence and Trust (BAIT) framework enables robots to manage human perceptions during repeated interactions by balancing long-term influence and trust. This approach, validated in lane-merging scenarios, effectively shapes human yielding behavior without severely eroding trust, operating in real-time.

## Problem

- Robots in repeated interactions face human adaptation, where predictable behaviors are exploited, leading to performance degradation.
- Existing long-term influence strategies often generate erratic robot actions, which undermine human trust and transparency.
- Computational complexity of current long-term influence frameworks limits their real-time deployment in dynamic Human-Robot Interaction (HRI).

## Method

- **BAIT** uses a ==hierarchical particle filter== to track both fast-changing human strategies and slow-evolving human beliefs about the robot.
- A ==belief-aware Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planner== optimizes robot actions by explicitly arbitrating between maintaining influence (via unpredictability) and preserving trust (via legibility).
- Real-time performance is achieved by employing a ==deterministic surrogate for belief propagation== within the MPPI, avoiding full sample-based filtering for each rollout.

## Results

- BAIT's hierarchical filter robustly estimated human states (e.g., long-term belief MSE of **0.04-0.05** and short-term strategy cosine similarity of **0.95-0.97** in simulation and similar in user study).
- In a lane-merging task, BAIT-adapt significantly reduced human yielding (maintained influence) while preserving human trust significantly better than pure influence policies (**p=0.0128**).
- The framework operated in real-time (approx. **0.11 seconds** per step) and successfully demonstrated its ability to influence human drivers in real-world vehicle deployments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective long-term HRI requires a robot to explicitly model and manage the trade-off between asserting influence to prevent exploitation and behaving transparently to build human trust.
> - Human learning in HRI occurs across distinct timescales—immediate tactical adjustments and slower shifts in overall robot perception—which can be leveraged for robust inference and planning.
> - Real-time, belief-aware robot control is feasible for complex HRI problems by using approximations that maintain core belief dynamics without exhaustive computation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of robots into human-centric environments necessitates frameworks that account for dynamic human adaptation during repeated interactions. Traditional approaches in human-robot interaction (HRI) often model human behavior as static or treat encounters as isolated events. These models typically employ frameworks such as fixed reward-maximizing agents, static behavior distributions, or offline-trained trajectory predictors. While sufficient for one-shot interactions, these stationary assumptions lead to performance degradation over time as humans learn to exploit predictable robot behaviors, diminishing task efficiency and efficacy.

A more recent line of research acknowledges long-term human adaptation and aims to actively guide this dynamic to maintain cumulative task performance. These methods, often based on deliberating generating unpredictable robot behavior, seek to prevent human exploitation and preserve robot influence over extended interactions. However, these influence-centric approaches frequently result in erratic robot behaviors that reduce transparency, erode human trust, and narrow safety margins. Furthermore, many existing long-term influence controllers, such as those relying on strict Stackelberg assumptions or computationally intensive Mixed Observability Markov Decision Process (MOMDP) solvers, face significant scalability limitations that hinder their deployment in real-time applications.

This work contributes to the field by addressing these identified gaps. It proposes a framework that explicitly models human adaptation over multiple timescales and, crucially, optimizes for a balance between maintaining long-horizon influence and preserving human trust, while simultaneously ensuring computational tractability for real-time deployment. By doing so, it attempts to advance HRI beyond systems that either achieve immediate task success at the cost of long-term adaptability or those that secure long-term influence at the expense of human trust and computational efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that human behavior is dynamic and adaptive during repeated interactions with robots. In scenarios such as autonomous vehicles navigating shared spaces or delivery robots encountering the same individuals, humans continuously observe robot actions, update their internal perceptions of the robot, and adapt their strategies accordingly. When robots are consistently predictable or defensive, humans may exploit this predictability, leading to degraded task performance for the robot (e.g., human drivers refusing to yield to a hesitant autonomous vehicle). Sustained task performance therefore depends on the robot's ability to shape human perception and future behavior over time.

Given this context, the key objectives of this work are:

- **Accurate Inference of Latent Human States:** To develop a robust mechanism for continuously inferring the human's unobservable internal states, specifically their short-term behavioral strategy and their slower-evolving long-term perception of the robot. This is crucial because human actions are influenced by these hidden states, and effective robot planning requires an understanding of them.
- **Belief-Aware Planning for Long-Term Influence:** To leverage the inferred human latent states within a planning framework that actively guides the evolution of human perception. The goal is to steer human beliefs toward configurations that secure favorable long-term outcomes, such as ensuring the robot retains the necessary influence to prompt human cooperation (e.g., yielding during a lane change), thereby preventing behavioral exploitation.
- **Balancing Influence and Human Trust:** To address the tension between maintaining long-horizon influence (which often involves some degree of unpredictability to prevent exploitation) and preserving human trust (which generally requires legible and predictable robot behavior). The objective is to explicitly optimize this trade-off as a primary design axis, rather than pursuing either goal in isolation.
- **Ensuring Real-Time Computational Tractability:** To overcome the scalability bottlenecks associated with existing long-term influence frameworks (e.g., full MOMDP solvers or complex Stackelberg formulations) by developing an architecture capable of real-time execution, making it viable for practical deployment in dynamic environments.
- **Enforcing Immediate Task Performance Constraints:** To ensure that any belief-shaping maneuvers or strategies to maintain influence and trust do not compromise immediate physical safety or the primary task objectives, such as collision avoidance and successful lane merging.

The research formalizes the problem as a MOMDP where the robot must optimize a joint objective that combines immediate task performance with a belief cost. This belief cost is designed to explicitly balance the robot’s behavioral unpredictability (for influence) against transparency (for trust), while enforcing safety as a strict constraint. This approach is motivated by the need for robots to operate effectively and acceptably in dynamic human environments over extended periods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce the Belief-Aware Influence and Trust (BAIT) controller to address the problem of robot decision-making in repeated human-robot interactions, particularly the trade-off between long-term influence and human trust. BAIT integrates a hierarchical particle filter with a belief-aware Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planner.

**Human Model with Latent Representations:**
The human's internal state is structured into a two-timescale hierarchy:
- **Short-term strategy ($z_t$):** A fast-changing, continuous latent variable (e.g., a weighting vector across task-related costs like forward progress vs. collision avoidance) that captures the human's instantaneous reactions to the immediate physical context.
- **Long-term belief ($\phi_t$):** A slow-evolving, continuous latent variable (e.g., perception of the robot's assertiveness, ranging from 0 for assertive to 1 for defensive) that represents the human's overarching perception of the robot, adapting gradually over multiple interactions.
The long-term belief $\phi_t$ modulates the short-term strategy $z_t$, implying that the human's overall perception influences their immediate tactical decisions. Human actions ($a_H$) are modeled using a maximum-entropy Boltzmann policy, parameterized by the strategy weights $z_t$. The overall problem is formulated as an MOMDP, where the robot seeks to optimize its actions ($a_R$) to shape $\phi_t$ and maximize cumulative task performance.

**Hierarchical Inference of Human Latent States:**
BAIT employs a two-layered particle filter to track the human's hidden state (joint posterior over ($\phi_t$, $z_t$)) in real time. This hierarchical structure is designed to leverage the timescale separation and conditional dependence ($p(z|\phi)$) of the latent variables, aiming for computational efficiency over a standard flat filter.
- **Structure:** The filter maintains $N_{\phi}$ long-term particles, each carrying its own conditional ensemble of $N_z$ short-term particles.
- **Prediction Step:**
 - **Long-term belief ($\phi$):** Propagates via a mixture Beta kernel, modeling bounded, gradual drift and a slow reset probability to prevent particle collapse. A concentration parameter ($\kappa$) controls drift variance, and a constant ($c$) shapes reset particle distribution.
 - **Short-term strategy ($z$):** Evolves via an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, mean-reverting toward a target attractor ($z_{tgt}$) determined by its parent $\phi_{t+1}$. A mean-reversion rate ($\alpha_{ou}$) dictates adaptation speed, and a noise scale ($\sigma_z$) models stochasticity.
- **Update Step:**
 - **Short-term $z$-particles:** Reweighted using action evidence from the Boltzmann log-likelihood of observed human actions over a decaying history length ($W$). A soft inertia blend ($\alpha_z$) is used for normalized weight updates.
 - **Long-term $\phi$-particles:** Updated by fusing aggregated $z$-ensemble action evidence with direct geometric evidence (e.g., lateral gap, closing rate for lane merging). A smooth yield-belief is maintained via an exponential moving average of an instantaneous yield-belief target extracted from kinematic features. Geometric evidence is only activated when the human is within a critical interaction zone and the robot is longitudinally ahead.
- **Resampling Step:** Resampling is independently triggered for each layer based on an effective-sample-size (ESS) criterion. The $\phi$-layer uses Liu-West kernel smoothing, while $z$-ensembles are resampled with partial injection of new particles from the prior $p(z|\phi^{(i)})$.

**Belief-Aware MPPI Planning:**
The MPPI planner uses the inferred posterior means ($\hat{\phi}$, $\hat{z}$) to optimize the robot's control sequence.
- **Cost Function:** The planner evaluates $K$ candidate action sequences against a belief cost ($C_{belief}$), which explicitly balances influence and trust using an arbitration parameter $\beta \in$. $C_{belief}$ is only applied when the robot is in an active interaction zone.
 - **Influence Cost ($C_{infl}$):** Aims to prevent human exploitation by promoting unpredictable robot behaviors (high entropy $H(\hat{\phi}_{k+1})$) while biasing the unpredictability towards an assertive outcome ($\phi \to 0$).
 - **Trust Cost ($C_{trust}$):** Promotes transparent trajectories that induce a predictable human perception (low entropy $H(\hat{\phi}_{k+1})$) and signal an accommodating intent ($\phi \to 1$).
- **Scalability:** To maintain real-time performance, the planner propagates a noise-free deterministic surrogate of the posterior moments for cost evaluation, rather than running a full sample-based filter for each rollout. This involves predicting human actions, advancing physical states, and updating the surrogate latent states using the geometric kernel and noise-free strategy dynamics.
- **Task Performance Constraint:** Collision avoidance and lane merging, defined as $C_{task}$, are treated as a strict Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) constraint. Rollouts exceeding an acceptable cost threshold ($\tau$) based on their mean and standard deviation are heavily penalized, effectively excluding unsafe trajectories.
- **Robot Control Modes:** The paper explores three variants of the BAIT controller: BAIT-trust ($\beta=0$, pure trust), BAIT-infl ($\beta=1$, pure influence), and BAIT-adapt (dynamically switches $\beta$ between 0 and 1 based on recent success rate and human belief to arbitrate between modes).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the BAIT controller through simulations, a human-subject study, and real-world vehicle deployments in repeated lane-merging scenarios.

**A. Hierarchical Particle Filter Estimation Robustness:**
- Table I presents the tracking accuracy of BAIT’s hierarchical particle filter across simulated and in-person user study environments, regardless of the robot's control mode (BAIT-trust, BAIT-infl, BAIT-adapt).
- The filter consistently demonstrated robust estimation accuracy for both short-term strategy ($\hat{z}$) and long-term belief ($\hat{\phi}$). For simulated humans, $\phi$ BCE ranged from 0.44 to 0.69, $\phi$ MSE from 0.04 to 0.05, $z$ MSE from 0.04 to 0.09, and $z$ Cosine Similarity from 0.95 to 0.97. Similar metrics were observed in the in-person user study.
- Trajectory prediction metrics (1-ADE, H-ADE, H-FDE) remained relatively consistent across BAIT variants, indicating that the posterior moments provide reliable input to the deterministic surrogate used in planning. This validation of the filter and the simulated human model supported their use as a reliable proxy for real human behavior.

**B. Task Performance (Human Lane Progress) in Simulation and User Study:**
- **Simulated Human (Fig. 3, left):**
 - Static Stackelberg and BAIT-trust policies resulted in significantly higher human lane progress, indicating that the simulated human agent exploited their predictable yielding.
 - Influence-optimizing models, Belief-Entropy Stackelberg and BAIT-infl, maintained low human lane progress, successfully influencing the human to yield.
 - BAIT-adapt successfully arbitrated between influence and trust, achieving a statistically significant reduction in human lane progress compared to Stackelberg and BAIT-trust, while resulting in higher lane progress than the pure influence approaches (BAIT-infl and Belief-Entropy Stackelberg).
 - This demonstrated BAIT-adapt's ability to balance these objectives.
- **In-Person User Study (Fig. 3, center and right):**
 - The results mirrored the simulation findings. BAIT-trust led to the highest human lane progress, as participants learned to exploit the robot’s predictable yielding over repeated episodes.
 - Both BAIT-infl and BAIT-adapt successfully asserted merging, maintaining lower human lane progress across episodes.
 - BAIT-adapt achieved a statistically significant reduction in lane progress compared to BAIT-trust (p<0.01) and showed equivalent efficacy to BAIT-infl (p=0.811) in maintaining influence.
- **Computational Scalability:** BAIT achieved real-time execution at approximately 0.11 seconds per step by utilizing a noise-free deterministic surrogate for belief propagation, avoiding the computational intensity of full MOMDP solvers.

**C. Subjective Human Trust and Comfort in User Study:**
- **Comfort:** Participants reported significantly higher comfort levels with BAIT-trust (median 5) compared to BAIT-infl (median 2) or BAIT-adapt (median 4), as indicated by both comfort and discomfort measures (p<0.005 for all pairwise comparisons). This suggests that sacrificing predictability for influence reduces human comfort.
- **Trust:** While BAIT-trust maximized trust (median 5), BAIT-adapt (median 3) preserved user trust at a level significantly higher than BAIT-infl (median 2, p=0.0128). This indicates that BAIT-adapt's controlled unpredictability mitigated the severe trust degradation observed with pure influence strategies.
- **Anticipation:** BAIT-adapt was perceived as less predictable (anticipation median 4) than BAIT-trust (median 6, p=0.003), aligning with its adaptive nature.
- **Conclusion:** BAIT-adapt effectively maintained influence to induce human yielding, similar to pure influence policies, but without the extreme erosion of human trust that pure influence policies incurred. While some comfort was inherently sacrificed compared to a purely trust-focused policy, the system successfully navigated the influence-trust trade-off.

**D. Real-World Experiment:**
- The BAIT-adapt controller was successfully deployed on physical Polaris GEM e2 vehicles, interacting with a human-driven GEM e4 in a scaled lane-merging scenario.
- The robot demonstrated real-time operation and successfully asserted its right-of-way, consistently prompting the human driver to yield across repeated interactions. This validated the practical viability and real-world applicability of the BAIT framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, BAIT, that addresses several critical challenges in designing intelligent robots for repeated human-robot interaction. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Addressing the Influence-Trust Dilemma:** The work provides a novel approach to explicitly manage the fundamental trade-off between maintaining long-term influence over human behavior and preserving human trust. By demonstrating that a robot can assert its intent and avoid exploitation without severely eroding trust, BAIT offers a more balanced and sustainable paradigm for HRI. This is crucial for the long-term acceptance and effective operation of autonomous systems in shared spaces.
- **Enhancing Robustness to Human Adaptation:** By integrating a hierarchical particle filter that tracks both fast-changing human strategies and slow-evolving human perceptions, BAIT equips robots with a more sophisticated understanding of human partners. This enables the robot to adapt its behavior proactively, preventing the common problem of humans exploiting predictable robot policies and leading to more robust and consistent task performance over time.
- **Improving Computational Scalability:** The use of a deterministic surrogate for belief propagation within the MPPI planner significantly reduces the computational burden compared to traditional MOMDP solvers or exhaustive sample-based simulations. This allows BAIT to operate in real-time on physical hardware, making it suitable for practical deployment in dynamic and safety-critical applications like autonomous driving.
- **Fostering Safer and More Efficient Autonomous Systems:** By ensuring immediate task performance through a strict CVaR safety constraint, BAIT combines its advanced influence and trust management with fundamental safety guarantees. This has direct implications for the development of safer and more efficient autonomous vehicles, delivery robots, and assistive robots that must repeatedly interact with humans.
- **Laying Groundwork for Future HRI Research:** The explicit formulation and arbitration of influence and trust, along with the hierarchical human modeling approach, opens avenues for further research. Future work could explore continuous optimization of the arbitration parameter, more complex human adaptation models, and the generalization of this framework to other interactive tasks and human-robot teams.

In summary, BAIT represents a step forward in designing autonomous systems that are not only capable of performing tasks but also of intelligently managing their long-term social interactions with humans, leading to more acceptable, reliable, and effective robotic deployments in diverse environments.
