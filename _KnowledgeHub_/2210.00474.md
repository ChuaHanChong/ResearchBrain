# Saving the Limping: Fault-tolerant Quadruped Locomotion via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Nanyang Technological University and NVIDIA AI Technology Centre developed a reinforcement learning framework enabling quadruped robots to maintain stable locomotion despite critical hardware failures like joint locking, achieving zero-shot transfer from simulation to a physical Unitree A1 robot. The approach improved post-failure survival rates to 100% on the physical robot for tested joint failures, compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots face significant challenges maintaining operation and stability when encountering hardware failures like joint locking in uncontrolled environments.
- Traditional control methods require extensive manual tuning and specific modeling for each failure scenario, limiting generalization across different failure types.
- Existing reinforcement learning solutions for fault tolerance primarily validate in simulation, struggling with the sim-to-real gap for hardware failures due to complex modeling challenges.

## Method

- A realistic joint locking simulation strategy was designed with randomized parameters (failure time, joint, tolerance) to train a generalized agent, complemented by physical locking mechanisms for real-world testing.
- A joint teacher-student ==reinforcement learning== framework was introduced, where the policy and student network are optimized simultaneously, adaptively fusing teacher's privileged information and student's inferred dynamics.
- Extensive domain randomization, including ground friction, PD controller settings, and motor strength, was applied to enhance the robustness and sim-to-real transferability of the control policies.

## Results

- On a physical Unitree A1 robot, the fault-tolerant agent achieved **100%** survival across all tested single joint locking scenarios (hip, thigh, calf, using softlock and hardlock), whereas a baseline agent achieved only 5-**20%** survival.
- In simulation, the fault-tolerant agent maintained a higher post-failure forward velocity (**0.47** m/s) and significantly longer survival times (averaging **56.5%** of the post-failure period) compared to a baseline agent (**0.42** m/s and **44.7%**).
- The built-in Unitree A1 controller consistently failed immediately (**0%** survival) when subjected to a hardlock, highlighting the improved resilience of the developed RL-based approach.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit privilege information regarding failure states is crucial for teacher policies to effectively learn and minimize the negative impact of hardware failures.
> - The joint teacher-student training approach, with adaptive fusion of latent representations, provides more robust and efficient knowledge transfer for fault-tolerant policies compared to traditional supervised transfer methods.
> - Randomizing failure scenarios and jointly optimizing the teacher-student network enables a generalized policy that can adapt even to untrained multi-joint failure scenarios on a physical robot.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadrupedal robotics has made substantial progress, enabling robots to traverse and sprint on complex terrains. However, a significant challenge remains in ensuring their survival and operational continuity when confronted with hardware failures in uncontrolled, remote environments. Current commercial quadruped robots often feature limited hardware failure detection and protection mechanisms, primarily focusing on system shutdowns rather than adaptive locomotion.

Traditional control methods, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Whole Body Control (WBC), have been applied to quadruped locomotion. While effective under normal conditions, these methods typically require extensive manual tuning of model parameters and specific modeling for different failure scenarios. This limits their generalization capabilities, especially when encountering unforeseen environmental conditions or diverse failure types. For instance, even within a single failure type like joint locking, different situations may necessitate separate models, hindering scalability and real-world applicability.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a promising alternative, capable of training control policies in simulated environments and subsequently transferring them to physical robots. This approach significantly reduces the need for explicit domain knowledge. Advanced simulators, such as Isaac Gym, offer high-fidelity physics and rendering, and techniques like domain randomization have been developed to mitigate the "sim-to-real gap"—the discrepancy between simulated and real-world performance. However, this gap remains substantial for hardware failures due to the difficulty of simulating every possible failure state and the simplification of physical robot models in simulation. Existing RL-based fault-tolerant controllers have primarily been validated in simulation, leaving their real-world transferability largely unexplored.

This research addresses these limitations by developing a methodology for training and validating hardware fault-tolerant controllers for quadruped robots that can be deployed directly in the physical world without fine-tuning. It builds upon previous RL-based locomotion work and sim-to-real transfer techniques, specifically adapting them to the challenge of joint locking failures. The paper positions itself by tackling the under-investigated problem of real-world hardware fault tolerance in quadrupeds, distinguishing itself from prior work that either relies on traditional control with limited generalization or RL solutions confined to simulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel methodology for training and testing hardware fault-tolerant controllers for quadruped locomotion, suitable for deployment in both simulated and physical environments. Specifically, the authors aim to enable quadrupeds to maintain stable locomotion when experiencing critical hardware failures, such as joint locking, and to achieve zero-shot transferability of these controllers from simulation to real robots.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations and existing limitations:

- **Real-World Deployment Challenges:** Modern quadruped robots are increasingly deployed in remote, uncontrolled, and dynamic environments for tasks like search and rescue, patrol, and delivery. In these contexts, critical hardware failures (e.g., joint locking, free swinging, broken brackets) are unpredictable and pose significant risks to robot functionality, human safety, and operational costs.
- **Inadequacy of Existing Solutions:**
 - **Commercial Robots:** Current commercial quadrupeds offer rudimentary failure detection (e.g., motor overheating) and protection (e.g., system shutdown), which are insufficient for continuous, adaptive operation in the face of hardware malfunctions.
 - **Traditional Control Methods:** Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Whole Body Control (WBC) require extensive manual tuning and precise modeling for each failure scenario, limiting their generalization and scalability across different failure types or robot configurations.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Limitations:** While RL shows promise, existing fault-tolerant RL solutions have largely been confined to simulated environments. The "sim-to-real gap" poses a significant hurdle, as it is challenging to accurately simulate all possible failure states and transfer policies trained on simplified models to complex physical systems without degradation. This gap is further exacerbated when considering hardware faults, which can be difficult to model realistically in simulation.
- **Need for Robustness and Generalization:** The inherently unstable nature of quadruped robots makes them particularly susceptible to failures. There is a clear need for controllers that can automatically adapt to and compensate for hardware failures at runtime, without requiring prior knowledge of the specific failure type or location, and without system disruption.
- **Zero-Shot Transferability:** The ability to train a controller entirely in simulation and deploy it directly on a physical robot without any further fine-tuning is highly desirable for practical applications, reducing deployment time and costs.

To address these motivations, the authors propose three key contributions:

1. **Realistic Joint Locking Simulation and Real-World Mechanism:** Designing a simulation strategy that accurately models joint locking failures with randomized parameters (failure time, joint, tolerance) to train a generalized agent. This is complemented by a physical locking mechanism for safe and realistic real-world testing.
2. **Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning Paradigm:** Adopting a teacher-student framework for single-phase training that facilitates efficient knowledge transfer and zero-shot deployment. The student model learns to infer necessary information from perceivable onboard sensor data, enabling real-time control in the physical world.
3. **Comprehensive Validation:** Conducting extensive experiments in both simulation and on a physical Unitree A1 robot to demonstrate the improved robustness and hardware fault tolerance of the developed RL-based control policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a methodology to train a control policy (π) that guides stable quadruped locomotion despite critical hardware failures, specifically joint locking. The policy is designed for zero-shot deployment in the real world using a teacher-student reinforcement learning framework.

**A. Joint Locking in Quadruped Locomotion**

- **Failure Type:** The study focuses on single joint locking failures, where a joint's motion is restricted within a limited range. This differs from free-swinging joints, which offer no support.
- **Joint Failure in Simulation:**
 - The Isaac Gym simulator is used to model joint locking.
 - In a `FailureEnv`, failure time (`T_f`), specific joint (`J_f`), and tolerance (`θ_tol`) are randomized for each virtual agent.
 - Joint locking is implemented by overwriting the selected joint's motion limits, restricting movement around a central failure angle (current joint position at failure).
 - This randomization aims to train a generalized policy, unlike previous methods with predefined, fixed failures.
- **Joint Failure in the Real World:**
 - **Hardlock:** A custom 3D-printed external locking mechanism (Figure 3) is used to physically restrict the motion of a joint (e.g., calf joint on Unitree A1). This provides a physically accurate representation of a locked joint but is limited by the robot's hardware design.
 - **Softlock:** A software-based approach where the desired joint position from the controller is clipped within a defined `θ_allowed` range before being sent to the onboard PD controller. This allows for testing any joint at any time but may differ slightly from a true physical failure.

**B. Reinforcement Learning Architecture**

- **Observation:** The policy receives low-level sensor data (joint encoders, IMU, foot encoders) and the previous action (`a_{t-1}`). A history of these observations (length `H`) is used to capture temporal information.
- **Action:** The policy outputs target joint positions (`hat{q}`), which are then processed by a PD controller to generate torques, where `K_p` and `K_d` gains are also subject to domain randomization.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function encourages stable and smooth forward locomotion at a target speed of 0.5 m/s, penalizing lateral/vertical movement, high acceleration, power consumption, and collisions. This aligns with existing benchmarks in RL-based locomotion.
- **Domain Randomization (beyond failure):** To enhance robustness, other parameters such as ground friction, PD controller settings, payload, and motor strength are randomized during simulation.

**C. Joint Teacher-Student Framework**

The core of the methodology is a teacher-student framework designed to achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer by implicitly identifying hidden environmental and robot dynamics.

- **Teacher Model (μ):** Takes privileged information (`e_t`) from the simulator (including domain randomization parameters `d_t`, clean robot states `s_t`, and height map `m_t`) and encodes it into a latent representation `z_t`.
- **Student Model (ϕ):** Learns to infer the latent representation (`hat{z}_t`) solely from historical perceivable observations (`o_{t-H:t}`), mimicking the teacher's encoding.
- **Policy Network (π):** The control policy takes the latent representation and current observation to output actions (`a_t`).
- **Joint Optimization Strategy:**
 - Instead of separate training stages (teacher first, then supervised student learning), the policy network is optimized jointly with the student network.
 - An adaptive ratio `α` is introduced to fuse the teacher's (`z_t`) and student's (`hat{z}_t`) latent representations into `z'_t = α*hat{z}_t + (1-α)*z_t`.
 - Initially, `α = 0`, meaning the policy relies entirely on the privileged `z_t` from the teacher.
 - As training progresses, `α` gradually increases, shifting reliance towards the student's `hat{z}_t`, eventually reaching `α = 1`. This allows the policy to adapt to potential differences between `z_t` and `hat{z}_t`.
 - The overall loss function `L = L_RL + β*L_adaption` combines the standard PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) loss (`L_RL`) with an adaptation loss (`L_adaption = ||z_t - hat{z}_t||^2`). The adaptation loss minimizes the difference between teacher and student latent representations.
 - The adaptive ratio `β` is negatively correlated with `α`, meaning the adaptation loss is weighted more heavily in the early stages when the teacher's influence is stronger, and less so as the student takes over. This strategy aims for faster convergence initially and a more robust student policy later, maximizing reward by adapting to student-inferred dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation was conducted through simulation and physical validation using a Unitree A1 robot, comparing fault-tolerant policies with baselines.

**A. Joint Locking Failure and Privilege Information Impact (Teacher Policies)**

- **Reward Drop with Failure:** Introducing joint locking failures in `FailureEnv` resulted in a reduction in average reward compared to training in a vanilla `BaseEnv`, which did not feature failures. This was expected, as locomotion with a locked joint inherently impacts speed and stability.
- **Effectiveness of Privilege Information:** When the `FailureEnv` teacher policy was provided with appropriate privilege information (including a failure flag `f_t`), it effectively learned to minimize the negative impact of joint failures, achieving higher rewards than a variant without the failure flag. This indicates the importance of explicit failure state information for robust policy learning.

**B. Teacher-Student Transfer**

- **Supervised Transfer Limitations:** Student policies trained using a traditional separate supervised transfer method (`[SS]`, inspired by RMA) showed a significant drop in reward, particularly for `FailureEnv`. This suggested that pure supervised transfer struggled to efficiently capture and generalize the unpredictable dynamics introduced by joint failures within a limited number of simulation steps.
- **Joint Training Effectiveness:** The proposed joint training (`[JT]`) approach demonstrated higher reward returns for student policies in both `BaseEnv` and `FailureEnv` environments compared to supervised transfer. This indicated that the adaptive fusion of teacher and student latent information, combined with joint optimization, facilitated more robust and efficient knowledge transfer, leading to better-performing student policies.

**C. Virtual Deployment**

- **Overall Performance (Table I):**
 - **Pre-Failure Velocity:** Both `BaseEnv` and `FailureEnv` agents achieved similar forward velocities (approximately 0.55 m/s) before joint failures occurred.
 - **Post-Failure Velocity:** After joint locking, the `FailureEnv` agent maintained a slightly higher forward velocity (0.47 m/s averaged across terrains) compared to the `BaseEnv` agent (0.42 m/s).
 - **Survival Time:** The `FailureEnv` agent exhibited significantly higher survival times after failure. On average, it survived 56.5% of the maximum 20-second post-failure period, compared to 44.7% for `BaseEnv`. The 50th percentile (P50) survival time for `FailureEnv` on smooth slopes was 100% (meaning half the instances survived the full 20s), while `BaseEnv` only achieved 36.5%. This indicates that the fault-tolerant agent was substantially more resilient to critical failures.
 - **Terrain Impact:** Performance for both agents, particularly survival time, significantly degraded on "discrete obstacle" terrains, likely due to the physical limitations of the Unitree A1 robot in navigating such challenging environments.
- **Failure Case Study (Fig. 5, 6):**
 - Analysis of worst-case failures revealed that the thigh joint was most vulnerable for the `FailureEnv` agent, while the calf joint was most vulnerable for the `BaseEnv` agent. This shift suggests that the `FailureEnv` agent successfully learned to compensate for calf joint failures.
 - Large torques were observed on locked joints when the desired position was outside the allowed range, identifying this as a key factor contributing to critical failures. The simulation strategy effectively replicated this torque behavior.

**D. Physical Validation**

- **Joint Motion Control (Fig. 8):** Both hardlock (using the 3D-printed mechanism on the calf joint) and softlock (software-based clipping) effectively limited joint motion on the physical Unitree A1 robot. Hardlock showed large torque patterns consistent with simulation, while softlock resulted in lower torques and occasional minor overshooting.
- **Survival Time (Table II):**
 - **Fault-Tolerant Agent (`FailureEnv`):** Achieved 100% survival across all tested joint locking scenarios (hip, thigh, calf, using both softlock and hardlock). This demonstrated robust real-world performance without fine-tuning.
 - **Baseline Agent (`BaseEnv`):** Struggled significantly, achieving only 20% survival with thigh hardlock and 5% with calf hardlock.
 - **Built-in Controller:** The Unitree A1's built-in controller consistently failed immediately (0% survival) when subjected to a hardlock.
 - **Multi-Joint Failures:** The `FailureEnv` policy also demonstrated the ability to move forward safely even when multiple joints were locked simultaneously, a scenario not explicitly trained for.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of resilient robotics, particularly for quadrupedal platforms operating in unpredictable environments.

- **Enhanced Robot Reliability and Safety:** The primary impact is enabling quadrupedal robots to maintain locomotion and stability in the face of critical hardware failures. This directly contributes to the reliability, operational duration, and safety of robots deployed in challenging real-world scenarios such as search and rescue, surveillance, and hazardous environment exploration, where immediate hardware replacement or repair is not feasible. By allowing a robot to "limp back home" or continue its mission despite a locked joint, the methodology reduces downtime, repair costs, and potential risks to human operators.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Failures:** The study effectively addresses a long-standing challenge in robotic control: the transferability of policies for hardware failures from simulation to the physical world. By proposing a novel simulation strategy for randomized joint locking and validating it with both hardlock and softlock mechanisms on a physical robot, the research offers a practical pathway to develop and test robust fault-tolerant behaviors.
- **Efficient Reinforcement Learning Paradigm:** The proposed joint teacher-student training framework represents a methodological contribution. It allows for single-phase optimization, effectively leveraging privileged information during early training stages while gradually transitioning to a student policy capable of real-world deployment. This efficiency in training and the demonstrated zero-shot transfer capability are valuable for developing complex robotic skills.
- **Generalization of Fault Tolerance:** The use of domain randomization for failure scenarios, rather than predefined fixed cases, trains a more generalized agent. The finding that the `FailureEnv` agent can adapt to new, untrained multi-joint failure scenarios further underscores the potential for developing highly adaptive and robust controllers.
- **Foundation for Future Robotics Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in fault-tolerant control. It opens avenues for investigating a wider range of failure modes (e.g., free-swinging joints, broken limbs), adapting the methodology to diverse quadrupedal platforms with varying morphologies and sensor suites, and integrating higher-level perception (e.g., depth cameras) for task-specific, fault-tolerant navigation in complex environments. This could lead to more autonomous and self-sufficient robotic systems capable of operating for extended periods in harsh conditions.
