# Outcome Rewards Do Not Guarantee Verifiable or Causally Important Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Research introduces Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR) and Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR) metrics to evaluate if large language models' generated reasoning chains are genuinely used and independently verifiable. The study finds that standard Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) often fails to improve these reasoning qualities, but methods like Supervised Fine-Tuning on expert traces or integrating auxiliary CIR/SR rewards can significantly enhance reasoning fidelity and verifiability while preserving task accuracy.

## Problem

- Current Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for LLMs assumes generated reasoning chains are faithful to the model's internal process and verifiable by external agents.
- It is unclear if optimizing for final answer correctness via RLVR inherently leads to genuinely causally important or sufficiently verifiable reasoning steps.
- Reasoning chains could be merely plausible-looking post-hoc rationalizations or incomplete, hindering transparency and auditability.

## Method

- Developed two novel metrics: ==Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR)== to measure if the final answer depends on the reasoning, and ==Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR)== to assess external verifiability.
- Experimentally evaluated standard outcome-based ==RLVR== on these metrics across diverse tasks using Qwen2.5 and Llama3.2 models.
- Explored two improvement methods: ==Supervised Fine-Tuning== on expert reasoning traces and ==auxiliary CIR/SR rewards== integrated into the RLVR objective.

## Results

- Standard RLVR often failed to improve CIR or SR, with **19 tasks** showing decreased CIR and **17** showing decreased SR on ReasoningGym.
- SFT with **64-512 expert reasoning examples** boosted CIR to approximately **0.4** and SR to **0.65-0.75** by the end of RL training.
- Training with auxiliary CIR/SR rewards improved both reasoning metrics while preserving task accuracy comparable to outcome-only RLVR.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standard outcome-based RLVR does not reliably improve the causal importance or verifiability of reasoning chains, and can even degrade these qualities for many tasks.
> - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on even a small number of expert reasoning traces can significantly bootstrap and improve both CIR and SR.
> - Integrating auxiliary rewards directly incentivizing CIR or SR into the RLVR objective can foster genuinely better reasoning while maintaining task accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research investigates the reliability of chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models (LLMs) that have undergone post-training using Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). RLVR has become a common component in LLM development, particularly for tasks where correctness can be automatically verified, such as mathematics and code generation. The prevailing assumption is that the explicit reasoning chains produced by these models accurately reflect the internal process by which they arrive at an answer, thereby offering transparency and interpretability.

Previous work has explored the concept of "faithfulness" in reasoning, examining whether an LLM's explanations accurately reflect its internal decision-making. Techniques like perturbing reasoning traces and observing output changes have been used to evaluate this. Concurrently, "verifiability" addresses whether a reasoning trace can be independently validated step-by-step by an external agent. Benchmarking efforts have highlighted that even plausible-looking chains can contain subtle errors, underscoring the need for robust verification. Some approaches have utilized neuro-symbolic methods to translate reasoning steps into formal statements for logical consistency checks or developed environments that provide verifiable rewards for training.

This paper builds upon these existing lines of inquiry by directly challenging the assumption that outcome-based RLVR inherently leads to faithful and verifiable reasoning. It introduces novel metrics (Causal Importance of Reasoning and Sufficiency of Reasoning) to quantify these properties in the context of RLVR. By doing so, it aims to uncover whether the widely adopted RLVR paradigm, which rewards only the final outcome, effectively cultivates robust reasoning processes or merely generates plausible-looking, but potentially unfaithful or unverifiable, explanations. The work distinguishes itself by proposing and evaluating methods to explicitly improve these reasoning qualities during RLVR using open-source models, moving beyond mere assessment to offer potential solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a critical examination of the fundamental assumption underlying the widespread use of Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) in training language models to generate chain-of-thought reasoning. The assumption is that the reasoning chains produced by models trained with outcome-based RLVR reliably represent the actual process through which the model derives its final answer, and that these chains are sufficiently clear for external validation. The authors identify two key properties central to this assumption:

1. **Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR)**: This property pertains to whether the reasoning chain is causally linked to the model's final answer. A causally important chain implies that the model's prediction is genuinely dependent on the sequence of reasoning steps it generates. If a reasoning chain is not causally important, it might function as a post-hoc rationalization or an incidental byproduct, rather than an integral part of the decision-making process.
2. **Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR)**: This property concerns whether the reasoning chain is self-contained and verifiable. A sufficient reasoning chain should provide enough explicit and reliable steps for an external verifier to arrive at the same unambiguous answer without needing additional context from the original question. If a chain lacks sufficiency, it could be vague, omit crucial steps, or fail to guide an external observer to the correct conclusion.

The authors argue that a reasoning chain could appear detailed and correct (verifiable) but not be what the model actually used (not causally important), or the model could genuinely rely on its reasoning (causally important) but express it in an incomplete or vague manner (not verifiable). In either scenario, the reasoning chain fails to provide reliable information for external validation or internal process understanding.

Against this background, the paper sets out to address three specific research questions (RQs):

- **R1: Does RLVR improve the causal importance and verifiability of the reasoning chains?** This question directly probes the efficacy of standard, outcome-based RLVR in fostering high-quality reasoning. The motivation here is to ascertain whether optimizing for final answer correctness inherently leads to models generating reasoning chains that are truly utilized and interpretable.
- **R2: How does supervised learning before RLVR affect reasoning causal importance and verifiability?** This question explores a common training paradigm, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) followed by RL, and its potential to mitigate any shortcomings of pure RLVR. The motivation is to determine if initial exposure to high-quality, expert-provided reasoning traces can bootstrap the model into a better "reasoning regime" before RLVR is applied.
- **R3: Can we jointly improve accuracy, causal importance, and verifiability during RLVR?** This question seeks to develop and evaluate novel modifications to the RLVR objective. The motivation is to overcome the limitations of outcome-based rewards by incorporating direct signals for CIR and SR into the training process, thereby fostering multi-objective optimization that simultaneously enhances performance and the quality of generated reasoning.

Ultimately, the core objective is to critically evaluate current LLM post-training practices and propose methods to ensure that LLMs not only provide correct answers but also derive them through genuinely transparent, reliable, and verifiable reasoning processes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach centered on two novel metrics and several experimental setups to evaluate and improve the quality of reasoning chains generated by LLMs.

#### 3.1. Metrics for Reasoning Quality

Two key metrics are introduced to quantify the quality of reasoning chains:

- **Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR)**: This metric assesses the extent to which a model's predicted answer distribution depends on its generated reasoning trace. For a given question ($q$), reasoning chain ($t = (t_1, \ldots, t_T)$), and answer ($y$), CIR is calculated by observing changes in the model's answer prediction when the reasoning chain is progressively truncated. Specifically, the probability of the final answer given the question and a truncated prefix of the reasoning ($p_k = p_{\phi}(y | q, t_{1:k})$) is compared to the probability given the full reasoning chain ($p_T = p_{\phi}(y | q, t_{1:T})$). The Jensen-Shannon divergence between Bernoulli distributions parameterized by $p_k$ and $p_T$ is computed for each truncation point $k$, and these divergences are averaged over all token indices. A high CIR indicates that the model's answer prediction significantly changes when reasoning is incomplete, suggesting the reasoning is causally important. A CIR of 0 implies the answer is solely dependent on the question, not the reasoning.
- **Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR)**: This metric measures how self-contained and verifiable a reasoning trace is. It determines if an external verifier can unambiguously arrive at the correct answer based *solely* on the reasoning chain, without access to the original question. The process involves:
 1. Sanitizing the reasoning chain ($t'$) by removing any explicit answer content to prevent trivial leakage.
 2. Instructing a strong verifier model (e.g., gpt-4o-mini) to predict an answer based on ($q, t'$) and then based solely on ($t'$).
 3. SR is defined as 1 if the verifier's greedily decoded answers under both conditions match, and 0 otherwise. A high SR suggests the reasoning is clear and complete enough for independent verification. A stricter variant, SR−, also removes paraphrases of the question from the reasoning chain.

#### 3.2. Datasets

The primary dataset for experiments is **ReasoningGym** (Stojanovski et al., 2025), which offers a diverse range of tasks beyond traditional math and code, each with a rule-based function for verifiable reward calculation. To manage computational resources and ensure meaningful learning, 40 tasks were selected based on intermediate base model accuracy (avoiding near-perfect or near-zero initial performance) and a preference for multi-class over binary tasks. Additional experiments were conducted on **Math-Hard** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) for broader evaluation.

#### 3.3. Models

The research utilized instruction-tuned LLMs from the **Qwen2.5 series** (1.5B, 3B, 7B) and **Llama3.2-3B** as base models. These models had not undergone prior RLVR training, allowing for observation of the direct impact of RLVR on reasoning quality. **gpt-4o-mini** was used as the verifier model for SR calculations, with robustness checked against gpt-4.1-mini.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setups

The study designed experiments to address each research question:

- **Standard RLVR Evaluation (for R1)**: Base instruction-tuned models were subjected to standard outcome-based RLVR training using GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). The training objective rewarded only the correctness of the final answer. Models were trained to generate a CoT within `<think>` tags before providing the answer within `<answer>` tags. Performance was compared to models trained to answer directly without reasoning chains.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) before RLVR (for R2)**: For tasks identified as having low CIR and SR after standard RLVR, expert reasoning traces were collected from an expert model (gpt-o3-mini). The base models were then supervised fine-tuned on these expert reasoning traces (only the `<think>` content, not the final answer) with varying numbers of examples (n = 2, 8, 64, 512). Subsequently, RLVR training continued from these SFT checkpoints. This aimed to evaluate if initial exposure to high-quality reasoning could bootstrap better performance.
- **Auxiliary Rewards during RLVR (for R3)**: The standard outcome-based RLVR reward was augmented with auxiliary reward signals derived directly from the CIR or SR metrics.
 - **SR as an auxiliary reward**: The binary SR score (0/1) for each rollout was added to the outcome-based reward: $r_{\text{train}} = r_{\text{out}} + \alpha \cdot \text{SR}$.
 - **CIR as an auxiliary reward**: A coarse estimate of CIR (averaged over 10th percentile truncations of the reasoning chain) was added to the outcome-based reward: $r_{\text{train}} = r_{\text{out}} + \beta \cdot \text{CIR}$.
 These experiments aimed to investigate whether directly incentivizing faithful and verifiable reasoning could improve these properties while maintaining task accuracy, without requiring expert human demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the impact of RLVR on the causal importance and verifiability of reasoning chains, and effective strategies to improve these qualities.

#### 4.1. RLVR Does Not Guarantee Causally Important or Verifiable Reasoning Chains (Addressing R1)

- **Limited Improvement in CIR and SR**: Across 40 ReasoningGym tasks using Qwen2.5-3B, RLVR did not reliably improve CIR or SR. 19 tasks showed a decrease in CIR, and 17 tasks showed a decrease in SR after training. A significant number of tasks (18 for CIR, more for SR) ended with very low values (e.g., CIR under 0.1), indicating that the models either did not causally use the generated reasoning for their final answer or produced reasoning too vague for external verification.
- **Correlation with Initial State**: Both final CIR and SR were positively correlated with their initial values (Spearman ρ = 0.40 for CIR, ρ = 0.62 for SR), suggesting that models starting with higher quality reasoning traces were more likely to maintain or improve them.
- **Qualitative Observations**: Examples demonstrated that on some tasks (e.g., "advanced geometry"), RLVR could produce concrete, causally-used, and verifiable computations. However, on other tasks (e.g., "manipulate matrix"), accuracy improved, but reasoning chains often collapsed into high-level plans or partial restatements, reducing both CIR and SR.
- **Performance Without Reasoning**: For tasks where final CIR and SR were low (< 0.15), training models to directly answer without generating reasoning chains yielded similar accuracy improvements (average final-accuracy difference of -0.053). This finding suggests that for these tasks, the model learned to solve them without actually relying on the explicitly generated chain-of-thought, implying the reasoning was largely incidental or post-hoc. In contrast, tasks with high CIR or SR showed a much larger performance gap (0.18) when reasoning was included.
- **Extreme Accuracy Improvements Only**: RLVR only reliably improved CIR and SR for a subset of tasks where accuracy gains were exceptionally large (above 50 percentage points). For tasks with more moderate accuracy improvements (0-50 points), CIR and SR often substantially declined. While accuracy improvement showed little correlation with CIR improvement (Spearman ρ = 0.17), it was strongly positively correlated with SR improvement (Spearman ρ = 0.57).

#### 4.2. CIR and SR Can Be Improved Through SFT on Reasoning Traces (Addressing R2)

- **Significant Gains from SFT**: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on a small set of expert reasoning traces (as few as 2-8 examples, significantly with 64-512 examples) dramatically improved both CIR and SR for tasks that initially exhibited low scores in these metrics (e.g., algorithmic and logical reasoning tasks).
- **CIR and SR Trajectories**: Without SFT, CIR and SR tended to drop quickly during subsequent RL training. With increasing amounts of SFT data (n=64 and n=512), CIR reached around 0.4 and SR around 0.65-0.75 by the end of RL training, a substantial increase from the low baseline.
- **Correlation with Performance**: These improvements in CIR and SR from SFT correlated with better downstream task performance. Larger SFT datasets led to models starting RL from a more robust reasoning state, which resulted in higher final accuracy (e.g., 0.5-0.63 for larger 'n' compared to ~0.3 for 'n=0').
- **Qualitative Improvement**: SFT + RLVR led to reasoning chains with concrete sequences of verifiable operations, including intermediate steps and calculations, which were both causally used and externally verifiable. Quality analysis confirmed that traces with higher CIR/SR scores generally contained more concrete steps, explicit calculations, and lexically rich wording.

#### 4.3. Impact on SR and CIR When Trained with Auxiliary Rewards (Addressing R3)

- **Joint Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards**: Augmenting the standard outcome-based RLVR reward with auxiliary signals derived from either SR or CIR successfully improved both metrics. Training with SR as an auxiliary reward led to improvements in both SR and CIR, and vice-versa.
- **Accuracy Preservation**: This multi-objective training approach maintained accuracy levels comparable to standard outcome-based RLVR, demonstrating that improvements in reasoning quality could be achieved without sacrificing task performance.
- **Robustness to Reward Hacking**: The use of a stricter SR− metric, which removes paraphrases of the question in addition to the answer, demonstrated that the auxiliary rewards genuinely enhanced verifiability rather than merely training the model to "reward-hack" by repeating the question.
- **Mechanism of Improvement**: Auxiliary rewards shaped how the model presented and used its reasoning, encouraging more task-specific intermediate steps and verifiable computations, rather than generic rationalizations. However, they did not provide new task information, meaning they did not reliably improve accuracy on tasks where the base model initially had near-zero performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the understanding and development of trustworthy language models.

Firstly, it rigorously challenges the assumption that Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), when relying solely on outcome-based rewards, consistently leads to the development of causally important and verifiable reasoning chains in large language models. The introduction of Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR) and Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR) metrics provides quantifiable tools to assess these critical aspects of model explanations. The finding that RLVR does not reliably improve these metrics, and can even degrade them for many tasks, indicates a limitation in current standard post-training recipes. This suggests that improvements in task accuracy do not inherently translate into more faithful or auditable reasoning processes.

Secondly, the study demonstrates that this identified shortcoming of outcome-only RLVR is not insurmountable. It presents two effective strategies to foster better reasoning quality:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with expert reasoning traces**: Even a small quantity of high-quality, expert-demonstrated reasoning can substantially improve both CIR and SR before and during subsequent RLVR, leading to more interpretable and verifiable reasoning.
- **Auxiliary rewards based on CIR and SR**: Integrating direct signals for causal importance and verifiability into the RLVR reward function can effectively guide models to produce higher quality reasoning, all while preserving the accuracy gains typically achieved by outcome-based RLVR. This approach is particularly significant as it does not necessitate additional expert human demonstrations.

The implications of these findings are substantial for the field of LLM development and deployment.
- **For model evaluation**: The proposed CIR and SR metrics offer valuable tools for a more nuanced evaluation of LLMs, moving beyond mere task accuracy to assess the underlying reasoning quality. This can help researchers and practitioners differentiate between models that merely get correct answers and those that do so through transparent and reliable processes.
- **For model training**: The research highlights the need for more sophisticated post-training objectives that explicitly target reasoning quality alongside task performance. It provides concrete methods—SFT with expert traces and auxiliary rewards—that can be integrated into existing RLVR pipelines to produce models with more trustworthy explanations.
- **For real-world applications**: In high-stakes domains such as scientific assistance, education, and decision support, the verifiability and causal importance of reasoning are paramount. Models generating explanations that are genuinely used and comprehensible to humans can enhance user trust, facilitate error detection, and enable learning from the model's process.

However, the authors also acknowledge potential risks. Persuasive but incorrect rationales could still lead to over-reliance, and detailed reasoning traces might inadvertently leak sensitive information from prompts or facilitate harmful problem-solving strategies. Therefore, the adoption of these methods should be paired with robust data handling, monitoring, and deployment safeguards to ensure responsible AI development.
