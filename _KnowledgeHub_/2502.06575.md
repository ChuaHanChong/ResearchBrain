# Predictive Red Teaming: Breaking Policies Without Breaking Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Google DeepMind and Princeton University developed RoboART, a framework for "predictive red teaming" that assesses visuomotor robot policy vulnerabilities against environmental changes using generative image editing and anomaly detection without requiring hardware evaluations. The approach accurately predicted performance degradation across 12 factors, enabling targeted data collection that improved policy success rates 2-7x in specific vulnerable conditions and 2-5x in other untargeted conditions.

## Problem

- Visuomotor policies exhibit brittleness and unpredictable vulnerabilities to environmental variations (e.g., lighting, distractors, object placement).
- Discovering these vulnerabilities through hardware evaluations is costly, time-consuming, and impractical for comprehensively testing diverse off-nominal scenarios.
- Scalable methods are needed to predict policy generalization capabilities in complex and varied environments prior to physical deployment.

## Method

- The **RoboART** (==Robotics Automated Red Teaming==) framework introduces a ==two-step pipeline: edit and predict==, specifically for visuomotor policies relying on RGB images.
- The "edit" step uses ==language-conditioned diffusion models== (e.g., Imagen 3) to synthetically modify nominal RGB observations, simulating various environmental changes, with a ==Vision-Language Model (VLM) critic== filtering for realism and relevance.
- The "predict" step utilizes a ==policy-specific anomaly detector== in the policy's latent embedding space to quantify how 'far' edited observations are from nominal, using anomaly rates to approximate expected performance degradation for each environmental factor.

## Results

- **RoboART** accurately identified policy vulnerabilities with high Spearman rank correlations (**0.8** for the hybrid policy, **0.7** for the vanilla diffusion policy) and low average prediction errors (**0.10** and **0.19**, respectively) compared to hardware evaluations.
- Targeted data collection, guided by **RoboART**'s predictions, improved the fine-tuned policy's performance by **2-7x** in the three off-nominal conditions where additional data was acquired.
- The fine-tuned policy also demonstrated cross-domain generalization, showing **2-5x** performance improvements in other background and lighting conditions for which no additional data had been collected.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy-specific anomaly detection, applied to synthetically altered observations within the policy's latent space, can effectively predict performance degradation due to hypothetical environmental changes before physical robot execution.
> - Generative image editing, when guided by precise language prompts and validated by VLM critics, provides a scalable method to create diverse and realistic off-nominal visual scenarios for proactive vulnerability assessment.
> - Identifying specific environmental conditions that cause performance degradation offline allows for targeted data collection, which can then be used to efficiently fine-tune and significantly improve policy robustness and generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research introduces "predictive red teaming" for robotics, addressing a gap in methods for assessing robotic policy vulnerabilities without extensive hardware testing. The work positions itself within several established research areas:

**Red Teaming:** The concept of red teaming originated in military strategy and has been adapted for evaluating artificial intelligence systems, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), to uncover biases, misuses, and harmful behaviors. While early efforts relied on human evaluators, recent work has aimed to automate parts of the red teaming process for LLMs. This paper extends the concept of automated red teaming to robotics. Existing robotic red teaming efforts have primarily focused on instruction generalization in simulation (e.g.,) or jailbreaking LLM-powered robots (e.g.,) by finding adversarial prompts. In contrast, this work focuses on identifying environmental factors that degrade visuomotor policy performance, such as lighting, background, or object locations, and doing so without real-world hardware evaluations in off-nominal conditions. Some related work uses simulation to assess policy generalization with respect to environmental factors (e.g.,), but the authors highlight the significant human effort often required to set up accurate simulators for RGB-based policies in new environments. The proposed RoboART pipeline is presented as a data-driven and automated alternative.

**Anomaly Detection and Failure Prediction:** This research leverages techniques from anomaly detection, which typically aim to identify conditions far from nominal that may lead to failures. Traditional failure prediction methods in robotics include reachability analysis, control barrier functions, formal methods, and learned predictors, often operating at runtime to detect potential failures and trigger remedial actions. Anomaly detection, sometimes incorporating conformal prediction for statistical assurances on false positive rates, also primarily functions at runtime. This paper differentiates itself by applying anomaly detection *predictively* to edited observations. Instead of detecting anomalies during robot operation, it uses anomaly detection on synthetically altered images to forecast performance under hypothetical environmental changes, prior to any physical deployment.

**Generative Image Editing:** The work incorporates recent advances in generative image editing, a field that has seen application in robotics for data augmentation, generating sub-goals for image-conditioned policies, and runtime observation editing for visual generalization. By utilizing language-conditioned diffusion models like Imagen 3, the authors generate realistic and targeted modifications to robot observations. This differs from prior uses by applying generative editing specifically to create hypothetical off-nominal scenarios for predictive vulnerability assessment, rather than for training data expansion or runtime adaptation.

**Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE):** The problem shares similarities with OPE in reinforcement learning, where the goal is to estimate a policy's performance using data collected by a different policy. Both aim to evaluate policy performance without direct robot execution. However, predictive red teaming, as defined here, focuses on predicting the performance of a *given* policy under *off-nominal conditions* by analyzing data collected under *nominal conditions*, rather than evaluating a target policy using data from a different behavior policy.

In summary, this paper introduces a novel application of automated red teaming to visuomotor robot policies, integrating generative image editing and anomaly detection to predict performance degradation due to environmental changes without physical hardware trials. It distinguishes itself from existing red teaming, anomaly detection, and image editing applications by its specific predictive, pre-deployment focus in the context of environmental robustness for robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and formalize the problem of "predictive red teaming" in robotics. This involves discovering vulnerabilities of a given robot policy with respect to changes in environmental factors (e.g., lighting, visual distractors, object placement) and predicting the corresponding performance degradation *without* performing hardware evaluations in these off-nominal scenarios.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges in deploying visuomotor policies:
1. **Brittleness and Unpredictable Vulnerabilities:** Visuomotor policies, often trained via imitation learning, are capable of complex manipulation tasks but frequently exhibit brittleness when faced with variations in lighting, visual distractors, and object locations. These vulnerabilities can arise unpredictably based on the specifics of policy training and architecture.
2. **Prohibitive Cost of Hardware Evaluations:** Discovering such vulnerabilities through hardware evaluations is time-consuming and expensive. This cost escalates when the goal is to comprehensively test the limits of safe deployment across a diverse range of off-nominal scenarios, making exhaustive physical testing impractical. For example, the paper illustrates that intuitively plausible predictions about policy generalization (e.g., to changed table height vs. a human distractor) can be incorrect, highlighting the need for systematic vulnerability assessment.
3. **Scalability for Complex Environments:** As robots are deployed in increasingly complex and varied environments, scalable methods are needed to predict their generalization capabilities when encountering unforeseen scenarios. Current hardware-centric approaches do not scale to this demand.

Addressing the problem of predictive red teaming is motivated by several important consequences and benefits:
1. **Targeted Deployment:** By understanding the range of conditions under which a policy will perform satisfactorily, deployment decisions can be informed, ensuring the robot operates within its known envelope of competence.
2. **Policy Comparison:** Knowledge of the relative vulnerabilities of different policies allows for informed selection of the most suitable policy for specific deployment needs, based on robustness to anticipated environmental variations.
3. **Targeted Data Collection for Policy Improvement:** Identifying specific environmental conditions that cause significant performance degradation enables focused data collection efforts under those adverse conditions. This targeted data can then be used to re-train or fine-tune the policy, thereby patching identified vulnerabilities and improving overall robustness and generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, named RoboART (Robotics Automated Red Teaming), consists of a two-step pipeline: **edit** and **predict**. The approach is specifically designed for visuomotor policies that primarily rely on RGB image observations.

#### 3.1. Generative Image Editing

The first step involves synthetically modifying nominal observations to reflect changes in selected environmental factors.
- **Selection of Environmental Factors:** The red team first defines a set of environmental factors (𝐹) that are hypothesized to potentially degrade the policy's performance. These factors can be fine-grained (e.g., specific lighting conditions, types of distractors, background colors) and are chosen based on deployment needs and plausible real-world variations. In experiments, 12 factors were chosen, including three lighting changes (red, green, blue), three background color changes (red, green, blue), four distractor objects (black/white trash can, laptop, candle), a human distractor, and a change in table height.
- **Generating Edited Observations:** State-of-the-art generative image editing tools are used to modify a set of nominal RGB observations (𝑆_val) to simulate the chosen changes. The authors utilize the Imagen 3 diffusion model, which performs language-prompted image editing (e.g., inpainting, outpainting, colorization). Detailed language instructions (e.g., "add a person close to the table," "change the background to red") are provided to generate realistic and globally consistent edits. For robots with multiple cameras (e.g., overhead and wrist), each observation is edited independently with the same prompt, aiming for per-view consistency in lighting, shadows, and composition. For the table height change, a color change is combined with a zoom. For experiments, 100 edited observations are generated for each factor from a held-out portion of training episodes.
- **VLM Critic for Filtering Edits:** To ensure the generated edited observations accurately reflect the desired environmental change and maintain quality, a Vision-Language Model (VLM) is employed as a critic. For each input image and prompt, a batch of four edited images is generated. The VLM (Gemini Pro 1.5 in this work) is then prompted with the original image, the four edited options, and a shortened version of the editing instruction. It is asked to judge if any option accurately reflects the desired change and to select the best one. If no satisfactory edit is found, the observation is discarded.

#### 3.2. Predicting Performance via Anomaly Detection

The second step uses the set of edited observations (𝑆_f) to predict the performance degradation for each factor.
- **Core Hypothesis:** The central idea is that a policy-specific anomaly detector can predict performance degradation. Specifically, the expected reward (𝑅_𝜋^𝑓) under factor 𝑓 is approximated by (1 − 𝛼_𝜋^𝑓), where 𝛼_𝜋^𝑓 is the anomaly rate for factor 𝑓. The anomaly rate is the proportion of edited observations flagged as anomalous.
- **Anomaly Detection Mechanism:**
 - **Policy Embedding Space:** The method quantifies how "far" an edited observation is from nominal observations from the policy's perspective. This is achieved by computing a latent representation (𝜙_𝜋(𝑜)) from the policy for a given observation 𝑜. For diffusion policies, the context vector provided to the denoising process is used as the latent representation.
 - **Anomaly Score:** A policy-specific anomaly score (𝑠_𝜋(𝑜, 𝑆_nom)) is defined as the mean of the 𝑘-nearest neighbor cosine distances between the embedding 𝜙_𝜋(𝑜) of the edited observation and the embeddings of a set of nominal observations (𝑆_nom, a subset of first-time-step observations from training episodes). This score intuitively measures dissimilarity to known nominal conditions.
 - **Anomaly Rate (𝛼_𝜋^𝑓):** For each factor 𝑓, the anomaly score is computed for all observations in 𝑆_f. The anomaly rate is the proportion of these observations whose anomaly score exceeds a predetermined threshold (𝜏).
- **Anomaly Threshold (𝜏):** The threshold 𝜏 is critical for relating anomaly rates to success rates. It is chosen such that the anomaly rate for nominal observations (𝛼_𝜋^nom) predicts the policy's nominal success rate (𝑅_𝜋^nom), i.e., 𝑅_𝜋^nom ≈ 1 − 𝛼_𝜋^nom. A validation set (𝑆_val) of nominal observations is used, and 𝜏 is set as the empirical quantile of the anomaly scores computed for 𝑆_val. Conformal prediction is used to set this quantile, providing statistical assurances that the probability of unseen nominal observations being flagged as anomalous is upper-bounded by 1 − 𝑅_𝜋^nom.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Hardware:** A bimanual manipulator with two Kuka IIWA robotic arms (left arm used), a Weiss gripper, and a dual-camera setup (fixed overhead and wrist-mounted) was used.
- **Training Data:** Over 3,000 demonstrations for grasping tasks were collected automatically in nominal conditions (fixed lighting, pink table background, varied objects like blocks, plush toys, cans, artificial fruits). Keypoints for target objects were automatically annotated. The task was chosen for its relative ease of learning, making red teaming more challenging by focusing on generalization limits.
- **Policies:** Two visuomotor diffusion policies with different architectures were evaluated:
 - **𝜋_hyb (Hybrid Policy):** Inspired by, combines trajectory optimization for free-space planning with reactive visuomotor diffusion. It uses two separate heads: one for a single SE(3) waypoint and one for a dense sequence of joint angles, plus a mode selection scalar. All heads are jointly trained via a diffusion objective. The latent embedding for anomaly detection is a ℝ^512 vector.
 - **𝜋_dfn (Vanilla Diffusion Policy):** A standard diffusion policy with a single head outputting a trajectory sequence. The latent embedding is a ℝ^(515x513) vector.
 Both policies achieved ~65% success rate in nominal conditions over 30 trials.
- **Evaluation:** For each of the twelve environmental factors, both policies were executed in 20+ hardware episodes to estimate their true success rates. This resulted in over 500 hardware trials for evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings across three main areas: predictive red teaming accuracy, effectiveness for targeted data collection, and the efficacy of anomaly detection as a proxy for performance degradation.

#### 4.1. Accuracy of Vulnerability Identification and Performance Prediction

RoboART's ability to identify vulnerabilities and predict policy performance degradation was evaluated using two metrics:
1. **Spearman Rank Correlation (𝜌):** Measures how accurately RoboART ranks factors by performance degradation.
2. **Average Prediction Error:** Calculates the mean absolute difference between predicted and real success rates.

- **Policy 𝜋_hyb (Hybrid Policy):**
 - Spearman 𝜌: 0.8
 - Average Prediction Error: 0.10
 RoboART effectively predicted that 𝜋_hyb was robust to object or person distractors, moderately impacted by background and lighting changes, and significantly impacted by table height changes.
- **Policy 𝜋_dfn (Vanilla Diffusion Policy):**
 - Spearman 𝜌: 0.7
 - Average Prediction Error: 0.19
 RoboART identified 𝜋_dfn as more vulnerable than 𝜋_hyb to factors like blue lighting and table height changes. The prediction errors were within the range of noise from estimating success rates via 20 trials.

**Ablation Studies (Appendix D):**
Ablations on the anomaly score function parameters (𝑘-nearest neighbors and size of nominal reference set 𝑆_nom) showed that 𝜋_hyb predictions remained accurate with varying 𝑆_nom and small 𝑘. For 𝜋_dfn (higher dimensional embedding space), predictions benefited from either a smaller 𝑆_nom or larger 𝑘.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness for Targeted Data Collection

The utility of predictive red teaming for policy improvement was demonstrated through targeted data collection.
- The three environmental factors predicted by RoboART to cause the highest performance degradation for 𝜋_hyb (blue lighting, changed table height, blue table background) were selected for additional data collection (approx. 100 trajectories per condition).
- The original 𝜋_hyb policy was fine-tuned with this new off-nominal data, using a mixture of 80% original and 20% new data in mini-batches, with a reduced learning rate.
- **Performance Improvement:** The fine-tuned policy (𝜋_ft_hyb) exhibited improved performance in nominal conditions. Critically, in the three off-nominal conditions where data was collected, performance increased by 2–7x.
- **Cross-Domain Generalization:** The targeted data collection also led to performance improvements of 2–5x in other background and lighting conditions for which no additional data was collected, indicating broader generalization benefits.

#### 4.3. Anomaly Detection as a Proxy for Performance Degradation (without image editing)

To evaluate the anomaly detection component in isolation, the detector was run on *real* robot observations (𝑆_real^𝑓) from the 20+ hardware episodes for each factor, rather than edited observations.
- **Policy 𝜋_hyb:**
 - Spearman 𝜌: 0.6
 - Average Prediction Error: 0.20
- **Policy 𝜋_dfn:**
 - Spearman 𝜌: 0.8
 - Average Prediction Error: 0.21
Predictions using real observations still showed correlation with actual performance. However, these predictions were based on approximately 5x fewer observations (around 20 real vs. 100 edited), which made them more susceptible to noise. For 𝜋_dfn, the anomaly threshold had to be adjusted with a slightly higher nominal success rate estimate to avoid overly conservative flagging due to the high-dimensional embedding space and smaller validation set.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces predictive red teaming and the RoboART framework as a method for evaluating robot policy robustness against environmental variations without requiring extensive real-world hardware testing.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Formalization of Predictive Red Teaming:** The paper defines a novel problem statement for proactively identifying policy vulnerabilities with respect to environmental factors and predicting performance degradation offline. This addresses a significant practical challenge in robot deployment.
- **RoboART Framework:** The proposed pipeline combines generative image editing with policy embedding-based anomaly detection. This integration allows for the synthetic generation of diverse off-nominal scenarios and an objective, policy-specific assessment of their impact on performance. The use of a VLM critic enhances the realism and relevance of the generated edits.
- **Empirical Validation on Hardware:** The methodology is validated through over 500 hardware trials across twelve distinct environmental factors and two different visuomotor diffusion policies. The results demonstrate that RoboART can accurately identify impactful environmental factors, predict both the relative ranking and absolute success rates of policies under these conditions, with an average prediction error below 0.19. This empirical evidence supports the efficacy of the approach for real-world robotic systems.
- **Enabling Targeted Policy Improvement:** A significant impact lies in RoboART's ability to guide targeted data collection. By identifying the most adverse conditions, the framework facilitates the focused acquisition of new training data. This targeted fine-tuning not only boosts performance in the identified vulnerable conditions (2–7x improvement) but also demonstrates cross-domain generalization, improving performance in other untargeted conditions (2–5x improvement). This capability offers a scalable and efficient path to enhance robot policy robustness.

**Potential Impact:**
The ability to perform predictive red teaming has several practical implications for robot development and deployment:
- **Enhanced Safety and Reliability:** By proactively identifying and mitigating vulnerabilities, robots can be deployed with a better understanding of their operational limits, contributing to safer and more reliable autonomous systems.
- **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** Shifting vulnerability discovery from costly and time-consuming hardware trials to an automated, offline process can significantly accelerate the development cycle and reduce resource expenditure.
- **Informed Deployment Decisions:** Predictions about policy performance under various environmental conditions can guide decisions regarding where and when a robot policy is suitable for deployment, matching policy capabilities with environmental demands.
- **Systematic Policy Comparison:** Developers can use predictive red teaming to quantitatively compare the robustness of different policies to a range of environmental factors, leading to better-informed choices for specific applications.
- **Data-Efficient Robustness Engineering:** The targeted data collection mechanism provides a data-efficient strategy for improving policy robustness, focusing resources on scenarios that yield the greatest benefit.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by authors):**
- **Edit-to-Real Gap:** While image editing tools are advanced, limitations remain in realism for certain edits (e.g., shadows for lighting changes) and ensuring multi-view consistency across different cameras. Future work could explore 3D scene editing tools for more geometrically consistent and depth-aware edits.
- **Anomaly-to-Failure Gap:** The correlation between anomaly rates and performance degradation is not perfect. Future research could investigate using anomaly detection on sequences of observations over time, or exploring alternative anomaly detection techniques that better predict performance degradation.
- **Hidden Environmental Factors:** RoboART relies on visual changes. It cannot identify vulnerabilities from changes that are not visually reflected (e.g., changes in object mass without visual alteration). Simulation-based predictive red teaming might address such cases, but would need to overcome the sim-to-real gap.
- **Multi-Round Predictive Red Teaming:** The current approach uses a fixed set of environmental factors. An iterative approach could allow for dynamic expansion of factors, exploring areas similar to those predicted to cause poor performance, enhancing the comprehensiveness of the vulnerability search.

Overall, this work lays a foundation for systematically assessing and improving the robustness of robotic systems, moving towards more reliable and safely deployable autonomous agents in complex real-world settings.
