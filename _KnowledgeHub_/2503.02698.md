# FlowPlan: Zero-Shot Task Planning with LLM Flow Engineering for Robotic Instruction Following

> [!summary] Summary
> FlowPlan introduces a zero-shot framework for robotic instruction following, utilizing a multi-stage large language model (LLM) workflow and context-aligned target localization. This approach significantly improved task success rates on the ALFRED benchmark compared to prior zero-shot methods and achieved 100% planning precision in real-world robotic experiments.

## Problem

- Existing training-based methods for robotic instruction following require vast, expensive datasets, limiting generalization to new tasks or environments.
- Previous zero-shot solutions, often single-stage LLM planners, struggle to parse complex instructions, adhere to operational constraints, or use contextual cues for precise object localization.
- Data-driven in-context learning approaches still rely on manually curated example sets, which hinders scalability for open-ended scenarios.

## Method

- A multi-stage LLM workflow decomposes complex instructions into four cascaded phases: ==Task Information Retrieval==, Language-Level Reasoning, Symbolic-Level Planning, and Logical Evaluation.
- Context-Aligned Target Localization precisely locates objects by integrating category-level co-location probabilities with instance-level contextual guidance from the LLM.
- The framework operates in a zero-shot manner using GPT-4, requiring no labeled data or example sets for new tasks or environments.

## Results

- On the ALFRED benchmark, FlowPlan achieved approximately double the Task Success Rate and Goal-Conditioned Success of previous zero-shot methods.
- Performance was competitive with data-driven in-context learning approaches on ALFRED, with a small gap to top-performing methods.
- In real-world robotic experiments, FlowPlan demonstrated **100%** task planning precision over 50 diverse trials, outperforming a single-stage LLM baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing complex robotic task planning into modular, interpretable LLM-driven stages (Flow Engineering) significantly enhances planning robustness and performance.
> - Explicit logical evaluation and an intermediate language-level reasoning stage are critical for self-correction, resolving ambiguities, and ensuring the coherence of robot action plans.
> - Robust zero-shot robotic instruction following, competitive with data-driven approaches, is achievable by structuring LLM interactions effectively.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of robotics, natural language processing (NLP), and artificial intelligence, specifically addressing the challenging problem of **Robotic Instruction Following (also known as Embodied Instruction Following, EIF)**. EIF requires autonomous systems to understand natural language instructions, plan a sequence of actions, perceive and locate objects in the environment, and execute physical manipulations to achieve a goal.

The current research landscape for EIF can be broadly categorized into three approaches:
1. **Training-based methods:** These approaches (e.g.,–) rely heavily on vast, human-annotated instruction-ground truth datasets for training deep learning models. While capable of impressive performance within their trained domains, they suffer from limited generalization to novel tasks or environments and demand significant resource-intensive data collection and annotation efforts.
2. **Data-driven in-context learning methods:** More recent advancements (e.g.,–) leverage large language models (LLMs) and in-context learning, reducing the need for extensive training data. However, they still depend on manually curated labeled example sets for similarity-based retrieval, which, while less demanding than full training datasets, still requires considerable human effort and limits scalability to truly open-ended scenarios.
3. **Zero-shot solutions:** These methods (e.g.,) aim to entirely eliminate the reliance on labeled data for new tasks or environments. While promising in their data efficiency, prior zero-shot approaches, often employing single-stage LLM planners, have struggled significantly. Their limitations include difficulty in parsing lengthy or ambiguous instructions into logically coherent actions under operational constraints, and an inability to effectively leverage contextual cues from the language for precise instance-level target localization. This often results in sub-optimal outcomes.

**FlowPlan** directly addresses the limitations of existing zero-shot solutions. It aims to elevate the performance of zero-shot task planning to bridge the gap with data-driven in-context learning methods, without requiring any labeled data or example sets. The key innovation lies in adopting a **multi-stage LLM workflow**, a concept increasingly recognized as "Flow Engineering" in the broader LLM research community (e.g.,–). This "flow engineering" approach, inspired by its success in complex reasoning tasks like code generation and arithmetic, posits that decomposing a complex problem into a series of interpretable, modular LLM-driven stages can significantly enhance overall performance and robustness compared to a single-shot prompt.

By integrating multi-stage planning with context-aligned target localization, FlowPlan seeks to provide a more robust, scalable, and generalizable solution for robotic instruction following, moving towards truly autonomous agents that can interpret and execute commands in unfamiliar and dynamic real-world settings with minimal human intervention. This work represents a significant step forward in making LLM-driven robotics more practical and deployable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of FlowPlan is to develop a robust and effective framework for **zero-shot task planning in robotic instruction following**, aiming to overcome the limitations of current approaches and significantly advance the field.

The primary motivations driving this research can be summarized as follows:

- **Addressing Data Dependency and Generalization Issues:** Existing task planning methods for robotic instruction following often fall into two categories:
 - **Training-based methods** necessitate vast, task-specific datasets, which are expensive and time-consuming to collect and annotate. This data dependency severely hinders the generalization of robotic systems to novel tasks, objects, or environments.
 - **Data-driven in-context learning approaches** reduce this dependency but still require manually curated example sets. While less burdensome than full datasets, they still demand human effort for specific contexts and limit scalability.
 The motivation for FlowPlan is to eliminate this data dependency entirely, enabling truly zero-shot deployment and enhancing generalization capabilities.

- **Overcoming Limitations of Single-Stage Zero-Shot Planners:** Previous zero-shot solutions, typically employing single-stage LLM planners, have demonstrated sub-optimal performance. They struggle with:
 - **Parsing lengthy and ambiguous instructions:** Long and complex natural language commands are difficult for a single LLM prompt to ground into logically coherent action sequences.
 - **Adhering to operational constraints:** Robots often have specific physical limitations and environmental rules (e.g., single-arm manipulation, object interactions). Integrating these constraints effectively within a single-shot planning process is challenging.
 - **Leveraging contextual cues for precise target localization:** Instructions often contain valuable spatial or relational information (e.g., "the rag on the tub"). Single-stage planners often fail to extract and utilize this contextual guidance for accurate instance-level object localization, which is crucial for navigation and manipulation.
 FlowPlan is motivated to address these specific technical shortcomings by breaking down the complex planning process.

- **Bridging the Performance Gap:** The researchers aim to demonstrate that a well-designed zero-shot pipeline can achieve performance competitive with, or even superior to, data-driven in-context learning methods. This would significantly validate the feasibility and potential of data-free approaches for real-world robotic applications.

- **Enhancing Interpretability and Robustness:** By decomposing the planning process into modular, interpretable stages (task information retrieval, language-level reasoning, symbolic-level planning, and logical evaluation), FlowPlan seeks to generate logically coherent action sequences that are easier to understand, debug, and verify. This modularity also inherently enhances robustness by allowing for targeted corrections and re-planning within specific stages.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to move towards more autonomous, adaptable, and deployable robotic systems that can robustly interpret and execute human instructions in diverse, previously unseen environments without requiring extensive pre-training or manual data curation for each new scenario.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FlowPlan proposes a structured multi-stage Large Language Model (LLM) workflow to enhance zero-shot task planning for robotic instruction following. The framework is composed of two main components: Multi-Stage Task Planning and Context-Aligned Target Localization.

#### 3.1. A. Multi-Stage Task Planning

Given a natural language instruction `I`, the task planner `f_TP` generates a sequence of executable action steps ` {T_n} `, where each step `T_n = (A_n, O_n, C_n)` includes a primitive action (`A_n`), a target object label (`O_n`), and contextual guidance (`C_n`) for instance grounding. This planning pipeline is implemented through four cascaded LLM-driven stages:

1. **Task Information Retrieval:**
 - **Purpose:** To classify the input instruction into a predefined task type (e.g., Pick & Place, Clean & Place, Heat & Place). Understanding the task type helps the LLM better comprehend the overall objective and apply relevant constraints.
 - **Mechanism:** An LLM acts as a classifier, trained (in-context, zero-shot) to identify one of seven defined task types and whether slicing is involved.
 - **Output:** `T_I`, which is concise task information including a description of the task's essence and pertinent operational rules/constraints (e.g., single-arm limitations, action chain integrity). This `T_I` is then incorporated into subsequent stages via templated prompts. Accuracy is validated through a voting mechanism or multiple LLM calls.

2. **Language-Level Reasoning:**
 - **Purpose:** To transform the instruction `I` into a clear, language-based task plan `T_L` *before* symbolic planning. This intermediate step addresses logical and grammatical ambiguities, implicit state transitions, and operational constraints inherent in natural language instructions, which single-stage planners often struggle with.
 - **Mechanism:** An independent LLM-driven component is prompted with its role, a list of executable primitive actions (e.g., pick up, put, open), general operational rules, and the `T_I` retrieved from the previous stage.
 - **Output:** `T_L`, a series of natural language steps that explicitly detail the sub-actions and their targets, making the logical flow coherent (e.g., "Open the drawer → Pick up the apple → Close the drawer"). This step acts as a powerful pre-processor for the symbolic planning.

3. **Symbolic-Level Planning:**
 - **Purpose:** To convert the language-based task plan `T_L` into an executable symbolic action sequence ` {T_n} `. This stage maps natural language steps to robot-executable functions.
 - **Mechanism:** An LLM is prompted with its role as a "robotic symbolic task planner," a list of available symbolic actions (e.g., `pick_up`, `open`), the required response format, and an example. It takes `T_L` as input.
 - **Output:** For each step `T_n`, it generates the symbolic action (`A_n`), the target object label (`O_n`), and crucial contextual guidance (`C_n`) extracted from `T_L` (e.g., "the cabinet beneath the coffee machine") to aid in subsequent target localization.

4. **Logical Evaluation:**
 - **Purpose:** To ensure the generated symbolic task plan adheres to logical coherence and operational constraints, correcting any errors or inconsistencies.
 - **Mechanism:** This stage comprises two evaluation types:
 - **Code-based Evaluation:** Uses predefined, easily adaptable rules (Table I provides examples) to verify fixed action sequences (e.g., `open` must be followed by `close`, `pick up` then `put`). This acts as a hard constraint checker.
 - **LLM-based Evaluation:** Addresses the correction of invalid items within the plan. For instance, if an action or object label is invalid in a close-set manipulation scenario, the LLM attempts to replace it with the most similar valid item (lexical matching).
 - **Output:** If the plan fails evaluation after several correction attempts, a re-planning phase is initiated by re-conducting the task planning process from an earlier stage. This focuses on fixing planning errors rather than scene-aware re-planning.

#### 3.2. B. Context-Aligned Target Localization

This component is responsible for precisely locating target objects in the scene to facilitate navigation actions. Given the current subtask `T_n` (with `O_n` and `C_n`) and an online-constructed semantic map `S` (composed of `M`: semantic top-down view tensor, and `L`: observed instance coordinates with categorical labels), the process `f_GP: (T_n, S) → G` maps to a navigation goal `G`.

1. **Object Co-location:**
 - **Purpose:** To estimate the likely location of unobserved targets based on their categorical relationships with observed landmarks (e.g., "knife" is likely near "kitchen counter").
 - **Mechanism:** An LLM is used to predict object co-location probabilities between the target object `O_n` and observed landmarks `Y ∈ L`. These predictions are aggregated to create a category-level probability map `D_o`.

2. **Context Alignment:**
 - **Purpose:** To refine target localization by utilizing specific contextual guidance `C_n` (e.g., "the cabinet beneath the coffee machine") extracted during symbolic planning.
 - **Mechanism:** A zero-shot LLM-driven component, aware of `T_n` and the observed instance list `L`, identifies the optimal instance from `L` by aligning the spatial relationships described in `C_n`. It outputs `(x, y)` coordinates, which are then transformed into a Gaussian distribution `D_c`.

3. **Integration and Navigation:**
 - The two distributions, `D_o` (category-level) and `D_c` (instance-level), are integrated via multiplication and normalization to form a unified probability distribution.
 - A final two-dimensional navigation goal `G` is sampled from this distribution.
 - A deterministic navigation approach (e.g., as in,) is then employed for motion planning and execution, followed by the execution of the target actions.

**Base LLM and Zero-Shot Nature:** FlowPlan uses GPT-4 (GPT-4-0125-preview) as its base LLM. Crucially, while a single example is provided in prompts to guide the LLM's response format, the method operates entirely in a zero-shot manner, requiring no labeled data for training or retrieval. This highlights its significant advantage in terms of data efficiency and adaptability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FlowPlan demonstrates significant improvements in zero-shot task planning for robotic instruction following, showcasing its competitive performance against data-driven methods and strong generalizability.

#### 4.1. A. Performance on the ALFRED Benchmark

Experiments on the ALFRED benchmark (Table II) reveal the following key findings:

- **Superior Zero-Shot Performance:** FlowPlan achieved remarkably higher success rates compared to existing zero-shot approaches. For instance, on the "Test Seen" set, FlowPlan achieved a Task Success Rate (SR) of 40.31% and a Goal-Conditioned Success (GC) of 49.35%. On the "Test Unseen" set, it scored 35.64% SR and 46.93% GC. This performance is approximately **double** that of the best previous zero-shot competitor, Socratic-Planner, which recorded 12.26% SR and 20.86% GC on "Test Seen," and 9.94% SR and 18.88% GC on "Test Unseen." This substantial improvement validates the efficacy of the multi-stage LLM workflow.
- **Competitive with Data-Driven In-Context Learning:** Despite being a zero-shot method, FlowPlan shows performance competitive with, and in some cases surpasses, data-driven in-context learning methods (e.g., LLM-Planner, HELPER-XP, OPEx, FLARE), which rely on labeled example sets. The performance gap to the top-performing in-context learning method was relatively small (e.g., 3.72% SR on "Test Seen"). This finding is crucial as it demonstrates that zero-shot capabilities can nearly match approaches that still require data curation.

#### 4.2. B. Ablation Study

An ablation study (Table III) on the validation set provided insights into the contribution of each component of FlowPlan:

- **Logical Evaluation is Critical:** Omitting the logical evaluation stage led to the most significant performance decline, over 50% drop in SR in both seen and unseen sets. This unequivocally highlights the importance of the systematic refinement and correction of task plans based on operational constraints and valid action sets.
- **Language-Level Reasoning is Substantial:** Removing the language-level reasoning stage resulted in approximately a 30% decrease in SR. This confirms that explicitly processing instructions at the natural language level before symbolic planning is vital for enhancing clarity, resolving ambiguities, and generating more accurate outcomes, reducing the burden on a single LLM call.
- **Task Information is Beneficial:** Knowledge of task-specific information, rules, and constraints (retrieved in the first stage) proved beneficial for LLMs in generating more effective task plans, though its impact was less dramatic than logical evaluation or language-level reasoning.
- **Contextual Guidance has Incremental Impact on ALFRED:** The impact of context alignment on overall performance was relatively incremental. This is attributed to the fact that only a small portion of instructions in the ALFRED benchmark provides rich spatial relationship guidance that can be effectively utilized for target localization improvement.

#### 4.3. C. Qualitative Analysis and Discussion

Qualitative analysis (Fig. 4) illustrated FlowPlan's ability to generate logically coherent action sequences that adhere to operational constraints, unlike previous methods which often failed to complete correct action chains.

Observed failure causes in ALFRED primarily include:
- **Perception failures:** Issues with semantic segmentation and depth prediction remain a common bottleneck, a problem shared across many embodied AI systems.
- **Ambiguous instructions:** The LLM-driven planner occasionally struggles with highly ambiguous natural language expressions (e.g., "green cup" being misidentified as a "trash bin").
- **Context alignment issues:** While overall impact was small, occasional failures in instance-level alignment occurred due to inaccurate instruction translation or inherent deficiencies in detailed spatial reasoning.

#### 4.4. D. Real-World Robotic Instruction Following Experiments

FlowPlan's versatility was validated in real-world applications using a Franka Emika FR3 platform (Table IV, Fig. 5).

- **Ease of Deployment:** Unlike data-driven approaches that require model retraining or example set reconstruction for new scenarios, FlowPlan only requires a few natural language descriptions of fundamental operational constraints for new setups. This dramatically reduces human effort.
- **High Real-World Planning Precision:** In 50 trials involving diverse, conversational instructions with object synonyms, FlowPlan achieved 100% task planning precision. In contrast, a "Vanilla LLM" (single-stage prompt) achieved only 76% precision. This demonstrates the robustness and practicality of the multi-stage approach in a tangible robotic system.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FlowPlan represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic instruction following, particularly for its contribution to zero-shot learning. Its potential impact extends across several key dimensions:

- **Revolutionizing Zero-Shot Task Planning:** The most profound significance of FlowPlan is its ability to bridge the performance gap between zero-shot and data-driven in-context learning methods. By achieving competitive results without relying on any labeled data or example sets, FlowPlan elevates zero-shot task planning from a challenging theoretical ideal to a viable and high-performing practical approach. This fundamentally alters the landscape by demonstrating that robust robotic instruction following can be achieved without the enormous costs and time associated with data collection and annotation.

- **Enhanced Generalizability and Scalability:** The data-free nature of FlowPlan means that robotic systems can be rapidly deployed in novel environments or tasked with new objectives without extensive retraining or manual curation. This dramatically increases the generalizability of robots to unfamiliar situations and significantly improves the scalability of deploying intelligent robotic assistants across diverse applications, from household chores to industrial settings. This is a critical step towards truly autonomous and adaptable robots that can function "off-the-shelf."

- **Improved Interpretability and Robustness through Modularity:** The decomposition of the complex task planning problem into four distinct and interpretable stages—task information retrieval, language-level reasoning, symbolic-level planning, and logical evaluation—is a key innovation. This modular design not only enhances the logical coherence of the generated action sequences but also makes the planning process more transparent and easier to debug. For safety-critical robotic applications, understanding *why* a robot plans a certain way, and being able to identify and correct errors in specific stages, is paramount. The logical evaluation stage, in particular, acts as a powerful self-correction mechanism, significantly boosting the overall robustness of the system.

- **Advancing LLM Application in Robotics:** FlowPlan demonstrates the power of "LLM Flow Engineering" (multi-stage prompting) as a robust paradigm for tackling complex, multi-faceted problems in robotics. This success could inspire similar structured LLM workflows for other challenging robotic tasks beyond instruction following, such as complex assembly, diagnostics, or human-robot collaboration, where nuanced reasoning and adherence to constraints are crucial.

- **Reducing Deployment Barriers:** For businesses and researchers, the ability to deploy complex robotic instruction following systems with minimal data preparation translates into significant reductions in development time, costs, and human effort. This lowers the barrier to entry for developing and integrating advanced robotic capabilities into new products and services, accelerating innovation in the robotics industry.

- **Future Research Directions:** FlowPlan also explicitly highlights areas for future work, such as integrating advanced vision-language models (VLMs) for even more enhanced contextual alignment and leveraging open-vocabulary detection for truly off-the-shelf deployment. This forward-looking perspective demonstrates the foundation FlowPlan lays for a new generation of intelligent, data-efficient, and highly adaptable robotic systems.

In summary, FlowPlan marks a significant milestone in making LLM-driven robots more practical, scalable, and deployable by demonstrating the power of structured zero-shot task planning.
