# Learning Video-Conditioned Policies for Unseen Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> ViP (Video-conditioned Policy learning) maps in-the-wild human demonstration videos to robot manipulation skills. A video encoder pretrained for action recognition on Something-Something-v2, fine-tuned with supervised contrastive learning, embeds both robot and human videos; a behavior-cloning policy is conditioned on robot trajectory embeddings, and at test time nearest-neighbor regression translates a human embedding into an in-distribution robot one. It reaches 71.3% average success zero-shot, versus 36.8% for DVD.

## Problem

- Reward design or goal images require expert operators, and fixing a task set during training blocks generalization to new manipulation tasks specified by non-experts.
- Language- and video-conditioned generalist policies need robot demonstrations annotated per skill, a labeling burden repeated for every new robot environment.
- Prior human-video imitation assumes demonstrations recorded in the robot's own lab scene, so it breaks under the large domain gap of natural, diverse human environments.

## Method

- ==Supervised contrastive learning== trains only a projection head atop a frozen ==SSv2== action-recognition backbone, so cosine distance between video embeddings becomes a task-similarity metric without any robot labels.
- A ==behavior-cloning== policy regresses actions from the current state plus the video embedding of the entire robot trajectory it belongs to, using unlabelled offline robot data.
- **ViP**'s inference-time translation module stores all training-trajectory embeddings in a library and averages the top-k most similar to the human embedding, keeping the conditioning in-distribution.

## Results

- Without any paired human-robot data, ViP averages **71.3%** success across four TableTop environments versus **36.8%** for DVD and **22.8%** for random selection.
- Using DVD's own similarity for a fair comparison, ViP still beats DVD's planner: **73.3%** vs **55.2%** with seen robot demos, **56.8%** vs **44.9%** with unseen ones, and runs an episode in under 1 second versus 16.
- Feeding human embeddings straight into the policy collapses to **20.3%**, below random; naive cosine distance in representation space reaches only **44.8%**, and k=1 or overly large k both degrade performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Encoders trained purely for human action recognition transfer as task embeddings for robot control, so internet-scale labelled human video can substitute for paired robot annotation.
> - Out-of-distribution conditioning silently breaks a conditional policy; retrieving a nearby in-distribution embedding is a cheap, general fix that also regularizes against adversarial matches.
> - Similarity metrics grouping all open-something actions ignore object identity, so fine-grained multi-object scenes like the Kitchen remain a failure mode of this recipe.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robot agents capable of performing a wide range of skills across diverse environments represents a significant area of research in robotics and artificial intelligence. A central challenge within this field is the ability to enable non-expert users to specify tasks and goals to robot systems in an intuitive and accessible manner.

Traditional approaches to defining robot tasks often rely on explicitly designed reward functions in reinforcement learning or on precise goal configurations in goal-conditioned learning. While effective for specific tasks, these methods typically require expert input, are difficult to generalize to new or unseen tasks, and hinder scalability.

More recent research has explored leveraging natural human interfaces, such as language instructions and human video demonstrations, to command robots. These approaches offer a user-friendly means of task specification and can potentially generalize to novel behaviors beyond simple goal-reaching. However, many existing methods still depend on large datasets of annotated demonstrations, which can be resource-intensive to collect, particularly when pairing human instructions with robot actions across various robot environments. Some works, like Domain-agnostic Video Discriminator (DVD), have attempted to use "in-the-wild" human videos, but still typically require some form of paired robot demonstrations for training video similarity functions.

This paper addresses the gap by focusing on a more challenging and realistic scenario: enabling robots to learn manipulation skills from human demonstrations recorded in natural, diverse human environments ("in-the-wild"), without requiring robot trajectories paired with human instructions during training. It seeks to reduce the supervision burden in data-driven robotics by leveraging readily available large-scale human video datasets for task specification and similarity learning, thereby pushing towards zero-shot generalization to unseen robot manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method, termed Video-conditioned Policy learning (ViP), that enables robot manipulation for previously unseen tasks, specified by human video demonstrations recorded in natural human environments. This is intended to empower non-expert users to command robots easily.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several key challenges and limitations in current robot learning paradigms:

- **User-Friendly Task Specification:** Existing methods for defining robot tasks (e.g., reward functions, specific goal observations) often require expert knowledge, making it difficult for non-technical users to interact with robots effectively. Human video demonstrations provide an intuitive way to convey desired tasks.
- **Generalization to Unseen Tasks:** Policies learned for a fixed set of tasks struggle to generalize to novel tasks. The authors aim for a system that can execute tasks it has not encountered during training, driven by new human video instructions.
- **Bridging the Human-Robot Domain Gap:** Human demonstrations are typically performed in diverse, natural environments, presenting a significant visual and kinematic domain gap compared to structured robot workspaces. A key motivation is to effectively bridge this gap without explicit, task-specific paired data.
- **Reducing Data Annotation Burden:** Many video-conditioned robot learning approaches rely on annotated demonstrations, requiring tedious collection of robot data paired with human instructions or goal states. The authors aim to minimize this supervision, specifically by removing the need for paired human-robot demonstration data during training. They propose to leverage large, readily available datasets of human action videos (e.g., Something-Something-v2) to derive task understanding, thereby making the approach more scalable.
- **Efficiency of Policy Execution:** The authors also implicitly aim for an efficient inference process compared to prior planning-based methods that involve generating synthetic videos, which can be computationally intensive.

In summary, the core motivation is to achieve zero-shot generalization of robot manipulation skills from "in-the-wild" human video demonstrations, with minimal supervision, by learning a policy conditioned on video embeddings that can bridge the human-robot domain gap without requiring paired training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Video-conditioned Policy learning (ViP), operates in two main phases: training and inference. The approach centers on learning a manipulation policy conditioned on video embeddings, which serve as a contextual representation of the target task.

**A. Method Overview**

ViP processes a human video of an unseen task and controls a robot arm to perform a similar task. It operates under the assumption of access to a dataset of unlabelled robot demonstrations $D_r$, consisting of robot videos $x_r$ and corresponding state-action sequences $(s_t, a_t)$. Critically, no reward functions or paired human-robot data are assumed to be available. The approach uses a video encoder $f_\theta$ pre-trained for human action recognition on the Something-Something-v2 (SSv2) dataset to map both human and robot videos into a common embedding space.

**B. Video-conditioned Policy Learning**

During training, a behavior cloning policy $\pi_\phi$ is learned to predict actions $a$ given the current robot state $s$ and a video embedding $e_r = f_\theta(x_r)$ of the full robot trajectory $x_r$. This policy aims to regress the action by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of observed actions:
$L_\pi(\phi) = -E_{s,a,x_r \sim D_r} \log \pi_\phi(a|s, f_\theta(x_r))$.
The video embedding acts as a task-defining context for the multi-task policy. All robot video embeddings from the training dataset $D_r$ are stored in an "embeddings library" $D_e$.

**C. Inference with Human Instructions**

At inference time, when a human video instruction $x_h$ is provided, the system first translates this human video into an appropriate robot video embedding $e_r$. This translation is critical because directly conditioning the policy on human video embeddings ($e_h = f_\theta(x_h)$) results in poor performance due to the domain gap and the fact that the policy was trained only on robot embeddings.

The translation process involves:
1. Encoding the human video instruction $x_h$ into a human embedding $e_h = f_\theta(x_h)$.
2. Performing k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) regression in the robot embedding space. Distances $d_i = d(e_h, e_{r_i})$ are computed between the human embedding $e_h$ and each robot embedding $e_{r_i}$ in the pre-computed library $D_e$.
3. Averaging the top $k$ robot embeddings from the library, based on highest cosine similarity, to obtain a "selected robot embedding" $e_r = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{k \tilde{i} \in 1} e_{r_{\tilde{i}}}$.
4. Finally, this selected robot embedding $e_r$ is used to condition the learned policy $\pi_\phi$, generating actions $a \sim \pi(.|s, e_r)$ at each step of the robot rollout.

**D. Learning a Task Similarity from Human Videos**

The similarity function $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ between videos is crucial for the k-NN regression step. The authors adapt Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon) for video action recognition using the SSv2 dataset.

The video encoder $f_\theta(\cdot)$ is composed of a backbone $f_b(\cdot)$ (which maps videos to representation vectors) and a projection network $f_p(\cdot)$ (which maps representation vectors to embedding vectors $e$).
Instead of training the entire encoder from scratch, the authors start with pre-trained backbones for SSv2 classification and then train only the projection network $f_p$ using the SupCon loss:
$L_{SupCon}(\theta) = \sum_{i \in I} \frac{-1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} \log \frac{\exp(\langle f_\theta(\tilde{x}_h^i), f_\theta(\tilde{x}_h^p) \rangle / \tau)}{\sum_{a \in A(i)} \exp(\langle f_\theta(\tilde{x}_h^i), f_\theta(\tilde{x}_h^a) \rangle / \tau)}$
where $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ denotes cosine similarity, $I$ is the batch index, $A(i)$ contains all indices except $i$, $P(i)$ contains indices of videos with the same label as $\tilde{x}_h^i$, and $\tau$ is a temperature hyperparameter.
This loss encourages embeddings of videos from the same class to be closer in cosine distance than those from different classes. After training, the cosine distance between the resulting embeddings $f_\theta(x_h)$ and $f_\theta(x_r)$ is used as the similarity metric $d(x_h, x_r)$. A key aspect is that this similarity metric is learned *only from human videos* (SSv2) and is then applied to compare human and robot videos, demonstrating its generalization capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on two challenging multi-task robot manipulation environments: TableTop (with four variations) and Kitchen.

**A. TableTop Environment (Comparison to DVD)**

The TableTop environment involves tasks such as "close the drawer," "push the cup," and "turn the faucet to the right." Robot experience for policy training was collected by controlling the robot end effector to go through randomly sampled keypoints.

- **Settings:**
 - **Seen robot demos:** DVD's video similarity was trained on a subset of SSv2 classes and *labelled robot demonstrations* for the target tasks.
 - **Unseen robot demos:** DVD's video similarity was trained on SSv2 and *labelled robot demonstrations for non-target tasks*.
- **Comparison to DVD:**
 - **ViP (using DVD similarity)** consistently outperformed DVD in both "seen robot demos" and "unseen robot demos" settings. For "seen robot demos," ViP achieved an average success rate of 73.3% (±11.6) compared to DVD's 55.2% (±2.9). For "unseen robot demos," ViP achieved 56.8% (±10.0) compared to DVD's 44.9% (±2.0).
 - The authors attribute ViP's superior performance to comparing human video prompts to full robot trajectories rather than shorter sub-trajectories, and not relying on synthetic video generation for control.
 - ViP demonstrated significantly faster inference times (less than 1 second per episode) compared to DVD (over 16 seconds).

**B. ViP without Paired Data (Zero-Shot Setting)**

This is a critical evaluation where the video similarity function is trained *only on human videos* (SSv2), without any robot data for similarity learning.

- **Performance:** ViP (Ours) achieved an average success rate of 71.3% (±13.5) across the TableTop environments.
- **Comparison:** In this zero-shot setting, ViP (Ours) significantly outperformed DVD, which achieved only 36.8% (±2.2) and performed slightly better than chance (random: 22.8%). This indicates ViP's ability to operate effectively without the burden of collecting paired human-robot data.
- **Ablations:**
 - **"Human video as input":** Directly using human video embeddings to condition the policy resulted in poor performance (20.3% ±9.6), confirming the necessity of the translation procedure (k-NN regression) to map human videos to the robot policy's input distribution.
 - **"ViP (Cosine distance in repr. space)":** Using a naive cosine distance on SSv2 action classification network representations with ViP achieved 44.8% (±13.1), outperforming DVD but underperforming ViP with the SupCon-trained similarity or DVD's similarity, highlighting the importance of the specifically trained distance metric.
- **Sensitivity to k-Nearest Neighbors (k):** The method was robust to varying values of $k$ (percentage of library size). Performance dropped for very high $k$ (diluting specificity) and for $k=1$ (potentially selecting an adversarial embedding), suggesting that averaging over a small number of similar embeddings provides a regularizing effect. Optimal performance was observed at 1% of the library size for TableTop.

**C. Kitchen Environment**

The Kitchen environment features tasks such as "open the microwave," "open the left door," and "open the sliding door." Robot demonstrations for these tasks were available but unannotated.

- **Comparison to R3M and Oracles:**
 - ViP (Oracle) (where the correct robot demonstration is chosen) achieved high success rates (e.g., 83.2% for "left door" (L view), 99.2% for "sliding door" (L view)), demonstrating the policy's ability to learn from multi-task data.
 - ViP (Oracle) was competitive with or superior to single-task R3M policies (R3M: 63.8% for "left door" (L view), 100% for "sliding door" (L view)), indicating the efficacy of the multi-task policy learning.
- **Zero-Shot Performance (ViP Ours):**
 - ViP (Ours) achieved 69.4% (±11.0) for "open left door" (L view) and 25.1% (±8.5) for "open microwave" (L view).
 - The method struggled with "opening sliding door" (41.7% ±27.6) and with all tasks from the right camera view, particularly "open microwave" (6.6% ±2.9).
- **Limitations Identified:** The results in the Kitchen environment suggest a limitation of the current similarity metric based on SSv2. The "opening something" label in SSv2 might group different opening actions, making it difficult for the robot to distinguish between opening a door, a microwave, or a sliding door when only a generic "opening" human video is provided. This implies the similarity metric focuses more on the general action rather than the specific object identity, leading to potential object confusion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents ViP as a method for learning video-conditioned policies for robot manipulation, demonstrating several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** The ability of ViP to map human video instructions to robot manipulation skills in a zero-shot manner, without relying on paired robot-human demonstration data during training, is a key advancement. This significantly reduces the data collection and annotation burden, which is a major bottleneck in scaling up robot learning.
- **Reduced Supervision:** By leveraging large datasets of human action videos (like SSv2) for learning task similarity, the approach enables less supervision in data-driven robotics. This shifts the reliance from meticulously curated robot datasets to more easily available human video data.
- **Leveraging Pre-trained Video Encoders:** The work demonstrates that video encoders pre-trained for human action recognition can provide relevant task embeddings for robot control, even across a significant domain gap between human and robot environments. This suggests that insights and models from large-scale human video datasets can be effectively transferred to robotics.
- **Efficient Inference:** Compared to prior planning-based methods that involve synthetic video generation (e.g., DVD), ViP operates in a pre-computed embedding space, leading to significantly faster inference times. This increased efficiency could facilitate real-world deployment.
- **Foundation for Generalist Robot Agents:** By enabling non-expert users to command robots through intuitive video demonstrations, ViP contributes to the development of more accessible and versatile generalist robot agents. This could expand the range of tasks robots can perform and the contexts in which they can operate.
- **Identification of Future Research Directions:** The limitations observed in the Kitchen environment, specifically the challenge of distinguishing between similar "opening" tasks for different objects, highlight areas for future work. This suggests a need for similarity metrics that can incorporate more fine-grained object and context information beyond generic action classification, potentially through combining language instructions or richer video representations.

Overall, ViP represents a step towards creating more autonomous and user-friendly robot systems by reducing the reliance on expert supervision and specialized robot data, making robot learning more scalable and broadly applicable.
