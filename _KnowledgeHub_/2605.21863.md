# OCELOT: Odometry and Contact Estimation for Legged Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> OCELOT, a proprioceptive-only odometry method for quadruped robots, integrates learned dynamics-based pseudo-measurements into an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (IEKF) using calibrated set-coverage statements rather than a Gaussian noise assumption. This method maintains estimator consistency under non-Gaussian errors and achieves competitive accuracy with perception-based systems in challenging real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Achieving accurate odometry for legged robots using only proprioceptive sensors is challenging due to complex, intermittent foot-ground contact dynamics and unpredictable slippage.
- Existing Zero Velocity Update (ZUPT) methods often assume perfect foot stationarity, which can lead to significant trajectory errors when contact is misidentified or slippage occurs.
- Current contact detection techniques lack robust adaptability to diverse terrains or fail to provide calibrated uncertainty estimates necessary for intelligent integration into filter-based state estimators.

## Method

- **OCELOT** employs an ==Error-State Extended Kalman Filter (ESEKF)== framework that fuses proprioceptive sensor data, incorporating a novel contact detection and adaptive uncertainty quantification module.
- A fused contact detection module combines a ==GMM-guided Finite State Machine (GMM-FSM)== for physical contact assessment with a ==Kinematic-Based Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT)== for kinematic stationarity.
- The system translates a continuous, fused contact quality score into a dynamically adaptive measurement covariance for the ESEKF, allowing the filter to intelligently weigh ==ZUPT== corrections based on their reliability.

## Results

- OCELOT demonstrated substantially lower errors across diverse terrains (concrete, tile, grass, pebble, rock), achieving up to a **7x** reduction in Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) compared to established proprioceptive baselines.
- The method consistently outperformed state-of-the-art monocular Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems on challenging outdoor terrains (grass, pebble, rock), where VIO often suffered from feature tracking failures.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the fused GMM-FSM and GLRT approach for contact detection provided superior and more consistent robustness, particularly in slippage-prone environments, compared to using individual detectors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robust contact detection for legged robots requires fusing complementary information, such as physical force and kinematic stationarity, to effectively identify and reject slippage events.
> - Dynamically quantifying measurement uncertainty based on a continuous contact quality score allows an ESEKF to adaptively trust or down-weight ZUPT corrections, significantly improving filter consistency and accuracy.
> - Adaptive thresholds for force-based contact detection, derived from Gaussian Mixture Models, enable the system to adjust to varying terrain characteristics without manual tuning, enhancing robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate odometry is a fundamental requirement for autonomous mobile robots, enabling self-localization, mapping, and navigation. For legged robots, achieving this accuracy using only onboard proprioceptive sensors presents a significant challenge due to their complex, intermittent contact dynamics with the ground. Unlike wheeled robots that can rely on continuous wheel encoder data, legged robots must infer their motion by exploiting kinematic and dynamic models, particularly during periods when their feet are in stable contact with the ground.

The dominant framework in legged robot odometry involves an Inertial Navigation System (INS) driven by an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) for state propagation. This propagation is periodically corrected through Zero Velocity Updates (ZUPTs), which assume that a foot in stance is stationary relative to the world frame. The effectiveness of ZUPTs critically depends on accurately identifying when a foot is truly stationary and in contact, making contact state estimation a core sub-problem in this field.

Existing contact estimation methods vary widely. Some approaches directly leverage Ground Reaction Force (GRF) data, using fixed thresholds, Finite State Machines (FSMs), or clustering techniques to identify contact. Other methods infer contact without direct force measurements, relying on robot dynamics or kinematics, often using simple thresholding on estimated GRF or analyzing foot velocity. A third category augments robots with additional sensors, such as leg-mounted IMUs, to provide more localized inertial information for contact detection. More recently, learning-based approaches, employing neural networks, have been introduced to estimate contact probability and body velocity, though they can present challenges related to uncalibrated uncertainty.

This paper addresses the limitations of existing proprioceptive odometry methods by focusing on the robust detection and quantification of contact quality, particularly in the presence of slippage. It aims to improve odometry accuracy by dynamically adjusting the confidence of ZUPTs based on a fused assessment of both physical contact and kinematic stationarity. Furthermore, the paper contributes to the availability of relevant benchmark data by introducing a new multi-modal dataset specifically designed for evaluating legged odometry in diverse and challenging terrains, which is an area where existing datasets have been noted to have limitations regarding sensor modalities and terrain diversity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an accurate and robust leg odometry pipeline for quadruped robots using only onboard proprioceptive sensors. Specifically, the authors aim to achieve reliable odometry estimates that can effectively handle slippage-prone environments, which pose significant challenges for conventional contact detection and state estimation methods.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges in legged robotics:

1. **Dependency on Accurate Contact Estimation:** Legged odometry frameworks, particularly those based on Error-State Kalman Filters (ESEKFs) or Invariant EKFs (InEKFs) with Zero Velocity Updates (ZUPTs), rely heavily on the assumption that a foot in stance is perfectly stationary. If this assumption is violated, for example by slippage, the ZUPT correction can introduce significant errors, leading to trajectory drift. Therefore, accurately knowing *when* a leg is stationary and *how reliably* that stationarity can be asserted is critical.
2. **Limitations of Existing Contact Detection Methods:** Current methods often employ fixed thresholds for force or velocity, which may not adapt well to varying terrain conditions. Force-based methods can confirm physical contact but are inherently blind to slippage. Kinematic-based methods can detect lack of motion but might be susceptible to sensor noise or inaccuracies in forward kinematics. Furthermore, some robust solutions require additional, often foot-mounted, sensors, increasing hardware complexity and cost. Learning-based methods, while promising, can produce uncalibrated uncertainties, which are problematic for filter-based state estimators.
3. **Need for Robustness in Diverse Terrains:** Legged robots are designed for unstructured and uneven terrains where wheeled robots struggle. These environments (e.g., grass, pebbles, rocks) often involve deformable or granular surfaces that induce slippage. A robust odometry system must therefore maintain accuracy even when confronted with these real-world complexities.
4. **Quantifying Measurement Uncertainty:** The reliability of a ZUPT correction within a Kalman filter framework is governed by the associated measurement covariance. If this covariance is static or improperly scaled, the filter might either over-correct based on unreliable measurements (e.g., during a slip) or under-utilize reliable information. Dynamically quantifying the uncertainty of each contact event is therefore crucial for intelligent filtering.

To address these motivations, the core contribution of the paper is a novel fused contact detection and uncertainty quantification module. This module is designed to:
- Explicitly identify and reject slippage by combining two parallel detectors.
- Provide a continuous contact quality score that reflects both physical loading and kinematic stationarity.
- Translate this quality score into a dynamic, adaptive measurement covariance for the ESEKF, allowing the filter to intelligently weight corrections based on their reliability.

Additionally, the authors motivate the release of their implementation and a new, comprehensive multi-modal dataset to facilitate reproducibility and further research, addressing a gap in publicly available data for challenging quadrupedal locomotion scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, named OCELOT, is built upon an Error-State Extended Kalman Filter (ESEKF) framework, which fuses proprioceptive sensor data. The core innovation lies in its contact detection and adaptive uncertainty quantification module that drives the ESEKF's correction step.

**A. Preliminaries: Error-State Kalman Filter Design**

1. **Kinematics:** The system utilizes forward kinematics to map joint angles ($q_i$) and velocities ($\dot{q}_i$) of a leg $i$ to its position ($p_B^i$) and linear velocity ($v_B^i$) in the robot's body frame $\{B\}$.
2. **State Definition:** The ESEKF operates with a non-linear nominal state **x** and a linear error state $\delta \mathbf{x}$.
 - **Nominal State x:** Comprises the robot's position ($p_W \in \mathbb{R}^3$), velocity ($v_W \in \mathbb{R}^3$) in the world frame $\{W\}$, rotation matrix ($R \in SO(3)$) from $\{B\}$ to $\{W\}$, and additive accelerometer ($b_a \in \mathbb{R}^3$) and gyroscope ($b_g \in \mathbb{R}^3$) biases in $\{B\}$.
 - **Error State $\delta \mathbf{x}$:** A 15-dimensional vector of small, zero-mean Gaussian perturbations to the nominal state.
3. **Prediction Step (Propagation):** The nominal state is propagated by integrating bias-corrected IMU measurements (accelerations $a_B$ and angular velocities $\omega_B$) using Euler integration. The error covariance $P$ is propagated using linearized error dynamics, defined by state transition matrix $F_d$ and process noise $Q_d$, which are derived from continuous error dynamics $F_c$ and noise input $G$.
4. **Correction Step (Zero-Velocity Update - ZUPT):** This step applies corrections when a foot is detected to be in a stationary stance, assuming its world-frame velocity ($v_{W_{foot,i}}$) is zero.
 - **Measurement Model:** The measurement $h(\mathbf{x}_k)$ estimates the stance foot's world-frame velocity by combining the body velocity $v_W$ with the relative foot velocity $v_{B_{rel,i}}$ (derived from $\omega_B$, foot position $p_B^i$, and joint kinematics).
 - **Measurement Jacobian:** The Jacobian $H_k = \partial h / \partial \delta \mathbf{x}$ linearizes the measurement model for the error state.
 - **Innovation and Gating:** To reject outliers (e.g., slips), the innovation $\nu_k = -h(\mathbf{x}_k)$ is subjected to a $\chi^2$ gating test using the innovation covariance $S_k = H_k P_k H_k^T + R_k$. Measurements that exceed a predefined threshold ($\gamma_{95}$) are rejected.
 - **State and Covariance Update:** Accepted measurements lead to the computation of the Kalman gain $K_k$, correction of the error state $\delta \mathbf{x}^+$, update of the covariance $P_k^+$ (using the Joseph form for numerical stability), and injection of errors back into the nominal state.

**B. Contact Detection**

The core of the OCELOT method is its fused contact detection module, designed to robustly estimate stance quality by combining two complementary detectors. This fusion aims to validate a measurement update only when a foot is both physically loaded and kinematically stationary, effectively rejecting slippage events.

1. **Detector 1: GMM-Guided FSM (GMM-FSM):** This detector provides a debounced estimate of physical contact using a Finite State Machine (FSM) for each foot.
 - **Adaptive Thresholds:** Instead of fixed thresholds, the FSM's touchdown ($F_{on}$) and liftoff ($F_{off}$) thresholds are dynamically determined. This is achieved by fitting a two-component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) to a sliding window of recent Ground Reaction Force (GRF) magnitudes ($F_{mag}$). The two components represent the low-force SWING distribution and high-force STANCE distribution.
 - **Threshold Derivation:** $F_{max}$ is the mean of the stance distribution. $F_{off}$ is set at $\mu_s + 3\sigma_s$ (mean + 3 standard deviations of the swing distribution), and $F_{on}$ incorporates a hysteresis margin relative to $F_{off}$ and $F_{max}$. This continuous adaptation to recent data allows the thresholds to adjust to varying surface characteristics without manual tuning.
 - **Continuous Quality Score ($q_{FSM}$):** Alongside the binary contact state, a continuous score is computed by linearly normalizing the measured force $F_{mag}$ between $F_{on}$ and $F_{max}$, bounded between 0 and 1.

2. **Detector 2: Kinematic-Based Detection (GLRT):** This detector evaluates foot stationarity, without requiring additional foot-mounted IMUs, by exclusively using the main body IMU and kinematics. It is inspired by Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT) methods.
 - **Hypotheses:** For a sliding window of $N_w$ velocity measurements with an isotropic covariance $\Sigma_0 = \sigma_v^2 I_{3 \times 3}$:
 - Null Hypothesis ($H_0$ - Stance): Foot is stationary, $v_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_0)$.
 - Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$ - Swing): Foot is moving, $v_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma_0)$ where $\mu \neq 0$.
 - **Test Statistic:** The GLRT test statistic $T_{GLRT} = \hat{\mu}^T \Sigma_0^{-1} \hat{\mu}$ (normalized by $N_w$) evaluates the log-likelihood ratio. A discrete STANCE state is declared if $T_{GLRT}$ falls below a scaled $\chi^2$ threshold $\gamma_{on}$.
 - **Continuous Quality Score ($q_{GLRT}$):** A continuous score is derived, linearly decreasing from 1.0 (perfectly zero test statistic) as the velocity variance approaches $\gamma_{on}$, bounded between 0 and 1.

3. **Fused Score and Adaptive Covariance:**
 - **Fusion:** The two continuous quality scores are combined multiplicatively to form a final score: $q_{final} = q_{FSM} \times q_{GLRT}$. This "soft AND-gate" mechanism ensures a high score only when the foot is both physically loaded and kinematically stationary, thereby providing robust slip rejection.
 - **Adaptive Covariance:** The $q_{final}$ score is then used to dynamically set the $3 \times 3$ measurement noise covariance matrix $R_i$ for each foot. A baseline noise $\sigma_{base}$ is defined for an ideal stance, and the adaptive standard deviation $\sigma_i = \sigma_{base} / \max(q_{final,i}, \epsilon)$ is computed. This constructs the final isotropic covariance matrix $R_i = \sigma_i^2 \cdot I$. A high $q_{final}$ yields a low covariance (high confidence), while a low $q_{final}$ (indicating slip or low quality) yields a high covariance, effectively causing the ESEKF to down-weight or ignore that measurement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the OCELOT method was evaluated through extensive experiments on a custom-collected dataset and benchmarked against both proprioceptive and exteroceptive odometry approaches.

**A. Experimental Setup:**
- **Robot:** Unitree Go2 quadruped robot.
- **Sensors:** Onboard proprioceptive sensors only: IMU, joint encoders, and foot force sensors, sampled at 500 Hz. External depth camera data (RGB and point cloud) was collected for validation but not used for estimation.
- **Dataset:** 29 sequences, totaling 2.4 km, covering diverse indoor (concrete, tile) and outdoor (grass, pebble, rock) terrains. Ground truth trajectories were established using surveyed waypoints and verified with synchronized video.
- **Metrics:** Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE), Absolute Heading Error (AHE), Relative Position Error (RPE Trans), Relative Rotation Error (RPE Rot), Final Position Error (FPE), Total Drift, Trajectory Length Error, and Fréchet distance. All metrics were calculated as Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).

**B. Proprioceptive Comparison (Table I):**
The OCELOT method was compared against two established proprioceptive leg odometry pipelines: Bloesch et al. and Hartley et al..
- OCELOT demonstrated substantially lower errors across all terrain types and metrics. For instance, on concrete, OCELOT achieved an ATE of 12.019m compared to 81.450m for Bloesch et al. and 50.785m for Hartley et al. Similar improvements were observed on tile, grass, pebble, and rock terrains, with OCELOT often achieving error reductions by a factor of 4 to 7 in ATE.
- The baseline methods exhibited specific weaknesses: Bloesch et al. showed significant scale drift (e.g., on concrete and pebble) and heading estimation errors across all terrains due to its fixed GRF threshold and static measurement covariance. Hartley et al., while employing an adaptive contact estimation, suffered from suboptimal filter certainty during stable stance phases because its covariance scaling was primarily based on the change in GRF ($\Delta GRF$), leading to yaw divergence despite better total distance estimation. OCELOT's fused, adaptive covariance effectively addressed these limitations.

**C. Exteroceptive Comparison (Table II):**
While OCELOT relies solely on proprioceptive sensors, its performance was also benchmarked against state-of-the-art monocular Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems: OpenVINS and VINS-Fusion. This comparison was limited to grass, pebble, and rock terrains.
- OCELOT consistently outperformed both VIO systems on these challenging outdoor terrains. For example, on grass, OCELOT recorded an ATE of 1.385m, significantly lower than OpenVINS (18.054m) and VINS-Fusion (4.677m). Similar trends were observed on pebble and rock.
- VIO methods were excluded from concrete and tile comparisons due to severe feature tracking failures caused by dynamic environments with reflective surfaces and a lack of distinct visual features. This highlights the robustness of proprioceptive-only approaches in scenarios where visual information is degraded or unreliable.

**D. Effect of FSM and GLRT (Table III & IV):**
An ablation study was conducted to evaluate the individual contributions of the GMM-FSM and GLRT detectors, as well as their fused combination.
- Each individual detector exhibited terrain-dependent failure modes. The GLRT-only detector accumulated significant ATE on concrete (23.161m vs. 12.025m for fused) due to the accumulation of forward kinematic noise over long trajectories. The FSM-only detector demonstrated failures on specific tile sequences (e.g., ATE of 9.577m on Tile sequence 1 vs. 3.730m for fused) due to misclassification of contact events based solely on force data.
- On grass, all configurations performed similarly, with ATE differences within 0.24m.
- On challenging granular terrains (pebble and rock), where slippage is frequent and critical, the fused model consistently achieved the lowest ATE (1.272m on pebble, 6.862m on rock). This outcome supports the theoretical motivation that a reliable measurement requires a foot to be both physically loaded (GMM-FSM) and kinematically stationary (GLRT). The fused model provided consistent robustness across the terrain spectrum, accepting a marginal difference in optimality on benign terrains for enhanced reliability in challenging conditions.

**E. Covariance Quantification (Figure 6):**
A sensitivity analysis on the $\sigma_{base}$ scalar coefficient, which modulates the measurement covariance, revealed its critical impact on trajectory accuracy.
- Improper quantification of measurement uncertainty, particularly underestimation (e.g., $\sigma_{base} = 0.25$ on rocky terrain), induced severe lateral drift and yaw divergence.
- The observed divergence pattern on rocky terrain when $\sigma_{base}$ was artificially reduced was geometrically similar to the trajectory errors of Hartley et al., suggesting that their covariance formulation, which scales inversely with $\Delta GRF$, might systematically underestimate true covariance during stable stance phases.
- This highlights the complex challenge of accurately transforming abstract binary classification uncertainty into a physically bounded, real-world covariance matrix for state estimation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OCELOT framework addresses a critical challenge in legged robotics by providing a robust and accurate proprioceptive odometry solution, particularly in environments prone to slippage. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enhanced Autonomy in Challenging Environments:** By robustly handling slippage and diverse terrain types (rigid, compliant, granular), OCELOT enables legged robots to operate more reliably in unstructured and complex environments where wheeled robots or systems reliant on external sensing (e.g., GPS-denied or visually degraded areas) might fail. This extends the operational envelope for applications such as search and rescue, exploration, and industrial inspection.
2. **Reduced Dependency on External Sensors:** The method relies exclusively on onboard proprioceptive sensors (IMU, joint encoders, foot force sensors), eliminating the need for foot-mounted IMUs or exteroceptive sensors like cameras or LiDAR for basic odometry. This simplifies hardware, reduces cost, and improves robustness in scenarios where external sensing might be obscured, occluded, or unavailable.
3. **Improved State Estimation Accuracy:** The substantial reduction in trajectory drift and errors (ATE, AHE, etc.) compared to existing proprioceptive baselines directly contributes to more precise robot localization. This accuracy is fundamental for downstream tasks such as motion planning, control, and mapping.
4. **Novel Contact Detection and Uncertainty Quantification:** The fused GMM-FSM and GLRT approach for contact detection and its translation into adaptive measurement covariance represents a methodological advancement. This dynamic uncertainty assignment allows the ESEKF to intelligently trust or down-weight measurements, making the filter more resilient to unmodeled events like slippage.
5. **Contribution to Reproducibility and Future Research:** The release of the open-source ROS 2 implementation and a new, comprehensive multi-terrain quadruped dataset is a significant contribution to the robotics community. This facilitates reproducibility of the results and provides valuable resources for other researchers to benchmark their algorithms and develop new methods for legged robot state estimation and contact modeling.
6. **Foundation for Sensor Fusion:** The robust proprioceptive backbone established by OCELOT serves as a strong foundation for future work on multi-modal sensor fusion. Future integration with exteroceptive modalities like monocular VIO could combine the strengths of both approaches, mitigating feature-tracking failures in dynamic visual environments while maintaining robust odometry from proprioceptive inputs.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in legged robot odometry by providing a robust, accurate, and self-contained proprioceptive solution, with a particular focus on intelligent handling of foot-ground interactions and associated uncertainties.
