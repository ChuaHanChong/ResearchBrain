# Is PRM Necessary? Problem-Solving RL Implicitly Induces PRM Capability in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from Huawei Technologies Ltd. and collaborators demonstrates that large language models trained with pure reinforcement learning for problem-solving implicitly acquire process reward model capabilities, outperforming models explicitly fine-tuned for process-level supervision on reasoning judgment benchmarks. The study also introduces Self-PRM, an introspective framework that leverages models' internal reward signals to enhance reasoning performance through self-reranking, though precision on difficult problems remains a challenge.

## Problem

- It was conventionally assumed that explicit process supervision (via Process Reward Models, PRMs) is necessary for Large Language Models (LLMs) to develop robust process-level reasoning abilities.
- Developing effective PRMs faces practical challenges, including high annotation costs for detailed process supervision and ambiguity in defining discrete reasoning steps.
- Existing research questioned whether models trained solely on outcome-based reinforcement learning (RL) could inherently develop PRM capabilities without direct process-level supervision.

## Method

- The study empirically evaluated the ==PRM capabilities== of various LLMs (proprietary, RL-trained, and explicitly PRM-tuned) on the PROCESSBENCH benchmark.
- Researchers analyzed the co-evolution of problem-solving proficiency and process supervision capabilities during RL training using learning curves and statistical methods.
- A novel introspective framework, Self-PRM, was proposed where LLMs autonomously evaluate and rerank their own generated solutions using internal reward signals.

## Results

- Pure RL-trained models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-**32B**) achieved average F1 scores above 83.5 on PROCESSBENCH, outperforming explicitly fine-tuned PRM models (e.g., Skywork-PRM-**7B** at 42.1, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-**72B** at 78.3).
- RL training curves for a Qwen2.5-**7B**-Base model showed parallel improvements in both final-answer accuracy and ProcessBench F1 scores, with process judgment gains often preceding accuracy.
- Self-PRM consistently outperformed direct sampling and majority voting in reranking (QwQ-**32B**: 86.7→90.0 on AIME24 at k=32), yet showed low precision on hard problems models initially failed (**3.6%** for QwQ-**32B** on CNMO24-index-1).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement learning (RL) alone can implicitly induce strong Process Reward Model (PRM) capabilities in LLMs, with problem-solving proficiency and process supervision co-evolving synergistically during training rather than being orthogonal, challenging the necessity of explicit process supervision.
> - External PRMs showed little to no performance benefit when applied to rerank outputs from strong RL-trained models, indicating misalignment or redundancy.
> - Self-PRM, leveraging a model's internal reward signals, can effectively enhance reasoning performance by reranking self-generated solutions, particularly at larger sample sizes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of advanced reasoning capabilities in large language models (LLMs) represents a significant area of research. Two prominent paradigms have emerged to enhance these capabilities: reinforcement learning (RL) and process reward models (PRMs). Recent advancements have demonstrated that RL, particularly through methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and related policy optimization algorithms (e.g., PPO, DPO), has enabled state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., Kimi 1.5, OpenReasonerZero, DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B, Claude-3.7-Sonnet) to achieve considerable proficiency in complex reasoning tasks, often without explicit process-level supervision.

Concurrently, Process Reward Models (PRMs) have been explored as a mechanism to provide more granular supervision by evaluating intermediate reasoning steps rather than just the final outcome. PRMs aim to foster structured, logical, and interpretable reasoning paths. While conceptually appealing for tasks requiring multi-step problem-solving, PRMs face practical challenges, including the ambiguity in defining discrete reasoning steps, high annotation costs for detailed process supervision, and potential vulnerabilities to reward hacking. Despite these limitations, research continues to explore PRM improvements, such as implicit reward mechanisms (e.g., PRIME), code verification for performance enhancement (e.g., GenPRM), and the creation of benchmarks for assessing reasoning traces (e.g., Qwen PROCESSBENCH).

This paper situates itself by questioning the fundamental necessity of explicit PRM training. It investigates whether LLMs trained purely with RL for problem-solving can inherently develop robust PRM capabilities—the ability to evaluate and judge the quality of reasoning processes—without any direct process-level supervision. This inquiry challenges the conventional assumption that explicit process supervision is indispensable for fostering process-level reasoning in LLMs, aiming to explore the intrinsic relationship and potential co-evolution between problem-solving proficiency and process supervision capabilities in RL-trained models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically investigate the relationship between RL training and the emergence of Process Reward Model (PRM) capabilities in large language models (LLMs). The work is motivated by the observation that state-of-the-art LLMs, such as DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B, have achieved high performance in complex reasoning tasks through pure RL training, seemingly without relying on explicit process supervision. This challenges the prevailing assumption that PRMs, which provide rewards for intermediate reasoning steps, are essential for developing robust reasoning abilities.

Specifically, the key objectives include:

- **To determine if RL training alone can induce PRM capabilities:** The primary motivation is to ascertain whether models trained solely with outcome-based reinforcement learning, without explicit process-level supervision, can implicitly develop the ability to evaluate and judge the quality of reasoning processes. This challenges the conventional wisdom that process supervision is a prerequisite for fostering process-level reasoning.
- **To analyze the co-evolution of problem-solving and process supervision skills:** The research aims to demonstrate that problem-solving proficiency and process supervision capabilities are not orthogonal but rather complementary competencies that co-evolve synergistically during pure RL training. This involves tracking the development of both skills over the course of RL training.
- **To empirically evaluate the efficacy of existing PRMs on strong RL-trained models:** A specific objective is to assess whether currently available PRMs, when applied to rerank outputs from strong RL-trained models, offer any significant performance improvements compared to simpler baselines like majority voting. This investigates whether external PRMs align with or enhance the internal reasoning of advanced models.
- **To propose and evaluate an introspective framework, Self-PRM:** The study aims to introduce a novel approach where models autonomously evaluate and rerank their own generated solutions using internal reward signals. This objective seeks to explore the potential of self-reward mechanisms for enhancing performance and to understand the limitations of such introspective evaluations.
- **To identify limitations and areas for improvement in introspective evaluation:** Through detailed analysis, the research intends to expose the inherent challenges of Self-PRM, particularly concerning its precision on difficult problems, where models may misclassify flawed solutions as valid. This aims to highlight areas for future research, such as strengthening reward alignment and introspective accuracy.

The overarching motivation is to provide actionable insights for building more reliable and self-aware complex reasoning models by understanding the emergent properties of RL training and potentially reducing the reliance on costly and ambiguous process-level human annotations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a systematic empirical approach involving various LLMs, benchmarks, and experimental settings to investigate the relationship between RL training and PRM capabilities.

#### 3.1. Evaluation of PRM Capabilities on PROCESSBENCH

- **Benchmark:** PROCESSBENCH, a publicly available benchmark for assessing LLM reasoning across mathematical problem-solving domains. It includes four datasets: GSM8K, MATH, OLYMPIADBENCH, and OMNIMATH.
- **Metrics:** Error Rate (incorrect judgments), Correct Rate (correct judgments), and F1 Score (harmonic mean of precision and recall for judgments).
- **Model Groups for Evaluation:**
 - **Proprietary LLMs (Critic Models):** Closed-source models like GPT-4o-0806 and o1-mini, serving as reference points for PRM performance.
 - **Qwen-series and DeepSeek-series Models (Prompted as Generative PRM):** Models trained primarily with RL for problem-solving (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B, R1-Distill-Qwen-32B). These models are *prompted* to act as PRMs, without direct fine-tuning on PRM datasets.
 - **Models Fine-Tuned with PRM Data (Discriminative PRM):** Models explicitly trained on datasets with PRM-style supervision (e.g., Math-Shepherd-PRM, Skywork-PRM-7B, RetrievalPRM-7B, RLHFlow-PRM-Mistral-8B, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B/72B).

#### 3.2. Statistical Analysis of Problem-Solving vs. Judgment

- **Method:** Chi-square tests are conducted to assess the correlation between problem-solving proficiency and process supervision capabilities within the same model.
- **Categorization:** For each model and problem, solutions are categorized as "True" (correct solution generated by LLM) or "False" (incorrect solution). Judgments are categorized as "Correct" (accurate evaluation of solution correctness) or "Error" (incorrect evaluation).
- **Hypothesis Testing:** The p-value from the chi-square test is used to test the null hypothesis that problem-solving proficiency and process supervision capabilities are independent.

#### 3.3. Analysis of PRM Capability During RL Training

- **Model:** Qwen2.5-7B-Base.
- **RL Training:** Policy optimization using DAPO algorithm on DAPO-Math-17k dataset.
- **Monitoring:** Learning curves are analyzed to observe changes in both final-answer accuracy and ProcessBench F1 scores as RL training steps increase.
- **Hyperparameters:** Specific values for learning rate (1e-6), batch size (256), prompt length (2048), output length (10240), group size (16), clipping ratio (0.28), overlong buffer length (4096), and overlong penalty factor (1.0) are noted.

#### 3.4. Self-Reference (Self-REF) Paradigm

- **Purpose:** To investigate whether a model's self-generated solutions can serve as effective supervisory signals for PRM alignment.
- **Models:** Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, QwQ-32B, and DeepSeek-R1.
- **Comparison:** PRM performance (F1 score) is compared between models prompted as generative PRMs and those enhanced with Self-REF, where the model incorporates its own solutions as supervisory signals. The analysis also explores the impact on weaker instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct).

#### 3.5. Evaluation of PRM Models for Enhancing Reasoning Models

- **Context:** Assessment of whether existing PRMs can improve the performance of strong reasoning models.
- **Datasets:** AIME24, AIME25, and CNMO24 (complex mathematical reasoning benchmarks).
- **Experimental Settings:**
 - **Direct Sampling (Pass@k):** Baseline performance of generating *k* solutions and checking if any are correct.
 - **Majority Voting:** Selecting the answer that appears most frequently among *k* sampled solutions.
 - **Best-of-N with External PRM (BoN w/ PRM):** Using an external PRM (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) to select the best output from *k* sampled solutions (k=8, 16, 32, 64) based on its minimum-step reward.
 - **Best-of-N with Self-PRM (BoN w/ Self-PRM):** The reasoning model (QwQ-32B or DeepSeek-R1) serves as its own PRM, reranking its *k* sampled outputs using internally derived reward scores.

#### 3.6. Analysis of Self-PRM Limitations

- **Method:** Fine-grained analysis at the problem level, particularly on problems that the models initially failed (Pass@1).
- **Sampling:** 64 solutions are sampled per problem instance.
- **Metrics:**
 - S_PRM: Number of solutions labeled as correct by the Self-PRM.
 - S_TP: Number of truly correct solutions among those labeled correct by S_PRM.
 - Precision: S_TP / S_PRM, indicating the accuracy of Self-PRM's judgment.
- **Focus:** Identifying instances where Self-PRM misclassifies incorrect reasoning traces as valid, particularly on high-difficulty problems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents several empirical findings concerning the relationship between RL training and PRM capabilities in LLMs:

#### 4.1. Emergence of PRM Capabilities in RL-trained Models

- **Superior Performance of RL-trained Models:** Models trained purely with RL, such as DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B, exhibit strong inherent PRM capabilities on the PROCESSBENCH benchmark. These models consistently outperform discriminative PRM models that were explicitly fine-tuned on PRM-annotated datasets. For instance, DeepSeek-R1 achieved an average F1 score of 83.5, and QwQ-32B achieved 83.7, surpassing all listed discriminative PRM models (e.g., Skywork-PRM-7B at 42.1, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B at 78.3). This suggests that explicit PRM supervision is not a prerequisite for developing process judgment abilities.
- **Statistical Correlation:** Chi-square tests indicate a statistically significant positive correlation (p < 0.05 in aggregate analysis) between problem-solving proficiency and process supervision capabilities across various mathematical reasoning tasks. This supports the hypothesis that these two competencies are intrinsically linked within LLMs. While some individual dataset/model pairs showed non-significant p-values (e.g., R1-Distill-Qwen-32B on GSM8K, QwQ-32B on MATH), the overall trend affirms the correlation.

#### 4.2. Co-evolution of Skills During RL Training

- **Parallel Enhancement:** Analysis of RL training curves for a Qwen2.5-7B-Base model demonstrates a consistent and parallel improvement in both final-answer accuracy and ProcessBench F1 scores as training steps increase.
- **Early Process Judgment Gains:** Improvements in process judgment (F1 score) often precede or surpass gains in final-answer accuracy, particularly in the early phases of training on benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K. This implies that RL helps models internalize valid reasoning structures before full mastery of solution strategies. Process-level learning also provides a more stable signal than accuracy on challenging (OLYMPIADBENCH) or domain-shifting (OMNIMATH) tasks.

#### 4.3. Impact of Self-Reference (Self-REF) on PRM Capability

- **Benefit for Non-RL Models:** Self-REF, where models use their own generated solutions as supervisory signals, primarily improves PRM performance for instruction-tuned models that have not undergone extensive RL training. For example, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct showed a substantial F1 score improvement from 45.1 to 63.9 (+18.8) with Self-REF. This suggests Self-REF can inject structure into process modeling when explicit reward-driven reasoning is absent.
- **Limited Impact on RL-trained Models:** RL-trained models like QwQ-32B and DeepSeek-R1 showed marginal or even negative changes in F1 scores with Self-REF (e.g., QwQ-32B: 83.7 → 83.0). This indicates that these models have already internalized process reasoning, and additional weakly supervised signals from self-generated traces may introduce noise. Weaker instruction-tuned models (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct) exhibited performance declines, indicating the effectiveness of Self-REF depends on the base model's reasoning quality.

#### 4.4. Performance of PRMs in Enhancing Reasoning Models

- **External PRMs' Limitations:** When applying external PRMs (BoN w/ PRM) for reranking outputs of strong RL-trained models (QwQ-32B, DeepSeek-R1) on AIME and CNMO benchmarks, there was little to no performance benefit. These external PRMs often performed comparably to or slightly worse than simple majority voting across various sample sizes (k=8, 16, 32, 64). This suggests that current external PRMs may not effectively align with or enhance the internal reasoning of already proficient RL-trained models.
- **Effectiveness of Self-PRM:** In contrast, the proposed Self-PRM method, where models use their own internal reward signals to rerank sampled outputs, consistently outperformed both direct sampling (Pass@k) and majority voting. This improvement was more pronounced at larger sample sizes (e.g., k=32, 64). For instance, QwQ-32B on AIME24 improved from 86.7 (majority voting) to 90.0 (BoN w/ Self-PRM) at k=32. This indicates that a model's internal reward signal is better aligned with its own reasoning behavior, allowing for more effective utilization of its latent PRM capabilities.

#### 4.5. Limitations of Self-PRM

- **Low Precision on Difficult Problems:** Despite aggregate performance gains, Self-PRM exhibits low precision at a fine-grained, problem-specific level, especially on challenging problems where the model initially fails (e.g., CNMO24, certain AIME25 instances). The ratio of truly correct solutions (S_TP) to solutions labeled correct by Self-PRM (S_PRM) was often low. For example, on CNMO24-index-1, QwQ-32B's Self-PRM selected 55 solutions as correct, but only 2 were truly correct (3.6% precision). This indicates that Self-PRM frequently misclassifies flawed solutions as valid, overestimating the correctness of its own outputs.
- **Correlation with Solver Strength:** Stronger models, such as DeepSeek-R1, generally show relatively higher precision in Self-PRM filtering compared to QwQ-32B, suggesting a better internal alignment between their reasoning and scoring mechanisms. For the same CNMO24-index-1 problem, DeepSeek-R1 achieved 25.6% precision, selecting 10 true positives out of 39 identified solutions. This highlights that while introspective evaluation is beneficial, its precision remains a challenge, particularly for problems outside the model's high-confidence domain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of reasoning capabilities in LLMs by challenging the established view regarding the necessity of explicit process supervision. The findings suggest that Reinforcement Learning (RL) alone, even without fine-grained process-level annotations, can implicitly foster robust Process Reward Model (PRM) capabilities within LLMs. This implies that problem-solving proficiency and the ability to judge the quality of reasoning steps are not independent but rather co-evolving skills.

The study has several potential impacts:

- **Reduced Reliance on Costly Annotations:** By demonstrating that pure RL training can induce PRM capabilities, the work suggests that the prohibitive annotation costs associated with collecting detailed process supervision data for PRMs might not be as indispensable as previously assumed. This could streamline the development and scaling of reasoning models, making advanced LLMs more accessible and efficient to train.
- **Rethinking Reasoning Model Architectures and Training:** The observed co-evolution of problem-solving and process judgment abilities in RL-trained models prompts a re-evaluation of how reasoning is cultivated in LLMs. It implies that current RL methods might inherently encourage the learning of internal reasoning consistency, even when optimizing for outcome-based rewards. This could lead to a focus on scaling RL training and refining reward alignment rather than prioritizing external process supervision.
- **Introduction of Self-Aware Mechanisms:** The Self-PRM framework, which leverages a model's internal reward signal for self-evaluation and reranking, represents a step towards building more introspective and self-aware LLMs. While current limitations in precision exist, the consistent performance improvements achieved by Self-PRM indicate a viable direction for enhancing model reliability without external supervision. Future research could focus on improving the precision of these self-evaluation mechanisms through continued RL scaling or targeted training.
- **Guidance for Future RL Research:** The detailed analysis of Self-PRM's limitations, particularly its low precision on challenging problems, provides specific directions for future RL research. It underscores the need to enhance reward alignment and improve the consistency between a model's problem-solving capabilities and its self-assessment abilities. This could involve exploring novel RL objectives or architectural designs that explicitly encourage more accurate self-reflection.
- **Deeper Understanding of LLM Cognition:** The finding that process judgment gains can precede or parallel accuracy improvements during RL training offers insights into how LLMs learn to reason. It suggests that models may first internalize structural understanding of valid reasoning paths before fully mastering the execution to yield correct final answers. This could inform foundational research into the cognitive mechanisms of LLMs.

Overall, the research provides empirical evidence that challenges conventional assumptions and offers actionable insights for developing more reliable, self-aware, and computationally efficient complex reasoning models.
