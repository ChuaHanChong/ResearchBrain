# DogLegs: Robust Proprioceptive State Estimation for Legged Robots Using Multiple Leg-Mounted IMUs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Bonn and Wuhan University developed DogLegs, an Extended Kalman Filter-based system that improves proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots by integrating a body-mounted IMU, joint encoders, and multiple low-cost leg-mounted IMUs. This framework fully exploits the 6-DOF pose capabilities of all IMUs, demonstrating superior accuracy and reduced drift, achieving a ~0.1m loop closure error and significantly lower attitude errors (e.g., 0.59° roll RMSE) compared to traditional leg odometry in challenging environments.

## Problem

- Traditional proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots faces limitations such as unreliable foot contact detection (prone to errors from slippage or sensor degradation) and error accumulation during the swing phase.
- Prior efforts to integrate leg-mounted IMUs did not fully utilize their 6-DOF pose estimation capabilities or comprehensively address sensor biases, thus not maximizing potential accuracy.
- Legged robots require robust state estimation that is independent of exteroceptive sensors, which can fail in adverse conditions like darkness, dense foliage, or feature-poor environments.

## Method

- A comprehensive ==error-state Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)== integrates measurements from a body-mounted IMU, joint encoders, and multiple leg-mounted IMUs.
- Foot contact is detected using a ==generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT)== on Leg-IMU accelerometer readings, avoiding error-prone force sensors.
- The EKF incorporates ==zero-velocity updates (ZUPT)== for Leg-IMUs during foot contact and relative position constraints between IMUs derived from leg kinematics to mitigate error drift.

## Results

- **DogLegs** achieved superior state estimation accuracy and significantly reduced drift compared to traditional leg odometry, demonstrating lower Absolute Pose Error (e.g., **4.07 m** APE translation on asphalt road vs. **5.76 m**).
- The system provided more stable and accurate attitude estimation, with consistently lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for roll and pitch angles (e.g., **0.59°** roll RMSE compared to **2.79°** for LegOdom).
- In GNSS-denied environments, **DogLegs** achieved a loop closure distance error of approximately **0.1 m**, less than half of the error observed with baseline methods, while maintaining real-time computational efficiency (**0.39 ms**/measurement).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating full 6-DOF pose estimation and bias estimation from multiple leg-mounted IMUs within an EKF framework enables more accurate and stable state estimation for legged robots.
> - Utilizing Leg-IMU accelerometer data for foot contact detection (via GLRT) provides a more robust alternative to traditional force-sensor-based methods.
> - Cross-constraining multiple IMUs through both zero-velocity updates and kinematic relative position information effectively mitigates individual IMU error drift and random errors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate and robust state estimation is a fundamental requirement for legged robots to operate effectively, particularly for tasks such as maintaining balance and navigating complex or unstructured environments. The research landscape for legged robot state estimation is broadly divided into two categories: systems relying on exteroceptive sensors (e.g., LiDARs, cameras) and those utilizing proprioceptive sensors (e.g., IMUs, joint encoders). While exteroceptive sensors can enhance localization accuracy, their reliability can be compromised in challenging conditions like darkness, dense foliage, or feature-poor environments. In such scenarios, proprioceptive sensors become critical, often serving as a primary or backup solution.

Traditional proprioceptive state estimation approaches for legged robots typically fuse data from a body-mounted Inertial Measurement Unit (Body-IMU), joint encoders, and foot force sensors. Methods pioneered by Bloesch et al. augment foot contact positions into the state, updating them via leg kinematics upon detected ground contact. This approach, often implemented with Extended Kalman Filters (EKF) or Unscented Kalman Filters (UKF), has formed the foundation for many subsequent frameworks. Advances have included the adoption of Invariant Extended Kalman Filters (InEKF), leveraging Lie Groups for state propagation and error tracking, and incorporating learned contact events or fixed-lag smoothers to improve accuracy and robustness.

However, these traditional methods face several limitations. Foot contact detection, often relying on empirically thresholding force sensor readings, can be prone to errors caused by slippage or sensor degradation. Furthermore, the positions of the feet during the swing phase are typically not updated, leading to potential error accumulation that only resets upon new ground contact.

More recently, researchers have explored the integration of additional IMUs mounted on the robot's legs (Leg-IMUs) to improve state estimation. Kolvenbach et al. used foot-mounted IMUs for specific inspection tasks but not for main body state estimation. Maravgakis et al. investigated Leg-IMUs for probabilistic contact state estimation. Yang et al. incorporated multiple foot-mounted IMUs to provide additional foot velocity measurements within an EKF structure similar to earlier works. Despite this, existing Leg-IMU approaches have not fully exploited the six degrees of freedom (6-DOF) pose estimation capabilities of IMUs, often treating them as only velocity sensors, nor have they comprehensively addressed the sensor biases of these multiple IMUs.

This work, "DogLegs," aims to address these limitations by fully leveraging the complete state estimation information from all individual IMU systems, including both Body-IMU and multiple Leg-IMUs. It integrates full 6-DOF pose estimation capabilities and estimates sensor biases within a comprehensive Extended Kalman Filter framework. The approach draws inspiration from foot-mounted inertial navigation systems (Foot-INS) used in pedestrian localization, which utilize zero-velocity updates (ZUPT) during foot-ground contact to mitigate IMU error drift. However, adapting Foot-INS to legged robots presents challenges due to different gaits, less stable foot-ground contact, and operation in diverse terrains. DogLegs specifically attempts to bridge this gap by integrating Foot-INS concepts into a multi-IMU framework for legged robots, aiming to reduce drift and enhance overall state estimation robustness and accuracy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and accurate proprioceptive state estimation system for the main body of legged robots. This system is designed to function effectively in extreme environments where exteroceptive sensors, such as LiDARs and cameras, may become unreliable.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in current legged robot state estimation practices:
1. **Reliability of Exteroceptive Sensors:** While exteroceptive sensors are valuable, their performance can degrade in adverse conditions (e.g., darkness, heavy rain, dense foliage, dust), necessitating robust proprioceptive alternatives.
2. **Limitations of Traditional Proprioceptive Systems:**
 - **Foot Contact Detection:** Existing methods often rely on force sensors, which are susceptible to errors from slippage or long-term degradation.
 - **Foot Position Updates:** Traditional systems typically only update foot positions upon ground contact, leading to accumulated error during the swing phase.
 - **Incomplete IMU Utilization:** Prior work incorporating Leg-IMUs often failed to leverage the full 6-DOF pose estimation capabilities of these sensors and did not comprehensively address their sensor biases. This oversight limits the potential for reducing random errors and enhancing accuracy.
3. **Need for Drift Reduction:** All inertial systems suffer from drift over time. A key motivation is to develop a system that minimizes this drift, thereby maintaining accuracy over longer operational periods.
4. **Hardware Efficiency and Integrability:** The goal is to achieve improved state estimation with minimal additional hardware and computational costs, ensuring the solution is applicable to existing legged robot platforms.

The authors explicitly make two major claims that serve as specific objectives for the DogLegs framework:
- **Performance Improvement:** DogLegs is claimed to achieve better performance in state estimation compared to traditional EKF-based leg odometry (which uses only a Body-IMU and joint encoders) across various terrains.
- **Error Drift Reduction:** DogLegs aims to reduce the error drift of individual IMU systems by incorporating relative position constraints between the multiple IMUs.

Additionally, the authors commit to making their datasets publicly available to support further research in the community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed DogLegs system is an error-state Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) that fuses measurements from a body-mounted IMU (Body-IMU), joint encoders, and multiple leg-mounted IMUs (Leg-IMUs). The Leg-IMUs are strategically mounted on the lower legs of the robot, close to the feet, to effectively treat the foot and lower leg as a single rigid body.

**System Architecture:** The system maintains a comprehensive error-state vector that accounts for errors across all IMU frames. Each IMU system (Body-IMU and all Leg-IMUs) independently performs strapdown Inertial Navigation System (INS) state propagation. The EKF then refines these propagated states using two types of observations: zero-velocity updates from Leg-IMUs during foot contact and relative position constraints derived from leg kinematics between the Body-IMU and Leg-IMUs.

**A. Foot Contact Detection:**
Instead of relying on force sensors, which can be prone to errors, DogLegs detects foot contact by analyzing the Leg-IMU accelerometer readings. When a foot is in stable contact with the ground, its acceleration is theoretically zero. The system employs a generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT) on a batch of accelerometer measurements to identify these zero-velocity instances. The criterion involves comparing the norm of the difference between instantaneous and average accelerometer measurements (corrected for gravity) over a batch against an empirical threshold (Equation 1).

**B. Error State Model:**
Each IMU's state (position, velocity, orientation) is propagated by integrating its gyroscope and accelerometer data using the strapdown INS equations. To manage nonlinearity and enable efficient filtering, an error-state representation is adopted. The full error-state vector for the EKF combines the error states of all N+1 IMUs (one Body-IMU and N Leg-IMUs). Each individual IMU error state (`δx`) consists of 15 parameters: position error (`δp`), velocity error (`δv`), attitude error (`δϕ`), and residual biases for the gyroscope (`b_g`) and accelerometer (`b_a`) (Equation 3). The total dimension of the error-state vector for the EKF is thus 15(N+1). The continuous-time error state propagation model (Equation 4) describes how these errors evolve over time, accounting for noise and bias dynamics.

**C. Observation Model:**

1. **Zero-Velocity Update (ZUPT):**
 When a foot maintains stable contact with the ground without slippage, its velocity in the world-frame is considered zero. The ZUPT measurement model is applied to Leg-IMUs upon detecting foot contact. It accounts for the physical offset between the Leg-IMU's mounting location and the actual foot contact point. The velocity of the foot contact point is estimated from the Leg-IMU's velocity and angular rate (Equation 5) and compared against the observed zero velocity (Equation 6) to form a measurement update for the Leg-IMU's velocity, attitude, and gyroscope bias errors (Equation 7). If all IMUs (body and legs) report zero-velocity, indicating a static robot state, ZUPT is applied to all IMUs.

2. **Relative Position Constraints:**
 The Body-IMU and Leg-IMUs are not independent but are kinematically linked through the robot's structure. Joint encoder readings allow for real-time calculation of the relative positions between these IMUs, which serves as a constraint to mitigate error drift.
 - **Initialization:** The initial pose of each Leg-IMU relative to the world-frame is determined. Roll and pitch are derived from initial stationary IMU data, initial translation from leg kinematics, and yaw is assumed to be zero due to careful alignment during setup.
 - **Foot Position Estimation:** Each Leg-IMU estimates the position of the foot contact point in the world-frame (`p^w_f`).
 - **Body-IMU Position Estimation from Leg-IMU:** Based on this, the position of the Body-IMU in the world-frame (`p^w_b`) can be derived (Equation 8).
 - **Relative Foot Position:** The relative position of the foot in the body-frame can then be expressed from the perspective of the Leg-IMU and Body-IMU (Equation 9).
 - **Kinematic Model:** Concurrently, the robot's leg kinematics model (`g(ϕ^)`), using joint angle measurements (`ϕ^`) from the encoders, provides an independent calculation of the foot's position in the body-frame (`p˜^b_f`) (Equation 10).
 - **Measurement Update:** The difference between the kinematically derived foot position and the IMU-derived relative foot position forms the measurement update for the EKF (Equation 11). This update constrains the errors across all IMUs.
 These relative position updates are performed for a Leg-IMU whenever foot contact is detected, in conjunction with the zero-velocity updates.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted using a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot. It was equipped with its internal Body-IMU and four custom-built Leg-IMUs. These Leg-IMUs utilized low-cost ICM20602 inertial sensors (approximately $3 USD), a rechargeable battery, and a Bluetooth module for data transmission. A GNSS/IMU integrated navigation system provided near-ground-truth trajectories for most outdoor experiments, while loop closure error was assessed for an environment where GNSS was obstructed. Data from IMUs and joint encoders were collected at 200 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate DogLegs against two baselines: `LegOdom` (traditional EKF-based leg odometry using only a Body-IMU and joint encoders) and `Foot-INS` (a single foot-IMU based localization system), across various outdoor terrains including asphalt road, parking lots, construction sites, offroad, and grass.

**A. State Estimation Performance:**
- **Overall Accuracy (Table I, Fig. 5):** DogLegs generally demonstrated superior performance in state estimation accuracy, as indicated by both Relative Pose Error (RPE) for translation and rotation, and Absolute Pose Error (APE) for translation and rotation. For instance, in the asphalt road sequence, DogLegs achieved a lower APE translation (4.07 m) compared to LegOdom (5.76 m). While LegOdom showed a slightly smaller translation error in the parking lots sequence, DogLegs exhibited more accurate trajectory alignment with ground truth as shown in Figure 5(b).
- **Drift Reduction (Fig. 5):** DogLegs exhibited significantly smaller drift compared to LegOdom. Notably, in the offroad sequence (Fig. 5d), LegOdom showed a substantial initial heading error leading to significant drift, whereas DogLegs maintained more stable heading and a more accurate trajectory.
- **Loop Closure (Fig. 6):** In the grass experiment, where GNSS signals were unavailable, DogLegs achieved a loop closure distance error of approximately 0.1 m. This was less than half the error observed with LegOdom, indicating improved long-term consistency in GNSS-denied environments.
- **Roll and Pitch Accuracy (Table II):** DogLegs consistently provided lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and maximum absolute error (MAX) for both roll and pitch angles across all four sequences. For example, on the asphalt road, LegOdom had an RMSE of 2.79° for roll, while DogLegs achieved 0.59°. This suggests that DogLegs offers more stable and accurate attitude estimation, which is critical for legged robot balance and dynamic control.

**B. Multi-IMU Constraint Effectiveness (Fig. 7):**
The analysis of individual Leg-IMU trajectories (estimated using Foot-INS) compared to DogLegs demonstrated the effectiveness of the multi-IMU constraints. Figure 7 illustrates that the DogLegs trajectory fell approximately in the middle of the varying trajectories from the four independent Leg-IMU systems. This indicates that by integrating diverse motion information and cross-constraining multiple IMUs, DogLegs effectively mitigates the random sensor errors inherent in individual IMUs, leading to a more accurate and stable overall state estimate.

**C. Foot Contact Detection (Fig. 8):**
A comparison of foot contact detection methods on the front-left foot in the asphalt road sequence showed that DogLegs, using Leg-IMU accelerometer measurements, detected more stable and consistent foot contact intervals. In contrast, the traditional force threshold-based method introduced outliers due to its reliance on a single-dimensional force measurement, which can be less robust.

**D. Computation Efficiency (Table III):**
DogLegs demonstrated real-time computational efficiency. On a standard desktop computer, the average total computation time per IMU measurement across all sequences was approximately 0.39 ms. The prediction step took about 0.19 ms, and the update step, which is not executed at every time step, averaged 0.87 ms when triggered. Given an IMU measurement interval of 5 ms (at 200 Hz), DogLegs operates well within real-time constraints.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DogLegs system represents a contribution to the field of legged robot state estimation, particularly for operations in challenging and unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Accuracy:** The system demonstrates improved accuracy and reduced drift compared to traditional proprioceptive state estimation methods. This advancement is crucial for maintaining robot balance and enabling precise navigation in environments where exteroceptive sensors may be compromised or unavailable.
- **Full Utilization of Sensor Data:** Unlike prior attempts, DogLegs fully leverages the 6-DOF pose estimation capabilities of Leg-IMUs and systematically addresses their sensor biases within a comprehensive EKF framework. This complete integration of information from multiple IMUs contributes to the observed performance improvements.
- **Cost-Effectiveness:** The use of low-cost, commercial-grade IMU chips (approximately $3 USD) makes the proposed solution economically viable for widespread adoption. This reduces the hardware cost barrier for implementing robust state estimation in legged robots.
- **Ease of Integration:** The Leg-IMUs can be seamlessly integrated into existing legged robot designs, potentially replacing or complementing existing foot force sensors without requiring significant structural modifications. This facilitates direct application to current robot platforms.
- **Improved Autonomy in Challenging Environments:** By providing a reliable and accurate proprioceptive state estimate, DogLegs enhances the robot's ability to operate autonomously in conditions such as darkness, heavy vegetation, or tunnels, where LiDARs and cameras may fail. It serves as a robust complement or backup to exteroceptive perception.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a baseline for incorporating multiple IMUs into legged robot state estimation. The authors identify future research directions, such as leveraging Leg-IMUs for advanced foot slippage detection and integrating foot contact uncertainty, which could further improve the system's capabilities.
- **Contribution to the Research Community:** The public availability of the datasets collected during the experiments fosters reproducibility and provides valuable resources for other researchers working on legged robot localization and navigation.

In summary, DogLegs advances the state-of-the-art in proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots by providing a robust, accurate, and computationally efficient framework that effectively integrates information from multiple low-cost IMUs and joint encoders, particularly in environments challenging for exteroceptive sensing.
