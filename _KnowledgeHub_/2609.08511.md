# PGMT: Perceptive General Motion Tracking for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> PGMT is a perceptive whole-body motion tracker for humanoid robots that adapts terrain-agnostic reference motions to rough terrain without paired motion-terrain data. It pretrains a general tracking and recovery prior, then injects motion-conditioned terrain glimpses into an intent-fusion attention module with terrain-aware tracking relaxation. It reaches 87.81% completion in simulation and deploys zero-shot on a Unitree G1 over stairs, vegetation, and 37 cm boxes.

## Problem

- Whole-body motion trackers succeed mainly on flat ground; terrain-agnostic references become physically infeasible on stairs, slopes, and boxes, forcing failures.
- Large human motion datasets lack synchronized terrain geometry, and collecting paired motion-terrain demonstrations scales poorly as motion and terrain diversity grow.
- Methods that build terrain-adapted references upstream depend on that intermediate stage's quality, letting its errors propagate into the downstream tracking policy.

## Method

- ==Intent Fusion Module==: ==RoPE cross-attention== fuses a history-conditioned proprioception token with future reference frames and terrain tokens into one motion-intent token for actor and critic.
- ==Terrain-Glimpse Encoder==: an MLP position selector predicts four glimpse locations on the elevation map; differentiable bilinear 5x5 crops give compact terrain tokens without foothold labels.
- Two-stage training with ==terrain-aware tracking relaxation== plus a ==multi-head split-return critic== (upper, lower, auxiliary, terrain-contact), with global position correction feeding planar error into the velocity command.

## Results

- PGMT completes **87.81%** of **9,600** episodes and **83.33%** at the hardest level L9, versus **40.02%/35.31%** for its non-perceptive pretrained variant and **55.1%** for a perceptive baseline.
- Learned glimpse selection beats alternatives most on hardest stairs: **78.12%** versus **64.58-69.79%** for CNN encoding, two glimpses, fixed locations, and zero terrain input.
- The split-return critic keeps improving after perception injection, reaching roughly **380-step** mean episodes and near **80%** timeout rate, while the aggregate-return critic stays near zero.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Terrain adaptation can live inside the tracking policy itself; an upstream terrain-matched reference generator is an avoidable source of error propagation.
> - Sparse, motion-conditioned attention over an elevation map matches dense encoding cheaply and pulls ahead when hard geometry and hard motions coincide.
> - Separating value heads per reward group matters when a new objective is added mid-training; an entangled scalar critic can stall learning entirely.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot control has made advancements in enabling robots to reproduce complex whole-body motions. However, the application of these motion trackers has been largely confined to controlled, flat-ground environments. This limitation stems from the fact that motion references, typically derived from human motion datasets, are often terrain-agnostic and may become physically infeasible when encountered with complex or uneven terrain. Existing approaches that attempt to address this issue face challenges such as the inherent difficulty in acquiring large-scale paired motion and terrain data, or rely on intermediate stages to construct terrain-adapted references. Such intermediate stages can introduce errors or limit scalability.

Previous efforts in perception-aware humanoid control have demonstrated capabilities like obstacle crossing and the use of teacher-student training to handle noisy terrain data. These methods have focused on improving terrain representations, employing elevation maps, and utilizing active gaze for relevant perceptual input. While effective in adapting to terrain, these solutions have typically been tied to specific locomotion or obstacle-crossing tasks, rather than providing a general framework for diverse whole-body motion tracking.

The present work, PGMT, addresses a gap by developing a perceptive motion tracker that learns to adapt motion references to terrain *directly within the policy itself*, without requiring pre-adapted references or extensive paired motion-terrain demonstrations. This approach aims to unify diverse whole-body behaviors, locomotion, and teleoperation under a single policy, making general motion tracking more robust and applicable to unstructured, real-world environments. It frames terrain adaptation as an intrinsic capability of the tracking policy, processing motion intent and terrain constraints at the same level.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research arises from the limitations of current humanoid motion trackers, which, despite their ability to reproduce diverse whole-body motions, fail to perform robustly on complex terrain. When terrain-agnostic motion references are given in environments with obstacles, slopes, or uneven surfaces, these references become physically impossible to execute strictly. Consequently, humanoid robots need to adapt their contact points, swing trajectories, and whole-body posture dynamically based on real-time terrain observations.

This perceptive adaptation is crucial for various practical applications, including:
- **Remote teleoperation and data collection:** Human operators or high-level planners cannot anticipate every local terrain feature, necessitating autonomous adaptation by the robot.
- **Search and rescue:** Operations in rough and unpredictable terrains demand highly adaptive locomotion.
- **Transportation through complex environments:** Robots require the ability to navigate varied landscapes while performing tasks.

The challenge in developing such a capability lies in the data: large-scale human motion datasets lack synchronized terrain geometry and terrain-specific adaptations. Collecting paired motion-terrain demonstrations is difficult to scale with increasing diversity of motions and terrains. Existing solutions, which construct terrain-adapted references prior to tracking, are constrained by the quality and generalization of these intermediate stages and their scalability.

Against this background, the key objectives of the PGMT research are:
- **To propose PGMT:** A general motion tracker equipped with perception that can directly track terrain-agnostic motion references over complex terrain without relying on pre-computed terrain-matched reference trajectories.
- **To design a progressively extensible architecture:** This architecture aims to decouple motion and perception encoding, allowing for the injection of new perceptual modalities while preserving the robot's learned motion tracking capabilities.
- **To conduct extensive evaluations:** Through simulations and real-world experiments across diverse terrains and various motion-reference command sources, the objective is to demonstrate the robust zero-shot generalization capability of PGMT.

Ultimately, the work aims to establish a reusable, low-level interface that translates terrain-agnostic motion commands into physically feasible, terrain-aware execution, thereby enabling a unified policy for locomotion, diverse whole-body behaviors, and teleoperation in complex environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PGMT formulates perceptive whole-body motion tracking as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). At each time step, the policy receives a perceptive observation and outputs target joint positions as the action. The observation includes current proprioception, a ten-frame proprioception history, future reference frames (sampled at exponentially increasing temporal offsets for motion intent), and an elevation map of the surrounding 2 m × 2 m region.

The training of PGMT is structured in two progressive stages:

**Stage 1: Tracking Pretraining Stage**
This stage establishes a general terrain-agnostic prior for whole-body motion tracking on flat terrain.
- **Terrain-agnostic Policy Architecture:**
 - **History Encoder:** Uses a multi-head cross-attention (MHCA) layer to process current proprioception as a query against a proprioceptive history, generating a compact `s_hist` token representing recent robot dynamics and temporal context.
 - **Intent Fusion Module (IFM):** Another MHCA layer, equipped with Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), takes `s_hist` as a query to attend to itself and future reference frames (`C_K`), producing a `s_int` token. This token selectively emphasizes future reference information conditioned on the robot's current state.
 - **Actor Network:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) concatenates the current proprioceptive observation with `s_int` to output target joint positions, which are then converted to torques by a low-level PD controller.
 - **Multi-Head Critic:** To handle diverse learning characteristics of different motion objectives, the critic provides a vector of value estimates, one for each of three reward groups: `r_upper` (upper-body objectives), `r_lower` (lower-body objectives), and `r_aux` (auxiliary objectives like root motion, recovery, safety, regularization). These heads share a common backbone but model reward groups separately.
- **Training Optimization:**
 - Training uses motions from the LAFAN1 dataset, retargeted to a humanoid robot, on flat terrain.
 - A root-centric tracking formulation is adopted, focusing on relative body motion and reducing dependence on absolute global position.
 - **Recovery Curriculum:** Incorporates an offline pool of post-fall robot states, progressively increasing the proportion of episodes initialized from this pool based on episode survival performance.
 - **Adaptive Sampling:** Assigns higher probabilities to motion segments associated with frequent tracking failures.

**Stage 2: Perception Injection Stage**
This stage progressively extends the PGMT policy to incorporate terrain perception, enabling terrain-aware adaptation.
- **Perception Injection Process:**
 - **Terrain-Glimpse Encoder:** Instead of encoding the entire elevation map densely, this module leverages motion-conditioned terrain glimpses. An MLP (position selector) predicts N_g = 4 glimpse locations based on `s_hist`, the elevation map, and `C_K`. These locations are used to crop 5x5 terrain patches, which are then encoded by an MLP into local terrain tokens (`z_j`). Bilinear sampling ensures differentiability for learning glimpse locations.
 - **Integration with IFM:** The local terrain tokens `{z_j}` are augmented into the keys and values of the IFM (from Eq. 3, `K = V = [s_hist; C_K; z_1; ...; z_Ng]`), allowing the motion-intent token (`s_int`) to incorporate terrain-aware information.
 - **Multi-Head Critic Extension:** An additional `terrain-contact` reward group (`r_terrain`) is introduced to encourage stable contacts. This group includes rewards for touchdown quality, reference contact match, and penalties for slip, stumbling, rapid contact switching, and excessive contact forces. A new value head `V_terrain` is added to the multi-head critic, providing a dedicated learning signal for terrain interaction.
- **Terrain-Adaptive Training Design:**
 - **Terrain-Aware Tracking Relaxation:** A relaxation mechanism is introduced for selected lower-body tracking objectives (link position, orientation, joint position) to allow necessary deviations from the reference without penalizing them. This relaxation is conditional on the terrain family and difficulty, preserving upper-body motion intent.
 - **Global Position Correction:** To mitigate accumulated global position error, the planar position error is fed back into the reference velocity command, enabling the robot to correct its global position.
 - **Terrain Curriculum and Reference Sampling:** Training is conducted on a curriculum across diverse terrains (flat, slopes, stairs, boxes, random rough terrain) with increasing difficulty levels. Environments advance upon success and regress upon failure. A coarse behavior-terrain compatibility rule filters clearly infeasible motion-terrain combinations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PGMT was conducted through extensive simulations and zero-shot real-world deployments.

**Simulation-Based Comparisons:**
- **Performance on Diverse Terrains:** PGMT-Ours achieved a completion rate of 87.81% over the full benchmark and 83.33% at the highest difficulty level (L9) across various terrains. This performance significantly surpassed flat-only baselines: PGMT-Pretrain (40.02% overall, 35.31% at L9), RGMT-Reimpl (46.68% overall, 40.52% at L9), and SONIC v1.1 External (25.09% overall, 20.73% at L9). It also outperformed the paper-reported Perceptive BFM (55.1%). This indicates that a general motion prior alone is insufficient for complex terrain, and terrain perception is crucial for robust tracking.
- **Motion and Contact Fidelity:** PGMT-Ours demonstrated competitive error metrics, including Body Position (0.0678m), Body Orientation (18.14°), Joint RMSE (0.2299 rad), and Contact F1 score (0.8217), indicating robust and precise motion execution on varied terrains.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Effectiveness of Terrain-Glimpse Encoder:** Ablations on the terrain perception mechanism revealed that PGMT-Ours, with its motion-conditioned spatial selection (Terrain-Glimpse Encoder), achieved the highest completion rates. PGMT-CNN, which uses a CNN to encode the entire height map, was comparable overall but showed a larger performance gap on L9 stairs, where PGMT-Ours achieved 78.12% compared to 64.58–69.79% for ablated variants. This suggests that dynamically selected glimpses are particularly beneficial for challenging terrains and complex motions.
- **Role of Multi-Head Critic:** A comparison between the multi-head split-return critic and a single-head aggregate-return critic demonstrated that while both learned the terrain-agnostic prior in Stage 1, only the split-return critic made sustained progress during Stage 2 (Perception Injection). The aggregate-return critic stagnated, indicating that decoupling reward components through a multi-head structure is essential for effective integration of terrain perception and complex learning objectives.

**Real-World Evaluation (Zero-shot Deployment on Unitree G1):**
- **Terrain-Adaptive Locomotion:** The PGMT policy successfully traversed diverse real-world environments, including uneven ground, unseen staircases, and obstacles up to 37 cm high. It also demonstrated stable locomotion on irregular outdoor terrains (grass, dense vegetation) and dynamic behaviors like jogging and sprinting on stairs, adapting foot placement, swing clearance, and body posture in real-time.
- **Teleoperation Modes:** PGMT supported two teleoperation modes:
 - **Whole-body teleoperation:** The robot executed diverse operator-commanded behaviors (e.g., punching, squatting, lying down, recovery) derived from PICO motion capture, adapting to the environment.
 - **Locomotion teleoperation:** Operators could control both locomotion and upper-body movements. The robot autonomously adapted lower-body motions to stairs while the upper body was teleoperated, relieving the operator from explicit terrain-dependent planning.
- **General Dynamic Whole-Body Motion Tracking:** The policy maintained the ability to execute highly dynamic whole-body motions, such as cartwheels, on both flat and non-flat terrains. It adapted contacts and posture to stairs and other uneven surfaces while preserving the structure of the commanded motion.
- **Disturbance Robustness and Fall Recovery:** PGMT exhibited robustness against external disturbances (pushes, kicks) during complex motions like dancing. In cases of significant perturbations leading to a fall, the robot autonomously recovered to a stable standing posture without requiring a dedicated recovery controller.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of PGMT marks a significant advancement in humanoid robot control by extending general whole-body motion tracking beyond controlled, flat-ground environments to complex and unstructured terrains. This work addresses a fundamental limitation in current humanoid capabilities, where terrain-agnostic motion references often lead to failure in real-world scenarios.

The primary impact of PGMT lies in its ability to:
- **Enable Robust Terrain Adaptation:** By learning terrain adaptation directly within the tracking policy, PGMT eliminates the need for pre-adapted reference trajectories or extensive paired motion-terrain demonstrations. This approach simplifies the control pipeline and enhances the scalability of humanoid operations in varied environments.
- **Provide a Unified Control Interface:** A single PGMT policy can handle diverse command sources and control modes. This unification encompasses terrain-adaptive locomotion, the execution of complex whole-body behaviors, and various teleoperation interfaces (joystick, whole-body, locomotion teleoperation). This offers a flexible and versatile solution for robot deployment.
- **Ground Terrain-Agnostic Commands:** PGMT functions as a perceptive low-level controller that can interpret high-level, terrain-agnostic motion commands from human operators or higher-level planners and translate them into physically feasible, terrain-aware actions. This abstraction layer is critical for real-world autonomy.
- **Enhance Real-World Deployability:** The demonstrated zero-shot generalization capabilities on the Unitree G1 robot across diverse real-world terrains, combined with robust disturbance rejection and autonomous fall recovery, significantly increases the practical applicability of humanoid robots in dynamic and unpredictable environments.

The potential applications and broader impact of this research include:
- **Enhanced Teleoperation:** Operators can focus on high-level tasks and upper-body control, with the robot autonomously managing lower-body adaptation to terrain, improving efficiency and reducing cognitive load in remote operation.
- **Advanced Mobile Manipulation:** Humanoids can perform manipulation tasks while navigating complex terrain, crucial for logistics, construction, and disaster response.
- **Search and Rescue Missions:** The ability to robustly traverse rough, uneven, and obstacle-rich environments could significantly improve the effectiveness of robots in dangerous or inaccessible areas.
- **General Human-Robot Interaction:** More capable and robust humanoid platforms could lead to new forms of interaction and assistance in human-centric environments.

A noted limitation is the reliance on elevation maps, which encode geometry but not environmental semantics or affordances. Future work incorporating richer perceptual representations could further extend PGMT towards more general humanoid interaction with objects and environments, moving beyond purely terrain-aware motion execution.
