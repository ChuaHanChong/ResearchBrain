# Don't Throw Away the Tail: Action Upcycling for Policy Acceleration

> [!summary] Summary
> Action Upcycling is a training-free add-on for chunked robot policies. Normally a policy executes only the first few actions of each predicted chunk and throws away the rest, called the tail. This method keeps executing tail actions while the predicted velocity stays smooth, using a fluctuation threshold calibrated to a target ratio. It cuts policy calls by 1.2-1.7x without lowering success rate.

## Problem

- A fixed execution horizon forces a trade-off: short prefixes keep the policy reactive but need many expensive forward passes, and the best horizon changes across tasks and within tasks.
- Attention-based adaptive-horizon methods such as AutoHorizon read model internals, so the layers, attention types and denoising steps to inspect must be chosen separately for each architecture.
- Sampling-based adaptive-horizon methods such as AAC and A3 draw several candidate chunks or run extra verification passes, which raises per-call latency and can erase the acceleration benefit.

## Method

- ==Velocity fluctuation== signal: sums the squared change between consecutive predicted velocities past the default horizon, so it stays low during smooth motion and rises once motion becomes erratic.
- ==Adaptive horizon selection==: executes tail actions up to the last step whose accumulated fluctuation stays under threshold tau, with tau=0 giving the default policy and tau=infinity executing whole chunks.
- ==Pool-based threshold calibration==: gathers fluctuation signals from the policy's own rollouts, with no demonstrations or success labels, and picks the smallest tau whose mean execution length reaches the target ratio.

## Results

- On pi0.5, success rises on LIBERO from 96.9 to **97.9%**, on LIBERO-Plus from 83.7 to **86.5%** and on RoboTwin 2.0 from 59.7 to **60.5%**, with **1.4-1.6x** fewer calls.
- It beats AutoHorizon and AAC on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus in both success and call reduction. Combined with FlashVLA it gives a **6.7x** total speed-up, and with 2-step sampling **2.8x**.
- Fixed-length execution with the same mean length loses **0.9-1.3 points** against adaptive execution. On a real arm with pi0.5, success rises from **72/80** to **77/80** and calls fall from 48.3 to 34.0.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Discarded chunk tails correlate strongly with the replanned actions (Pearson 0.89-0.98), so the usual habit of throwing them away wastes compute the model already spent.
> - The action chunk alone can serve as a confidence signal for how far to trust it, with no need for model internals or extra samples, so the method works on any architecture, including world action models.
> - Reducing the number of policy calls is a separate lever from making each call cheaper, so it stacks multiplicatively with distillation, caching and streaming decoders.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot running a π0.5 policy is instructed to open a drawer, pick up a black ball, put it in the drawer, and close the drawer. The policy predicts a sequence of actions, executes only an initial portion, then calls the policy again from a fresh observation. A short executed portion makes a robot more responsive to new observations, but it requires more policy calls. The authors' approach reuses part of the sequence the policy would otherwise discard. It continues through the tail while the predicted motion remains smooth, then replans when a motion-change signal crosses a threshold. They call this Action Upcycling. In one paired episode, a baseline that re-queries on a fixed schedule times out at 60 seconds, while Action Upcycling succeeds in 30.9 seconds.

Baseline, π0.5, episode 08: fixed-clock replanning every 15 ticks. This clip makes 119 policy calls and times out with a failure at 60.0 seconds.

Action Upcycling, same policy, checkpoint, episode, and task: the policy continues through the tail while velocity stays smooth. This clip succeeds in 30.9 seconds after 49 calls.

This is a single episode, not an average result. Action Upcycling is a training-free way to trade fewer policy calls for longer open-loop execution. It does not make each call cheaper. The signal comes from the already-predicted chunk, without model internals or extra samples.

### 2. Reading the tail for smoothness

An action chunk is a predicted sequence. Its length is the prediction horizon $H$. The execution horizon $h$ is the prefix ordinarily executed before replanning, and the remaining sequence is the tail.

The authors' empirical finding is that discarded actions correlate with their replanned versions, and the discrepancy grows as predicted velocity fluctuates. They report Pearson correlations of 0.89–0.98 across four policies. This is evidence for a signal. It is not proof that smoothness is the sole cause of reliability, and it is not a guarantee for every policy and task.

The method uses that relationship in four steps:

1. It derives a velocity from each action. For relative-displacement actions, the velocity is the action itself. For absolute joint positions, it is the difference between consecutive positions.
1. It accumulates the size of changes between consecutive velocities, starting from the first tail action.
1. It reuses tail actions while this accumulated fluctuation stays below threshold $\tau$.
1. It re-queries the policy when the fluctuation crosses the threshold.

The threshold comes from a pool of these signals drawn from the policy's own predicted chunks. The method picks the smallest threshold that reaches a chosen mean execution length. Building the pool needs no additional rollouts, demonstrations, or success labels.

Schematic of the rule. The baseline executes prefix $h$ and discards the remainder of the chunk. Action Upcycling retains part of the tail, giving a longer mean execution length $rh$. Below, a velocity trace is smooth and then fluctuates. Tail actions satisfying $c_{k} \leq \tau$ are kept, and the remainder is discarded.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

The broad evidence comes from simulation. The tests cover three VLAs and FastWAM, a World Action Model, on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin 2.0. Across 11 tested model–benchmark cells, policy calls fall by 1.2–1.7× and reported success rates match or exceed the corresponding baselines.

For π0.5 on LIBERO:

- Reported success rate rises from 96.9% to 97.9%.
- Calls per episode fall from 32.4 to 21.9 (1.48× fewer).
- Inference time per episode falls from 4.41 to 3.00 seconds.
- Per-call latency is essentially unchanged, at 136 versus 137 ms.

The gain could come merely from executing more actions per call, rather than from adapting the length to each chunk. To test this, the paper compares adaptive execution with fixed-length execution set near the same mean execution length on LIBERO. Action Upcycling beats fixed-length execution in success rate for all four policies, by 0.9–1.3 percentage points. This supports the value of adaptive scheduling over a comparable fixed length. It does not isolate the velocity-fluctuation signal as the only possible cause of the difference.

The results also show a trade-off against [[2602.21445|AutoHorizon]], which uses action self-attention as a proxy for the model's predictive limit. On RoboTwin 2.0 with π0.5, AutoHorizon makes 28.0 calls per episode, slightly fewer than Action Upcycling's 29.8. Its success rate, however, is 56.8%, below the 59.7% baseline. Action Upcycling reports 60.5% success and still reduces calls.

### 4. Closing

On a real robot, the authors tested fine-tuned π0.5 on a YAM arm across eight tasks of ten episodes each:

- Success went from 72/80 to 77/80.
- Calls per episode went from 48.3 to 34.0.
- Elapsed time per episode went from 24.9 to 21.5 seconds.

Not every task became faster. One pick-and-place task took 14.4 → 17.4 seconds.

The method does not eliminate the need to reobserve. It uses the predicted chunk's motion changes to decide when to reobserve, retaining smooth tail actions and replanning at fluctuation. Its main boundary is the upcycling ratio. On LIBERO, success eventually declines as the ratio increases beyond the beneficial range, which the authors suggest may be because a larger ratio admits tails with more fluctuation.
