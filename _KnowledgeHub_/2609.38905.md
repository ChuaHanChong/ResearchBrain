# EmbodiRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement for Data-Efficient Robot Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> EmbodiRSI is an agentic real-to-sim-to-real system for recursive self-improvement of manipulation policies. It rebuilds the target workspace in simulation from real images. It then loops policy rollouts, agent-guided recovery from failed states and failure-targeted demonstration collection, retraining a π0.5 policy with RECAP-style advantage conditioning. Over two rounds, simulated success rises from 50.4% to 83.5%. Ten real refinement trajectories per subtask reach 83.1% real success, beating 200 real demonstrations.

## Problem

- Adapting manipulation policies to new deployment environments takes a lot of data, and fixed training sets miss failure modes that shift as the policy improves.
- Real2Sim2Real systems mostly use reconstructed workspaces only to produce a seed dataset, not as persistent environments for repeated evaluation, failure diagnosis and targeted data generation.
- DAgger-style aggregation and intervention methods turn execution errors into supervision but do not decide which configurations need new expert demonstrations, and they often rely on costly human intervention.

## Method

- ==Agent-orchestrated scene construction==: SAM 3 masks, SAM 3D meshes and ==Agent-Guided Adaptive Collision Rectification== build a reusable Isaac Sim workspace, and a ==trajectory agent== validates demonstrations with GraspGen and cuRobo.
- ==Collaborative Error Correction==: an ==agentic rollout monitor== fuses camera images and simulator state to decide continue, recover or complete, then moves grasps to current object poses and replans corrections without resetting.
- **EmbodiRSI**'s ==Adaptive Data Collection== blames each failure on the source-object or target-anchor reset, flags grid cells with above-average failure rates, and mixes a ==hard-case distribution== into configuration sampling for the next demonstrations.

## Results

- Scene-balanced autonomous simulation success rises from **50.4%** at R0 to **70.7%** at R1 and **83.5%** at R2, improving all **14** subtasks; black-pen placement goes from **14.0%** to **76.5%**.
- At a matched **400** simulated trajectories per subtask, adaptive collection beats static collection by **20.6** points in simulation (**79.9** vs **59.3**), **12.9** points zero-shot real, and **14.2** points after refinement.
- R2 plus **ten** real human-in-the-loop trajectories per subtask reaches **83.1%** real success, versus **75.0%** for **200** real demonstrations per subtask; R2 zero-shot already reaches **62.8%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The training-data distribution should evolve with the policy: concentrating collection on its currently diagnosed weak configurations beats uniform collection at equal trajectory counts.
> - A reconstructed digital twin is most valuable kept as a persistent practice workspace for repeated rollout, diagnosis and recovery, not as a one-shot data generator.
> - Caution: there are no component ablations separating recovery from recollection, evaluation covers only scene-specific seen workspaces, and training-time recovery relies on privileged simulator state.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Consider a robot placing a black pen in its holder in a new workspace. The initial policy succeeds only 14.0% of the time in simulation, but succeeds 76.5% after two RSI updates. The approach is easiest to understand from what happens. After the robot tries the task, the system uses what happened to decide how to recover and what to practice next. That system is EmbodiRSI. It practices in a task-specific simulation reconstructed from the target workspace. The current policy's feedback then shapes both recovery during an episode and the demonstrations collected for the next update.

Across three tabletop scenes and 14 subtasks, scene-balanced autonomous simulation success rises from 50.4% at R0 to 83.5% at R2. R0, R1, and R2 use cumulative budgets of 50, 200, and 400 retained simulated training trajectories per subtask. The scene-balanced mean first averages subtasks within each scene, then gives the three scenes equal weight.

Autonomous simulation success across rounds (R0/50, R1/200, R2/400 cumulative retained trajectories per subtask) in Office (S1), Kitchen (S2), and Household (S3). Thin lines show individual subtasks. Larger markers and error bars show each scene's mean ± sample SD across its subtasks. Scene means: S1 28.2%, 49.0%, 74.4%; S2 70.3%, 84.5%, 90.8%; S3 52.7%, 78.5%, 85.4%.

### 2. A failure becomes the next lesson

Feedback works on two timescales.

**Within an episode**, Collaborative Error Correction starts from the intermediate state the policy actually reached. An agent re-grounds the task and plans a correction without resetting. A successful continuation can supply training supervision.

**Across episodes**, Adaptive Data Collection attributes final failures to source-object or target-anchor reset configurations and identifies weak regions. It then biases later expert demonstrations toward those regions while retaining access to the base distribution.

Accumulated rollout-derived episodes and validated new demonstrations update the policy. The next rollout supplies fresh evidence.

The data rules are strict. The monitor uses observations and simulator state during training, but simulator object poses are not learned-policy inputs. A recovery request is not necessarily an executed correction or a successful episode. Unsuccessful monitored rollouts inform collection but are excluded from policy training. Only retained successful episodes count toward the stated training budgets, including episodes completed after correction.

### 3. Feedback versus simply collecting more

The cleanest comparison holds retained data fixed. In S1 (Office) and S3 (Household), both methods use the same initial 50 trajectories per subtask, policy architecture, training recipe, and cumulative budget. Under those conditions, adaptive EmbodiRSI reaches 79.9% scene-balanced autonomous simulation success at 400 trajectories, versus 59.3% for static collection: a 20.6-percentage-point difference.

This result compares the **combined** EmbodiRSI additions against static demonstrations under the original configuration distribution. Those additions are rollout-derived experience plus targeted recollection. The comparison does not isolate the effect of either mechanism. A matched retained-trajectory count is also not a matched acquisition cost. Task-level exceptions remain: adaptive collection is higher on nine of ten S1/S3 tasks at both budgets, and the S3 pencil-case task is slightly higher with Static.

### 4. From simulation to the physical robot

One setting combines 400 adaptive simulated trajectories with ten real-world human-in-the-loop (HIL) refinement trajectories per subtask. Under this setting, autonomous success is 83.1% scene-balanced, versus 75.0% for adaptation using 200 real-world demonstrations per subtask. Before refinement, R2 alone is at 62.8%, so the result describes a workflow of simulation plus limited physical refinement. Both settings are evaluated by the learned policy alone. External agent takeover, planner recovery, and human correction are disabled, while policy-generated reattempts remain allowed.

Real-world autonomous success. R1/R2 indicate 200/400 simulated trajectories per subtask. +10 adds ten real HIL trajectories per subtask. Real-only counts are physical demonstrations per subtask. In the scene-balanced column, R2+10 reaches 83.1% versus 75.0% for Real-only 200. Each subtask-setting uses ten autonomous physical test trials. ± values are sample SD across subtasks sharing one scene policy.

### 5. What it shows, and where it stops

EmbodiRSI's contribution is that adaptation improves when feedback changes the policy and also the next experience supplied to it. The evidence supports data-efficient adaptation in these evaluated scenes. It does not show zero-data deployment or a general claim that simulation can replace physical refinement. The pretrained policy contributes, and HIL refines residual real-world errors. Held-out workspaces and sustained improvement beyond the evaluated rounds remain future evaluations.
