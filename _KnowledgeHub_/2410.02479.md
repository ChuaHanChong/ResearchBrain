# Cross-Embodiment Dexterous Grasping with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Peking University and BAAI researchers developed CrossDex, a reinforcement learning method that enables a single policy to control diverse robotic hands for dexterous grasping. The policy, which uses human-inspired action representations and unified observations, achieves an 80% success rate on trained hands and demonstrates 35.2% zero-shot generalization to unseen robotic hand embodiments.

## Problem

- Existing dexterous grasping policies are typically specialized for individual robotic hands, necessitating extensive retraining and data collection for each new hand.
- The significant structural variations across different multi-fingered robot hands (e.g., number of fingers, DoFs, joint limits) impede the creation of a unified observation and action space.
- Prior cross-embodiment learning efforts primarily focused on morphological variants of the same hand type, struggling to generalize between fundamentally different dexterous hand designs.

## Method

- CrossDex introduces a universal action space based on human hand eigengrasps, which are low-dimensional representations of common human grasping synergies.
- A unified observation space is designed by focusing on key geometric features like fingertip and palm positions, rather than hand-specific joint angles, to promote generalization.
- A multi-task reinforcement learning framework is employed to co-train state-based policies across multiple diverse dexterous hands, subsequently distilled into a single vision-based policy with neural network-based retargeting for efficiency.

## Results

- The vision-based CrossDex policy achieved an 80% grasping success rate across 45 YCB objects when controlling four distinct dexterous hands used during training.
- Demonstrated 35.2% zero-shot grasping success on two previously unseen dexterous hands (LEAP Hand, Inspire Hand), which substantially outperformed all baseline methods.
- Finetuning CrossDex-initialized policies on unseen hands showed enhanced efficiency, achieving 87.2% success for state-based and 64.3% for vision-based policies on YCB objects, and 74.0% on unseen GRAB objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human hand eigengrasps provide an effective, low-dimensional, and universal action representation for controlling diverse robotic dexterous hands.
> - Restricting observations to general geometric features (fingertip/palm positions) and removing embodiment-specific proprioception is critical for achieving zero-shot generalization to unseen hand morphologies.
> - Co-training a single policy across multiple distinct dexterous hands within a parallel simulation environment encourages the acquisition of more generalizable and transferable grasping skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic dexterous grasping has been a long-standing challenge in robotics, crucial for embodied agents to interact skillfully with the physical world. While traditional parallel grippers offer simpler control, multi-fingered dexterous hands provide unparalleled manipulation capabilities, mimicking human hand dexterity. Recent advances, particularly with data-driven methods and deep reinforcement learning (RL), have enabled robots to grasp a wide variety of objects. However, a significant limitation of existing approaches is their specialization: policies are typically learned for *specific* robotic hands (e.g., ShadowHand, LEAP Hand). Developing a new policy for a different hand often requires extensive data collection, simulation, and fine-tuning, posing a major bottleneck to widespread deployment and versatility.

This paper addresses this crucial limitation by venturing into **cross-embodiment learning** for dexterous grasping. Cross-embodiment learning is a rapidly growing area in robotics aiming to leverage shared structural features among diverse robots to acquire generalized skills, enabling policies to transfer across different physical forms. While previous research has explored cross-embodiment policies for robot arms (e.g., for different manipulator kinematic structures), robots with varied morphologies (e.g., using graph neural networks), and even for combined navigation and manipulation tasks, applying this concept to the high-dimensional and structurally diverse space of *dexterous hands* has remained largely unexplored.

Existing efforts like GET-Zero (Patel & Song, 2024) have shown generalization across morphological *variants* of the same hand type (e.g., LEAP Hand variants) but struggle to generalize between entirely different dexterous hands. The core challenge lies in the significant variations in the number of fingers, degrees-of-freedom (DoFs), joint limits, and physical dimensions across different dexterous hands, making it difficult to define a unified observation and action space. Yuan et al.'s work directly tackles this gap by proposing the first universal grasping policy capable of zero-shot generalization to diverse dexterous hands, moving beyond single-robot specific policies and limited intra-type generalization. This research marks a significant step towards creating more adaptable and broadly applicable robotic manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a **cross-embodiment dexterous grasping policy (CrossDex)** that is applicable to a variety of dexterous robotic hands. Specifically, the authors aim to achieve a "universal policy" that can control diverse multi-fingered hands for complex real-world grasping tasks, overcoming the current paradigm of hand-specific policy learning.

The motivations behind this ambitious objective are manifold:
1. **Reduce Development Cost and Time:** Current methods necessitate substantial data collection, costly simulations, and hyperparameter tuning for each new robotic hand. A universal policy would significantly alleviate this burden, accelerating the development and deployment of dexterous manipulation systems.
2. **Enhance Generalization and Adaptability:** By learning from and for multiple embodiments, the policy is expected to acquire more generalized skills, enabling it to transfer to previously unseen hands with minimal or no additional training. This enhances the robustness and versatility of robotic systems.
3. **Draw Inspiration from Human Capabilities:** The authors are inspired by the inherent human ability to teleoperate various robotic hands without extensive prior training or specific knowledge of each robot's mechanics. This human-centric approach suggests that a shared, abstract representation of hand poses could serve as a universal control interface.
4. **Address Unique Challenges of Dexterous Hands:** Dexterous hands present unique challenges for cross-embodiment learning due to their high DoFs and structural heterogeneity (varying finger counts, joint limits, link shapes). Unifying action and observation spaces for such diverse mechanisms is a core technical hurdle this work seeks to overcome.

In essence, the paper seeks to bridge the gap between specialized robotic hand control and a more general, adaptable paradigm, akin to how humans intuitively adapt their motor skills across different tools and embodiments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CrossDex method is built upon novel designs for a universal action space and a unified observation space, coupled with a multi-task reinforcement learning framework.

#### 3.1. Unified Action Space: Eigengrasp Actions

Inspired by human teleoperation, where a single human hand pose can control various robot hands, the authors propose a universal action space based on human hand poses.
- **Human Hand Model:** They utilize the MANO hand model (Romero et al., 2022) to represent human hand poses as a combination of axis angles ($\theta$) and hand shape parameters ($\beta$).
- **Eigengrasps:** To reduce the high dimensionality of human hand poses (45 dimensions when ignoring wrist and shape for simplicity) and capture meaningful, natural hand configurations, they adopt **eigengrasps** (Ciocarlie et al., 2007). Eigengrasps are derived by applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to a dataset of human hand poses (GRAB dataset), yielding low-dimensional eigenvectors that represent common grasping synergies. The policy outputs a k-dimensional vector `w` representing a weighted sum of these eigengrasps, which reconstructs a human hand pose ($\theta$).
- **Retargeting Mapping:** The reconstructed human hand pose ($\theta$) is then converted into specific joint positions for each dexterous robot hand. Initially, an optimization-based retargeting algorithm like DexPilot (Handa et al., 2020) is used, which minimizes the difference in fingertip and palm positions between the human and robot hand while respecting joint limits. Critically, to accelerate the computationally intensive optimization-based retargeting during RL training, the authors train **neural networks (MLPs)** for each dexterous hand to learn this mapping from human hand poses to robot joint positions. This allows for fast, batch-wise computation within parallel simulation environments.
- **Arm Actions:** In addition to hand actions, the policy also outputs target joint positions for the 6-DoF robot arm, which are passed to a PD controller.

#### 3.2. Unified Observation Space

To ensure that the policy can generalize across different hands, the observation space is simplified and unified:
- **Elimination of Hand-Specific Proprioception:** The hand-specific joint positions (`J_h`) are removed from the observation space, as these vary greatly across embodiments and can hinder generalization.
- **Focus on Key Geometric Features:** The observation space for the hand's proprioception is restricted to the 3D positions of the **fingertips and the palm (`x_h`)**. These positions are crucial for grasping, are consistent across different hand morphologies, and are also used in the retargeting process, providing a shared state representation.
- **Object Perception:** For the object, during state-based policy learning, its pose (`b`) and a visual code (`v_ω`) are used. For the final vision-based policy, a **point cloud (`p`)** of the object, captured by cameras, is used, processed by a simplified PointNet backbone.
- **Other Observations:** Arm joint positions (`J_a`) and the previous action (`a_t-1`) are also included.

#### 3.3. Multi-Task Learning and Training Framework

The training process follows a teacher-student framework, common in multi-task RL for complex manipulation:
- **Simulation Environment:** Training is conducted in parallel environments within **IsaacGym**, a high-performance GPU-accelerated physics simulator.
- **Teacher Learning (State-based Policies):** For each object in the YCB dataset, a separate state-based policy (`π_S`) is trained using **PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)**. These policies operate on object pose and simplified hand proprioception. Importantly, these state-based policies are co-trained across *all four training dexterous hands simultaneously* within parallel environments. This co-training encourages the policy to learn generalizable skills from the outset.
- **Student Learning (Vision-based Policy):** All learned state-based teacher policies are then distilled into a single vision-based policy (`π_V`) using **DAgger (Dataset Aggregation)**, an online imitation learning algorithm. The vision-based policy takes point cloud observations of the object. Crucially, during distillation, robot-specific labels are *removed* from the vision-based policy's observations to enhance generalization.
- **Embodiment Randomization:** To further enhance robustness and transferability, Gaussian noise is added to the joint connecting the hand and the arm, simulating variations in hand attachment.
- **Reward Function:** A unified reward function is used across all objects and hands, encouraging the robot to minimize the distance to the object, lift it to a target height, and avoid horizontal displacement, with a bonus for successful grasps.

This comprehensive methodology, combining human-inspired action representations, unified observations, efficient retargeting, and a robust multi-task RL framework, enables CrossDex to tackle the complex problem of cross-embodiment dexterous grasping.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the efficacy of CrossDex across various metrics, showcasing its robust performance, zero-shot generalization, and finetuning efficiency.

#### 4.1. Cross-Embodiment Learning Performance

- **High Success Rates on Training Hands:** The vision-based CrossDex policy achieves an impressive 80% success rate in grasping 45 diverse objects from the YCB dataset across four distinct dexterous hands (ShadowHand, Allegro Hand, Schunk SVH Hand, Ability Hand) used during training. This indicates its capability to effectively control different embodiments with a single, unified policy. Success rates exceeded 60% for 42 out of 45 YCB objects (Figure 5 in Appendix).
- **Superior to Baselines:** CrossDex outperforms all baseline multi-task RL methods, including those using raw action/observation spaces (MT-Raw-OA, MT-Raw-A, MT-Raw-O), particularly in vision-based policies (0.800 for CrossDex vs. 0.782, 0.728, 0.779 for baselines on training hands).
- **Co-training Benefits:** Cross-embodiment training (co-training across all hands) demonstrated slightly enhanced training efficiency and stability compared to training individual policies for each dexterous hand (Figure 3).

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Generalization to Unseen Embodiments

- **Significant Zero-Shot Performance:** A standout finding is CrossDex's strong zero-shot generalization capabilities. It achieves a 35.2% success rate on two previously unseen hands (LEAP Hand and Inspire Hand), which is substantially higher than all baselines (ranging from 14.5% to 21.0%). This highlights the unique transferability of skills learned using the human-inspired unified action and observation spaces.
- **Impact of Unified Spaces:** Baselines that rely on hand-specific joint positions or raw actions perform poorly on unseen hands, often showing state-based policies performing worse than vision-based ones for unseen hands, due to reliance on specific (and unprovided) robot labels in state-based training. CrossDex's design, which discards such embodiment-specific observations in its vision policy, proves crucial for generalization.

#### 4.3. Efficient Embodiment Transfer via Finetuning

- **Enhanced Finetuning Efficiency:** CrossDex provides a superior pre-trained policy for finetuning tasks. When finetuning state-based policies on the unseen LEAP Hand for YCB objects, CrossDex-initialized policies achieve an 87.2% success rate, significantly outperforming learning from scratch (No-Pretrain, 75.8%) and other baselines.
- **Multi-task Finetuning:** For vision-based multi-task finetuning on all YCB objects with the unseen LEAP Hand, CrossDex achieves 64.3% success, again surpassing baselines and significantly outperforming learning from scratch (43.6%).
- **Generalization to Unseen Objects:** When finetuning the vision-based policy for 55 *unseen objects* from the GRAB dataset using the unseen LEAP Hand, CrossDex achieves 74.0% success, demonstrating its adaptability to new objects as well. This indicates that CrossDex serves as a robust foundation for learning new tasks and adapting to new embodiments efficiently.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Retargeting Method Robustness:** CrossDex is robust to the choice of optimization-based retargeting algorithm (Position, Vector, or DexPilot retargeting), with all methods yielding similar training and test performance (Table 3).
- **Eigengrasp Action Space:** Using eigengrasps improves generalization compared to raw MANO axis angles. The method is largely insensitive to the specific number of eigengrasps (k=1 to 36), though k=1 showed slightly decreased performance, suggesting a minimum capacity is needed (Figure 4).
- **Embodiment Randomization and Observations:** Applying randomization to the hand mount position improves generalizability. Crucially, including embodiment-specific information (like one-hot robot labels) in observations harms generalization to unseen hands, reinforcing the value of a unified, embodiment-agnostic observation space (Table 4).

#### 4.5. Real-World Experiments

The paper mentions successful real-world validation using a RealMan RM65 robot arm paired with a LEAP Hand, with video records available on their project page. This provides crucial evidence of the sim-to-real transferability of their learned policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "CROSS-EMBODIMENT DEXTEROUS GRASPING WITH REINFORCEMENT LEARNING" represents a significant step forward in robotic manipulation, particularly for dexterous hands. Its core contributions and potential impacts are:

1. **Towards Universal Robotic Skills:** By proposing a single policy capable of controlling diverse dexterous hands, this research moves beyond the current paradigm of robot-specific skill learning. This is a critical step towards developing truly "universal" robotic skills, enabling robots to adapt to different tools and physical forms seamlessly.
2. **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** The ability to learn a policy once and deploy it across multiple or unseen robot hands dramatically reduces the engineering effort, data collection, and computational resources traditionally required. This democratizes access to advanced dexterous manipulation capabilities for a wider range of robotic platforms and applications.
3. **Enhanced Adaptability and Robustness:** The cross-embodiment training and the unified action/observation spaces foster policies that are more robust to variations in robot morphology and more adaptable to new, unseen hardware. This is vital for real-world deployments where robot fleets may consist of diverse models.
4. **Leveraging Human Intuition:** The inspiration drawn from human teleoperation and the adoption of human hand eigengrasps as a universal action interface is a clever approach that intuitively aligns robot control with natural human motor skills. This highlights the potential of human-centric design principles in complex robotic control.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a strong foundation for future research in cross-embodiment learning for dexterous manipulation. It opens avenues for exploring more complex manipulation tasks (e.g., in-hand reorientation, dynamic handover, functional grasping) with universal policies, as well as investigating an even wider range of dexterous hand embodiments.

**Limitations:** The authors acknowledge that the work has limitations, including training on a relatively small number of dexterous hands (four during training, two for zero-shot testing). Expanding the diversity and number of hands in future training could further improve the generalizability of the policy. Additionally, the current focus is on grasping tasks, and extending CrossDex to more complex, multi-stage manipulation tasks remains an area for future exploration.

Despite these limitations, CrossDex offers a compelling solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics. Its demonstration of high success rates, significant zero-shot generalization, and efficient finetuning capabilities positions it as a landmark contribution towards more versatile, adaptable, and cost-effective dexterous robotic manipulation.
