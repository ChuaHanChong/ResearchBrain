# Robot Tool Design from Scratch via Behavior-Aware Hierarchical Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> HOT designs robot tools from scratch by jointly optimizing discrete structure, continuous shape and action as a bilevel problem. An upper-level Bayesian search over grammar-built structures uses milestone progress from differentiable-simulation action optimization as evidence. A second stage then co-refines shape and action. It finds working tools for four tasks after evaluating under 1% of design spaces of up to 56 million structures, and 3D-printed tools succeed on a real arm.

## Problem

- Tool selection methods choose only among predefined candidates, assuming a suitable tool with the right structure and shape already exists for the task.
- Tool optimization methods refine continuous geometry and actions under a fixed structure template, so the discrete composition of the tool is never actually designed.
- Methods that generate structures by part assembly or vision-language models fix them before physical optimization, so optimized task outcomes never feed back to revise the structure.

## Method

- **HOT** ==bilevel formulation==: an upper level searches discrete tool structures for most milestones achieved, while a lower level optimizes shape and action in the ==DiffRedMax== differentiable simulator.
- **BASS** structural search: grammar-built partial structures are canonicalized into a ==DAG==, and ==Beta posteriors== over milestone stopping probabilities are updated from completed descendants, guided by ==two-step Bayesian lookahead==.
- ==Two-stage approximation==: Stage 1 evaluates candidates with action-only optimization on frozen primitive shapes, and Stage 2 alternates shape perturbation with action re-optimization, accepting only successful lower-loss designs.

## Results

- The first successful tool appears after evaluating only **0.20 to 9.46 per mille** of the design space, for example **11,363** of **55.8 million** structures on ScoopBalls.
- On TorqueBolt, BASS reaches first success at a median of **12,263** evaluations versus **766,123** for random sampling, and two-step lookahead stays effective when calibration shrinks from **9,800** to **980** rollouts.
- Stage 2 refinement lowers task loss on every task, including **46.2%** on an asymmetric scoop and wrench rotation mismatch cut from **11.97** to **6.95 degrees**. 3D-printed tools complete all four real tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When partial designs carry no task value of their own, the outcomes of their completed and optimized descendants can still supply useful Bayesian evidence to guide combinatorial search.
> - Graded milestone progress is a much denser search signal than binary success, letting sparse successes in huge discrete spaces be found with a small evaluation budget.
> - Informative priors and nonmyopic lookahead complement each other: lookahead matters mainly when prior calibration data is scarce, while greedy search suffices once priors are well calibrated.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Robots can use tools, but designing the right tool for a task is harder. The assembly, its geometry, and the action must work together, and a poor design can make the desired physical interaction impossible. Take a robot that must sweep two balls into a dustpan. It needs more than a motion. It also needs a tool assembly and geometry that can make that physical effect possible.

HOT starts from the task's physical outcome rather than from a catalog or a prescribed tool template. It searches over assemblies, tests how they behave, and refines the designs that succeed. The authors report successful execution of all four designed tasks on a physical robot, using 3D-printed tools and actions found in simulation. This is evidence on four specified tasks and a limited construction grammar. It does not show a general ability to invent arbitrary tools.

The authors' SweepBalls physical demonstration, in which the robot sweeps two balls into a dustpan. The optimized tools are 3D-printed and mounted on a 7-DOF RealMan RM75-B arm.

### 2. The loop that makes a tool

HOT's design has three parts:

- **Structure** is how primitive components are assembled.
- **Shape** is the continuous geometry of those components.
- **Action** is the robot's control trajectory.

HOT expresses the task as desired physical outcomes and ordered milestones, rather than as a named tool or a prescribed assembly.

In Stage 1, BASS builds candidate assemblies from a grammar. The grammar combines a cylindrical handle with a searchable library of three differently sized cuboids, subject to construction constraints. BASS tests each complete candidate in simulation, holding the library shapes fixed while it optimizes the action.

A partial assembly cannot be tested on the task by itself. BASS therefore records the milestone progress of tested complete descendants. It shares that evidence across equivalent construction states in a canonical directed acyclic graph. Bayesian lookahead then selects promising partial states for further completions.

In Stage 2, HOT keeps a retained successful structure fixed. It alternates plausible shape changes with action re-optimization.

This is a two-stage approximation, not exhaustive joint optimization of every possible assembly. Stage 1 freezes shape, and Stage 2 does not reopen structural search. Existing robotic design methods often optimize shape and action only after a structure is fixed or generated, which leaves that structure outside the physical feedback loop.

The HOT framework. The left side shows the task scene and desired outcome, along with the asset library of a fixed handle and searchable primitives. The upper path is Stage 1/BASS, where two-query lookahead selects partial states in a canonical structural graph, samples complete assemblies, and passes behavioral feedback back. The lower path is Stage 2, where shape and action are co-refined.

### 3. What the search establishes

The key metric is the **First-Success Index (FSI)**. It counts the terminal structures evaluated from the start of calibration through the first successful structure.

The decisive comparison is on TorqueBolt. Across multiple seeds, BASS's reported median FSI is **12,263** (range 10,658–19,951). Uniform random sampling needs **766,123** (range 426,699–1,202,597). This compares the number of evaluated structures. It does not measure elapsed time, compute, or physical robot trials.

Across the four tasks, BASS's first success occurs after examining 0.20–9.46‰ of the respective terminal spaces. The two largest spaces reach first success at 0.20‰ and 0.34‰. These are first-success results. They do not come from exhaustive search, and they do not show that HOT finds the best possible tool.

Stage 2 then refines the successful designs. The paper reports lower task loss on all four tasks while retaining success. Because actions are re-optimized along with shape, geometric changes alone should not be credited with every improvement.

### 4. From simulation to physical tasks

In HOT, the task's physical outcome guides both how a tool is used and which assembly is tested and then refined. For the physical tests, the authors 3D-printed representative final geometries and mounted them on a 7-DOF RealMan RM75-B arm. They then executed actions replayed from successful simulation rollouts, and all four tasks were completed in the real-world demonstrations.

The main boundary is the construction grammar. It uses a fixed handle and a compact primitive library. The authors identify richer assets and randomized task settings as future work.
