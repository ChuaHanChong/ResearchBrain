# HarnessBandit: Joint Learnability-Transferability Scheduling for Multi-Harness Agentic Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> HarnessBandit is an online scheduler layered on GRPO that picks one execution harness per optimizer step for a shared language-model agent policy. It fuses learnability (mean absolute advantage) with transferability (cosine between a gradient sketch and other harnesses' moving averages), sampling via a UCB-softmax rule with an exploration floor. Training Qwen3.5-2B across six harnesses beats static mixtures on PinchBench and ClawEval.

## Problem

- Policies trained through a single harness fail to transfer: system prompts, tool schemas, and control loops induce different trajectory distributions even when task and reward are fixed.
- Static multi-task mixtures assume a fixed sampling distribution over sources and cannot react to whether an on-policy batch currently yields non-degenerate advantages.
- Curriculum and data-mixing methods tune weights offline or via proxy models, never observing whether one source's gradient aligns with or conflicts with the others.

## Method

- ==Harness-pure rollout batches==: every prompt in a step comes from one harness's independent task pool, making the batch gradient attributable to a single bandit arm.
- Two trainer-native signals: ==learnability== as group-averaged mean absolute advantage, and ==transferability== as cosine between a 65,536-dimensional proportional coordinate subsample of last-four-block gradients and per-harness ==EMA sketches==.
- Fusion and sampling: pooled sliding-window min-max normalization, convex combination at alpha 0.5, EMA value update, a visit-count bonus, ==Boltzmann softmax== at temperature 0.3, plus a 0.15 uniform floor.

## Results

- PinchBench mean **0.506** versus **0.493** for the two-harness OpenClaw-Codex mixture, **0.331** for the six-harness mixture, and **0.330** for the untuned base model.
- ClawEval pass@3 **41.6%** and mean score **0.556** beat both mixtures (**38.5%**/**0.510** and **29.8%**/**0.490**); strict Pass3 **26.7%** matches base, which both mixtures fall below.
- Across **eleven** PinchBench categories HarnessBandit leads **seven**, the two-harness mixture **four**, the six-harness mixture none; final visit counts span **21-32**, a mild reallocation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Execution interface, not task identity, can be the scheduling unit: the same tasks under different scaffolds behave like distinct non-stationary bandit arms.
> - Learnability and transferability are not redundant; a harness can give big advantages yet push directions that conflict with other interfaces.
> - Adding interfaces to a shuffled mixed batch is not monotonically better; six-harness mixing erased the fine-tuning gain, so allocation matters more than coverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Agentic reinforcement learning and interface variation

The paper addresses reinforcement learning for language-model agents that interact with tools and environments. In this setting, a model does not operate directly on a task alone; it acts through a software “harness.” A harness includes the system prompt, tool definitions, control loop, trajectory format, session state, and related bookkeeping. Examples used in the study include OpenClaw, Codex, Gemini CLI, OpenCode, Qwen Code, and Pi.

Prior research has shown that the interface used by an agent can substantially affect its behavior and success rate. Work on software-engineering agents, tool-use models, and general-purpose agent benchmarks has therefore treated scaffolding and agent-computer interfaces as important components of the overall system. The present paper extends this perspective by treating harness choice as a training-time allocation problem.

#### 1.2. Relation to multi-task learning and curriculum design

The problem also relates to multi-task reinforcement learning, data mixture optimization, curriculum learning, and multi-armed bandits. However, the authors distinguish their setting from conventional multi-task learning. The underlying task collection is shared across harnesses, so the training sources are not separate semantic domains. Instead, the same task is presented through different execution interfaces, which induce different trajectory distributions and gradient directions.

Static mixtures assign fixed sampling proportions to the available harnesses. Curriculum methods generally use task difficulty or learning progress to determine what should be sampled next. HarnessBandit instead uses two signals observed directly during on-policy training: whether a harness currently produces a useful learning signal and whether its update is aligned with the updates associated with other harnesses.

The work is also related to gradient-alignment methods for multi-task learning. Unlike gradient surgery or gradient projection approaches, HarnessBandit does not modify gradients after they are computed. It chooses which harness should generate the next batch. This makes it a scheduling layer placed on top of an existing policy-optimization algorithm rather than a new policy objective.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. The training problem

A language model trained with only one harness may learn behaviors that are specific to that interface. For example, it may become adapted to a particular tool-call syntax, prompt structure, planner, or observation format. Training with multiple harnesses can potentially improve robustness, but it introduces a choice about how optimizer steps should be allocated.

A mixed batch containing examples from several harnesses provides no simple way to attribute the resulting gradient to a single interface. It may also combine updates that are useful individually but conflict when applied together. Conversely, a fixed schedule may continue allocating steps to a harness that is currently producing little useful policy-gradient information.

The authors formulate this as a non-stationary multi-armed bandit problem. Each harness is an arm, and the utility of each arm changes as the shared policy evolves. The scheduling objective is not to maximize the immediate reward of the sampled trajectories directly. Instead, it is to select training batches that are likely to produce informative and broadly useful policy updates.

#### 2.2. Learnability

The first proposed signal is **learnability**, defined using the mean absolute advantage generated by a batch. In group-relative policy optimization, multiple trajectories are sampled for the same prompt and compared through their rewards. If all trajectories in a group receive the same reward, their relative advantages are close to zero, and the policy update contains little useful learning information.

A high mean absolute advantage indicates that the trajectories within a group differ in outcome and that the policy optimizer has a nontrivial signal with which to change behavior. The measure is therefore intended to identify harnesses that are currently producing informative batches. It does not necessarily mean that the resulting update is broadly useful; a harness can generate strong but highly interface-specific learning signals.

#### 2.3. Transferability

The second signal is **transferability**. The authors estimate whether a current harness-specific gradient is aligned with the historical gradients associated with the other harnesses. High cosine similarity suggests that the update may improve behavior in directions shared across interfaces. Low or negative similarity suggests possible interface-specific behavior or interference.

The two signals are complementary. Selecting only by learnability could favor a harness with large but idiosyncratic updates. Selecting only by transferability could favor an aligned harness whose current batch has almost no effective advantage signal. HarnessBandit therefore combines normalized versions of both measures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Training setup and harness-pure batches

HarnessBandit is implemented on top of group-relative policy optimization, specifically a Dr.GRPO configuration. At each optimizer step, the scheduler selects one harness. All prompts in that rollout batch are drawn from the selected harness’s task pool, producing a harness-pure batch.

The authors use six harnesses and maintain an independently shuffled copy of the same 2,000 ClawGym tasks for each one. This Cartesian product produces 12,000 task–harness rows. Because every harness uses the same task universe, the design reduces confounding between harness identity and task difficulty.

Pure batches serve two purposes. First, the resulting gradient can be attributed to one harness. Second, that gradient can be compared with historical gradients from the other harnesses. The authors also retain leftover groups produced by sampling filters, avoiding unnecessary loss of scored trajectories.

#### 3.2. Learnability measurement

After rollout and advantage computation, the system calculates the token-masked mean absolute advantage for each trajectory. These values are averaged within prompt groups and then across groups, with equal weighting at the group level. This prevents long trajectories from dominating the signal.

The resulting quantity is the harness’s raw learnability observation. A batch in which all trajectories receive identical rewards produces a value near zero and is treated as uninformative, even if the absolute task rewards are high.

#### 3.3. Gradient sketches for transferability

Full gradient storage is impractical for a 2-billion-parameter model. HarnessBandit therefore constructs a low-dimensional sketch of the current batch gradient. It retains gradients from the last four transformer blocks and excludes embeddings and the output head, which the authors argue may reflect token frequency and task sampling more than harness-specific policy pressure.

A fixed proportional coordinate subsample produces a 65,536-dimensional vector. The sketch is reduced across parallel workers and normalized. For each harness, the system stores an exponential moving average of its unit gradient sketch on the CPU.

For the selected harness, transferability is the mean cosine similarity between the current gradient sketch and the stored exponential moving-average sketches of all other harnesses with available histories. During the initial visit to each harness, transferability is undefined, so the scheduler relies on learnability alone.

#### 3.4. Normalization and score fusion

Learnability and transferability have different numerical scales. The method applies pooled sliding-window min–max normalization over the last 32 observations across all harnesses. Pooling is important because per-harness normalization would remove the cross-harness differences needed for scheduling.

The normalized signals are combined with equal weighting:

\[
S_h = 0.5\widetilde{L}_h + 0.5\widetilde{T}_h.
\]

If only one signal is available, the available signal is used. Each harness maintains an exponential moving-average value with update rate 0.3.

#### 3.5. Exploration and sampling

The scheduler adds a visit-dependent bonus to each harness value and samples through a Boltzmann distribution. The implementation uses a temperature of 0.3, an exploration floor of 0.15, and a bonus coefficient of 0.2. The exploration floor guarantees that every harness retains a minimum probability of selection.

The first 30 optimizer steps use round-robin warmup. This ensures that every harness has been observed before the bandit begins adaptive scheduling. The additional computation consists mainly of collecting and reducing gradient sketches and evaluating a small number of cosine similarities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Benchmark performance

The model is Qwen3.5-2B, trained for 150 optimizer steps on ClawGym using four GPUs. The rollout batch contains eight prompts, with eight trajectories per prompt. The comparison methods use the same optimizer, training data product, hardware, and step budget.

On PinchBench, which contains 147 held-out tasks evaluated through OpenClaw, HarnessBandit obtains a mean score of **0.506**. The two-harness OpenClaw–Codex mixture scores **0.493**, while the six-harness mixed-batch method scores **0.331**, almost identical to the untuned base model’s **0.330**.

On ClawEval, the model is evaluated on 161 held-out tasks through a native loop and interface not included among the six training harnesses. HarnessBandit reaches an average score of **0.556** and a pass@3 rate of **41.6%**. The six-harness mixed method reaches **0.510** and **38.5%**, the base model reaches **0.534** and **36.0%**, and the OpenClaw–Codex mixture reaches **0.490** and **29.8%**.

For the stricter Pass3 metric, which requires all three trials to pass, HarnessBandit achieves **26.7%**, equal to the base model. The six-harness and two-harness mixtures score **21.7%** and **19.9%**, respectively. Thus, HarnessBandit improves average score and pass@3 on the out-of-distribution harness, but the evidence for consistent success across all repeated trials is more limited.

#### 4.2. Category-level results

On PinchBench categories, HarnessBandit has the highest reported score in analysis, integrations, log analysis, productivity, research, skills, and writing. The OpenClaw–Codex mixture performs better in coding, CSV analysis, meeting analysis, and memory. The six-harness mixture does not lead any category and records a zero score in writing for this run.

These results suggest that adaptive scheduling can produce broader gains than a narrow two-harness mixture, although the category pattern is not uniformly favorable.

#### 4.3. Scheduler behavior and signal analysis

The scheduler does not collapse onto one harness. Its final probabilities are approximately 0.204 for OpenClaw, 0.197 for Pi, 0.184 for OpenCode, 0.151 for Gemini CLI, 0.137 for Codex, and 0.127 for Qwen Code. Final visit counts range from 21 to 32, compared with roughly 25 visits per harness under uniform allocation.

The diagnostics indicate that the two signals provide different information. Pi has the highest mean normalized learnability, approximately 0.75, but only moderate transferability. OpenCode scores relatively high on both measures. OpenClaw has higher transferability than learnability, while Qwen Code has higher learnability than transferability. Pairwise gradient cosines fluctuate around zero and sometimes become negative, indicating that no harness is consistently aligned with all others throughout training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main contribution is to identify harness selection as a distinct optimization problem in agentic reinforcement learning. It shows that execution interfaces can be treated as dynamic training sources even when they wrap the same underlying tasks. The proposed scheduler offers a way to allocate limited on-policy training steps without changing the base policy objective or requiring full per-harness gradient storage.

If supported by further experiments, this approach could be useful for training agents intended to operate across multiple software stacks, tool schemas, or control loops. The lightweight gradient-sketch mechanism also provides a practical diagnostic for studying cross-interface interference.

Several limitations remain. The study uses one base model, one task collection, six harnesses, and a relatively short 150-step training run. The reported evidence does not establish how sensitive performance is to the scheduler’s hyperparameters, random seeds, sketch dimension, selected layers, or weighting between learnability and transferability. The experiments also do not provide full ablations isolating each signal. Finally, learnability and gradient alignment are proxy measures rather than direct estimates of held-out task performance. Broader validation across models, environments, training durations, and independent harness sets would be needed to determine the generality of the method.
