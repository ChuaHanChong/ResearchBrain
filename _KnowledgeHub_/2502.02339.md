# AStar: Boosting Multimodal Reasoning with Automated Structured Thinking

> [!summary] Summary
> The AStar framework from Tsinghua University and Chinese Academy of Sciences introduces a training-free, automated structured thinking paradigm to enhance multimodal reasoning in Large Language Models. It achieves state-of-the-art performance, surpassing GPT-4o on benchmarks like MathVerse (53.9% vs. 50.2%) and MathVision (32.7% vs. 30.4%), while reducing data requirements by 520-fold and eliminating training time.

## Problem

- Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) struggle with complex visual reasoning tasks that require sophisticated, 'System 2' problem-solving strategies due to a scarcity of high-quality, long-chain reasoning data.
- Existing explicit search methods (e.g., MCTS) are computationally inefficient due to extensive exploration of solution spaces during inference.
- Post-training methods (e.g., SFT, RL) demand massive datasets and computational resources, often face training instability, and exhibit limited generalization beyond their training distribution, with reliance on proprietary models for data synthesis creating accessibility barriers.

## Method

- AStar implements a ==training-free, inference-time framework== for 'Automated Structured thinking' to boost multimodal reasoning by dynamically leveraging external reasoning guidelines.
- It constructs abstract =='thought cards'==—high-level reasoning patterns derived from only 500 prior samples—using ==Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)== to acquire optimal paths and a VOC-inspired metric to distill these paths.
- During inference, AStar adaptively retrieves the most relevant thought cards for a new query based on problem complexity and semantics, generating candidate solutions that are then verified using ==unified self-consistency checks== and text-domain outcome reward models.

## Results

- AStar (with Qwen2.5-7B) achieved state-of-the-art accuracy, surpassing GPT-4o on MathVerse (**53.9%** vs. **50.2%**) and MathVision (**32.7%** vs. **30.4%**), and consistently outperformed other open-source MLLMs.
- The framework demonstrated extreme efficiency, requiring only **0.5K** prior samples and **50 minutes** for thought card generation, representing a **520-fold** reduction in data compared to search-based methods and eliminating training time.
- AStar showed strong transferability and flexibility, enhancing GPT-4o on benchmarks like MMMU (**73.2%** vs. **70.3%**) and GAOKAO-MM (**52.2%** vs. **47.8%**), and boosting smaller models (e.g., AStar+Qwen2-VL-2B outperformed Qwen2-VL-7B).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Abstract, high-level reasoning patterns, when systematically distilled into 'thought cards,' can effectively guide MLLMs for complex tasks without requiring extensive post-training or large datasets.
> - Integrating explicit external reasoning guidelines with an MLLM's internal implicit knowledge through adaptive retrieval at inference time leads to superior performance and computational efficiency.
> - The training-free nature of AStar, combined with its compact 'thought card' library, enables significant reductions in data and training time while providing robust cross-domain generalization capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated capabilities across various tasks and domains, integrating information from both visual and textual modalities. However, a notable challenge for these models lies in performing complex visual reasoning, which necessitates sophisticated problem-solving strategies beyond direct prediction. This limitation often arises from the scarcity of high-quality, long-chain reasoning data required to implicitly train such capabilities.

Current efforts to enhance MLLM reasoning predominantly fall into two categories:
1. **Explicit Search Methods:** These approaches, such as those employing beam search or Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with specialized reward models, aim to explore potential solution paths. Examples include AR-MCTS and Mulberry. However, they frequently encounter computational inefficiency due to the extensive exploration of large solution spaces.
2. **Post-Training Methods:** This category involves fine-tuning MLLMs to develop structured Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning. Techniques include Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) (e.g., URSA, Math-LLaVA) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) (e.g., PPO, GRPO, LMM-R1, R1-VL, MM-Eureka). These methods typically demand substantial training data and computational resources. SFT-based methods are often reliant on proprietary models like GPT-4o for data synthesis, posing accessibility barriers. RL-based methods can face challenges with exploration depth and training stability, and primarily bias the model's output distribution without inherently introducing external knowledge, which may limit their reasoning capacity compared to the base models.

The AStar framework positions itself as an alternative to these methods by proposing a training-free paradigm. It aims to address the computational inefficiency of search-based methods and the data/resource demands and instability of post-training methods. By introducing "thought cards" derived from abstract reasoning patterns, AStar seeks to provide external explicit guidelines that complement an MLLM's internal implicit reasoning, thereby enhancing performance and efficiency without requiring extensive training or search operations at inference time. This approach contributes to the broader goal of developing more efficient, flexible, and transferable reasoning capabilities for MLLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) in complex visual reasoning tasks by introducing an efficient and adaptable structured thinking paradigm. The authors identify several motivations for pursuing this objective:

1. **Addressing MLLM Limitations in Complex Visual Reasoning:** While MLLMs show proficiency in diverse tasks, they often struggle with problems requiring advanced visual reasoning and sophisticated problem-solving strategies. This gap is particularly evident in tasks that demand "System 2" slow-thinking capabilities, which current MLLMs, primarily reliant on "System 1" fast thinking, do not natively possess to a sufficient degree.

2. **Overcoming Inefficiencies of Existing Reasoning Enhancement Methods:**
 - **Computational Inefficiency of Search-Based Methods:** Existing explicit search methods, such as those utilizing beam search or Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), involve extensive exploration of large solution spaces during inference. This leads to high computational costs and makes them impractical for many real-world applications.
 - **Data and Resource Intensive Nature of Post-Training Methods:** Approaches based on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) or Reinforcement Learning (RL) require substantial quantities of high-quality reasoning data (often hundreds of thousands to millions of samples) and significant computational resources for training. This limits their accessibility, particularly for researchers without access to large-scale proprietary models for data generation, and can lead to training instability in RL methods.

3. **Improving Data Utilization and Accessibility:** The reliance on large datasets for training structured reasoning capabilities is seen as inefficient. The authors are motivated to develop a method that can extract and leverage reasoning patterns from a small number of prior samples, thereby reducing data requirements and democratizing access to advanced reasoning techniques.

4. **Enhancing Flexibility and Transferability:** The aim is to create a plug-and-play solution that can be easily integrated with existing MLLMs and potentially with other post-training techniques, rather than requiring extensive model modifications. Furthermore, the authors seek to develop reasoning patterns that are not domain-specific but rather abstract and transferable, allowing them to benefit a wider range of reasoning tasks, including those outside the domain they were initially derived from.

5. **Integrating Internal and External Reasoning Capabilities:** The research is motivated by the idea of combining an MLLM's implicit knowledge with explicit, structured guidance. This integration is intended to provide a more robust and adaptable reasoning framework than either implicit or explicit guidance alone.

In summary, the key objective is to propose AStar, a training-free, automated structured thinking paradigm that utilizes lightweight "thought cards" to efficiently guide MLLMs through complex multimodal reasoning tasks, addressing the limitations of prior search-based and post-training methods in terms of efficiency, data requirements, and stability, while ensuring flexibility and broad transferability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AStar is proposed as a training-free, Automatic Structured thinking paradigm for multimodal reasoning, consisting of two main steps: Thought Card Construction and Adaptive Reasoning and Verification.

#### 3.1. Visual Reasoning Action Definition

The framework is built upon six defined vision-language reasoning actions, inspired by System 2 (slow, deliberative) thinking in human cognition:
- **Visual Parsing (VP, a₁):** Decomposing visual information into manageable components.
- **System Analysis (SA, a₂):** Analyzing relationships and structures within the parsed visual data.
- **One-Step Thought (OST, a₃):** Performing a single, direct reasoning step.
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT, a₄):** Executing a sequence of interconnected reasoning steps.
- **Divide and Conquer (DC, a₅):** Breaking down complex problems into smaller, solvable sub-problems.
- **Self-Reflection (SR, a₆):** Evaluating and refining generated reasoning steps or solutions.

These actions serve as atomic components for constructing reasoning paths.

#### 3.2. Thought Card Construction

This phase focuses on creating "thought cards," which are lightweight, generalizable templates representing high-level reasoning patterns. This process uses a small set of 500 prior samples and involves two sub-phases:

- **Phase 1: Acquire reasoning paths for seed data.**
 - **MCTS Formulation:** Each multimodal reasoning problem (input question and image) is treated as a tree search problem. The input query `q` is the root node, and subsequent nodes represent reasoning steps (actions and their outcomes) generated by a policy MLLM (π_θ). The state `S_(t-1)` is the trajectory `q, s₁..., s_(t-1)`.
 - **MCTS Operations:**
 1. **Selection:** Utilizes the Upper Confidence Bounds applied to Trees (UCT) formula (`UCT(s) = Q(s) + w * sqrt(ln N(p) / N(s))`) to balance exploration and exploitation, identifying promising nodes for expansion. `Q(s)` is the reward value, `N(s)` is the visit count, `p` is the parent node, and `w` is an exploration weight.
 2. **Expansion:** The selected leaf node is expanded by sampling `n` actions from the policy MLLM, generating `n` child nodes (reasoning outcomes) which are added to the tree.
 3. **Simulation:** From the expanded node, reasoning steps are iteratively sampled and expanded until a terminal state (e.g., maximum depth, final answer) is reached.
 4. **Backpropagation:** Information (visit counts and reward values) is propagated backward up the simulation path. `Q(s)` values are updated using a weighted average (`Q(p) ← (1 - α)Q(p) + αQ(s)`), where `α` is a discount factor. For terminal nodes, the reward value is defined by the likelihood of a self-consistency majority vote, enabling supervision-free generalization.

- **Phase 2: Distill paths into thought cards.**
 - **Optimal Path Selection:** After MCTS, multiple valid reasoning paths are available for each seed question. A Value of Computation (VOC)-inspired metric is used to select the optimal path `p_best`: `Score(q, p_q) = k * R(p_q|q) - (1 - k) * C(p_q)`. Here, `R` is the path's final reward (leaf node's Q-value), `C` is the reasoning cost (number of actions), and `k` balances benefits and costs.
 - **Thought Card Abstraction:** The selected `(question, p_best)` pairs are stored in a repository `D`. These pairs are then distilled into abstract thought cards `C`. Each card encapsulates a high-level thought template (e.g., `a₁ → a₂ → a₄`) and is characterized by two attributes:
 - **Problem Complexity (PC):** Derived from the input image-text pair `(i, t)` using a lightweight 2B MLLM.
 - **Text-Image Semantics (TIS):** A joint semantic representation based on CLIP model encodings of `(i, t)`: `E_q(i, t) = (E_I(i) + E_T(t)) / 2`.
 - Each thought card stores the average PC and TIS for multiple questions sharing the same abstract template.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Reasoning and Verification

During inference for a new multimodal test query `q_t`:
- **Card Matching:** The PC and TIS of `q_t` are computed. Nearest neighbor matching is performed against the constructed thought cards `C` to identify the five most relevant cards. This selection is based on a combined ranking score `R(q_t, c) = R_TIS(c) + R_PC(c)`, where `R_TIS` and `R_PC` are independent rankings based on TIS similarity (cosine similarity) and PC proximity (inverse absolute difference), respectively.
- **Reasoning and Verification:** The selected five thought card templates are instantiated for `q_t` to generate five candidate solutions. To determine the best reasoning trajectory and final answer, a combination of self-consistency checks (across the candidate solutions) and text-domain outcome reward models is employed. This mechanism leverages the diversity of reasoning paths provided by the thought cards and verifies their outputs.

This methodology aims to seamlessly integrate the MLLM's internal reasoning with external, structured guidance from thought cards, providing an adaptive and efficient problem-solving framework without requiring extensive retraining.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across performance enhancement, efficiency, flexibility, and transferability, supported by experiments on various multimodal reasoning benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Performance Enhancement

AStar demonstrates consistent improvements across mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MathVerse, MathVista, and MathVision.
- **Overall Performance:** AStar, using Qwen2.5-7B as its reasoning backbone, achieved 53.9% accuracy on MathVerse, surpassing GPT-4o's 50.2%. It also outperformed large-scale CoT-trained models like URSA-8B (by 8.2%) and RL-trained R1-VL-7B (by 13.9%) on MathVerse. On MathVision, AStar achieved 32.7% overall accuracy, exceeding GPT-4o's 30.4%.
- **Reasoning Type Specificity:** AStar showed strong performance in logical reasoning (LOG, 59.5% on MathVista, outperforming URSA-8B by 37.9%) and statistical reasoning (STA, 69.1% on MathVista).
- **Modality Distribution Robustness:** Performance gains were consistent across different multimodal information distributions, from vision-dominant (VD: 64.4% on MathVerse) to text-dominant (TD: 56.4% on MathVerse), and even in text-only (TO) scenarios, indicating robustness regardless of modality balance.
- **Efficiency with Smaller Models:** AStar demonstrated the ability to boost smaller models significantly. For instance, AStar (Qwen2-VL-2B) surpassed larger baselines like Qwen2-VL-7B and InternVL2-40B on MathVerse, indicating its capacity to enable competitive performance with fewer parameters.

#### 4.2. Efficiency Improvement

AStar shows significant efficiency advantages compared to search-based and post-training methods.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** AStar requires only 0.5K prior samples for thought card generation, a substantial reduction compared to Mulberry (260K) and URSA-8B (1100K).
- **Elimination of Training Time:** The method involves 50 minutes of preprocessing for thought card construction, eliminating the need for any model training or parameter updates, contrasting sharply with URSA-8B's 3-day training requirement.
- **Performance vs. Efficiency:** Despite these efficiency gains, AStar (Qwen2.5-7B) maintained superior performance on MMStar (62.3% vs. Mulberry's 61.3%), MathVerse (53.9% vs. URSA-8B's 45.7%), and MathVision (32.7% vs. MM-Eureka-7B's 26.9%).

#### 4.3. Flexibility

AStar functions as a plug-and-play inference framework, capable of integration with existing post-training methods.
- **Integration with RL-trained Models:** When combined with a PPO-trained LMM-R1 (Qwen2.5-VL-3B), AStar consistently enhanced performance across benchmarks. For example, on MathVerse, the combination achieved 48.3% accuracy, a 6.5% improvement over the base RL model (41.8%). This suggests that AStar captures complementary reasoning patterns not fully developed by post-training.

#### 4.4. Transferability

The thought cards generated from mathematical reasoning tasks exhibit strong transferability to diverse non-mathematical domains.
- **Cross-Domain Generalization:** AStar demonstrated consistent improvements across general reasoning (MMMU), domain-specific scientific reasoning (GAOKAO-MM), visual perception (MMStar, BLINK), and chart understanding (ChartQA).
- **Example Gains:** AStar with Qwen2-VL-2B achieved gains on GAOKAO (44.3% vs. 35.3% SC@5) and MMStar (52.0% vs. 48.8%). Even for GPT-4o, AStar provided enhancements on MMMU (73.2% vs. 70.3%) and GAOKAO-MM (52.2% vs. 47.8%). This indicates that abstract, high-level reasoning patterns are robust across different domains.
- **Weak-to-Strong Generalization:** Reasoning guidance derived from smaller models (Qwen2-VL-7B) was shown to enhance the performance of larger models like GPT-4o, supporting the framework's universal applicability.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study and Analysis

- **Structured Reasoning Patterns:** Replacing thought cards with random action sequences led to a 9.5% performance drop, underscoring the importance of systematically extracted reasoning patterns.
- **Problem-Pattern Matching:** Disabling adaptive card matching (e.g., using random cards) resulted in a 5.7% drop, validating the crucial role of selecting relevant thought cards based on problem characteristics.
- **Verification:** While replacing the verification module with simpler alternatives (e.g., self-consistency alone) showed a modest degradation (1.5%), it indicated that thought cards effectively enable robust solution generation even with basic verification.
- **Seed Dataset Size:** Performance generally improved with increased seed dataset size, from 33.5% (50 samples) to 44.1% (1,000 samples) average accuracy. Notably, competitive accuracy was achieved even with 100 samples, demonstrating practicality in resource-constrained settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AStar framework presents a notable contribution to the field of multimodal reasoning in Large Language Models, with several potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Reasoning Capabilities for MLLMs:** AStar provides a method to integrate external, explicit reasoning guidelines with MLLMs' internal implicit reasoning, thereby improving their ability to tackle complex visual reasoning tasks. The demonstrated performance on benchmarks like MathVerse and MathVision, surpassing models such as GPT-4o, indicates a tangible enhancement in problem-solving accuracy.

2. **Increased Efficiency and Accessibility:** By offering a training-free paradigm that requires only a small number of prior samples (0.5K) and minimal preprocessing time (50 minutes) for thought card construction, AStar significantly reduces the computational and data resource requirements for developing advanced reasoning capabilities. This addresses a major limitation of current search-based and post-training methods, making sophisticated multimodal reasoning more accessible to researchers and practitioners with limited computational budgets and data resources.

3. **Flexible and Complementary Framework:** AStar is designed as a plug-and-play, test-time inference method. Its compatibility with existing post-training techniques (SFT, RL) allows for seamless integration and potential synergistic performance gains, positioning it as a valuable complement rather than a replacement for current MLLM enhancement strategies. This flexibility supports modular development and iterative improvement in MLLM architectures.

4. **Robust Cross-Domain Transferability:** The demonstration that thought cards generated from mathematical reasoning can effectively generalize to diverse non-mathematical tasks (e.g., scientific reasoning, visual perception, chart understanding) highlights the abstract and fundamental nature of the extracted reasoning patterns. This transferability suggests that AStar can serve as a general mechanism for injecting structured thought processes into MLLMs, potentially reducing the need for domain-specific training data in new application areas.

5. **Boosting Smaller Models:** AStar's capacity to enable smaller MLLMs (e.g., 2B and 7B parameter models) to achieve competitive performance against significantly larger and more expensive models (e.g., 40B, 78B, or even GPT-4o) has implications for deploying advanced reasoning models in resource-constrained environments. This could facilitate wider adoption of MLLMs in edge devices or applications where computational overhead is a critical factor.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** The success of "thought cards" as a lightweight library of high-level reasoning patterns provides a new direction for research into explicit knowledge injection and structured thinking for AI systems. It encourages further exploration of how abstract cognitive patterns can be systematically derived, stored, and adaptively applied to enhance AI's reasoning faculties.

In conclusion, AStar offers an efficient, adaptable, and generalizable approach to improving multimodal reasoning. Its ability to achieve high performance with reduced resource demands and its broad transferability positions it as a significant advancement with potential to impact the development and deployment of more capable and accessible MLLMs.
