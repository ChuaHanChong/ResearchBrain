# LTLDiff: Finite Linear Temporal Logic-Guided Data Generation and Diffusion Policies for Multi-agent Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> LTLDiff conditions multi-agent manipulation on finite linear temporal logic. An LLM writes an LTLf formula per task from natural language; the formula is parsed to an abstract syntax tree and recursively encoded as a 128-dim vector that conditions both offline demonstration generation and diffusion policy training. LTLMAG adds per-agent denoising plus regressor-guided joint-trajectory correction, raising RoboFactory success rates over baseline and LTLDOG-R on most tasks.

## Problem

- Rule-based multi-agent controllers guarantee temporal and safety constraints but adapt poorly to novel tasks and changing environments.
- Learning-based multi-agent policies adapt flexibly yet lack any mechanism enforcing temporal ordering, coordination, or safety requirements at execution time.
- Prior constraint-aware data generation applies compositional constraints only during collection, so trained policies still desynchronize and misorder actions during execution.

## Method

- ==LTLf generator==: an LLM samples ten candidate formulas per task from description plus target condition, then re-reads both to select the best candidate.
- ==LTLf encoder==: tokenize and parse the formula into an ==abstract syntax tree==, one-hot leaves, concatenate operator one-hot with averaged child embeddings recursively, zero-pad root to 128 dimensions.
- **LTLMAG**: conditioned diffusion policy plus MLP satisfaction regressor; ==per-agent local denoising== then ==regressor gradient guidance== on the concatenated joint trajectory, linearly scheduled weight around 0.1.

## Results

- Two-agent averages beat baseline at every dataset size: **33** vs **13** at 50 demos, **38** vs **33** at 100, **47** vs **28** at 150 demos.
- Three-agent averages gain **25**, **32** and **16** points over baseline; Lift Barrier reaches **96 percent** and Camera Alignment **73 percent** at best settings.
- Ablation shows conditioning matters more than guidance alone: train-only LTLf averages **44** vs gen-only **38** on two-agent tasks at 150 demos, both below full LTLDiff.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditioning a generative prior on symbolic structure beats relying on inference-time gradient guidance, which is unreliable at high-noise early denoising steps.
> - Coordination can emerge without a joint policy: independent per-agent policies sharing one specification plus joint-trajectory correction scale across agent counts.
> - Logic conditioning does not fix long-horizon pipelines; failures persist where errors compound, suggesting closed-loop replanning rather than richer specifications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-agent robotic manipulation combines several difficult problems: visual perception, low-level motion control, task sequencing, safety, and coordination among agents with potentially different observations and roles. Existing approaches generally fall between two methodological poles.

The first is **rule-based planning and control**, including motion planning and temporal-logic-based methods. These approaches can encode safety and ordering requirements explicitly, such as requiring one action to occur before another or preventing collisions throughout execution. However, they usually depend on manually specified rules, known environmental models, and carefully designed task structures. This can limit their adaptability to new objects, scenes, and manipulation procedures.

The second is **learning-based control**, including behavior cloning, offline reinforcement learning, and diffusion policies. These methods learn flexible action distributions from demonstrations and can handle visual inputs and complex continuous motions. Diffusion policies, in particular, model multimodal action trajectories and have been applied to visuomotor control and planning. Their limitation in multi-agent settings is that temporal and logical requirements may only be represented implicitly in the demonstrations. This can result in desynchronization, incorrect action ordering, collisions, or failures to complete dependencies between agents.

LTLDiff is positioned as a hybrid approach. It uses **Finite Linear Temporal Logic (LTLf)** to represent task-level constraints while retaining diffusion policies for trajectory generation. The paper builds on two closely related systems. RoboFactory incorporates spatial, logical, and temporal constraints into multi-agent demonstration generation, but the resulting policy does not explicitly receive those constraints during execution. LTLDOG applies LTL-guided gradient information to diffusion-based planning, but was designed for single-agent settings. LTLDiff extends these ideas by using LTLf both when generating demonstrations and when training and sampling multi-agent diffusion policies.

The broader contribution is therefore an attempt to connect formal task specification, language-based instruction interpretation, offline demonstration generation, and decentralized multi-agent diffusion control.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to train multi-agent manipulation policies that produce physically feasible trajectories while respecting task-level temporal and coordination requirements. The paper focuses on offline learning, meaning that policy training does not involve additional trial-and-error interaction with the environment.

The motivation is based on a gap between what demonstrations show and what task specifications require. A trajectory may be physically plausible but still violate the intended task structure. For example, a robot might attempt to lift an object before grasping it, two robots might fail to grasp a barrier simultaneously, or a handoff might occur before the receiving agent is ready. These errors are particularly likely when agents act from local views and when task success depends on a sequence of events involving multiple robots.

LTLf is used because it can express finite-horizon requirements involving:

- **Ordering**, such as grasping an object before lifting it.
- **Persistence and safety**, such as requiring an object to remain stable after placement.
- **Conditional dependencies**, such as requiring a target event only after several prerequisite events.
- **Multi-agent coordination**, such as requiring multiple agents to complete actions before a shared object can be moved.

The paper has three stated contributions. First, it introduces an LTLf generator that converts natural-language task descriptions into formulas using a large language model. Second, it proposes LTLMAG, a multi-agent LTLf-guided diffusion planning procedure based on a learned regressor. Third, it evaluates the combined framework on RoboFactory manipulation tasks and compares it with a diffusion baseline and LTLDOG-R, a regressor-guided extension of LTLDOG.

An additional objective is to avoid hard symbolic enforcement during execution. Rather than rejecting every action that violates a rule, LTLDiff uses LTLf as a conditioning signal and a soft gradient-based bias. This is intended to preserve the adaptability of learned policies while encouraging logical consistency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system contains \(N\) robotic agents operating in a shared environment. Each agent receives an egocentric RGB observation, while the overall system may also use a global scene view. A joint trajectory contains the observations and states of all agents over a finite horizon.

Each task is associated with an LTLf formula \(\phi\) defined over atomic propositions. These propositions describe events or states such as “meat grasped,” “barrier reaches target height,” or “agents grasp camera sides.” The offline dataset consists of trajectory–formula pairs:

\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(\tau^{(k)},\phi)\}_{k=1}^{K}.
\]

The target is a conditional diffusion policy \(\pi_\theta(\tau \mid z_\phi)\), where \(z_\phi\) is a fixed-dimensional embedding of the formula.

#### 3.2. LTLf Generation from Natural Language

The process begins with a task description and a target condition written in natural language. The paper uses the Qwen large language model to generate an LTLf specification. Each task is processed ten times, after which Qwen re-examines the candidates and selects the formula judged most appropriate.

The output is formatted as JSON containing the task name, atomic propositions, and the formula. The formulas use temporal operators such as:

- \(F\), meaning “eventually.”
- \(G\), meaning “globally” or “always.”
- \(X\), meaning “at the next step.”
- \(U\), meaning “until.”

Logical operators include conjunction, disjunction, implication, and negation. The supplementary task formulas encode concrete dependencies. For example, the Pick Meat task requires the meat to be grasped before it is lifted and lifted before it reaches the target height. The Lift Barrier task requires both agents to grasp the barrier before it is raised and requires the barrier to remain stable.

This language-to-logic stage provides a structured representation of information that may otherwise remain implicit in the task description.

#### 3.3. AST-Based Formula Encoding

To provide the formula to neural networks, LTLDiff parses it into an abstract syntax tree (AST). Atomic propositions are represented as leaf nodes, while logical and temporal operators are internal nodes. This preserves the hierarchical structure of the formula rather than treating it as an unstructured sequence of tokens.

Leaf nodes are encoded using one-hot vectors. An internal node combines a one-hot representation of its operator with the average of its child embeddings. The embedding of the root node represents the complete formula. This representation is then zero-padded to a fixed dimension of 128.

The stated purpose of the AST encoder is to create a fixed-size vector while retaining compositional structure and temporal relationships. The resulting embedding is used during both offline data generation and diffusion policy learning.

#### 3.4. LTLf-Guided Offline Data Generation

Demonstrations are generated using a motion planner and task-specific motion primitives. The LTLf formula divides the task into semantically valid phases based on atomic propositions and temporal structure. For example, a manipulation task may be divided into grasping, lifting, positioning, and placement phases.

The formula embedding acts as a conditioning bias on motion-planning parameters, including velocity, acceleration smoothness, and object position. The presence of particular propositions determines which parameters can be modulated, while embedding values influence the magnitude and direction of the adjustment. The intended result is a set of demonstrations that are not only physically executable but also consistent with the temporal and coordination structure of the task.

This differs from generating demonstrations solely according to local feasibility. In the latter case, the dataset may contain trajectories that are individually plausible but globally inconsistent with the required action order.

#### 3.5. LTLMAG Multi-Agent Diffusion Guidance

LTLMAG extends regressor-guided diffusion planning to multiple agents. At inference time, the diffusion policy begins with Gaussian noise and performs iterative reverse denoising. Each agent locally updates its trajectory using its own observations and the shared LTLf embedding.

At every diffusion step, the individual trajectories are concatenated to form a joint trajectory. A learned regressor then predicts a correction intended to move the joint trajectory toward higher LTLf satisfaction. The guidance is applied after local denoising, allowing the regressor to account for cross-agent dependencies.

The regressor is trained using a mean-squared error loss against a target satisfaction-gradient direction, together with a penalty on excessively large gradients and an auxiliary binary cross-entropy loss for predicting formula satisfaction when labels are available. The guidance weight is time-dependent. The paper reports a default value of 0.1, with stronger guidance during earlier denoising steps and weaker guidance later.

LTLDiff makes three changes relative to LTLDOG-R. It conditions the diffusion policy itself on LTLf, replaces the original graph convolutional regressor with a feed-forward network, and performs local per-agent denoising followed by joint trajectory guidance. Coordination is therefore achieved without a single joint policy; the agents share the task specification and are coupled through the joint regressor update.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation uses 11 RoboFactory tasks involving one to four agents. Policies are trained independently for each task with 50, 100, or 150 expert demonstrations. The compared methods are a standard diffusion baseline, LTLDOG-R, and LTLDiff.

For **single-agent tasks**, LTLDiff generally performs best on Pick Meat and Strike Cube. With 150 demonstrations, its success rates are 87% for Pick Meat and 75% for Strike Cube, compared with 58% and 40% for the baseline. However, Stack Cube is an exception: LTLDiff achieves 21%, below both the baseline at 44% and LTLDOG-R at 32%. The paper attributes the difficulty to visual manipulation and the dynamics of cube placement, including bouncing or rolling.

For **two-agent tasks**, LTLDiff provides its clearest gains on Lift Barrier and Two Robots Stack Cube. With 150 demonstrations, it achieves 96% on Lift Barrier and 60% on Two Robots Stack Cube, compared with 58% and 20% for the baseline. Place Food reaches 25%, equal to its 50-demo result and only slightly above the baseline. Pass Shoe remains difficult, with LTLDiff scoring 7% at 150 demonstrations, lower than both comparison methods.

Average two-agent success rates for LTLDiff are 33%, 38%, and 47% for 50, 100, and 150 demonstrations, respectively. These are higher than the corresponding baseline averages of 13%, 33%, and 28%, although the table contains task-specific reversals.

For **three-agent tasks**, LTLDiff improves substantially on Camera Alignment. Its success rates are 56%, 73%, and 67% across the three dataset sizes, compared with baseline rates of 7%, 10%, and 19%. The Three Robots Stack Cube task remains difficult, and its performance declines to 7% with 150 demonstrations. Average LTLDiff performance is 33%, 38%, and 37%, compared with baseline averages of 8%, 6%, and 21%.

For **four-agent tasks**, results remain low. LTLDiff reaches 12%, 12%, and 24% on Take Photo, while Long Pipeline Delivery achieves 0% at every dataset size for all methods. The average four-agent success rate is only 6%, 6%, and 12%. These results indicate that the method does not reliably solve long-horizon tasks with multiple sequential handoffs.

The ablation study separates the effects of LTLf-guided data generation from LTLf conditioning during diffusion training. The “Gen” setting uses LTLf during demonstration generation only, while the “Train” setting retains LTLf constraints in diffusion training but not data generation. Both settings generally underperform the full LTLDiff configuration. For example, at 150 demonstrations, the average three-agent success rate is 29% for Gen and 35% for Train, compared with 37% for LTLDiff. The results support the paper’s conclusion that both stages contribute, while policy conditioning is particularly important for complex coordination.

The paper does not report variance across random seeds, confidence intervals, or formal statistical tests. Consequently, the reported improvements should be interpreted as empirical comparisons within the stated benchmark rather than statistically established effects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LTLDiff is significant as a framework for combining formal task semantics with data-driven multi-agent control. Its use of the same LTLf specification during demonstration generation, policy conditioning, and inference creates a consistent interface between natural-language instructions, symbolic planning, and continuous trajectory synthesis.

Potentially, this approach could improve the reliability of multi-robot systems in tasks involving ordered manipulation, shared objects, handoffs, and simultaneous actions. The decentralized execution structure may also support scaling better than a fully joint policy, although the experiments show that scaling to four-agent, long-horizon tasks remains unresolved.

The main limitations are dependence on the correctness of language-model-generated formulas, fixed task specifications, offline distribution shift, and weak performance on long sequences. The paper proposes closed-loop feedback and receding-horizon replanning as future directions. Such mechanisms could allow the system to detect execution errors and update trajectories using current observations rather than relying entirely on open-loop demonstrations. Further evaluation on physical robots, varied environments, formula-generation errors, and larger numbers of agents would also be needed to assess generality and practical robustness.
