# OPRIDE: Offline Preference-based Reinforcement Learning via In-Dataset Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> OPRIDE, an algorithm for offline Preference-based Reinforcement Learning, introduces an in-dataset exploration strategy for efficient query selection and a variance-based discount scheduling mechanism to mitigate reward overoptimization. The framework consistently achieves superior performance with significantly fewer human queries on Meta-World, Antmaze, and Atari benchmarks compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Designing appropriate reward functions in real-world reinforcement learning is challenging, leading to reliance on Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL).
- Offline PbRL struggles with inefficient exploration during query selection, often focusing on entire state-action spaces rather than policy-relevant regions.
- Reward functions learned from binary and sparse human preference feedback are prone to overoptimization, which can result in the extraction of suboptimal policies.

## Method

- **OPRIDE** employs a principled ==in-dataset exploration== strategy that maximizes information gain about the optimal policy by selecting trajectory pairs with the greatest disagreement among an ensemble's value functions.
- A variance-based discount scheduling mechanism is introduced to mitigate reward overoptimization, dynamically adjusting the discount factor to a smaller value in state-action regions with high Q-value uncertainty.
- The framework iteratively trains an ensemble of reward and value networks, collects human preferences for informative queries, and then extracts a robust policy using an offline RL algorithm with the adjusted discount factors.

## Results

- OPRIDE consistently outperformed state-of-the-art offline PbRL baselines across Meta-World and Antmaze tasks, achieving strong performance with significantly fewer human queries, including compelling results with as few as ten queries on Meta-World.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the in-dataset exploration strategy (value difference-based) and the variance-based discount scheduling mechanism are crucial for the observed performance gains and improved robustness.
- The algorithm achieved state-of-the-art results on pixel-based Atari environments using real human preference feedback, demonstrating its robustness to complex visual inputs and inherent noise in human labeling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritizing information gain about the optimal policy through value function differences is more effective for active query selection in offline PbRL than solely refining reward function accuracy.
> - Introducing pessimism into policy extraction via a dynamic, variance-based discount factor effectively regularizes learning and mitigates overoptimization stemming from noisy or sparse preference feedback.
> - Offline querying can be significantly more sample-efficient than online methods, with theoretical analysis showing a `1/(1-γ)` factor improvement in the preference error term.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated efficacy in mastering complex sequential decision-making tasks, from playing games like Go to controlling robotic systems. However, a significant challenge in real-world RL applications is the design of appropriate reward functions, particularly when objectives are difficult to formalize numerically. Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) emerged as an alternative paradigm, enabling agents to learn from human feedback in the form of pairwise preferences, which are generally more interpretable and less demanding to provide than scalar rewards. This approach has shown promise in learning novel behaviors and aligning agents with human intentions.

Despite the advantages of PbRL, a critical bottleneck remains: the acquisition of human feedback is often expensive and time-consuming, limiting its scalability. This challenge is particularly pronounced in offline PbRL, where learning occurs from pre-collected, reward-free datasets without further online interaction. Existing offline PbRL methods typically face two primary issues: inefficient exploration during query selection and overoptimization of the learned reward functions. Inefficient exploration can lead to querying preferences in state-action regions that are not critical for learning the optimal policy. Overoptimization, especially with binary and sparse preference feedback, can result in learned reward functions that inaccurately represent the true objectives, leading to suboptimal policies.

This work positions itself within the landscape of offline PbRL by directly addressing these issues. It proposes a novel algorithm, OPRIDE, to systematically enhance query efficiency by focusing exploration on policy-relevant information rather than solely on reward function accuracy, and by mitigating overoptimization through a dynamic discount factor adjustment. While related work includes other offline PbRL methods that use disagreement-based or information-gain-based query selection (e.g., OPRL, IDRL) and those that employ Transformers for preference modeling (e.g., Preference Transformer), OPRIDE differentiates itself by prioritizing information gain about the *optimal policy* and by introducing a variance-based mechanism to improve policy extraction robustness. Furthermore, the paper acknowledges emerging single-stage PbRL paradigms that bypass explicit reward modeling but commits to a two-stage structure to leverage established offline RL algorithms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance the query efficiency of offline Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL). This overarching goal is driven by specific challenges inherent in the current state of PbRL applications.

The authors pinpoint two primary reasons for the low query efficiency in offline PbRL:
1. **Inefficient Exploration:** Traditional approaches to query selection in PbRL, such as those based on disagreement among reward models or maximizing information gain about the reward function, often focus on refining reward estimates across the entire state-action space. However, this can lead to querying preferences in regions of the state space that are ultimately irrelevant to the optimal policy. The motivation here is that not all accurate reward information contributes equally to identifying the best policy. Human feedback, being a scarce resource, should be utilized to gain the most relevant information for policy optimization, rather than merely improving the fidelity of the reward function everywhere. The authors propose that a more principled exploration strategy should maximize the information gain concerning the *optimal policy* itself.
2. **Overoptimization of Learned Reward Functions:** Reward functions learned from human preferences are susceptible to overoptimization. This issue is exacerbated in PbRL because the feedback is typically binary (e.g., trajectory A is preferred over trajectory B) and sparse. An overoptimized reward function, especially in areas with limited or noisy feedback, can lead to an overestimation of the value function for certain policies. Subsequently, an agent trained with such a distorted reward function may converge to a suboptimal policy, despite seemingly maximizing the learned reward. The motivation is to introduce a regularization mechanism during policy learning that accounts for and mitigates the uncertainty and potential inaccuracies in the learned reward function.

In response to these identified challenges, OPRIDE is motivated by the need for a more robust and sample-efficient offline PbRL framework. Its design directly addresses:
- The inefficient use of human queries by proposing an exploration strategy that intelligently selects trajectory pairs to maximize their informativeness regarding the optimal policy.
- The problem of reward overoptimization by introducing a mechanism that adjusts the learning process based on the confidence (or lack thereof) in the learned reward function's estimates, particularly in uncertain regions.

By tackling these core issues, the research aims to significantly reduce the number of human queries required for offline PbRL to achieve strong performance, thereby making PbRL more practical and scalable for real-world applications where human feedback is a precious resource.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OPRIDE (Offline PbRL via In-Dataset Exploration) is structured as a two-phase algorithm designed to enhance query efficiency. The core components include a principled exploration strategy for query selection and a variance-based discount scheduling mechanism for robust policy extraction.

**Phase 1: Offline Query Selection with In-Dataset Exploration**
This phase focuses on identifying the most informative trajectory pairs from a given unlabeled offline dataset for human annotation. The process is iterative:

1. **Ensemble Reward and Value Learning:** The algorithm begins by training an ensemble of `M` reward networks, denoted as `{r_θ_i}`, using the currently available preference dataset `D_pref`. This training utilizes a cross-entropy loss function (Equation 6) to model human preferences based on the Bradley-Terry model. Following the reward function training, a corresponding ensemble of value functions (`{V_ψ_i}` and `{Q_ϕ_i}`) is trained for each `r_θ_i` using an offline RL algorithm, such as Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), on the unlabeled offline dataset `D`.
2. **Exploration Objective Maximization:** To select the most informative queries, OPRIDE employs an exploration objective (Equation 7) that aims to maximize the difference of value differences between two candidate trajectories (`τ_1`, `τ_2`) from the offline dataset `D`. Specifically, it selects `(τ_1, τ_2)` that maximizes:
 `argmax_(τ_1,τ_2)∈D argmax_(i,j)∈[M] | (V_ψ_i(τ_1) - V_ψ_j(τ_1)) - (V_ψ_i(τ_2) - V_ψ_j(τ_2)) |`
 This objective seeks to identify trajectory pairs where there is the greatest disagreement among the ensemble's value functions regarding their relative desirability. The intuition is that such queries provide maximal information gain about the true optimal policy, effectively narrowing down the uncertainty set of policy values rather than merely refining the reward function in irrelevant regions.
3. **Human Feedback and Dataset Update:** Once the two most informative trajectories (`τ_k,1`, `τ_k,2`) are selected, a human annotator provides a preference label (`o_k`). This new preference is then added to `D_pref`, and the process iterates for a predetermined query budget `K`.

**Theoretical Underpinnings of Exploration (Algorithm 2):**
The paper provides a theoretical analysis of this exploration strategy:
- **Confidence Set for Return Function:** A confidence set `C_k(R)` for the true return function `R` is constructed based on the collected preference data, encompassing return functions close to the maximum likelihood estimator.
- **Pessimistic Value Function:** For each reward function within `C_k(R)`, a pessimistic value function `bq_R` is computed using a Bellman-consistent pessimism approach (Algorithm 3 in Appendix A.1). This helps account for approximation errors and distribution shifts inherent in offline RL.
- **Candidate Policy Set:** A set `Π_k` is formed, comprising policies that are potentially optimal given the current uncertainty in `C_k(R)` and the pessimistic value functions. This constrains the exploration to policies relevant to the current understanding of the optimal behavior.
- **Exploratory Policy Selection:** Within `Π_k`, two policies (`π_k,1`, `π_k,2`) are selected that maximize the disagreement in values between different reward functions from `C_k(R)` (Equation 12). This theoretically formalizes the empirical exploration objective by finding policies that, when compared, provide the most significant reduction in the uncertainty of the optimal policy's value.
- **Theoretical Guarantees:** Theorem 4 presents a suboptimality upper bound for the learned policy, demonstrating that the sample complexity is influenced by both offline data size (`N`) and query budget (`K`). Crucially, the preference error term indicates that offline querying can be significantly more sample-efficient than online methods due to a `1/(1-γ)` factor.

**Phase 2: Policy Extraction with Variance-Based Discount Scheduling**
This phase focuses on robustly extracting a policy from the reward-annotated dataset, specifically addressing the overoptimization issue.

1. **Dataset Annotation:** After `K` queries, an averaged reward function (`b_r = 1/M * sum(r_θ_i)`) derived from the ensemble is used to annotate the entire unlabeled offline dataset `D`, yielding a labeled dataset `b_D`.
2. **Variance-Based Discount Scheduling (Equation 8):** To mitigate overoptimization, a dynamic discount factor `b_γ(s, a)` is introduced. For state-action pairs where the variance across the ensemble's Q-value estimates (`Var{Q_ϕ_i(s, a)}`) is high (specifically, above a top `m%` percentile threshold within a batch), the discount factor is set to a smaller value (`γ_small`). Otherwise, it remains `γ`.
 `b_γ(s, a) = γ_small, if Var{Q_ϕ_i(s, a)} > Top m%(...) ; γ, else`
 This mechanism introduces pessimism in regions of high uncertainty, effectively reducing the impact of potentially overoptimistic reward estimates by diminishing future returns. A smaller discount factor provides more robust, albeit pessimistic, guarantees.
3. **Policy Extraction:** Finally, a standard offline reinforcement learning algorithm (e.g., IQL) is applied to the labeled dataset `b_D` using the adjusted discount factors `b_γ` to train and extract the final policy (`π_ξ`).

**Computational Efficiency:** OPRIDE incorporates practical modifications to reduce computational cost. Instead of training reward functions by traversing the entire dataset, it samples `S` segments per iteration. Furthermore, a multi-head mechanism is used for the reward ensemble, allowing different reward functions to share a common neural network backbone while having distinct final layers. This reduces the computational cost from `N × M × K` to `S × (1 + (M − 1)/L) × K`, where `L` is the number of neural network layers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of OPRIDE was conducted across various locomotion, manipulation, and navigation tasks from the Meta-World and Antmaze benchmarks, and further extended to Mujoco, Kitchen, and pixel-based Atari environments with human-in-the-loop feedback. The results demonstrate OPRIDE's enhanced query efficiency, superior performance, and the effectiveness of its core components.

**Overall Performance and Query Efficiency:**
- **Superior Performance:** OPRIDE consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to state-of-the-art offline PbRL baselines, including OPRL, Preference Transformer (PT), PT with Provable Data Sharing (PT+PDS), and Information Directed Reward Learning (IDRL), across most Meta-World (Table 1) and Antmaze (Table 2) tasks.
- **Reduced Query Requirements:** OPRIDE achieved strong performance with significantly fewer queries. Notably, compelling results were observed with as few as ten queries on Meta-World tasks. Figure 2 illustrates that OPRIDE's performance improves rapidly with initial queries and maintains a lead over baselines across the query spectrum, often achieving good performance where baselines require substantially more queries or show limited improvement.
- **Robustness to Baseline Variations:** OPRIDE also outperformed alternative reward baselines, such as using zero, random, or negative rewards (Survival Instinct), particularly in challenging datasets with limited scripted policy data (Table 4).
- **Comparison with Other Frameworks:** The method demonstrated stronger performance than one-stage preference learning frameworks (IPL, CPL) and sequential ranked list methods (LiRE), indicating the value of OPRIDE's iterative two-stage approach with enhanced query exploration (Table 14).
- **Pixel-based and Human-in-the-Loop Validation:** OPRIDE achieved state-of-the-art results on pixel-based Atari environments with actual human preference feedback, suggesting its robustness to complex visual inputs and inherent noise in human labeling (Table 15).

**Ablation Studies on Component Effectiveness:**
- **In-Dataset Exploration (IDE):** Ablation studies (Table 3) confirmed that OPRIDE's value difference-based exploration strategy significantly outperforms disagreement-based or random query selection. Specifically, comparing "VDS + Disagreement" with "OPRIDE (VDS+IDE)" shows a substantial performance gain, supporting the premise that maximizing information gain about the *optimal policy* (via value function differences) is more effective than focusing solely on the reward function (Table 8).
- **Variance-Based Discount Scheduling (VDS):**
 - The VDS mechanism was shown to be robust in reducing reward overestimation, improving performance over baselines (Table 3).
 - The choice of `γ_small` values (0.7-0.8) demonstrated robustness across tasks (Table 7).
 - The VDS approach (penalizing the discount factor) was found to be more effective than directly penalizing rewards (Table 9).
 - An ablation on the hyperparameter `m` (percentile for variance threshold) indicated a trade-off, suggesting optimal `m` depends on task complexity, with 30% showing strong overall performance (Table 10).
 - A continuous annealing (soft) discount mechanism showed comparable performance to the threshold-based approach, validating the efficacy and simplicity of the chosen method (Table 11).

**Theoretical Guarantees:**
- Theorem 4 provides theoretical backing for OPRIDE's efficiency, stating that its exploration strategy is provably efficient under mild assumptions. The derived suboptimality bound neatly decomposes into terms related to offline data size (`N`) and the number of preference queries (`K`). The factor of `1/(1-γ)` in the preference error term highlights the theoretical benefit of offline querying in terms of sample efficiency compared to online approaches.

**Computational Cost:**
- Practical optimizations, such as segment sampling during reward training and a multi-head ensemble architecture, contributed to OPRIDE's computational efficiency. Empirical results demonstrated that the computational cost does not increase significantly with a larger number of ensembles, showcasing the effectiveness of these modifications (Table 12).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OPRIDE framework makes several contributions to the field of offline Preference-based Reinforcement Learning, with notable significance and potential impact:

1. **Alleviating the Query Efficiency Bottleneck:** The primary impact of OPRIDE is its demonstrated ability to significantly reduce the number of human queries required for effective offline PbRL. This addresses a critical limitation that has historically hampered the practical application and scalability of PbRL methods in real-world scenarios. By enabling robust learning with fewer human interventions, OPRIDE lowers the operational cost and time commitment associated with collecting human feedback, making PbRL a more viable solution for complex problems.

2. **Principled and Efficient Exploration:** OPRIDE introduces a novel and theoretically grounded exploration strategy that shifts the focus of active query selection from merely refining the reward function to directly maximizing information gain about the *optimal policy*. This represents a conceptual advancement in PbRL query design, as it systematically identifies queries that are most pertinent to policy optimization rather than global reward accuracy. The empirical validation confirms this approach leads to more efficient learning outcomes.

3. **Enhanced Robustness to Reward Uncertainty:** The variance-based discount scheduling mechanism provides a practical method to mitigate the effects of reward overoptimization, a common challenge when learning from sparse and binary human preference feedback. By adaptively introducing pessimism in regions of high reward uncertainty, OPRIDE improves the stability and reliability of the learned policies. This contributes to building more robust RL agents that are less susceptible to noise and inaccuracies in human feedback.

4. **Strong Theoretical Foundations:** The provision of theoretical guarantees for the algorithm's efficiency strengthens the scientific understanding of query-efficient PbRL. The suboptimality bounds offer insights into the interplay between offline data, query budget, and learning performance, confirming the benefits of offline querying in a principled manner.

5. **Broad Applicability and Versatility:** OPRIDE's empirical success across a diverse range of challenging tasks—including locomotion, manipulation, navigation, and even pixel-based Atari environments with real human feedback—underscores its versatility. This suggests that the core mechanisms (in-dataset exploration and variance-based discount scheduling) are broadly applicable and can generalize across different domains and state representations, increasing the potential for widespread adoption.

In conclusion, OPRIDE offers a promising and principled approach to make Preference-based Reinforcement Learning more practical and efficient. By addressing the core challenges of query inefficiency and reward overoptimization through its innovative exploration and policy extraction strategies, OPRIDE moves PbRL closer to robust deployment in applications where complex objectives are best specified through human judgment.
