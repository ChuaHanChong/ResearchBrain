# Initiation Safety: A Missing Dimension in Generalist-Robot Safety

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of New South Wales define "initiation authorization" as a new safety layer for generalist robots, proposing a probe-authorize-speak (PAS) system that uses staged nonverbal cues and an adjustable authorization gate to manage when robots initiate social interactions. This system is designed to prevent socially inappropriate first actions and is demonstrated on a physical robot.

## Problem

- Current robot safety frameworks primarily focus on physical and ongoing social-interaction safety, overlooking the critical decision of when to start an interaction.
- Generalist robots often interpret ambiguous social cues (e.g., presence, moderate engagement) as sufficient permission to initiate, leading to potentially intrusive "first hard-to-undo social actions."
- Existing "post-plan guardrails" and alignment methods address safety after a plan or dialogue has begun, failing to prevent harm from ill-timed or unconsented initial approaches.

## Method

- Formalized ==initiation authorization== as a distinct third safety layer, preceding physical and social-interaction safety, focusing on "When to act, whom to address."
- Developed the **PAS (probe–authorize–speak)** system, which uses ==staged nonverbal probes== (e.g., eye contact, body orientation) to gauge receptiveness before authorizing speech or other irreversible social actions.
- Implemented an ==authorization gate== within **PAS**, requiring both a sufficient engagement score and advancement through nonverbal probes, with a tunable "boldness" dial for context-specific adjustment.

## Results

- Successfully formalized "initiation authorization" as a distinct and necessary safety layer, providing a clear conceptual framework.
- Presented the detailed design and implementation of the PAS system on a **PAL Robotics' ARI** humanoid robot, demonstrating its feasibility in separating probing from authorization.
- Illustrated PAS functionality through a logged engagement trace, showing how it structurally delays initiation based on nonverbal cues, even when engagement scores are high, compared to direct-initiation methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Social harm can arise from a robot's "first hard-to-undo social action," even if subsequent interactions are physically safe or well-managed.
> - Ambiguous social cues like a person's presence or a moderate engagement score do not inherently grant authorization for a robot to initiate interaction.
> - A dedicated, context-aware initiation authorization layer is crucial for generalist robots, especially those leveraging foundation models, to prevent inappropriate actions at the outset.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is increasingly focused on developing "generalist robots" capable of performing a wide array of tasks and interacting with humans in diverse, unstructured environments. A central challenge in this domain is ensuring safety, which traditionally encompasses two primary layers: physical safety and social-interaction safety.

Physical safety concerns preventing collisions, limiting contact forces, and respecting joint limits to avoid direct bodily harm to humans and damage to property. Research in this area involves robust motion planning, control systems, and hardware design (e.g.,). Social-interaction safety, on the other hand, addresses the robot's behavior once an interaction has commenced. This includes managing dialogue, maintaining social norms, and recovering from social errors (e.g.,). Related work in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) often focuses on aspects like engagement recognition, proactive interaction, and turn-taking, aiming to improve the quality and fluidity of ongoing interactions (e.g.,). More recently, with the advent of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language-action (VLA) models, there has been a focus on "post-plan guardrails" and "alignment" to prevent harmful or "jailbroken" outputs *after* a plan has been generated or dialogue initiated (e.g.,).

This paper argues that a critical third dimension of safety, termed "initiation authorization," is largely overlooked or conflated with existing social-interaction safety. It posits that current frameworks rarely treat the decision of *whether to start an interaction at all* as a distinct safety layer. Existing approaches often rely on simple thresholds based on engagement scores or direct rollouts of VLA models, treating these signals as implicit permission to act. The authors contend that "seeing a person is not the same as having their consent to be addressed" and that harms can arise from the *first hard-to-undo social action*, even if the subsequent interaction is well-managed or physically safe. This work seeks to fill this gap by proposing and formalizing initiation authorization as a prerequisite safety check that operates *before* any significant social engagement begins, thus complementing and preceding existing safety layers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and establish "initiation authorization" as a distinct and necessary safety layer for generalist robots. The authors contend that this dimension is missing from most current discussions and deployed robot safety stacks.

The motivation for this objective stems from several observations regarding current robot behaviors and existing safety frameworks:

1. **Ambiguity of Social Cues:** Current systems often interpret ambiguous social cues, such as a person's presence or a moderate engagement score, as sufficient permission to initiate an interaction. The paper highlights that an engagement score measures perceived receptiveness but does not inherently grant authorization for the robot to act, especially when signals are unclear (e.g.,).
2. **Harm from First Actions:** The authors argue that significant social harm can occur at the very beginning of an interaction. Examples include a mistimed greeting that interrupts a phone call or conversation (e.g.,), an uninvited grasp by a manipulation VLA (e.g.,), or stepping into a person's personal space without consent. These "first hard-to-undo social actions" can make the robot feel unsafe, intrusive, or awkward, even if subsequent actions are perfectly aligned or physically harmless.
3. **Limitations of Existing Safety Layers:**
 - **Physical Safety** (e.g., collision avoidance, force limits) is essential but does not address social appropriateness of *starting* an interaction. A physically safe robot can still initiate interaction inappropriately.
 - **Social-Interaction Safety** typically governs behavior *after* interaction has begun, including error recovery. It does not provide mechanisms for deciding *whether* to initiate in the first place.
 - **Post-plan Guardrails** (e.g.,) and VLA alignment (e.g.,) are designed to catch unsafe or "jailbroken" plans or dialogue *after* they have been generated. They operate at a later stage and do not prevent the robot from deciding to initiate interaction at an inappropriate moment (e.g.,).
4. **Assumptions of Single-Purpose Robots:** Many safety approaches are built on assumptions suitable for single-purpose robots (e.g., one engagement threshold for all, observable cues are sufficient, speaking too soon and too late are equally bad, one initiation script for all venues). Generalist robots, operating in varied contexts with diverse users, cannot rely on these fixed assumptions. They require tunable and context-aware authorization.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out three specific contributions:
1. **Formalization:** To introduce and define initiation authorization as a distinct safety layer, differentiating it from physical shields, post-plan VLA guardrails, post-dialogue preference alignment, and engagement optimization.
2. **Mechanism Design:** To illustrate a concrete implementation of this concept, called PAS (probe–authorize–speak), on a physical robot.
3. **Evaluation Protocol and Future Work:** To propose an evaluation protocol for comparing different initiation policies and to elaborate on open questions regarding metrics, governance, and the integration of initiation safety with foundation models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes a conceptual framework for initiation authorization and proposes a concrete system, PAS (probe–authorize–speak), to implement this safety layer. It then outlines a protocol for evaluating this system.

**A. Conceptual Framework: Three Safety Layers**
The authors define initiation authorization as the decision of "whether the robot may take its first hard-to-undo social action at all." This is positioned as the third safety layer, distinct from:
1. **Physical Safety:** Protecting against unintended physical contact, collisions, and excessive forces.
2. **Social-Interaction Safety:** Ensuring perceived safety and adherence to social norms during an *ongoing* interaction, including recovery from errors.

Table I in the paper summarizes these layers, with initiation authorization focused on "When to act, whom to address" to prevent issues like "Acting too soon." Table II maps common safety concerns (e.g., venue/context, willingness to interact, social risk preference, timing, permissions) directly to decisions required before the first act. The authors emphasize that initiation is not limited to speech but includes any irreversible first social action, such as grasping an object or stepping into someone's space.

**B. PAS (Probe–Authorize–Speak) System Design**
PAS is designed to introduce small, reversible probes before any hard-to-undo action, incorporate explicit policy settings (bold/conservative), and perform runtime checks before foundation-model dialogue. The system is implemented on a PAL Robotics' ARI humanoid robot and operates in a 10 Hz control loop, following three steps:

1. **Perception:** In each cycle, the robot tracks visible individuals and fuses various cues (gaze, speech activity, approach, dwell) into an "engagement score" (e_t).
2. **Staged Probes:** The robot employs four levels of nonverbal signaling. At each level, the robot's actions are strictly limited to non-speech behaviors:
 - **Level 0:** A person is nearby. Robot stays still.
 - **Level 1:** Brief eye contact from the person. Robot turns its head toward the person.
 - **Level 2:** Sustained attention from the person, who is not walking away. Robot turns its body to face the person.
 - **Level 3:** Person stays nearby for several seconds. Robot keeps facing the person, making it eligible for the authorization gate.
 The probe level can drop if the person looks away, reflecting dynamic assessment of receptiveness. Crucially, the probe module is structurally prevented from emitting any speech.
3. **Authorization Gate:** This gate is the only mechanism that can trigger the robot's first verbal word. It opens only if two conditions are met simultaneously:
 - The engagement score (e_t) is greater than or equal to a threshold (τ(ρ)).
 - The current probe level is 2 or higher.
 The threshold τ(ρ) is a deploy-time adjustable "dial" (ρ) that allows operators to set the robot's initiation policy to be more "bold" or "conservative" based on the venue or user preferences (e.g.,). When the gate opens and the robot speaks its first word, the system logs a "margin" (Δ_init = e_t - τ(ρ)), which quantifies how much the engagement score exceeded the threshold at the moment of initiation. After passing the gate, the dialogue model can then run.

**C. Proposed Evaluation Protocol**
The authors propose a between-subjects user study to evaluate PAS against alternative initiation policies.
- **Study Design:** Each participant would complete one doorway interaction session under one of three conditions, while the robot's perception, scenario, and dialogue stack remain constant.
- **Scenario:** Participants would be presented with scripted roles: being on a phone call, being mid-conversation with another person, or being clearly open to interaction.
- **Conditions:**
 1. **PAS:** The proposed probe–authorize–speak system with a default 'ρ' setting.
 2. **Direct-init:** A baseline policy where the robot speaks as soon as its engagement score (e_t) crosses a fixed threshold (θ), without staged probes or an explicit authorization gate.
 3. **Passive-wait:** The PAS system configured with a conservative 'ρ' setting, requiring higher engagement or longer probing before authorization.
- **Endpoints:** The study would collect both objective and subjective data:
 - **Logged Data:** Probe actions, gate state, current probe stage, and Δ_init at the moment of the robot's first word.
 - **User-Reported Data:** Subjective ratings on 7-point Likert scales regarding perceived success of the interaction, awkwardness experienced, and naturalness of the robot's initiation.
The logged Δ_init would compare PAS with direct-init, while subjective ratings would compare all three policies. The study would also analyze how varying 'ρ' affects initiation frequency and Δ_init.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As the paper is a preprint outlining a novel conceptual framework and a proposed system with an evaluation protocol, it does not present empirical results from a completed user study. Instead, the main findings and results are primarily conceptual, design-oriented, and illustrative:

1. **Formalization of Initiation Authorization:** The paper successfully defines and distinguishes "initiation authorization" as a standalone safety layer necessary for generalist robots. This is a foundational conceptual contribution, filling a identified gap in the existing bipartite safety discourse (physical vs. social-interaction). The authors present a clear taxonomy (Table I) and map common social concerns to this pre-interaction decision stage (Table II).
2. **Design and Implementation of PAS:** The authors present a detailed design and implementation of the PAS (probe–authorize–speak) mechanism on a physical robot (PAL Robotics' ARI humanoid). This demonstrates the feasibility of separating the decision to probe from the decision to authorize speech. The modular design, incorporating perception, staged nonverbal probes, and an explicit authorization gate with a tunable boldness/conservatism dial (ρ), represents a concrete operationalization of the initiation authorization concept.
3. **Illustration of PAS Functionality:** The paper includes a replay of a logged engagement trace (Figure 2) that visually contrasts the behavior of the PAS system with a "direct-init" approach. This illustration serves as a proof-of-concept for the proposed mechanism. Figure 2 demonstrates that:
 - In the "direct-init" scenario, the robot would initiate speech as soon as the engagement score crosses a threshold (e.g., at t≈3s with Δ_init=0.06).
 - In the PAS scenario, even if the engagement score crosses the threshold early, speech is withheld until the robot has successfully moved through its staged nonverbal probes (reaching at least Level 2). This results in a delayed, but potentially more authorized, initiation (e.g., at t≈10s with Δ_init=0.03), showcasing how PAS enforces structural authorization beyond mere engagement signals. The delay is attributed to the "structural authorization" rather than a change in the underlying engagement signal.
4. **Identification of Open Questions and Future Work:** The paper outlines extensions for future work, including applying PAS to multi-party scenes, integrating it with VLA action policies (for reach and approach), and developing an open log format for first-act decisions. Furthermore, it details critical "open questions" across measurement, governance, and the layering with foundation models. These include how to quantify "too soon" errors, who should control robot boldness settings, and how to integrate initiation checks into end-to-end VLA and LLM systems without compromising naturalness or enabling harmful first actions.

In summary, while the paper does not present empirical user study results, it provides substantial conceptual and architectural contributions, alongside an illustrative demonstration of its core mechanism, laying a foundation for future empirical research in robot initiation safety.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds several significant implications and potential impacts for the development and deployment of generalist robots:

1. **Enhanced Robot Safety and Acceptability:** By introducing initiation authorization as a distinct safety layer, the work addresses a previously under-recognized source of harm: socially inappropriate or premature first actions. This directly contributes to making generalist robots more socially competent, less intrusive, and ultimately more acceptable to users in various environments. Preventing awkward interruptions or uninvited approaches can significantly improve user perception and trust.
2. **More Robust and Nuanced Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** The PAS framework promotes a more sophisticated approach to interaction initiation than simple thresholding. By requiring non-verbal probes and explicit authorization based on multiple cues, robots can better gauge a human's true receptiveness and context, leading to more natural, appropriate, and less frustrating interactions. This moves HRI research towards proactive, context-aware initiation.
3. **Improved Governance and Accountability:** The introduction of a tunable "bold/conservative dial" (ρ) for the authorization gate offers operators and deployers a transparent and adjustable mechanism to tailor robot behavior to specific venues (e.g., home, clinic, retail) and user preferences. This provides a clear point of control and accountability that is lacking when initiation logic is embedded opaquely within complex model weights, facilitating safer and more ethically governed robot deployments.
4. **Critical for Generalist and Foundation Model-Enabled Robots:** The rise of end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g.,) and LLM-controlled robots magnifies the need for initiation authorization. These models can combine perception, decision-making, and action generation into a single rollout, potentially leading to immediate and irreversible social actions without an intervening safety check. This work highlights that "jailbreaking" can occur not just in harmful replies but in harmful *initial* moves (e.g.,), making a pre-action authorization layer vital for the safe integration of advanced AI into physical robots.
5. **New Avenues for Research and Standardization:** The paper's explicit articulation of open questions regarding measurement, governance, and integration with foundation models is a significant contribution. It establishes a research agenda for quantifying initiation safety, developing benchmarks, and exploring ethical considerations for robot autonomy in social contexts. This could lead to the development of new metrics (e.g., Δ_init, user-reported awkwardness specific to initiation) and potentially new industry standards or regulatory guidelines for robot initiation behaviors.
6. **Complementary, Not Replacement, Safety:** The work emphasizes that initiation authorization complements existing physical and social-interaction safety layers rather than replacing them. This holistic view of safety contributes to the development of comprehensive safety stacks, where multiple layers work in conjunction to ensure overall robot trustworthiness and reliability.

In essence, this research identifies a fundamental missing piece in the robot safety puzzle, offering both a conceptual solution and a practical framework that can significantly impact the future design, deployment, and social acceptance of generalist robots.
