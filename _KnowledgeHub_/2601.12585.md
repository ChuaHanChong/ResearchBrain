# Do MLLMs See What We See? Analyzing Visualization Literacy Barriers in AI Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Toronto introduced the first systematic taxonomy of visualization literacy barriers for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), identifying both common and machine-specific failure patterns. The study found that MLLMs struggle particularly with color-intensive, segment-based visualizations like pie charts and stacked bar charts due to errors in value extraction and reasoning logic.

## Problem

- There was a lack of systematic understanding regarding the specific reasons why Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) fail in data visualization interpretation tasks.
- Prior MLLM evaluations on visualization tasks were susceptible to 'data leakage,' where models might retrieve memorized answers rather than demonstrating genuine interpretive abilities.
- A comprehensive framework for MLLM visualization literacy barriers, similar to those established for human cognition, was absent.

## Method

- A ==barrier-centric, open-coding methodology==, inspired by human visualization literacy research, was applied to analyze MLLM failures systematically.
- Four state-of-the-art MLLMs (GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4, Gemini 2.5, Qwen3-VL) were evaluated using the ==reVLAT benchmark==, which features synthetic data to mitigate data leakage concerns.
- The evaluation process was augmented to collect detailed reasoning rationales and proxy ==points of focus== (x,y coordinates) from MLLMs to enable qualitative analysis of their decision-making processes.

## Results

- Overall accuracy varied among models: Qwen achieved **86.42%**, followed by Claude (**74.72%**), Gemini (**73.21%**), and GPT (**49.06%**).
- MLLMs consistently performed poorly on 'color-intensive, segment-based visualizations' such as pie charts (Claude: **46.70%** accuracy) and stacked bar charts (Claude: **72.00%** accuracy), indicating specific vulnerabilities.
- The extended taxonomy categorizes MLLM failures into four classes: Translation Barriers, Visual Perception Barriers, and the machine-specific Visual Reasoning Barriers and Coherence Barriers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The study developed the first systematic taxonomy of MLLM visualization literacy barriers, extending human-centric frameworks by identifying novel, machine-specific failure modes.
> - MLLMs exhibit unique 'Visual Reasoning Barriers' (e.g., incorrect comparison, perceptual–logic mismatch) and 'Coherence Barriers' (e.g., self-consistency issues, sensitivity to answer choice ordering) that distinguish their interpretive challenges from human cognition.
> - The findings provide actionable insights for MLLM developers to enhance model architectures and training objectives, particularly for improving visual reasoning and internal consistency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The present work sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: data visualization, human-computer interaction, and artificial intelligence, particularly concerning Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs).

**The Role of Data Visualization and Visualization Literacy:** Data visualization is fundamental to modern data analysis and communication, converting complex datasets into interpretable insights. Visualization literacy, defined as the ability to read, interpret, and reason about visual data representations, has been extensively studied in human populations, with standardized assessments like the Visualization Literacy Assessment Test (VLAT) and frameworks for analyzing human cognitive barriers. This established body of knowledge forms the conceptual bedrock for evaluating any new "reader" of visualizations.

**Emergence of MLLMs as New "Readers":** The rapid advancement of MLLMs (e.g., GPT, Claude, Gemini) capable of processing both text and image inputs has introduced a new category of agents that can interpret visualizations. These systems are increasingly deployed as assistants in various contexts—professional, educational, and analytical—to extract data, synthesize information, and interpret complex visualizations. This emerging role necessitates a thorough understanding of their capabilities and limitations.

**Prior MLLM Evaluation and Identified Gaps:** Initial research has explored MLLM capabilities in basic visual tasks, showing competitive yet imperfect performance. These studies primarily focused on quantitative metrics like accuracy. For instance, early LLMs showed reasonable performance on Chart QA when underlying data was provided but suffered significantly without it. More recent MLLMs achieved near-human performance on VLAT, raising concerns about "data leakage" due to the high likelihood of open datasets like VLAT being included in MLLM training corpora. To address this, reVLAT was introduced, regenerating VLAT visualizations with synthetic data to test generalization. Findings from reVLAT indicated a significant drop in MLLM accuracy, suggesting potential flaws in their fundamental understanding rather than just memory recall.

While these prior works established that MLLMs *can* interpret visualizations and noted some failure patterns (e.g., value retrieval errors, struggles with complex chart types, memory/bias issues), they largely lacked a *systematic and comprehensive framework* for understanding *why* these failures occur. Unlike the robust taxonomies developed for human visualization literacy barriers, an equivalent framework for MLLMs was missing. This gap is critical because MLLMs' internal mechanisms differ fundamentally from human cognition, potentially leading to distinct failure modes.

**This Work's Contribution to Filling the Gap:** This paper directly addresses the aforementioned gap by shifting the focus from mere accuracy measurements to a deep, qualitative analysis of MLLM reasoning rationales. By systematically open-coding erroneous responses from state-of-the-art MLLMs, it develops the *first systematic taxonomy of visualization literacy barriers for MLLMs*. This taxonomy not only identifies errors akin to human cognitive barriers but, crucially, also reveals novel, machine-specific failure patterns. By providing a structured understanding of MLLM failures, this research moves beyond superficial performance metrics to uncover the underlying reasons for MLLM limitations, paving the way for more robust and trustworthy AI-driven visualization assistants.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) in interpreting data visualizations, juxtaposed with a significant lack of understanding regarding the *reasons behind their failures*. As MLLMs become integral assistants in data analysis and communication, their reliability and trustworthiness are paramount. Uncovering the "why" behind their imperfections is therefore critical for enhancing human-machine collaboration.

Specifically, the authors were motivated by the following:
- **The need for systematic understanding:** While preliminary studies noted MLLMs' competitive yet imperfect performance in visualization tasks, and identified isolated failure patterns, no comprehensive framework akin to human visualization literacy barriers existed for MLLMs.
- **Comparison to human cognition:** A fundamental question arises: do MLLMs encounter similar interpretive barriers as humans, or do they exhibit distinct failure patterns due to their fundamentally different perceptual and reasoning mechanisms? Answering this question is key to both understanding MLLMs and potentially designing better systems.
- **Mitigating data leakage concerns:** Prior evaluations of MLLMs on visualization tasks (e.g., VLAT) raised concerns about models simply recalling answers from their training data rather than genuinely interpreting the visualization. The use of reVLAT with synthetic data directly addresses this, ensuring that observed failures are due to genuine interpretive limitations.

Based on these motivations, the study set out to achieve the following key objectives:
1. **Conduct a comprehensive study of MLLM failure cases:** Systematically investigate how and why MLLMs fail in visualization interpretation tasks.
2. **Develop an extended taxonomy of MLLM visualization literacy barriers:** Open-code erroneous MLLM responses to derive a systematic categorization of their failure modes. This taxonomy was intended to build upon and extend existing human visualization literacy frameworks.
3. **Assess overall MLLM visualization literacy:** Provide insights into the capabilities and limitations of state-of-the-art MLLMs on the reVLAT benchmark.
4. **Identify machine-specific failure patterns:** Determine if MLLMs exhibit unique barriers not observed in human participants, beyond those categorizable by existing human-centric frameworks.
5. **Inform future AI system design and evaluation:** Use the derived insights to guide the development of more reliable and effective AI-driven visualization assistants and improve future evaluation protocols.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic, barrier-centric, open-coding methodology, inspired by human visualization literacy research, to analyze MLLM failures.

**Study Design:**
The core methodology adapted the evaluation pipeline from (reVLAT) for MLLM response collection, combined with the qualitative, barrier-centric analysis approach inspired by Nobre et al. for humans.

**Models Evaluated:**
Four state-of-the-art Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) were selected for evaluation to provide a broad insight into contemporary capabilities and failure patterns:
- GPT-5 (OpenAI)
- Claude-Sonnet-4 (Anthropic)
- Gemini 2.5 (Google)
- Qwen3-VL (Qwen)
All models were accessed via the OpenRouter API, capable of taking images as direct input.

**Dataset:**
The study utilized the **reVLAT** benchmark. This dataset is a regenerated version of the original VLAT, featuring 53 questions across 12 common chart types. Crucially, each chart in reVLAT is regenerated with a new set of *synthetic underlying data* using a fixed random seed. This approach mitigates concerns about data leakage, where MLLMs might rely on memorized answers from their training corpora, ensuring that performance reflects genuine interpretive abilities. All visualization images were high-resolution (1233 × 600 pixels), and color palettes and font sizes were standardized.

**Response Augmentation:**
To facilitate qualitative analysis of MLLMs' reasoning, the standard multiple-choice question-answering pipeline was augmented to collect three distinct types of responses:
1. **Multiple-choice selection:** In addition to standard answer choices, an "I’m not sure" option was included, allowing models to explicitly state uncertainty rather than guessing.
2. **Rationale:** Models were prompted to output their reasoning supporting their selected choice, limited to 5 sentences.
3. **Points of Focus:** Models were asked to provide up to five (x,y) coordinates highlighting where they "looked" during the task, along with a brief description for each point. This served as a proxy for visual attention, replacing direct sketching capabilities not yet stable in MLLMs.

Models were minimally prompted to output these three response components in a structured JSON format.

**Pilot Experiments:**
A pilot study was conducted with Claude 4 on a random sample of 20 reVLAT questions. Key insights derived were:
- **MLLM Temperature:** No significant change in accuracy was observed when varying the temperature parameter (T = 0.0, 0.5, 1.0). Therefore, T was set to 0.0 for the full experiment to ensure deterministic and reproducible responses.
- **Choice Ordering:** Pilot results confirmed prior findings that the ordering of answer choices significantly affects MLLM responses (accuracy ranging from 67% to 79%). This necessitated sampling multiple orderings in the full experiment.

**Full Experiment:**
Based on pilot insights, the full experiment involved:
- Generating all possible choice orderings for each of the 53 questions.
- Randomly sampling 5 orderings without replacement for each question.
- Running each question-ordering pair in a separate session to prevent context leakage.
In total, 1060 trials were conducted (53 questions × 5 orderings × 4 models), resulting in 265 trials per model. The computational cost was 3.86 million tokens, costing $11.79 USD.

**Multi-level Analysis:**
The collected responses underwent a multi-level analysis:
1. **Performance Analysis:** Calculation of overall accuracy metrics across different chart types and model families.
2. **Open Coding:** This was the core qualitative step. Following the methodology of, 309 erroneous responses (across all four models) were systematically open-coded. This iterative process involved reviewing reasoning traces and points of focus to identify recurring failure patterns and construct an extended taxonomy of MLLM visualization literacy barriers.
3. **Quantitative Analysis:** The coded barrier distributions were numerically visualized to show their prevalence at the model-level and chart-level, providing insights into specific weaknesses.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded a comprehensive understanding of MLLM visualization literacy, including their overall performance, specific chart-type vulnerabilities, and a novel taxonomy of failure modes.

**Overall Performance and Performance by Chart Type:**
- **Overall Accuracy:** Qwen achieved the highest overall accuracy at 86.42%, followed by Claude (74.72%), Gemini (73.21%), and GPT (49.06%). This shows varying levels of capability among state-of-the-art models.
- **Chart-Type Vulnerabilities:** A consistent pattern emerged across models: performance significantly dropped for "color-intensive, segment-based visualizations" such as pie charts and stacked bar charts. For example, Claude's accuracy on pie charts was 46.70% and on stacked bar charts was 72.00%. Even Qwen, the best overall performer, saw its accuracy decrease to 66.70% on pie charts and 52.00% on stacked bar charts. In contrast, models performed strongly on simpler charts like bar charts, histograms, and line charts.
- **Insights:** The authors hypothesize that the dual dependence on color (for categorical encoding) and magnitude (for quantitative estimation like angle, height, or area) introduces significant complexity. This makes quantitative judgments less stable and prone to error. They also suggest that these specific chart types might appear less frequently in MLLM pretraining corpora, limiting familiarity.

**Extended Taxonomy of MLLM Visualization Literacy Barriers:**
The open-coding of 309 erroneous responses revealed a taxonomy of MLLM failures, categorized into four main classes, two of which are specifically machine-centric, extending prior human-focused frameworks:

1. **Translation Barriers (T):** These conceptual barriers occur during the initial interpretation of the question.
 - **T1: Misunderstands the task:** The MLLM fails to correctly identify the intended visual analytic task (e.g., computing a difference instead of a range).
 - **T2: Misaligns ambiguous terms:** The MLLM misinterprets descriptive or ambiguous terms (e.g., "large," "small") or focuses on irrelevant elements due to unclear instructions.
 *(These are similar to human cognitive barriers.)*

2. **Visual Perception Barriers (V):** These barriers arise when the MLLM struggles to correctly extract low-level visual information.
 - **V1: Misinterprets color:** The MLLM misreads color encodings, especially in gradients or quantized scales (e.g., misinterpreting a legend interval on a choropleth map).
 - **V2: Misreads values:** The MLLM incorrectly extracts numerical values, either directly stating a wrong value or implying an incorrect perception through subsequent reasoning.
 - **V3: Attention misalignment:** The MLLM's "attention focus" lands on an incorrect visual region or element, leading to misidentification or imprecise visual grounding.
 *(These are similar to human encoding-related issues.)*

3. **Visual Reasoning Barriers (R):** These are **machine-specific** barriers where the MLLM accurately perceives elements but fails to construct a correct logical inference.
 - **R1: Incorrect comparison:** The MLLM identifies relevant values but draws an incorrect comparative conclusion (e.g., concluding a higher bar is smaller).
 - **R2: Incorrect reasoning logic:** The MLLM applies a flawed causal or inferential structure, introducing unsupported assumptions or improper logic.
 - **R3: Perceptual–logic mismatch:** The MLLM correctly reads values but derives a contradictory conclusion (e.g., "A=79, B=60" but concludes "A is smaller than B"). This highlights a disconnect between visual decoding and reasoning.
 - **R4: Incomplete reasoning logic:** The MLLM reaches a conclusion without incorporating all relevant visual information or articulating necessary intermediate steps, "jumping to conclusions."

4. **Coherence Barriers (C):** These are also **machine-specific** barriers, reflecting failures to maintain internal consistency or sensitivity to external factors.
 - **C1: Self-consistency issues:** The MLLM produces reasoning that contradicts its final answer, or generates conflicting internal claims (e.g., "A > B" and "B > A").
 - **C2: Ordering affects answer:** The MLLM's response changes based on the sequence of information or answer choices, indicating reliance on positional heuristics or latent biases rather than grounded visual reasoning.

**Barrier Analysis Across Models (Example: Claude):**
For Claude, V2 (Misreads values) accounted for a substantial proportion of errors, particularly in color-rich charts like stacked bar and pie charts. This suggests that when categorical color encodings interact with quantitative magnitude estimation, MLLMs struggle to interpret or estimate numeric values reliably. For visually simpler charts, errors were more evenly distributed across Visual Reasoning and Coherence Barriers.

**Illustrative Example (Claude on Bar Chart Q4):**
When asked "How many countries in Asia have an average Internet speed slower than South Korea?", Claude incorrectly reported 11 countries instead of 8. The reasoning trace revealed:
- **R1 (Incorrect comparison):** Mismapping bar height to slower speeds (e.g., identifying China (16.8 Mbps) as slower than South Korea (~14.5 Mbps)).
- **R4 (Incomplete reasoning logic):** Failing to correctly compare all relevant bars.
- **C1 (Self-consistency issues):** The model stated, "Wait, let me recheck – South Korea actually appears to be around 14.5 Mbps, and counting countries below this level, I count 11 countries with slower speeds," demonstrating instability in its decision process. This example clearly showed that MLLMs can localize chart elements but struggle with accurate comparative reasoning across multiple objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in understanding the capabilities and limitations of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) in interpreting data visualizations. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

**1. A Foundational Taxonomy for MLLM Visualization Literacy:**
The most profound contribution is the development of the *first systematic taxonomy of visualization literacy barriers for MLLMs*. This moves beyond simply reporting accuracy scores to providing a structured, qualitative understanding of *why* MLLMs fail. By extending human-centric frameworks, it establishes a common language for discussing MLLM interpretive challenges, much like how human cognitive biases are categorized in visualization research.

**2. Identification of Machine-Specific Failure Modes:**
The study's explicit identification of "Visual Reasoning Barriers" and "Coherence Barriers" as uniquely machine-specific extends our understanding beyond mere analogies to human errors. These categories highlight intrinsic challenges arising from the generative nature of MLLMs (e.g., self-consistency issues, sensitivity to ordering) and their mechanisms for integrating visual perception with logical inference. This distinction is crucial for developing targeted solutions rather than simply porting human-centric interventions.

**3. Informing Future MLLM Design and Development:**
By pinpointing specific failure patterns—such as struggles with color-intensive, segment-based visualizations, and inconsistencies in comparative reasoning—the findings offer actionable insights for MLLM developers. Future models can be designed with enhanced architectures or training objectives that specifically address these weaknesses, potentially by:
 - Improving how models disentangle categorical color encodings from quantitative magnitude estimations.
 - Developing more robust multi-step reasoning capabilities to prevent perceptual-logic mismatches or incomplete reasoning.
 - Implementing mechanisms to enhance internal consistency and reduce sensitivity to input ordering during the generation process.

**4. Guiding More Robust Evaluation Protocols:**
The research underscores the inadequacy of solely relying on overall accuracy metrics. It advocates for evaluation frameworks that incorporate qualitative analysis of reasoning traces, attention mechanisms, and systematic testing across diverse chart types and presentation variations (e.g., choice orderings). This will lead to more comprehensive and diagnostic evaluations that reveal the *nature* of MLLM understanding, not just its surface-level correctness.

**5. Enhancing Human-AI Collaboration and Trustworthiness:**
Understanding the specific "cognitive limitations" of MLLMs in visualization interpretation is vital for their responsible deployment as analytical assistants. If users know *where and why* an MLLM might falter (e.g., on a pie chart or when comparing nuanced values), they can better calibrate their trust, intervene more effectively, and design collaborative workflows that leverage MLLM strengths while mitigating their weaknesses. This transparency fosters greater trustworthiness and reduces the risk of AI-generated misinformation from visualization misinterpretations.

**6. Future Research Directions:**
The limitations acknowledged (e.g., minimal prompting, current MLLM sketching limitations) and the suggested future work (scaling behavioral sampling, automating coding, targeted experimental interventions) provide a clear roadmap for subsequent research. This study serves as a strong foundation, encouraging further empirical work to causally test the identified barriers and explore more sophisticated prompting strategies or multimodal output capabilities in future MLLMs.

In conclusion, this paper provides novel and fundamental insights into the "cognitive landscape" of MLLMs concerning data visualization. By moving beyond performance metrics to systematically categorize interpretive barriers, it offers a critical resource for advancing the reliability, trustworthiness, and effectiveness of AI systems in analytical tasks. The findings will undoubtedly assist in establishing guidelines for the more reliable and trustworthy use of MLLMs’ visual reasoning capabilities in an increasingly data-driven world.
