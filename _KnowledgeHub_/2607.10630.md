# World Models as Adversaries: Multi-Agent Self-Play Fine-Tuning for Robust Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Hong Kong Polytechnic University and Tongji University developed Adversarial World Modeling (AWM), a multi-agent self-play fine-tuning framework that leverages behavioral world models as adversaries to enhance the robustness of autonomous vehicle motion planners. This method led to improvements in closed-loop planning performance, including a +2.60 gain on the nuPlan Test14-hard NR benchmark and a +5.81 gain on the InterPlan-LongTail benchmark, while maintaining nominal driving capabilities.

## Problem

- Modern autoregressive motion planners exhibit brittleness in rare, safety-critical multi-agent interaction scenarios due to underrepresentation in training data.
- Existing adversarial training frameworks are often incompatible or inefficient for high-dimensional, tokenized generative planners, relying on external or heuristic attackers.
- Optimizing adversarial world models faces challenges such as combinatorial credit assignment ambiguity, instability of min-max optimization, and potential degradation of nominal driving performance.

## Method

- **AWM** formulates robust planner learning as a ==constrained min-max game==, repurposing ==behavioral world models== as structured adversaries within a shared ==autoregressive rollout== structure.
- A decoupled two-stage solver is proposed: Stage A (**Adversarial Coalition Learning**) optimizes a scene-adaptive adversarial policy using ==role-conditioned generation==, ==sparse coalition learning==, and ==counterfactual credit assignment==.
- Stage B (**Constrained Robust Best Response**) fine-tunes the ego planner against the induced adversarial risk distribution, employing regret-aware ==tail-risk (CVaR) optimization== and reference-anchored ==trust region regularization==.

## Results

- AWM-Planner achieved significant improvements in closed-loop planning performance, including **+2.60** on nuPlan Test14-hard NR scenarios and **+5.81** on the InterPlan-LongTail benchmark, while preserving nominal driving scores.
- AWM functions as a transferable adversarial simulator, reducing the closed-loop scores of various planning architectures by an average of **2.78 points**, demonstrating its ability to expose vulnerabilities effectively.
- Algorithmic analysis confirmed that role-conditioned counterfactual credit assignment and adaptive coalition learning produce realistic, strong adversarial behaviors, and the decoupled regret-aware CVaR optimization is crucial for robust planner adaptation against rare, severe risks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Behavioral world models can be effectively repurposed as endogenous adversaries, enabling direct integration of adversarial training with high-dimensional autoregressive planners.
> - Decoupling the min-max optimization into dedicated adversary learning and planner adaptation stages provides stability and improved performance compared to simultaneous updates.
> - Scene-adaptive coalition learning with counterfactual credit assignment enables efficient discovery and realistic generation of sparse, synergistic multi-agent attacks, overcoming combinatorial challenges in multi-agent environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The work addresses the challenge of robust motion planning for autonomous vehicles (AVs), particularly in dense traffic scenarios characterized by rare and safety-critical interactions. The current paradigm in motion planning is shifting from rule-based systems to generative motion modeling, often employing autoregressive discrete architectures inspired by Large Language Models (LLMs). These modern planners demonstrate performance on naturalistic driving data but exhibit brittleness in interactive, long-tail scenarios, such as aggressive cut-ins or coordinated blocking maneuvers by surrounding vehicles.

Existing approaches to addressing these vulnerabilities often involve adversarial training, which seeks to identify and rectify weaknesses by exposing the planner to challenging scenarios. However, current adversarial frameworks face limitations when applied to modern autoregressive planners. Many rely on external adversary agents, heuristic scenario perturbations, or simulator-intensive rollouts. These methods are frequently designed for low-dimensional control policies or handcrafted rules, which restricts their scalability and integration with high-dimensional, tokenized generation paradigms.

This research proposes an alternative by repurposing behavioral world models, which are inherently autoregressive and learn multi-agent traffic evolution, to parameterize both naturalistic and adversarial traffic. This approach aims to integrate adversarial interactions directly into closed-loop training within a unified motion manifold, bypassing the need for external, handcrafted attackers. The work positions itself as a self-play adversarial fine-tuning paradigm that bridges adversarial world modeling with constrained planner adaptation using a shared rollout structure, a departure from methods relying on external simulators.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for learning robust motion planners for autonomous vehicles capable of handling rare but safety-critical interactive scenarios. The authors aim to overcome the limitations of existing adversarial training frameworks, particularly their incompatibility with modern autoregressive generative motion planners.

The motivation for this work stems from three key challenges in developing robust motion planners:

1. **Brittleness in Long-Tail Scenarios:** Modern autoregressive planners, despite their performance on naturalistic data, are vulnerable to rare but critical interactive events (e.g., aggressive cut-ins, coordinated blocking) because these scenarios are underrepresented in training datasets. Adversarial training offers a solution by actively searching for such hard scenarios.
2. **Incompatibility of Existing Adversarial Frameworks:** Current adversarial training methods often rely on external, hand-designed attackers, heuristic perturbations, or simulator-heavy rollouts. These approaches are not well-suited for high-dimensional, tokenized autoregressive planners, making them difficult to scale and integrate. The authors argue for an "endogenous solution" where the environment simulator itself (specifically, behavioral world models) can be repurposed as an adversary.
3. **Optimization Challenges in Adversarial World Modeling:** Translating the concept of using a world model as an adversary into a trainable framework presents significant optimization difficulties:
 - **Combinatorial Credit Assignment Ambiguity:** In multi-agent environments, coordinating synergistic adversaries within a world model to attribute fine-grained marginal contributions of individual agents to an attack is challenging, especially in high-dimensional action spaces. Standard reward-sharing mechanisms may not effectively determine attack initiation, emergence, or coordination.
 - **Instability of Min-Max Optimization:** Simultaneously optimizing two large autoregressive models (planner and adversary) is inherently unstable. A planner attempting to converge against a continually shifting adversarial distribution often leads to gradient divergence.
 - **Degradation of Nominal Driving Performance:** Unconstrained adversarial fine-tuning can lead to planners becoming overly conservative in hard cases, potentially sacrificing proficiency in nominal driving situations without explicit behavioral regularization.

To address these challenges, the research proposes a theoretically grounded multi-agent self-play fine-tuning framework, Adversarial World Modeling (AWM), which formulates robust planner learning as a constrained min-max game.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Adversarial World Modeling (AWM), is a multi-agent self-play fine-tuning framework designed to address robust motion planning by casting it as a constrained min-max game. The framework leverages a shared tokenized autoregressive rollout structure, integrating adversarial interactions directly within the environment dynamics. Recognizing the intractability of solving the exact min-max game, AWM introduces a decoupled two-stage solver:

**Overall Framework: Constrained Min-Max Game Formulation**
The problem is formulated as:
`max_{π_plan} min_{π_awm, {A_s}} E_{s~D} [U_s(π_plan, π_awm, A_s)]`
subject to two constraints:
1. **Attack Budget:** `|A_s| ≤ K_max, A_s ⊆ V_s` (sparse and reasonable attack)
2. **Trust Region:** `E_{x~D} [D_trust (π_plan(· | x) || π_nom_plan(· | x))] ≤ ϵ` (preservation of nominal driving behavior)

The ego planner (outer player) maximizes its utility, while the Adversarial World Model (AWM) `π_awm` (inner player) minimizes the ego planner's utility by optimizing the behavior of a budgeted subset of active adversaries `A_s`.

**Decoupled Solver: Two Principled Stages**

**Stage A: Adversarial Coalition Learning (Inner Minimization)**
In this stage, the ego planner (`π_nom_plan`) is frozen, and the non-ego factors are optimized to learn a scene-adaptive adversarial coalition distribution. The objective is to find `π_awm` and `P_adv` that minimize the ego planner's utility. This involves a sequence of mechanisms:

1. **Role-Conditioned Adversarial Generation:** AWM converts a benign world model (`π_pred`) into an adversary by assigning a latent role (`ρ_i ∈ {bg, atk}`) to each non-ego agent `i`. The role conditioning is injected into both internal agent and decoder representations, altering behavioral logits. This allows for counterfactual evaluations by simply switching roles without changing the underlying network parameters. A background role `ρ_i = bg` recovers the naturalistic behavior of `π_pred`.
2. **Sparse Coalition Learning:** To avoid computationally prohibitive exhaustive subset searches, AWM employs a structured sequence:
 - **Proposal Probing:** A lightweight background probe rollout (all agents as `bg`) extracts heuristic threat signals (proximity, TTC, blocking, cut-in) for each non-ego agent. A penalized signal incorporates kinematic, off-road, and static obstacle violation costs to ensure realism. A sparse proposal set `P_s` is formed by selecting candidates based on their comprehensive threat score and residual threat coverage.
 - **Primary Selection:** A learned primary selector `f_pri` chooses the most impactful primary attacker `i_s*` from `P_s` by fusing neural hidden states with proposal statistics.
 - **Conditional Pairing:** Conditional on `i_s*` acting as an attacker, a pair ranker `f_pair` selects a candidate partner `j_s^` by scoring relational features capturing conditional interaction dynamics.
 - **Scene-Adaptive Calibration:** A binary calibrator `q_η` predicts the probability `p_s` of a positive marginal improvement from adding `j_s^` as an attacker, based on scene-level features. The pair is admitted only if `p_s` exceeds a threshold, leading to scene-adaptive coalition sizes.
3. **Counterfactual Credit Assignment:** To attribute scene-level utility to individual attackers and their joint behavior, AWM uses role-conditioned counterfactual self-play. For an active coalition `A_fact_s`, `leave-one-out` marginal contributions (`∆_loo_i`) are calculated by switching one attacker `i` to `bg`. A `team contribution` (`∆_team_s`) is calculated by switching all attackers in `A_fact_s` to `bg`. A hybrid reward `∆_hyb_i` combines these terms, providing a gradient-aligned signal for training the AWM.

**Stage B: Constrained Robust Best Response (Outer Maximization)**
With the adversarial policy `π_awm*` and calibrator `q_η*` frozen, the ego planner `π_plan` is optimized as a constrained robust best response.

1. **AWM-Induced Adversarial Risk Distribution:** The frozen AWM generates three explicit rollout branches for each scene: a normal branch (using `π_pred`), a top-1 branch (only `i_s*` is `atk`), and a pair branch (both `i_s*` and `j_s^` are `atk`, with probability `p_s`). This creates a discrete two-point risk distribution (`D_θ_s`) over regret values.
2. **Reference-Anchored Trust Region Regularization:** To prevent degradation of nominal driving performance, the planner's update is constrained to a trust region around a nominal reference planner `π_ref`. This involves:
 - An asymmetric degradation penalty (`ψ_norm_s`) that activates only if the planner's nominal utility drops below an acceptable margin.
 - A KL divergence penalty (`D_norm_KL`) on the token distribution to prevent large behavioral drift in normal rollouts.
 - These terms are combined into a nominal-branch objective `L_nom`.
3. **Adversarial Tail-Risk Optimization:** The planner optimizes its performance against adversarial scenarios by minimizing reference-relative regret. Regret (`∆_b_s`) is defined as `U_s(π_ref) - U_s(π_θ)` for each adversarial branch `b`. To focus on rare but critical failures, a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) operator is applied to this regret distribution at a specified tail level `τ`. Since the distribution is two-point, a closed-form analytical solution for CVaR weights (`w_top1_s`, `w_pair_s`) amplifies low-probability, high-regret pair attacks. The robust objective `L_rob` scales policy gradient losses with these CVaR weights.
4. **Safety-Constrained Dual Update:** Explicit safety constraints (e.g., collision, off-road) are enforced through a dual update mechanism. Violation indicators `v_b_s,m` are aggregated, and Lagrangian dual variables `ζ` are updated to dynamically adjust cost multipliers `λ_m` via a softmax projection. Training alternates between minimizing the planner objective (`L_nom + L_rob`) and maximizing the dual objective (`L_dual`).

**Theoretical Justification:** The paper provides theoretical analyses that bound the local approximation error of the decoupled solver, justify the scene-adaptive pair admission as a Bayes gate, interpret role-switched counterfactual credit as a difference-reward estimator, and demonstrate regret as a variance-reducing control variate for CVaR.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of AWM was conducted on the nuPlan and InterPlan benchmarks, focusing on closed-loop planning performance, transferability of adversarial interactions, and the contribution of individual optimization components.

#### 4.1. Closed-Loop Planning Performance

- **nuPlan Benchmark:** AWM-Planner demonstrated improvements in five out of six nuPlan settings compared to the Plan-R1 reference planner. Notable gains were observed on Test14-hard NR and R scenarios (+2.60 and +1.28, respectively). The Test14-random NR score remained largely unchanged (-0.01), while the R score increased (+0.67), suggesting that improvements in hard cases did not broadly sacrifice nominal driving behavior.
- **InterPlan Benchmarks:** Significant improvements were noted on interaction-heavy benchmarks: +2.10 on full InterPlan, +4.97 on InterPlan10, and +5.81 on InterPlan-LongTail. A per-template breakdown revealed gains in seven of eight templates, with the strongest improvements in close straight-driving and medium-density yielding cases, indicating targeted improvement in recoverable interactive failures.

#### 4.2. AWM as a Transferable Adversarial Simulator

- **Cross-Planner Evaluation:** When AWM was deployed as a non-ego sim-agent traffic model, it reduced the closed-loop score for all seven tested planners (rule-based, optimization-based, diffusion-based, transformer-based). The mean score across planners decreased from 70.24 to 67.46, with Plan-R1 experiencing the largest drop. This suggests that AWM generates transferable adversarial behaviors that expose vulnerabilities across different planning architectures.
- **Robustness of AWM-Planner:** The AWM-Planner, after fine-tuning, exhibited a higher starting score under a normal host than Plan-R1 (77.67 vs. 75.86) and suffered a smaller AWM-induced degradation (-3.14 vs. -4.15).
- **Interaction Stress Profile:** Adversarial impact was primarily observed in progress and Time-to-Collision (TTC) metrics, while drivable-area, speed-limit, and driving-direction scores remained largely unaffected. Simulator diagnostics confirmed that AWM utilized nearly the full two-agent attack budget while adhering to feasibility constraints, implying that score degradation resulted from controlled adversarial interaction, not simulator collapse.

#### 4.3. Algorithmic Analysis

- **Planner-World Cross-Validation:** A cross-validation analysis revealed that the post-trained planner improved nominal reward and progress under the normal world. However, larger gains were observed under the AWM world, with adversarial reward increasing by 0.0119 and Tail-CVaR increasing from 0.8660 to 0.9095. This asymmetric improvement confirms that fine-tuning specifically enhanced recovery in the adversarial lower tail while maintaining nominal rollout quality. The study also showed that AWM's Pair and Adaptive branches produced greater lower-tail stress than the single-agent Top-1 branch, with the adaptive branch exhibiting controlled aggressiveness due to scene-level admission.
- **Mechanistic Analysis of AWM (Ablations):**
 - **Credit Assignment:** Role-conditioned counterfactual credit assignment yielded substantially stronger lower-tail degradation compared to global reward broadcast or assignment-only variants. This was achieved without the severe trajectory-fidelity collapse observed in coarser credit baselines, indicating its effectiveness in learning coordinated adversarial behavior realistically.
 - **Coalition Learning:** Ablations on coalition structure showed that while forced K=2 attacks could increase raw stress, they often led to less realistic generator behavior. The adaptive calibrated AWM preserved most of the controlled attack effect by selectively admitting pair attacks, maintaining a balance between attack strength and realism. Comparisons with adapted prior generators (CAT*, STRIVE*, KING*) showed that AWM achieves a balanced operating point, producing sufficient adversarial stress for robust training without generating purely destructive or unrealistic test-time attacks.
- **Planner Adaptation Ablations:**
 - **Decoupled Optimization:** The proposed decoupled Stage A/B pipeline achieved the best adversarial Tail-CVaR, highest closed-loop scores, and lowest variability compared to planner-only refinement, simultaneous alternating self-play, and alternating warmup. Simultaneous updates required more computation and exhibited weaker stability. This supports the design choice of first learning a fixed opponent and then optimizing the planner against its induced risk distribution.
 - **Regret-Aware Objective:** Ablations confirmed the necessity of the regret-aware objective. Expected-risk and no-CVaR variants maintained competitive averages but lost adversarial Tail-CVaR and Test14-hard NR scores, supporting the focus on rare severe risks. Removing normal retention improved adversarial reward but degraded nominal progress and scores.

#### 4.4. Case Studies

Qualitative examples illustrated that AWM-Planner reacts earlier than the original Plan-R1, avoiding collisions or TTC failures in the same adversarial scenes. These cases included scenarios like stopping with a lead vehicle, curved low-speed interactions, pedestrian crossings, right turns, and pick-up/drop-off situations. An example of a K=2 cooperative attack demonstrated how two adversarial non-ego agents could jointly constrain the ego corridor, inducing collision or TTC failure, consistent with the observed impact on progress and TTC metrics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous driving by proposing a novel, theoretically grounded framework for learning robust motion planners. The key significance and potential impacts include:

- **Enhanced Robustness in Long-Tail Scenarios:** The AWM framework demonstrates the ability to significantly improve a planner's robustness against rare but safety-critical multi-agent interactions, which are underrepresented in naturalistic data. This directly addresses a central open problem in autonomous driving, making AVs potentially safer in complex, interactive environments.
- **Integration of Adversarial Training with Modern Planners:** By repurposing predictive world models as structured adversaries within a shared autoregressive interface, the work provides a method for adversarial training that is compatible with high-dimensional, tokenized generative motion planners. This offers a scalable and efficient alternative to traditional external or heuristic-based adversarial generation methods.
- **Principled Multi-Agent Adversary Learning:** The proposed mechanisms for scene-adaptive coalition learning and counterfactual credit assignment enable the AWM to discover sparse, synergistic multi-agent attack coalitions efficiently. This overcomes the combinatorial challenges and credit assignment ambiguities inherent in multi-agent adversarial environments, leading to more informative and plausible adversarial scenarios.
- **Stable and Effective Planner Adaptation:** The decoupled solver, combined with regret-aware tail-risk optimization and reference-anchored trust region constraints, allows for stable and effective fine-tuning of the planner. This ensures that robustness gains in hard cases do not come at the expense of nominal driving performance, which is crucial for practical deployment.
- **Transferability of Adversarial Interactions:** The learned adversarial world model demonstrates transferability, effectively stressing various planning architectures beyond the one it was trained against. This indicates its potential utility as a standardized tool for stress-testing and evaluating the robustness of different AV planning systems.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge that AWM has limitations, including its reliance on the fidelity of the tokenized world model. The framework currently lacks exhaustive scenario coverage and does not offer formal closed-loop safety guarantees. Future work might focus on enhancing world model fidelity and exploring pathways toward formal verification of safety.

Overall, this work presents a step towards developing more resilient autonomous driving systems by equipping them to handle complex and unpredictable human-like interactions, bridging the gap between advanced generative planning models and the demands of real-world safety.
