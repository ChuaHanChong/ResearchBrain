# InstructMove: A Text-Indispensable Benchmark for Instruction-Following Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> InstructMove is a text-indispensable simulation benchmark for instruction-following pick-and-place, where semantic distractors make several actions visually and physically plausible but only one matches the instruction. Built in Isaac Lab from 1,757 curated assets, it spans category, attribute, spatial, and compositional tasks with stage-wise Reach/Lift metrics, exposing that leading VLA policies grasp even under empty or absent instructions.

## Problem

- Existing manipulation benchmarks place one plausible target per scene, letting policies infer the correct action from vision alone and reducing language to a task identifier.
- Aggregate success rates conflate instruction grounding with low-level control, so a failure cannot be attributed to wrong target selection versus failed grasping or placement.
- Spatial vision-language resources test understanding in open-loop question answering, not closed-loop settings where spatial phrases must directly determine executed robot behavior.

## Method

- **Text-indispensability** formalized per episode: multiple physically actionable candidate goals exist, only one is instruction-consistent, enforced by a ==constraint-based layout engine== guaranteeing target uniqueness.
- Four ==capability suites== with cue-matched distractors: category grounding against cross-category objects, attribute grounding within a category, robot-centric spatial relations, and compositional pick-and-place.
- Data infrastructure with ==EmbodiedGen== assets, ==VLM attribute annotation== plus scale and grasp quality curation, ==validator-filtered expert trajectories==, and stage scores decomposing ==Reach==, ==Lift==, ==ReachPlace==, and ==Place==.

## Results

- Among **four** policies, the strongest reached **0.82** Reach and **0.57** Lift on pick category, but all struggled with spatial grounding, where the best Lift was only **0.15**.
- Counterfactual diagnostics on **100** fixed scenes showed **0.99** to **1.00** any-object Reach and **0.64** to **0.66** any-object Lift under generic, empty, and absent-object instructions.
- Real-world transfer improved Reach from **19/50** with **200** real demos to **28/50** with **2,000** synthetic demos, and **34/50** Reach with **18/50** Lift when mixed **9:1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Benchmarks must make language causally necessary; otherwise apparent instruction following measures visual saliency, and high success rates certify nothing about grounding.
> - Policies exhibit a grasping prior decoupled from language: instructions redirect which object is targeted but do not gate whether a grasp begins at all.
> - Failure analysis showed most wrong picks shared color or shape with the target, indicating partial attribute matching rather than binding the full referring expression.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models has advanced the feasibility of general-purpose robot manipulation by allowing robots to interpret and act upon natural language instructions. The broader research landscape features a variety of benchmarks designed to evaluate different facets of robot manipulation, such as long-horizon planning (e.g., CALVIN, VLABench), sim-to-real transfer (e.g., SimplerEnv), bimanual coordination (e.g., RoboTwin), and knowledge transfer (e.g., LIBERO). These benchmarks have facilitated progress in areas including task diversity, generalization capabilities, and complex execution.

However, the authors identify a gap in many existing benchmarks: the design of evaluation scenes frequently presents scenarios where the intended object or destination is visually salient or uniquely feasible. In such cases, policies can successfully complete tasks by inferring the correct action from visual cues alone, effectively reducing the natural language instruction to an auxiliary task identifier rather than a necessary signal for grounding. This phenomenon, termed "visual shortcuts," means that success in these benchmarks does not definitively confirm that a VLA policy genuinely understands and follows the language instruction. Prior work, such as LIBERO-CF, has also highlighted this issue by demonstrating that policies may prioritize vision-induced priors over provided language under counterfactual instructions.

InstructMove addresses this identified gap by introducing the principle of "text-indispensability." This principle mandates that evaluation episodes must contain multiple visually plausible and physically feasible candidate actions, with only one being uniquely consistent with the given language instruction. By adhering to this principle, InstructMove positions itself as a benchmark specifically designed to expose visual shortcuts and ensure that successful robot actions are contingent upon explicit language grounding. It aims to provide a controlled testbed to diagnose whether policies truly rely on language for action selection, differentiating itself from benchmarks that may inadvertently allow for vision-only solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of InstructMove is to establish a benchmark for robot manipulation where natural language instructions are functionally necessary for a robot to select the correct action, thereby ensuring "text-indispensable" instruction following. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Exposing Visual Shortcuts:** The central motivation is to overcome the limitation of existing benchmarks where VLA policies can succeed by relying on visual saliency or unique feasibility of targets, rather than truly grounding the natural language instruction. The authors contend that in many current evaluation scenes, the correct action can be inferred from vision alone, reducing the language instruction to a descriptive element. InstructMove aims to mitigate this by designing scenes with "semantic distractors"—objects or destinations that are visually plausible and physically feasible, but inconsistent with the instruction. This design forces policies to rely on language for disambiguation.

2. **Enabling True Language Grounding Evaluation:** By making language indispensable for action selection, InstructMove seeks to provide a more rigorous test of a policy's ability to interpret and act upon instructions. It aims to ensure that successful task completion reflects genuine understanding and grounding of the instruction, rather than merely reactive behavior to visual cues.

3. **Decomposing Instruction Following Capabilities:** The benchmark is motivated by the need to diagnose specific aspects of language grounding. It decomposes the complex problem of instruction following into four complementary capabilities:
 - **Category Identification:** Assessing the ability to select an object based on its category among cross-category distractors.
 - **Attribute Discrimination:** Evaluating the skill to differentiate objects within the same category based on specific attributes like color, size, or material.
 - **Spatial Reasoning:** Testing the understanding of spatial relations relative to other objects (e.g., "left of," "in front of").
 - **Compositional Pick-and-Place:** Examining the capacity to integrate object selection and placement destination grounding into a multi-stage manipulation sequence. This decomposition allows for a granular assessment of where a policy's language grounding strengths or weaknesses lie.

4. **Providing Controlled and Reproducible Evaluation:** The authors are motivated to create a standardized and reproducible evaluation environment. This includes developing a dedicated data infrastructure with curated assets, a constraint-based layout engine for scene generation, and a scalable demonstration synthesis pipeline. This infrastructure supports a clear train-eval protocol with held-out tasks for assessing generalization and specific diagnostics for language dependence.

5. **Localizing Failure Modes:** The benchmark's design, including stage-wise metrics, is motivated by the desire to differentiate between instruction-grounding failures (e.g., attempting to grasp a distractor) and low-level control failures (e.g., failing to grasp the correct object). This distinction provides clearer feedback for model developers to identify and address specific deficiencies in VLA policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

InstructMove implements its text-indispensability principle through a structured approach focused on pick-and-place tasks within simulated environments.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Text-Indispensability

The benchmark formulates instruction-following manipulation as language-conditioned control. An episode is defined by an initial state, manipulable objects, placement destinations, a natural language instruction, an instruction-consistent goal, and a set of plausible candidate goals. Text-indispensability requires that there are at least two plausible candidate goals, the instruction-consistent goal is among them, and *only* the instruction-consistent goal matches the language instruction. This design ensures that visual feasibility alone cannot identify the target. Semantic or spatial distractors are specifically introduced to expose grounding failures.

#### 3.2. Benchmark Instantiation Pipeline

The benchmark environments are developed using NVIDIA Isaac Lab and Isaac Sim. The instantiation pipeline comprises three aligned components:

- **Asset Library:** A curated collection of 1,757 objects, selected from 3,253 available assets, forms the semantic and physical basis for task construction. These objects span diverse household categories, attributes, and geometric variations. The assets are generated by EmbodiedGen, then undergo VLM-assisted attribute annotation (e.g., super-category, color, shape, material) and quality curation. Quality checks include metric scale verification, grasp-feasibility trials, and cross-validation of semantic metadata. The library supports reproducible sampling and includes manipulation affordances and capability tags.
- **Task and Layout Library:** This component defines the benchmark episodes across four task families. It uses a constraint-based layout engine to instantiate scenes with diverse object arrangements. The engine ensures that multiple candidate objects or goals are physically plausible in each scene, but only one is consistent with the instruction.
- **Expert Trajectory Dataset:** InstructMove constructs a dataset of expert trajectories for training and evaluation. Manipulation tasks are decomposed into atomic actions (e.g., Pick, Move, Place). Grasp poses are sampled and filtered based on kinematic constraints and joint-space cost. Motion planning generates smooth, executable joint-space trajectories. An automatic validation stage ensures that only trajectories satisfying task-specific success criteria (e.g., correct grasping, lifting, placement) are retained. The validated trajectories, along with multimodal observations (RGB, depth, robot joint states, TF records), are recorded in MCAP format. The valid synthesis rate for trajectories is approximately 70% for both Franka and PiperX embodiments.
- **Instruction Generation and Language Diversity:** Natural language instructions are generated by combining task-level paraphrases (e.g., varying manipulation verbs) with object-level referring expressions. Object-level expressions, generated by large language models, describe discriminative visual and semantic properties, ensuring linguistic diversity without altering the core task.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** To enhance visual robustness and aid sim-to-real transfer, InstructMove integrates photometric randomization (perturbing scene illumination) and appearance randomization (altering surface materials and textures of objects and tables). These factors are declaratively configured and applied at each environment reset.

#### 3.3. Capability Axes and Task Suites

InstructMove evaluates semantic-spatial grounding through four task families:

- **Pick Category:** Policies must identify and pick an object based on its category among five cross-category distractors. Evaluation includes "seen" (training categories, held-out layouts), "unseen-instance" (seen categories, novel object instances), and "unseen-category" (novel target and distractor categories) splits.
- **Pick Attribute:** Policies must discriminate an object by its attribute (color, size, shape, material) from two same-category distractors.
- **Pick Spatial:** Policies must identify an object based on its spatial relation to a reference object, with a same-category distractor. The spatial relations are robot-centric (e.g., "left of," "in front of").
- **Compositional Pick-Place:** Policies must compose object selection and placement destination grounding, with multiple plausible pick objects and placement goals.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocol and Stage Metrics

InstructMove employs a train-eval protocol where policies are trained on InstructMove data and evaluated on held-out tasks. To provide detailed diagnostics, it reports stage-wise metrics:

- **For pick-only tasks (S_pick):**
 - `Reach`: End-effector within a predefined distance of the instruction-consistent target (primarily evaluates visual grounding).
 - `Lift`: Target successfully grasped and raised (primarily evaluates manipulation control).
- **For compositional pick-and-place tasks (S_pnp):**
 - `ReachPick`, `LiftPick`: Corresponding metrics for the instructed object.
 - `ReachPlace`, `Place`: Metrics for approaching and completing the instruction-consistent placement.

This stage-wise breakdown allows for the localization of failures, distinguishing instruction-grounding errors (e.g., reaching a distractor) from control failures (e.g., failing to grasp the correct target).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated four VLA policies (π0, π0.5, GR00T N1.7, and Motus) under the InstructMove train-eval protocol, fine-tuning them for 5,000 steps on NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs.

#### 4.1. Train-Eval Main Evaluation

Experiments on PiperX (pick category, pick attribute, place a2b) and Franka (pick spatial, place a2b) revealed performance differences:
- **Target Localization (Reach):** π0.5 demonstrated the strongest ability in semantic pick tasks, achieving the highest `Reach` scores for both Pick Category (0.82) and Pick Attribute (0.81), indicating superior grounding of category and attribute instructions. GR00T N1.7 followed in `Reach` performance. For Pick Spatial, π0.5, GR00T N1.7, and Motus obtained comparable `Reach` rates (0.46, 0.50, 0.51 respectively), suggesting that spatial-relation grounding represents a common bottleneck across these models.
- **Manipulation Control (Lift):** A notable gap between `Reach` and `Lift` scores highlighted differences in converting correct target localization into successful manipulation. π0.5 maintained substantially higher `Lift` rates (0.57 for Pick Category, 0.62 for Pick Attribute) once the target was reached, indicating robust execution. In contrast, GR00T N1.7, despite often reaching the correct target, rarely completed the lift (0.17 for Pick Category, 0.07 for Pick Attribute).
- **Compositional Pick-Place:** π0.5 also performed most robustly on compositional tasks, achieving an S_pnp score of 0.52.

#### 4.2. Pick-Category Generalization

On generalization tasks, π0.5 maintained performance levels close to its seen-split results on both unseen-instance (Reach 0.74, Lift 0.48) and unseen-category (Reach 0.81, Lift 0.54) splits. This indicates a capacity for generalizing category grounding to novel object instances and entirely unseen categories. GR00T N1.7 also showed relatively high `Reach` on unseen splits but continued to exhibit substantially lower `Lift` rates, suggesting persistent challenges in executing manipulation post-localization, even when new categories or instances are introduced.

#### 4.3. Counterfactual Language-Dependence Diagnostic

A counterfactual analysis with π0.5 on fixed scenes revealed the following:
- **Instruction-Dependent Target Redirection:** When the instruction was changed to refer to an "Alternative target" (another visible object), the policy successfully redirected its target selection, achieving a `Target Reach` of 0.72 and `Target Lift` of 0.53 for the new object. This demonstrates the policy's ability to use a valid language description to locate a different instruction-consistent target.
- **Prevalence of Grasping Prior (Visual Shortcut):** Under conditions where no unique valid target was specified ("Generic object," "Empty instruction," "Absent object"), the policy frequently initiated a generic grasp, resulting in `Any-object Reach` rates of 0.99–1.00 and `Any-object Lift` rates of 0.64–0.66. This suggests that while language can guide target selection, it does not fully gate the initiation of grasping, indicating a strong grasping prior consistent with a visual shortcut. The study concluded that success in scenes without distractors would not reveal this underlying behavior.
- **Failure Case Analysis:** Analysis of `Reach`-failed episodes for π0.5 indicated that 15 out of 18 failures on the pick category seen split involved policies lifting distractors that shared salient cues (e.g., color, shape) with the intended target. This suggests instances of partial grounding, where the policy responds to some attributes in the instruction but fails to reliably bind the full referring expression to the correct object.

#### 4.4. Real-World Transfer with Simulation Data

Experiments using a dual-arm PiperX robot assessed the transferability of InstructMove simulation data to real-world performance:
- **Simulation Data Benefits:** Policies trained solely on 200 simulation demonstrations (200 sim) improved `Reach` from 19/50 (for 200 real-world demonstrations) to 26/50, while `Lift` remained comparable at 11/50. This indicates that the greater intra-category instance and appearance diversity provided by simulation data benefits instruction-conditioned target grounding.
- **Scaling Simulation Data:** Increasing the simulation data scale from 200 to 2,000 demonstrations further improved performance, with `Reach` increasing to 28/50 and `Lift` to 15/50.
- **Mixed Data (Sim + Real):** The best performance was achieved by combining 2,000 simulation demonstrations with 200 real-world demonstrations, resulting in `Reach` of 34/50 and `Lift` of 18/50. This suggests that real-world data provides complementary signals not fully captured in simulation, further strengthening transfer.
The results collectively suggest that scalable simulation data can improve real-world manipulation performance, and a small amount of real-world data can further enhance this transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

InstructMove represents a development in the evaluation of robot manipulation capabilities, particularly regarding genuine language grounding. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

1. **Addressing a Critical Evaluation Gap:** The benchmark directly confronts the issue of "visual shortcuts" in VLA models, a prevalent challenge in existing benchmarks. By ensuring that language is indispensable for action selection, InstructMove provides a more rigorous and accurate assessment of whether a robot genuinely understands and follows instructions, rather than relying on visual saliency alone. This is critical for advancing the development of truly instruction-following robotic agents.

2. **Enhanced Diagnostic Capabilities:** The design of InstructMove, incorporating semantic distractors, stage-wise metrics, and counterfactual language-dependence diagnostics, offers a detailed framework for understanding policy behavior. It enables researchers to clearly differentiate between failures caused by inadequate language grounding (e.g., reaching a distractor) and those arising from control execution limitations (e.g., failing to grasp the correct object). This granular attribution of failures can significantly accelerate model development by providing specific targets for improvement.

3. **Structured Task Taxonomy:** The decomposition of instruction following into distinct capabilities—category identification, attribute discrimination, spatial reasoning, and compositional pick-and-place—provides a systematic approach to evaluating various facets of language understanding required for manipulation. This taxonomy can guide the design of future VLA architectures that are robust across different types of linguistic commands.

4. **Scalable and Reproducible Data Infrastructure:** The benchmark's robust data infrastructure, including a curated asset library, constraint-based layout engine, and an automated, validated trajectory synthesis pipeline, allows for the generation of diverse and high-quality synthetic data. This reduces reliance on expensive and labor-intensive real-world data collection, making research and development more accessible and reproducible.

5. **Demonstrated Sim-to-Real Utility:** The experiments on real-world transfer demonstrate that InstructMove's simulation data can effectively improve real-world manipulation performance, particularly in instruction-conditioned target grounding. This highlights the practical utility of the benchmark as a training resource for robotic systems, suggesting that simulation-trained policies can translate to tangible benefits in physical environments.

6. **Guidance for Future VLA Model Development:** The findings, particularly from the counterfactual analysis and failure case studies, reveal that even advanced VLA policies can exhibit partial grounding or strong visual priors that supersede language. This insight provides valuable guidance for researchers to develop more robust VLA models that ensure complete and reliable instruction grounding before executing actions.

In conclusion, InstructMove's principled approach to creating text-indispensable manipulation tasks has the potential to significantly impact the field of robot learning by fostering the development of VLA policies that are genuinely responsive to natural language, rather than merely reacting to visual cues. Its diagnostic capabilities and scalable data generation pipeline provide foundational tools for future research in this area.
