# Quantification of Sim2Real Gap via Neural Simulation Gap Function

> [!summary] Summary
> Neural simulation gap functions quantify the state- and input-dependent mismatch between a nominal mathematical model and a high-fidelity simulator. A neural network is trained via a scenario convex program with Lipschitz-bounded layers enforced by a matrix inequality, then a Lipschitz-based correction term extends the bound to unseen states. Treating the gap as bounded disturbance, symbolic controllers synthesized on the nominal model satisfy reach-avoid and invariance specifications in Gazebo and PyBullet.

## Problem

- Controllers designed on approximate mathematical models fail on real systems because the unmodelled discrepancy between model and reality is never formally quantified.
- Gaussian-process and data-driven identification methods need costly real-world data and typically give only probabilistic guarantees on the estimated dynamics.
- Prior formal gap quantification fixes the gap as a linear combination of user-chosen basis functions, so a wrong structure makes the resulting bound badly conservative.

## Method

- **Neural simulation gap function**: a per-state-dimension network taking concatenated state and input, replacing fixed basis expansions as a universal approximator of the model-simulator mismatch.
- ==Scenario convex program== over sampled state-input balls, trained with ==hinge sub-losses== penalizing gap exceeding the bound and mismatch exceeding the gap, plus a ==log-det matrix-inequality loss== enforcing a ==Lipschitz bound==.
- ==Coverage theorem== adds a constant built from the network and mismatch Lipschitz constants times sampling radii, extending the bound to all unseen points; the gap then enters symbolic control as bounded disturbance.

## Results

- Mecanum bot in Gazebo, state set **3 by 3** metres, sampling **0.3 s**, discretizations **0.01** and **0.014**: optimization yields per-axis bounds of **0.11** and **0.08**.
- Estimated mismatch Lipschitz constants near unity (**1.03**, **1.02**, **1.03**, **1.01**) with network bounds fixed at **10** and single **400-neuron** hidden layers; full data collection plus optimization took roughly **1.5 days**.
- Nominal-model controllers hit obstacles in Gazebo and violate the pendulum invariance set in PyBullet, while gap-augmented controllers satisfy both specifications, with the invariance region visibly shrunk.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning an uncertainty bound rather than the unknown dynamics lets mature robust and symbolic control tools be reused unchanged, with the gap entering as bounded disturbance.
> - Formal generalization to unseen states costs only a Lipschitz-times-sampling-radius margin, so guarantee tightness is bought directly with sampling density.
> - Flexibility has a price: bisection over the bound converges slowly, and days-long data plus optimization time limits scaling to higher-dimensional systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of control systems for real-world applications often involves designing controllers based on mathematical models. However, a significant challenge arises because these mathematical models are frequently approximations of actual physical systems, leading to a discrepancy between predicted and observed behavior. This divergence, commonly referred to as the "Sim2Real gap" or "model-reality gap," means that controllers optimized for a mathematical model may underperform or fail when deployed in a real environment.

Traditionally, addressing this gap has involved two main approaches:
1. **Model-based control:** This relies on precise mathematical models. When models are inaccurate, controllers may lack robustness or require extensive tuning.
2. **Data-driven control:** This approach uses real-world data to identify system dynamics or directly synthesize controllers without an explicit model. While robust to model uncertainties, data-driven methods often demand substantial amounts of real-world data, which can be costly, time-consuming, and impractical to collect, especially for complex or safety-critical systems.

The advent of high-fidelity simulators, such as Gazebo, CARLA, Webots, and PyBullet, has offered a solution to the data collection bottleneck. These simulators, incorporating advanced sensor models, physics engines, and uncertainty models, can generate vast datasets more efficiently than real-world experiments. This capability has fueled the "Sim2Real transfer" paradigm, where control policies are trained in simulation and then adapted for real-world deployment. However, even high-fidelity simulators are not perfect representations of reality, maintaining their own "simulation gap" relative to real systems, and often a "model-simulator gap" relative to simpler mathematical models.

Existing research has attempted to bridge the Sim2Real gap through various strategies, including domain randomization and specific quantification methods. Some studies, like, quantify the Sim2Real gap as a numerical value with probabilistic guarantees. More relevantly, quantified this gap as a function dependent on state and input with formal guarantees. However, the approach in assumed the gap function could be represented as a linear combination of predefined basis functions. This assumption can introduce conservatism or inaccuracy if the true gap function has a complex, nonlinear structure not well-captured by the chosen basis.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitation of prior quantification methods. It seeks to provide a formal and less conservative quantification of the *model-simulator gap* using neural networks, which are universal function approximators capable of capturing complex nonlinear relationships. By quantifying this specific gap, the work aims to enable the application of established model-based control design techniques (which typically operate on mathematical models) to effectively control systems that are accurately represented by high-fidelity simulators, thereby indirectly facilitating Sim2Real transfer if the simulator itself closely mirrors reality. The key contribution is the development of a neural simulation gap function that offers formal guarantees across the entire state space, including unseen data points, overcoming the structural limitations and potential conservatism of earlier approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the disparity between theoretical control design for mathematical models and its practical implementation on physical systems. Controllers designed for simplified mathematical models frequently fail when applied to real-world systems or even high-fidelity simulators due to unmodeled dynamics, disturbances, and inherent differences between the theoretical abstraction and the concrete realization.

Collecting sufficient real-world data to develop robust data-driven controllers is often prohibitive in terms of time, cost, and safety. While high-fidelity simulators mitigate the data collection challenge by offering a controlled and parallelizable environment, they still present a discrepancy relative to the abstract mathematical models used in theoretical control design. Leveraging these simulators effectively requires understanding and accounting for this "model-simulator gap."

Previous attempts to quantify this gap, while providing some guarantees, often faced limitations:
- Some methods provided only numerical values or probabilistic guarantees, which may not be sufficient for safety-critical applications.
- Other methods quantified the gap as a function but assumed a predefined, often linear, structure for this function (e.g., a linear combination of basis functions). This assumption can lead to an overly conservative estimate of the gap or an inaccurate representation if the true gap is highly nonlinear, thereby limiting the effectiveness of controllers designed using such a quantified gap.

Against this background, the paper outlines two key objectives:

1. **Formal Quantification of the Simulation Gap:** The primary objective is to formally quantify the discrepancy between a known nominal mathematical model ($\Sigma$) and an unknown high-fidelity simulator model ($\hat{\Sigma}$). This quantification is to be represented by a function, $\gamma(x, u): X \times U \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^n$, such that for all states $x$ in the state space $X$ and all inputs $u$ in the input space $U$, the absolute difference between the $i$-th component of the simulator's next state and the mathematical model's next state is bounded by the $i$-th component of the gap function:
 $| \hat{f}_i(x, u) - f_i(x, u) | \le \gamma_i(x, u)$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.
 A critical aspect of this objective is to model $\gamma(x, u)$ using a neural network to capture complex, nonlinear relationships without requiring a predefined basis structure, and to provide formal guarantees on this quantification across the entire state and input space, including data points not explicitly used during training.

2. **Controller Design with Guaranteed Performance in the Simulator:** The second objective is to utilize the formally quantified neural simulation gap function to design a controller. This controller, developed using existing model-based tools (which typically operate on the nominal mathematical model), should be able to enforce desired specifications (e.g., reachability, invariance) when applied to the high-fidelity simulator. The aim is to formally guarantee a satisfactory transition and performance from the mathematical model's design to the high-fidelity simulator's execution. By effectively bridging the model-simulator gap, the approach allows sophisticated model-based control theory to be applied to systems represented by high-fidelity simulators, which are considered closer to reality than simplified mathematical models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology focuses on quantifying the simulation gap between a nominal discrete-time mathematical model, $\Sigma: x(k+1) = f(x(k), u(k))$, and an unknown discrete-time high-fidelity simulator model, $\hat{\Sigma}: x(k+1) = \hat{f}(x(k), u(k))$.

The core idea is to define a "simulation gap function" $\gamma(x, u) = [\gamma_1(x, u); \dots; \gamma_n(x, u)]$ such that for each state dimension $i$, the absolute difference between the next state predicted by the simulator and the mathematical model is bounded: $|\hat{f}_i(x, u) - f_i(x, u)| \le \gamma_i(x, u)$.

The methodology addresses the challenges of this quantification through the following steps:

1. **Formulating the Problem as Robust Optimization:** The goal is to find a function $\gamma_i(x,u)$ that provides the tightest possible upper bound for the difference $|\hat{f}_i(x,u) - f_i(x,u)|$ across the entire state and input space $X \times U$. This is initially framed as a Robust Optimization Problem (ROP) for each $i$:
 $\min_{\gamma_i \in \mathcal{H}, \eta_i \in \mathbb{R}} \eta_i$
 subject to $\gamma_i(x,u) \le \eta_i$ for all $x \in X, u \in U$, and
 $|\hat{f}_i(x,u) - f_i(x,u)| - \gamma_i(x,u) \le 0$ for all $x \in X, u \in U$.
 The challenges with this ROP are its infinite constraints (due to continuous state and input spaces) and the unknown structure of $\hat{f}_i(x,u)$ and $\gamma_i(x,u)$.

2. **Neural Network Approximation for the Gap Function:** To address the unknown structure and allow for flexible representation of complex nonlinearities, each component of the simulation gap function, $\gamma_i(x,u)$, is modeled as a neural network, denoted $\gamma_w^{(i)}(x,u)$, parameterized by weights $w$. This choice leverages the universal approximation capabilities of neural networks. The neural network's architecture is defined with input layer neurons equal to the sum of state and input dimensions ($n+m$), a single output neuron, and specified hidden layers with an activation function (e.g., arctangent, softmax, ReLU).

3. **Data Collection for Training:** A finite dataset is generated to train the neural networks. This involves:
 - Creating cover sets for the state space $X$ and input space $U$. This is done by sampling $N$ points from $X$ (denoted $x_r$) and $M$ points from $U$ (denoted $u_s$). Balls of radius $\epsilon_x$ around each $x_r$ and $\epsilon_u$ around each $u_s$ are defined to ensure coverage of $X$ and $U$, respectively.
 - For each pair $(x_r, u_s)$, data points are collected from both the mathematical model and the high-fidelity simulator, yielding $f_i(x_r, u_s)$ and $\hat{f}_i(x_r, u_s)$ for all $i$.

4. **Training via Scenario Convex Program (SCP):** The ROP (4) is then reformulated as a Scenario Convex Program (SCP) (9) using the collected finite dataset. This addresses the challenge of infinite constraints by considering only the sampled data points. However, training a neural network solely based on this SCP does not inherently guarantee the satisfaction of the gap condition for *unseen* data points across the entire continuous state and input space.

5. **Formal Guarantees for Unseen Data (Lipschitz Continuity):** To extend guarantees beyond the training data, the methodology incorporates Lipschitz continuity.
 - **Lipschitz Constraint for Neural Network:** The neural network $\gamma_w^{(i)}(x,u)$ is enforced to be Lipschitz continuous with respect to the concatenated state-input vector $(x,u)$ with a bound $L_1^{(i)}$. This is achieved by imposing a matrix inequality constraint (11) on the network's weights during training, derived from prior work.
 - **Loss Functions:** The training procedure utilizes a combination of loss functions:
 - $L_1^{(i)}(w)$: Penalizes cases where $\gamma_w^{(i)}(x,u)$ exceeds the current upper bound $\eta_i$.
 - $L_2^{(i)}(w)$: Penalizes cases where the true difference $|\hat{f}_i(x,u) - f_i(x,u)|$ exceeds $\gamma_w^{(i)}(x,u)$.
 - $L_M^{(i)}(w, \Lambda)$: A logarithmic penalty for the Lipschitz matrix inequality (11), which helps enforce Lipschitz continuity.
 - **Theorem 4.1** states that if these loss functions are minimized to zero, the trained neural network $\gamma_w^{*(i)}(x,u)$ satisfies the SCP conditions for the training data.

6. **Quantifying the Gap for the Entire Space (Theorem 5.2):** This is the central theoretical contribution for formal guarantees.
 - **Assumption 5.1:** It is assumed that the function $|\hat{f}_i(x,u) - f_i(x,u)|$ is also Lipschitz continuous with respect to $x$ and $u$, with constants $L_{2x}^{(i)}$ and $L_{2u}^{(i)}$ respectively. The paper references for methods to estimate these constants from finite data.
 - **Theorem 5.2:** This theorem formally quantifies the simulation gap for *all* $(x,u) \in X \times U$. It states that for the optimal neural network $\gamma_w^{*(i)}(x,u)$ obtained from the SCP, the true difference is bounded by:
 $|\hat{f}_i(x,u) - f_i(x,u)| \le \gamma_w^{*(i)}(x,u) + L^{(i)}$,
 where $L^{(i)} = L_1^{(i)}\sqrt{\epsilon_x^2 + \epsilon_u^2} + L_{2x}^{(i)}\epsilon_x + L_{2u}^{(i)}\epsilon_u$. This constant $L^{(i)}$ explicitly accounts for the maximum possible deviation between any point $(x,u)$ and its closest sampled point $(x_r, u_s)$, considering the Lipschitz constants of both the neural network and the true difference function, and the radii of the cover sets. This addition ensures formal guarantees across the continuous space.

7. **Controller Synthesis:** Once the neural simulation gap function $\gamma(x, u)$ is formally quantified for the entire state space, the mathematical model (1) is augmented to represent an uncertain system:
 $x(k+1) \in f(x(k), u(k)) + [-\gamma(x(k), u(k)), \gamma(x(k), u(k))]$.
 This augmented system is then amenable to standard robust control design techniques for uncertain systems, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), adaptive control, or symbolic control. The paper specifically employs a symbolic controller designed using the SCOTS toolbox to enforce desired specifications (e.g., reach-while-avoid, invariance) on the high-fidelity simulator. The bisection method is used to solve the underlying optimization problem during training, with recognition that alternative methods could reduce convergence time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research introduces the concept of a neural simulation gap function and provides a formal methodology for its quantification and application in control design. The main findings are both theoretical and empirical, demonstrated through case studies.

**Theoretical Findings:**
- **Neural Simulation Gap Function:** The paper successfully defines a neural simulation gap function, $\gamma_w(x, u)$, which uses a neural network to approximate the absolute difference between the next states predicted by a nominal mathematical model and a high-fidelity simulator. This approach offers flexibility in capturing complex, nonlinear discrepancies compared to methods relying on predefined basis functions.
- **Scenario Convex Program for Training:** The robust optimization problem of finding the tightest simulation gap function is reformulated into a data-driven Scenario Convex Program (SCP). This addresses the challenge of infinite constraints in continuous state and input spaces by utilizing a finite set of sampled data points.
- **Formal Guarantees for Unseen Data:** A critical theoretical result is the extension of the quantified gap's validity to the entire continuous state and input space, including data points not explicitly used during training. This is achieved by:
 - Enforcing Lipschitz continuity on the neural network $\gamma_w(x, u)$ during training through a specific matrix inequality constraint (Equation 11).
 - Deriving a formal upper bound (Theorem 5.2) which states that the true simulation gap at any point $(x,u)$ is bounded by the trained neural network output $\gamma_w^{*(i)}(x,u)$ plus a constant $L^{(i)}$. This constant $L^{(i)}$ depends on the Lipschitz constants of both the neural network and the true model-simulator difference, as well as the discretization radii ($\epsilon_x, \epsilon_u$) used for data collection. This provides a rigorous, worst-case guarantee for the entire domain.
- **Controller Synthesis for Uncertain Systems:** The formally quantified simulation gap function allows the augmentation of the nominal mathematical model into an uncertain system (Equation 17). This transformed model can then be used with established model-based control synthesis tools (e.g., symbolic control) to design controllers that are robust to the identified model-simulator discrepancy, enabling reliable control in the high-fidelity simulator.

**Empirical Findings (Case Studies):**
The methodology was validated using two case studies: a Mecanum bot simulated in Gazebo and a pendulum system simulated in PyBullet.

1. **Mecanum Bot Case Study:**
 - **Setup:** A kinematic model of a differential drive Mecanum bot was used as the mathematical model, and the Gazebo simulator represented the high-fidelity system. Discretization parameters $\epsilon_x = 0.01$ and $\epsilon_u = 0.014$ were used for data collection.
 - **Quantification:** The neural simulation gap functions $\gamma_1(x,u)$ and $\gamma_2(x,u)$ were trained, yielding optimal bounds $\eta_1 = 0.11$ and $\eta_2 = 0.08$. Lipschitz constants for the true gap function were estimated as $L_{2x}^{(1)} = 1.03, L_{2x}^{(2)} = 1.02, L_{2u}^{(1)} = 1.03, L_{2u}^{(2)} = 1.01$. The total process of data collection and optimization took approximately 1.5 days.
 - **Control Performance:**
 - A symbolic controller designed for a reach-while-avoid specification solely based on the *nominal mathematical model* failed when applied to the Gazebo simulator, resulting in the bot hitting an obstacle (Figure 2, top).
 - When the neural simulation gap function $\gamma(x, u)$ was *incorporated* into the mathematical model's dynamics, and a new controller was synthesized for this augmented model, the Mecanum bot successfully met the reach-while-avoid specification in the Gazebo simulator (Figure 2, bottom). This empirically demonstrates that the quantified gap enables the design of controllers robust to simulator inaccuracies.

2. **Pendulum System Case Study:**
 - **Setup:** A standard mathematical model of a pendulum was used, with the PyBullet simulator serving as the high-fidelity environment. Discretization parameters $\epsilon_x = 0.0022$ and $\epsilon_u = 0.01$ were applied.
 - **Control Performance:**
 - A symbolic controller designed for an invariance property (keeping the state within a defined region) based on the *nominal mathematical model* violated this property when applied to the PyBullet simulator (Figure 4, top).
 - When the neural simulation gap function $\gamma(x, u)$ was *incorporated* into the model, the synthesized controller successfully maintained the invariance property within the PyBullet environment (Figure 4, bottom). Figure 5 further illustrates how the invariance region is expanded and appropriately shifted when the simulation gap is considered, preventing violations.

In summary, the empirical results from both case studies provide evidence that the proposed methodology for quantifying the simulation gap using neural networks, coupled with formal guarantees, enables the design of robust controllers for high-fidelity simulators, thereby improving performance and safety compared to designing controllers purely based on simplified mathematical models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of control systems, particularly in bridging the gap between theoretical model-based design and practical application in simulated or real-world environments. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood from several perspectives:

1. **Formal and Guaranteed Quantification of Model-Simulator Gap:** The most direct impact is the introduction of a formal method to quantify the discrepancy between a simplified mathematical model and a complex high-fidelity simulator. Unlike previous approaches that relied on fixed function structures or offered only probabilistic guarantees, this work leverages neural networks for flexible, nonlinear approximation and provides explicit, worst-case guarantees across the entire continuous state and input space. This level of formal assurance is critical for developing safety-critical systems.

2. **Enabling Robust Model-Based Control for Complex Systems:** By formally quantifying the model-simulator gap, the research allows for the continued use of well-established and powerful model-based control synthesis techniques (e.g., symbolic control, Model Predictive Control, adaptive control). Instead of discarding mathematical models due to their inaccuracies or resorting to purely data-driven methods that may lack formal guarantees, the quantified gap transforms the simplified model into an uncertain system. This enables the design of robust controllers for high-fidelity simulators, which are considered closer representations of reality than pure mathematical models. This reduces the need for extensive real-world experimentation.

3. **Reduced Conservatism:** Prior methods of gap quantification, especially those relying on linear combinations of predefined basis functions, could lead to overly conservative estimates if the true nonlinear dynamics were poorly represented. The use of neural networks, as universal function approximators, allows for a more accurate and less conservative representation of the simulation gap, potentially leading to less restrictive control designs and improved performance.

4. **Facilitating Sim2Real Transfer:** If the high-fidelity simulator accurately mimics the real world, the formally quantified model-simulator gap can be seen as a critical component in a larger Sim2Real pipeline. By ensuring that controllers designed for mathematical models work reliably in high-fidelity simulators, this work effectively establishes a reliable stepping stone towards real-world deployment. The explicitly stated future work of extending this approach to the real world highlights this potential.

5. **Cost and Time Efficiency:** The methodology leverages high-fidelity simulators for data collection, significantly reducing the time and cost associated with gathering real-world data. This allows for rapid iteration and validation of control strategies in a virtual environment before costly real-world deployment. The reported 1.5 days for data collection and optimization for the Mecanum bot, while not trivial, is likely substantially less than comparable real-world system identification and control development.

6. **Enhanced Safety and Reliability:** The provision of formal guarantees on the simulation gap across unseen data points is paramount for safety-critical applications in domains such as autonomous driving, robotics, and aerospace. Knowing the precise bounds of model uncertainty allows control systems to be designed with a higher degree of confidence in their safety and reliability in environments represented by high-fidelity simulators.

In summary, this research provides a rigorous, flexible, and practical framework for addressing the critical model-simulator gap, thereby enhancing the reliability of control system design. Its impact lies in enabling the principled application of classical control theory to complex systems represented by advanced simulators, ultimately accelerating the development and deployment of robust and safe autonomous systems.
