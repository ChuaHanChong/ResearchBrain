# Learning from 10 Demos: Generalisable and Sample-Efficient Policy Learning with Oriented Affordance Frames

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the QUT Centre for Robotics and University of Adelaide developed a method utilizing Oriented Affordance Frames for robots to learn complex manipulation policies with high sample efficiency and generalization from just 10 demonstrations per sub-task. This approach achieves a 30-fold improvement in data efficiency over image-based policies and demonstrates robust performance with novel object instances and out-of-distribution spatial configurations.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning methods for robotic manipulation require vast datasets, making deployment costly and time-consuming for real-world tasks.
- Current state representations (image-based inputs, global coordinate frames) struggle with generalization to novel spatial configurations and object instances, often capturing task-irrelevant visual clutter.
- Decomposing long-horizon tasks into sub-policies faces challenges related to distribution shifts and the need for complex arbitration mechanisms between them.

## Method

- Introduced **Oriented Affordance Frames (OAFs)**, a novel task-aligned ==3D coordinate system== that defines a compact, object-centric state representation.
- Developed a perception pipeline leveraging pre-trained vision foundation models (==Grounding DINO==, ==SAM==, ==Foundation Pose==) for marker-free detection and tracking of OAFs in real-time.
- Integrated ==Diffusion Policy== for learning individual sub-policies and augmented them with a ==self-progress prediction== mechanism for autonomous and robust policy arbitration.

## Results

- Achieved an average of **90.9%** success rate for base tasks, learned from just **10** demonstrations per sub-task, significantly outperforming end-effector-centric (**48.1%**) and global-frame (**59.7%**) representations.
- Demonstrated a **30-fold** improvement in data efficiency, with the method requiring only **10** demonstrations for the 'cup rearrangement' task compared to **305** for an image-based policy.
- Exhibited robust generalization to out-of-distribution spatial configurations (**83.1%** average success) and novel object instances (e.g., handling **8 out of 10** unseen teacups), and enabled seamless task composition via self-progress prediction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured, abstract, object-centric representations like Oriented Affordance Frames can intrinsically provide strong spatial, intra-category, and compositional generalization with minimal demonstration data.
> - The orientation component of OAFs, which aligns a reference frame with the robot's tool at task initiation, is crucial for consolidating diverse demonstrated trajectories and achieving high sample efficiency.
> - Leveraging pre-trained vision foundation models allows for practical, marker-free deployment of abstract affordance-based representations, bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks and real-world robotic systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses critical and long-standing challenges in **imitation learning (IL)** and **behavior cloning (BC)** for robotic manipulation, particularly concerning **sample efficiency** and **generalization** in complex, long-horizon, multi-object tasks.

The current landscape of robotic policy learning is characterized by two main trends:
1. **Large-scale data collection**: Many state-of-the-art methods, such as RT-1, RT-X, Mobile ALOHA, and UMI, rely on vast datasets of human demonstrations or robot-collected interaction data. While these approaches have enabled impressive low-level manipulation skills and some degree of spatial generalization, they often require an impractical number of demonstrations to cover the myriad variations in real-world environments, making them costly and difficult to deploy for novel tasks or unseen objects.
2. **Architectural invariances**: Other efforts focus on developing neural network architectures that inherently possess certain invariances (e.g., E(2)-equivariant CNNs) to improve generalization. While beneficial, these typically operate on raw visual inputs, which can still be overwhelmed by task-irrelevant visual clutter.

A promising avenue, which this work builds upon, is the **decomposition of long-horizon tasks into simpler sub-policies**. However, this approach faces its own set of challenges: identifying effective task partitions, managing distribution shifts when composing sub-policies, and learning an arbitration mechanism to switch between them.

The paper critically examines the limitations of common state representations in behavior cloning:
- **Image-based inputs**: These are high-dimensional and prone to "covariate shift," where minor visual variations can significantly degrade policy performance. They often capture task-irrelevant details, hindering generalization.
- **Global coordinate frames**: Policies trained in a fixed global frame require demonstrations to cover all possible spatial configurations of objects and the robot, leading to poor spatial generalization if objects appear in unseen locations.
- **Simple relative frames (e.g., end-effector-centric or object-center-centric)**: While improving on global frames, they still require extensive data to generalize across all relative poses between the robot and objects, or struggle with object rotations and intra-category variations.

This work fits into the growing trend of leveraging **structured or abstracted representations** to distill task-relevant information and improve data efficiency. Specifically, it advances **keypoint-based or affordance-centric representations**, which focus on salient object regions. Prior keypoint methods often focused on open-loop, single-step tasks or still operated with limited spatial invariance. The unique contribution here is the introduction of **oriented affordance frames (OAFs)**, a novel task-aligned 3D coordinate system designed for **closed-loop behavior cloning** that intrinsically enables spatial, intra-category, and compositional generalization with very few demonstrations.

Furthermore, the paper integrates **pre-trained vision foundation models** (e.g., Grounding DINO, SAM, Foundation Pose) into its perception pipeline. This addresses a significant practical hurdle: how to detect and track these abstract affordance frames in real-world, marker-free environments, bridging the gap between theoretical representation and practical deployment. This aligns with a broader research direction of leveraging the power of large language and vision models for robotic perception and reasoning.

In essence, this work stands out by proposing a robust, sample-efficient alternative to large-scale data collection, using an intelligently designed, structured state-action representation that inherently fosters generalization and compositionality, making it highly relevant for the development of more adaptable and practical robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to enable robots to learn and execute complex, long-horizon, multi-object manipulation tasks with unprecedented **sample efficiency** and **robust generalization**. The authors aim to achieve this by addressing the fundamental limitations of existing imitation learning approaches.

The specific objectives are to train state-conditioned robotic policies that are:
1. **Sample-efficient**: Capable of being learned effectively from as few as 10 human demonstrations per sub-task.
2. **Spatially invariant**: Robust to variations in the spatial configuration and placement of task-relevant objects in the workspace.
3. **Intra-category invariant**: Able to generalize to novel object instances that were unseen during training, exhibiting different geometries and appearances but belonging to the same functional category (e.g., different types of cups or teapots).
4. **Composable**: Designed to allow independent sub-policies to be seamlessly chained together to solve multi-step and long-horizon tasks involving interactions with multiple objects.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the significant hurdles currently faced by robotic systems attempting to operate in unstructured domestic or real-world environments:
- **Complexity of real-world tasks**: Tasks like preparing tea or tidying a room involve sequential interactions with multiple objects, precise manipulation, and dynamic environments, which quickly make end-to-end policies infeasible due to scaling complexity.
- **Impractical data requirements**: Traditional imitation learning methods demand an impractically large number of demonstrations to cover all possible task variations, making real-world deployment costly and time-consuming.
- **Limitations of current representations**: Image-based inputs and global coordinate frames struggle with generalization because they either capture excessive, task-irrelevant information (images) or fail to account for relative spatial variations (global frames), leading to poor performance when faced with novel scenarios.
- **Challenges of policy composition**: While task decomposition into sub-policies is a promising strategy, existing methods struggle with distributing shifts between sub-policies and require complex arbitration mechanisms to decide when to switch tasks.

By introducing oriented affordance frames and a self-progress prediction mechanism, the authors are motivated to overcome these challenges, thereby making imitation learning a more practical and scalable solution for deploying dexterous robots in diverse and complex real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology lies in replacing conventional high-dimensional state representations (images, point clouds) with a highly structured, abstract, and object-centric representation called **Oriented Affordance Frames (OAFs)**. This is coupled with a robust perception pipeline and a novel policy arbitration mechanism.

#### 3.1. Task Decomposition and Structured Representation

The approach begins by decomposing long-horizon, multi-object tasks into smaller, functionally distinct **affordance-aligned sub-tasks**. Each sub-task is centered around a specific interaction with an object (e.g., "grasping a cup," "pouring tea").

For each sub-task, the method defines two key local reference frames:
- **Affordance Frame**: Anchored at a task-relevant functional region on an object (e.g., the handle of a cup for grasping, the spout of a teapot for pouring).
- **Tool Frame**: Located either on the robot's end-effector (for direct interaction) or on a tool held by the robot (e.g., the scoop of a spoon).

The central innovation is the **Oriented Affordance Frame (OAF)**. An OAF is derived by taking the affordance frame and rotating it such that one of its axes (consistently the x-axis, termed the "funnel axis") points towards the origin of the tool frame *at the initiation of the task*. Crucially, while the OAF's origin can move if the object is manipulated, its orientation remains anchored relative to that initial tool frame direction. This anchoring ensures that all demonstrated trajectories within an OAF tend to align along a consistent axis, independent of the absolute spatial configuration of the robot or object.

#### 3.2. State and Action Representation

- **State (s_t)**: The policy's input state comprises the current pose of the tool frame relative to the OAF ($^{o-aff}T_{tool}$), a binary gripper state, and the target object's rotation relative to its anchored OAF ($^{o-aff}R_{aff}$). Rotations are represented as 6D vectors for continuity. This compact, 16-dimensional state representation significantly reduces the dimensionality and task-irrelevant information compared to raw sensor inputs.
- **Action (a_t)**: The policy outputs a sequence of $N=16$ desired next poses of the robot's end-effector (or tool frame) in the OAF, along with a sequence of gripper actions.

#### 3.3. Perception Pipeline (for Marker-Free Deployment)

To enable real-world application without fiducial markers, the authors developed a perception pipeline leveraging recent advances in vision foundation models (Appendix A.1, Fig. 9):
1. **Affordance Frame Detection**: At the start of a sub-task, given an external camera image and a user-provided object name (e.g., "teapot") and three reference points for the affordance:
 - **Grounding DINO** detects a bounding box for the object.
 - **SAM** generates a segmentation mask.
 - **CSM Cube's Image-to-3D model** generates a textured mesh.
 - **Foundation Pose** estimates the object's pose relative to its mesh center.
 - **DINO-ViT features** are extracted from the image and matched against features of the human-provided reference points to transform the mesh-centric pose to the specific affordance region.
2. **Affordance Frame Tracking**: Once initialized, Foundation Pose continuously tracks the object's pose (and thus the affordance frame) at ~20 Hz, updating the OAF for policy execution. For multi-object tasks, all affordances are initialized and tracked in parallel.

#### 3.4. Policy Learning and Arbitration

- **Policy Learning**: The authors use **Diffusion Policy**, a state-of-the-art behavior cloning algorithm, to learn each sub-policy. Diffusion Policy models the conditional action distribution using a denoising diffusion probabilistic model, which helps represent the multi-modality often present in human demonstrations. Each policy is trained for 4500 epochs.
- **Policy Arbitration by Self-Progress Prediction**: To enable autonomous switching between sub-policies, each policy is augmented to predict a continuous **self-progress indicator** ($a_{progress} \in$). During training, this value is computed by linearly interpolating from 0 to 1 over the demonstration trajectory duration. During execution, when a policy's predicted progress exceeds a predefined threshold, the system automatically transitions to the next sub-policy in the task sequence. This eliminates the need for a separate, complex arbitration policy.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted using a Franka Panda manipulator arm with Intel Realsense cameras. Demonstrations were collected via a GELLO teleoperation device. Evaluations were performed using both ArUco markers (for ground-truth pose and isolating the impact of OAFs) and the full marker-free vision pipeline. Tasks focused on a multi-step "tea-serving" scenario, along with "coffee making" and "shoe racking" for qualitative evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments presented in the paper rigorously validate the effectiveness of the proposed oriented affordance frames and the overall methodology across several key dimensions:

- **Exceptional Sample Efficiency**: The method achieved high success rates with an unprecedentedly small number of demonstrations. For the primary "tea-serving" task, the policies learned from *just 10 demonstrations per sub-task* achieved an average of 90.9% success rate across seven base tasks. This performance significantly surpassed baselines, with composite tasks (e.g., "cup rearrangement," "utilize teapot") still showing high success (78.8% average) and the full "serve tea" task reaching 81.8% success.
- **Superior Performance over Baselines**:
 - **State Representation Comparison**: Our method (Oriented Affordance Frame) drastically outperformed end-effector-centric (48.1% avg. for base tasks) and global-frame (59.7% avg. for base tasks) representations in in-distribution scenarios (Table 1).
 - **Image-Based Policy Comparison**: For the "cup rearrangement" task, an image-based policy required 305 demonstrations to achieve similar performance to our method's 10 demonstrations, demonstrating a **30-fold improvement in data efficiency**.
- **Critical Role of Orientation**: An ablation study comparing oriented vs. non-oriented affordance frames showed that the "oriented" component is vital. Removing the orientation aspect significantly reduced success rates (nearly halved at 10 demos) and prevented policies from reaching high performance even with 50 demonstrations, underscoring the importance of aligning the reference frame with the tool.
- **Robust Spatial Generalization**: Policies trained with oriented affordance frames demonstrated strong generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) spatial configurations. Even when objects were placed in entirely new parts of the workspace, the method achieved 83.1% average success for base tasks and 63.6% for the full tea-serving task. In stark contrast, policies using global frames completely failed (0% success) in OOD scenarios.
- **Effective Intra-Category Generalization**: The method successfully generalized policies to novel objects unseen during training, exhibiting significant variations in appearance and geometry (Fig. 7, 8). For example, policies trained on a single teacup and teapot successfully handled 8 out of 10 unseen teacups and all 3 unseen teapots for tasks like "cup rearrangement" and "utilize teapot." This capability is attributed to the abstract, affordance-centric representation and the robust perception pipeline.
- **Seamless Task Composition via Self-Progress Prediction**: The continuous self-progress prediction signal proved highly reliable for autonomous policy arbitration. It enabled smooth transitions between sub-policies for composite tasks and the full "tea-serving" sequence without external supervision or an additional arbitration policy. Furthermore, it demonstrated robustness to external disturbances, correctly decreasing and then increasing as the robot recovered from perturbations (Fig. 14).
- **Practicality of Marker-Free Perception**: The integrated vision pipeline, utilizing large vision models, demonstrated high efficacy. For the "cup rearrangement" task, switching from ground-truth ArUco markers to the marker-free pipeline resulted in only a minimal decrease in performance (8/10 success with markers vs. 7/10 with vision pipeline), with the single additional failure attributed to a tracking error. This validates the real-world deployability of the system without special fiducials.
- **Applicability to Mobile Manipulation and Closed-Loop Control**: Supplementary experiments demonstrated that the policy's invariance to the robot's base position allows for continued operation even with a moving mobile base (Fig. 10). The use of Diffusion Policy in conjunction with OAFs also enabled learning of robust closed-loop policies for tasks beyond simple pick-and-place, such as pushing, exhibiting reactivity to object perturbations (Fig. 11).

In summary, the main findings confirm that oriented affordance frames, combined with a capable perception system and self-progress prediction, effectively address the critical challenges of sample efficiency and generalization in robotic manipulation, enabling learning of complex skills from extremely limited data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in robotic manipulation and imitation learning, with substantial potential impact across several domains:

- **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Dexterity**: The most immediate impact is the dramatic reduction in data requirements for learning complex robotic skills. By enabling robust policy learning from just 10 demonstrations, the method makes it far more practical and less costly to teach robots new, dexterous behaviors for intricate, multi-object tasks. This directly addresses one of the biggest bottlenecks in deploying robots in diverse, unstructured environments like homes or dynamic industrial settings.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities**: The core innovation of oriented affordance frames fundamentally improves generalization across three crucial dimensions:
 - **Spatial Generalization**: Robots can adapt to arbitrary object placements and robot start configurations.
 - **Intra-Category Generalization**: Policies transfer successfully to novel instances of objects (different appearances, geometries) within the same functional category.
 - **Compositional Generalization**: Complex, long-horizon tasks can be solved by seamlessly combining independently learned sub-skills.
 These combined generalization capabilities are paramount for truly autonomous and adaptive robotic systems.
- **Paradigm Shift in State Representation**: The work advocates for a shift from raw, high-dimensional sensor inputs (images, point clouds) to intelligent, structured, and abstracted representations. This re-allocates complexity, moving the burden from demanding policy learning algorithms (which struggle with irrelevant visual details) to a dedicated perception system focused on extracting task-relevant geometric and semantic information. This is a powerful conceptual contribution that could guide future research in robot learning.
- **Leveraging Vision Foundation Models for Robotics**: The paper demonstrates a practical and effective pipeline for integrating cutting-edge generalist vision foundation models (Grounding DINO, SAM, Foundation Pose) into a real-time robotic perception system. This shows how advances in broader AI can be directly translated into tangible benefits for robotics, particularly for marker-free object detection and tracking, which is essential for real-world deployment.
- **Enabling Complex Domestic and Service Robotics**: The demonstrated ability to autonomously perform multi-step, multi-object tasks like serving tea, making coffee, or racking shoes with high success rates from minimal data directly addresses the requirements for future domestic and service robots. These tasks require precise, sequential interactions and adaptability to varying environments, which this method successfully provides.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work encourages further exploration into structured representations, incorporating strong priors, and exploiting compositionality in behavior cloning. It lays the groundwork for more scalable and generalizable robotic learning algorithms, potentially inspiring new hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of data-driven learning with knowledge-based representations.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration and Teaching**: With reduced demonstration requirements, humans can more easily teach robots new skills, making robot programming more intuitive and accessible, fostering better human-robot collaboration.

In conclusion, "Learning from 10 Demos" offers a compelling solution to fundamental challenges in robotic learning. By introducing an elegant, task-centric representation and integrating advanced perception, the authors have significantly advanced the state of the art in sample-efficient and generalizable robotic manipulation, paving the way for more autonomous, adaptable, and deployable robotic systems in the real world.
