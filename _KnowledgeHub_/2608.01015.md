# KING: Embodiment-Aware Kinematic Graph Neural Network for Unified Motion Representation of Legged and Wheeled Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> KING, an embodiment-aware Kinematic Graph Neural Network, unifies motion representation for legged and wheeled robots to provide accurate odometry estimation. The model demonstrates effective generalization to unseen robot morphologies and achieves sim-to-real transfer with only one minute of real-world adaptation data.

## Problem

- Traditional odometry methods struggle with featureless environments (exteroceptive sensors), accumulating drift (IMUs), and accounting for complex robot-terrain interactions like slip (kinematic models).
- Existing learning-based kinematic models for odometry often lack generalization across diverse robot embodiments, requiring re-training for different morphologies or parameter changes due to varying input dimensionality.
- A unified approach was needed to represent the kinematics of fundamentally different robot types (wheeled and legged) under a single learning model.

## Method

- Developed **KING**, a Kinematic Graph Neural Network, by establishing a ==unified kinematic model== where wheeled robot kinematics are interpreted as a special case of a 1-DOF manipulator.
- Encoded robot embodiments as a ==heterogeneous graph== with Base, Joint, and Contact nodes, integrating time-series proprioceptive measurements and kinematic parameters.
- Trained KING as a ==message-passing GNN== using a node-regression approach to predict body twist and contact poses, leveraging an automatic simulation data collection pipeline with diverse morphologies and ==domain randomization==.

## Results

- Cross-class transfer experiments empirically validated the unified kinematic formulation, showing improved performance (reduced RMSE) when training on one robot class and evaluating on another.
- KING demonstrated generalization to unseen robot morphologies in zero-shot scenarios and achieved substantial performance improvements with minimal (e.g., **0.1%** or approximately **1 minute**) few-shot adaptation data.
- Successful sim-to-real transfer was achieved on unseen real 4WD skid-steering and quadruped robots with only **1 minute** of real-world fine-tuning data, providing accurate odometry and outperforming model-based kinematic methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The kinematics of wheeled robots can be conceptually unified with legged robots by interpreting a wheel as a 1-DOF manipulator, allowing a common graph representation for both types.
> - Explicitly encoding robot embodiment and kinematic structure into a graph enables a single GNN to learn shared underlying motion principles across diverse morphologies and varying parameters.
> - Training with domain randomization on embodiment parameters and incorporating time-series sensor data enhances model robustness to noise and facilitates effective sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robust odometry estimation is a fundamental requirement for autonomous navigation systems in robotics. Traditional methods for odometry often rely on fusing data from exteroceptive sensors (e.g., LiDAR, cameras), Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs), and kinematic models. However, each of these sensing modalities presents limitations. Exteroceptive sensors struggle in featureless environments where feature matching fails, while IMUs accumulate drift over time, particularly from double integration of linear accelerations. Kinematic models, which use proprioceptive measurements like encoder data, can offer reliable motion constraints with less integration effort than IMUs. Yet, conventional kinematic models are often insufficient to capture complex, terrain-dependent phenomena such as wheel or foot slip and ground deformation, which can lead to estimation inaccuracies.

The field of learning-based kinematic models has emerged to address these limitations by implicitly modeling complex robot-terrain interactions, thereby achieving more accurate odometry estimation than purely model-based approaches. However, a significant challenge within this area is the generalization of these learned models. Most existing learning-based models are typically trained on a single robot embodiment (morphology and continuous kinematic parameters like wheel radii or link lengths) and tend to perform poorly when applied to different robots or even the same robot with modified parameters. This lack of generalization stems from the varying meanings and dimensionality of low-level proprioceptive measurements across diverse embodiments, including differences in the number of joints and ground-contact elements (e.g., wheels, feet).

Prior research aimed at improving generalization has explored various avenues. For terrain generalization, methods have adapted online to current ground conditions after pretraining on diverse surfaces. For cross-embodiment learning, many approaches rely on higher-level abstractions like body velocity from SLAM or end-effector motions in local frames, rather than directly sharing low-level motion representations. These methods often avoid explicitly encoding embodiment-specific parameters or joint-space states. Some approaches, such as LocoFormer, unify observations into fixed-length vectors but do not explicitly encode structural embodiment information. In contrast, methods like NerveNet and GCNT represent robot morphology as a graph to condition policies on structure, but primarily focus on legged robots and do not fully integrate low-level motion data in a unified way across fundamentally different robot types.

This work directly addresses the challenge of embodiment generalization for learning-based kinematic models by proposing a unified representation for both wheeled and legged robots. It aims to develop a single learning-based model that can operate across diverse robot morphologies and embodiment parameters by explicitly incorporating the robot's kinematic structure and proprioceptive measurements into a common graph representation, thereby overcoming the limitations of previous methods regarding variable input dimensionality and robot-specific training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning-based kinematic model, named KING (Kinematic Graph Neural Network), that provides a unified motion representation for both legged and wheeled robots, enabling accurate odometry estimation across diverse robot embodiments.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges in autonomous robotics:

1. **Enhancing Odometry Robustness:** Reliable odometry estimation is critical for autonomous navigation. While exteroceptive sensors (LiDAR, cameras) provide robust cues in feature-rich environments, their performance degrades in featureless settings. IMUs, while providing continuous motion data, suffer from integration drift over time. Kinematic models offer an alternative, relatively stable source of motion constraints from proprioceptive sensors (encoders), which are less prone to drift than IMUs and independent of external features. However, traditional kinematic models often fail to account for complex, nonlinear robot-terrain interactions, such as wheel or foot slip, and ground deformation.

2. **Overcoming Generalization Limitations of Existing Learning-Based Kinematic Models:** Learning-based kinematic models have demonstrated superior accuracy by implicitly modeling complex interactions. However, a significant drawback is their limited generalization. These models are typically trained and validated on a single robot embodiment and terrain type. The input representations (e.g., number of joints, contact elements, specific kinematic parameters) vary drastically across different robot designs, making direct transfer or generalization difficult. Retraining a model from scratch for every new robot platform or even minor changes in embodiment parameters is resource-intensive and impractical. The current state of the art largely relies on high-level abstractions or avoids directly handling low-level, embodiment-dependent information when attempting cross-embodiment learning.

3. **Establishing a Unified Kinematic Representation:** A core motivation is the theoretical and practical need to bridge the gap between seemingly distinct robot types—wheeled and legged. If a common underlying kinematic formulation can be established, it would provide a principled basis for developing a single, generalized learning model that covers both. This unified representation would allow motion data from different robot classes to contribute to learning a shared underlying structure, rather than being treated as disparate.

4. **Enabling Efficient Adaptation to New Embodiments:** The goal is to reduce the operational burden of deploying learned kinematic models on new robot platforms. By designing a model that can leverage a shared understanding of kinematics, the authors aim to achieve few-shot learning, where a pretrained model can be rapidly adapted to an unseen robot morphology with a minimal amount of new data, avoiding the need for extensive data collection and full retraining.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on developing KING, an embodiment-aware Kinematic Graph Neural Network (GNN), which unifies the kinematic representation of wheeled and legged robots through a common graph structure.

**A. Unified Kinematic Model Formulation:**
The paper first establishes a theoretical basis by demonstrating that kinematic models for both wheeled and legged robots can be expressed within a unified framework, specifically leveraging the kinematics of robot manipulators.
- **Legged Robots:** The kinematics of a leg are described similarly to a serial manipulator, where the body translational velocity is related to the j-th foot (end-effector) velocity via its Jacobian matrix and joint angular velocities ($L v_B = -J_j \dot{\theta}_j$). This equation holds under assumptions of no slip and rigid ground.
- **Wheeled Robots:** For a wheeled robot, the body translational velocity is related to the j-th wheel velocity by its radius and angular velocity ($W v_B = -r \dot{\theta}_j$).
- **Equivalence Derivation:** The paper demonstrates that the wheel kinematic model can be interpreted as a special case of a 1-DOF manipulator (a 1-DOF leg). By taking the time derivative of a 1-DOF manipulator's end-effector position and setting the joint angle to zero (representing ground contact) and the link length to the wheel radius, the resulting end-effector velocity expression becomes equivalent to the wheel kinematic model. This conceptual unification allows both types of robots to be represented within a common kinematic framework.

**B. Graph Structure with Unified Kinematic Representation:**
Based on this unified formulation, robot embodiments are encoded as a heterogeneous graph, suitable for processing by a GNN.
- **Nodes:** The graph comprises three types of nodes:
 - **Base Node:** Represents the robot's main body. Its features include the 6-DOF relative pose between the base and IMU frames, and time series (current and k past steps) of linear accelerations and angular velocities from the IMU.
 - **Joint Nodes:** Represent the robot's actuated joints. Each m-th Joint node's features include the initial 6-DOF relative pose from its parent joint frame, and time series (current and k past steps) of its joint angle and angular velocity (represented as 3D vectors to encode rotation axis). For wheel joints, the joint angle is set to 0 to align with the 1-DOF manipulator analogy.
 - **Contact Nodes:** Represent ground-contact elements (wheels or feet). Each n-th Contact node's features include the 6-DOF relative pose from the adjacent leaf joint frame, a contact size parameter ($r_c$, e.g., wheel radius or foot sphere radius), and a time series (current and k past steps) of a binary contact state (1 for contact, 0 for no contact).
- **Edges:** Edges connect nodes according to the robot's kinematic connectivity, reflecting the physical links between the base, joints, and contact points.
- **Input Features:** To enhance robustness against sensor noise, time series data (current and k=2 previous time steps) for all sensor measurements are concatenated into each node's feature vector.

**C. Training KING via Message-Passing Node Regression:**
KING is trained as a message-passing-based GNN, designed as a node-regression problem.
- **Encoding:** Input node features are first mapped into latent embeddings using type-specific linear encoders (E_B, E_J, E_C), then projected into a 90-dimensional GNN hidden space.
- **Message Passing:** Node embeddings are iteratively updated through L layers of message passing. Each node aggregates messages from its adjacent nodes, transformed by learnable weight matrices (e.g., $W_B^{(l)}$, $W_{J \to B}^{(l)}$), and combines them with its own transformed embedding. A permutation-invariant aggregation operator (e.g., mean) is used. This process allows the model to naturally handle variable graph structures (different numbers of nodes/edges).
- **Decoding:** After L layers, the final embeddings of the Base node and Contact nodes are decoded by type-specific linear decoders ($D_B$, $D_C$).
- **Outputs:** The GNN outputs two main predictions:
 - A 3-DOF body twist ($\xi_t$) at the Base node (translational and rotational velocity).
 - The 6-DOF poses of all N contact elements in the base frame ($\{^B T_{C_n,t}\}_{n=1}^N$).
- **Loss Function:** The model is trained using a composite loss function, L, which is a sum of weighted mean squared errors (MSEs) for the base twist loss ($L_\xi$) and the contact pose loss ($L_c$). For $L_\xi$, an uncertainty-based weighting method is employed to balance translational and rotational components and prevent trivial solutions. For $L_c$, constant weights are used. The AdamW optimizer is used for training.

**D. Automatic Data Collection for Diverse Robot Embodiments:**
To overcome the challenge of acquiring large-scale real-world data for diverse robots, an automatic data collection pipeline is implemented in a physics simulator (Gazebo).
- **Morphologies:** Data is collected from six distinct robot morphologies: 2WD/4WD skid-steering, 3WD/4WD Ackermann-steering, quadruped, and hexapod robots. These morphologies vary in wheel configurations and leg joint structures (e.g., mammal-like vs. insect-like legs).
- **Domain Randomization:** To further increase data diversity and improve generalization, embodiment parameters (e.g., link lengths, wheel radii, IMU placement) are randomized for all robots within each morphology.
- **Motion Data:** Robots are commanded to execute diverse planar motion trajectories (straight, turning, slalom, acceleration/deceleration) to cover a wide range of dynamic behaviors.
- **Data Augmentation:** Noise is added to all proprioceptive measurements and embodiment parameters during data collection to enhance the model's robustness and mitigate the sim-to-real gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of KING focused on validating the unified kinematic representation, assessing generalization to unseen morphologies, and demonstrating sim-to-real transfer.

**A. Validation of the Unified Kinematic Model Representation:**
To empirically support the derived unified formulation, cross-class transfer experiments were conducted.
- **Experiment Setup:** The dataset was split into 'wheeled' (skid-steer and Ackermann robots) and 'legged' (quadruped and hexapod robots) classes. KING was trained primarily on one class and evaluated on the other, while progressively increasing the proportion of source-class data (0%, 10%, 20%). A minimal amount (0.01%) of target-class data was included in all settings to ensure contact-state pattern observation.
- **Results (Table I):** Increasing the amount of source-class training data (either wheeled or legged) consistently led to a reduction in the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for both translational and rotational twists on the target class. For instance, when training primarily on wheeled data and evaluating on legged, RMSEs decreased from 0.206 m/s and 0.184 rad/s (0% source data) to 0.160 m/s and 0.051 rad/s (20% source data). Similar improvements were observed when training primarily on legged data and evaluating on wheeled.
- **Conclusion:** These results indicate positive transfer between wheeled and legged robot data, confirming that their kinematic models are compatible under the proposed common graph representation. This empirically validates the theoretical unified kinematic model derived in the paper.

**B. Cross-validation on Unseen Robot Morphologies:**
The generalization capability of KING to entirely unseen robot morphologies was assessed using a leave-one-morphology-out cross-validation strategy.
- **Experiment Setup:** One morphology was held out as the target, and KING was pretrained on the remaining five. Performance was evaluated in zero-shot (K=0%) and few-shot adaptation (K=0.1%, 0.5%, 1% of target data for fine-tuning) settings. A "using all morphologies" baseline (trained on 20% of data from all morphologies) was included to evaluate generalization to unseen embodiment parameters within known morphologies.
- **Results (Table II):**
 - **Zero-shot (K=0%):** KING showed varying performance. Simpler wheeled morphologies (2WD skid-steer: 0.074 m/s, 0.007 rad/s; 3WD Ackermann: 0.060 m/s, 0.004 rad/s) achieved lower errors, possibly due to fewer nonlinear effects compared to more complex morphologies like the quadruped (0.210 m/s, 0.011 rad/s).
 - **Few-shot Adaptation:** Even with a minimal amount of target data (K=0.1%, approximately 1 minute), substantial performance gains were observed across all morphologies. For example, for the 2WD skid-steer, RMSEs dropped from 0.074 m/s to 0.038 m/s translational and 0.007 rad/s to 0.005 rad/s rotational. Similar significant improvements were seen for other morphologies, often closely approaching the "using all morphologies" baseline. With K=0.5% (5 minutes of data), performance generally improved further and began to saturate.
 - **Generalization within Morphologies:** The "using all morphologies" baseline consistently showed low RMSEs, indicating that KING effectively generalizes to variations in embodiment parameters even within morphologies it has previously encountered during training.
- **Conclusion:** KING demonstrates effective generalization to unseen robot morphologies and can rapidly adapt to them with minimal target-domain data through few-shot fine-tuning.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer to Unseen Real Robots:**
To demonstrate practical applicability, KING was evaluated on real robots whose morphologies were excluded from the simulation pretraining dataset.
- **Experiment Setup:** Two real-world testbeds were used: a 4WD skid-steering robot (Rover Robotics Rover Mini) and a quadruped robot (Unitree Go1). KING was pretrained on simulation data *excluding* these morphologies, then fine-tuned with approximately 1 minute of real-world motion data collected on a flat indoor floor for each robot. A LiDAR-IMU SLAM system provided reference trajectories. A model-based analytic kinematic odometry served as a baseline.
- **Results (Table III, Fig. 6):**
 - **Accuracy:** For the 4WD skid-steering robot, KING achieved RMSEs of 0.038 m/s (translational) and 0.022 rad/s (rotational). For the quadruped, RMSEs were 0.049 m/s (translational) and 0.026 rad/s (rotational). These real-world accuracies were comparable to the "using all morphologies" simulation results for corresponding robot types, suggesting successful sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Trajectory Comparison (Fig. 6):** Visual comparison of estimated trajectories showed that the model-based baseline exhibited noticeable drift, likely due to unmodeled slip and contact effects. In contrast, KING's trajectories remained considerably closer to the reference SLAM trajectories, indicating its ability to better capture these complex interactions.
- **Conclusion:** The results demonstrate that KING, with few-shot sim-to-real adaptation (using only 1 minute of real data), can be effectively transferred from simulation to real robots with previously unseen morphologies, providing accurate odometry estimation and outperforming conventional model-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robot odometry and learning-based kinematic models, offering several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Unified Kinematic Representation:** The most substantial contribution is the establishment and empirical validation of a unified kinematic model formulation for both wheeled and legged robots. By demonstrating that both can be represented within a common framework based on manipulator kinematics, the work provides a principled basis for designing generalizable learning models across diverse mobile robot types.

2. **Enhanced Generalization and Adaptability:** KING addresses a critical limitation of previous learning-based kinematic models—their poor generalization across different robot embodiments. By explicitly encoding embodiment information into a graph structure, the model can learn shared underlying kinematic principles. This enables robust zero-shot inference on new morphologies and, more importantly, efficient few-shot adaptation with minimal data (demonstrated to be as little as one minute of real-world data). This capability drastically reduces the data collection and retraining effort typically required for deploying learned models on new robot platforms.

3. **Improved Odometry Robustness:** The proposed model offers a method for accurate odometry estimation, particularly in challenging environments where exteroceptive sensors may fail (e.g., featureless terrains) and IMU integration suffers from drift. By implicitly capturing nonlinear robot-terrain interactions (like slip), KING surpasses the performance of traditional model-based kinematic odometry, contributing to more reliable autonomous navigation.

4. **Practical Deployment Efficiency:** The ability to perform effective sim-to-real transfer with minimal real-world fine-tuning means that kinematic models can be predominantly developed and pretrained in simulation, then rapidly deployed on physical robots. This accelerates the development cycle and lowers the cost associated with real-world robot experimentation and data collection.

5. **Foundation for Future Robot Intelligence:** KING lays groundwork for the concept of "odometry foundation models." The authors propose extending KING with online learning to adapt to heterogeneous robot embodiments *and* varying terrain conditions. This suggests a future where a single foundational model could provide robust odometry for a vast array of robotic systems operating in diverse, unstructured environments, moving towards more versatile and adaptable robotic intelligence.
