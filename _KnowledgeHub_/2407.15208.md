# Flow as the Cross-Domain Manipulation Interface

> [!summary] Summary
> Im2Flow2Act introduces object flow as a cross-domain manipulation interface, enabling robots to learn real-world skills from human demonstration videos and simulated robot play data without requiring real-world robot training. The framework achieved up to 90% success rates on diverse manipulation tasks involving rigid, articulated, and deformable objects in real-world environments.

## Problem

- Scaling robot skill acquisition is challenging due to the high cost and time required for collecting real-world robot training data.
- Bridging the 'embodiment gap' between human demonstration videos and robot kinematics, and the 'sim-to-real gap' between simulated and real environments, remains a barrier for effective policy transfer.
- Existing manipulation interfaces often struggle to represent diverse tasks involving rigid, articulated, and deformable objects simultaneously, limiting versatility.

## Method

- The paper proposes Im2Flow2Act, a two-stage framework that uses 'object flow' (motion of manipulated objects) as a unifying, cross-domain, and cross-embodiment manipulation interface.
- A flow generation network (Im2Flow) learns to produce complete object-centric task flows from action-less human demonstration videos, leveraging pre-trained vision models like Stable Diffusion.
- A flow-conditioned imitation learning policy (Flow2Act) translates the generated object flow into robot actions, trained entirely on task-agnostic simulated robot play data using a diffusion policy and a temporal alignment module.

## Results

- Im2Flow2Act achieved real-world success rates of 90% for Pick&Place, 80% for Pouring, 85% for Drawer opening, and 70% for Cloth folding in language-conditioned settings, using no real-world robot training data.
- The framework consistently outperformed baselines, demonstrating an average 30% higher success rate than grid-flow methods (like ATM) in simulation, confirming the efficacy of object-centric flow for cross-embodiment learning.
- Ablation studies showed that the temporal alignment module was essential for robust real-world performance, improving success rates for tasks like Pick&Place from 55% to 95% in demonstration-conditioned settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object-centric flow serves as an embodiment-agnostic interface, allowing robots to learn from human demonstration videos and general simulated robot data without requiring real-world robot training data.
> - A learning-based policy, rather than heuristic methods, is necessary for translating the generated object flow into robust robot actions, particularly for articulated and deformable objects, and for handling real-world noise.
> - A temporal alignment module is crucial for adapting the policy, trained on diverse simulated exploration data, to the varied speeds and execution patterns observed in human demonstrations or real-world scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is actively pursuing methods to scale up skill acquisition beyond expensive and time-consuming real-world robot data collection. Two prominent, yet challenging, directions have emerged:
1. **Learning from Cross-Embodiment Videos:** This involves leveraging readily available data, such as human demonstration videos, to teach robots. The main hurdle is the "embodiment gap," meaning the visual appearance and kinematics of a human demonstrator are vastly different from a robot, making direct action transfer difficult.
2. **Learning from Simulated Robot Data:** This offers a cost-effective alternative by training policies in virtual environments. However, it faces the "sim-to-real gap," where discrepancies in visual fidelity, physics, and dynamics between simulation and the real world hinder direct deployment. Furthermore, building task-specific simulated environments, especially for contact-rich manipulation, is complex and labor-intensive.

Prior work has attempted to mitigate these gaps through various strategies, including visual pretraining, learning reward functions, extracting affordances, hand pose detection, and domain translation. However, many of these approaches still require some form of in-domain robot data (e.g., teleoperation data) to bridge the remaining discrepancies. Flow-based methods have also gained traction, particularly for articulated or deformable object manipulation, but often rely on heuristic policies, real-world teleoperation, or are limited to specific tasks. For example, ATM uses grid flow for manipulation but still requires in-domain robot data to align the generated flow distributions.

"Im2Flow2Act" positions itself at the intersection of these challenges by proposing a novel framework that *circumvents the need for real-world robot training data entirely*. It addresses both the embodiment gap and the sim-to-real gap by introducing **object flow** as a unifying, cross-domain, and cross-embodiment manipulation interface. This is a significant departure from approaches that attempt to directly translate visual observations or actions across domains. By focusing on the *motion of the manipulated object itself*, rather than the agent's motion or appearance, the framework abstracts away the differences in embodiment and environment. This allows it to leverage two distinct, cost-effective data sources: action-less human videos for high-level task understanding (flow generation) and task-agnostic simulated robot play data for low-level execution (flow-conditioned policy). The work represents a crucial step towards truly scalable robot learning, where robots can acquire diverse skills from readily available, unannotated data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of Im2Flow2Act is to enable robots to acquire a wide range of real-world manipulation skills efficiently and scalably, *without requiring any real-world robot training data*. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Scalability:** Collecting real-world robot data is notoriously expensive, time-consuming, and often requires expert teleoperators. This severely limits the diversity and quantity of tasks robots can learn. The authors aim to bypass this bottleneck by leveraging readily available, cheaper data sources.
- **Bridging Domain Gaps:** The inherent differences between human bodies and robot manipulators (embodiment gap), and between simulated and real environments (sim-to-real gap), pose significant challenges for direct policy transfer. The core motivation is to find an intermediate representation that is robust to these domain shifts.
- **Expressiveness and Versatility:** Current manipulation interfaces often struggle to represent diverse tasks involving rigid, articulated, and deformable objects simultaneously. The authors seek an interface capable of capturing the complex motion dynamics required for such varied manipulations.
- **Interpretability:** Unlike abstract vector-based state representations, a physically intuitive interface can facilitate human-robot interaction and debugging.

To achieve these motivations, the paper proposes "object flow" as the central unifying interface. Its key benefits, which form the rationale for its selection, are highlighted:
- **Embodiment Agnostic:** Object flow describes *object state changes* rather than agent actions, making it independent of the specific manipulator.
- **Minimal Sim-to-Real Gap:** By focusing on motion dynamics rather than visual appearance, flow reduces the visual inconsistencies between sim and real, aiding generalization.
- **Expressive Task Descriptions:** Object flow can represent complex changes like object pose, articulation, and deformation, enabling a single policy for diverse tasks.
- **Interpretability:** Its physical meaning makes it suitable for human understanding and interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Im2Flow2Act comprises two main, independently trained components that operate sequentially during inference: a flow generation network and a flow-conditioned policy. The central idea is to use **object flow**—the motion of manipulated objects, explicitly excluding background or embodiment movement—as the universal interface.

#### 3.1. Flow Generation Network (Im2Flow)

This network is responsible for high-level task planning by learning from cross-embodiment videos (human demonstrations). Its goal is to generate a *complete object-centric task flow* for the entire task.

- **Input:** Initial RGB scene image and a natural language task description.
- **Process:**
 - **Object Detection and Keypoint Initialization:** Grounding DINO first detects the object of interest and provides its bounding box. Uniform sampling within this bounding box generates initial keypoints (a rectangular flow image `F0` representing (u,v) coordinates and visibility). For multiple objects, keypoints are sampled proportionally to bounding box areas.
 - **Flow Sequence Generation:** TAPIR is used to generate rectangular flow sequences `F1` (keypoint trajectories over time) from demonstration videos.
 - **Latent Space Compression:** To improve computational efficiency and leverage strong image priors, object flow is compressed into a compact latent space using a finetuned autoencoder (AE) adapted from Stable Diffusion (SD). The SD encoder `E` is fixed, and the decoder `D` is finetuned for flow images.
 - **Video Diffusion Model:** The core flow generator is built upon Animatediff, which inflates a pre-trained Stable Diffusion U-Net along the temporal axis. This model, conditioned on the initial RGB frame and task description, learns to generate the future object flow in the latent space. Only LoRA layers are inserted into the SD model, with motion modules trained from scratch, enabling efficient adaptation.
 - **Post-processing:** Generated flows are processed by **motion filters** (e.g., removing static background points) and SAM filters to retain only keypoints truly on the object, producing the "object-centric task flow."

- **Key Design Choices & Benefits:**
 - **Complete Task Flow Generation:** Generating the full flow at the beginning, rather than step-by-step, avoids issues where the agent (human or robot) occludes the object during execution, minimizing visual appearance gaps.
 - **Object-Centric Flow:** By focusing *only* on the object's motion, the generated flow is independent of the embodiment (human vs. robot), significantly reducing the embodiment gap and allowing training solely on human videos without requiring robot-specific real-world data.
 - **Leveraging Pre-trained Models:** Utilizing large-scale pre-trained models like Stable Diffusion and Animatediff allows the system to benefit from their strong content priors and temporal modeling capabilities, leading to more efficient and robust training.

#### 3.2. Flow-Conditioned Imitation Learning Policy (Flow2Act)

This policy focuses on low-level execution, mapping the generated object flow to robot actions. It is trained *entirely on simulated robot play data*, which is easier to collect and more diverse than task-specific simulations.

- **Input:** The complete task flow (`F0:T`) from the flow generation network, the current state representation (`st`), and robot proprioception (`ρt`). The state `st` is derived from online tracked keypoint locations (`ft`) and initial 3D keypoint coordinates (`x0`).
- **Components:**
 - **Online Point Tracking:** TAPIR is used during inference to track the initial object keypoints on the real-time robot observations, providing `ft`.
 - **State Encoder (`ϕ`):** A transformer-based network processes the `N` current 2D keypoint locations (`ft`) and their corresponding initial 3D coordinates (`x0`) to generate a compact state representation `st`. Initial 3D coordinates provide robustness against noisy 2D flow.
 - **Temporal Alignment Module (`ψ`):** This crucial transformer-based module predicts the *remaining* task flow (`zt`) by comparing the complete task flow (`F0:T`) with the current keypoint locations (`ft`) and robot proprioception (`ρt`). This enables the policy to track task progress and condition its actions on the relevant future trajectory, even if the robot's execution speed differs from the demonstration. It's trained with an L2 loss against a ground truth latent representation of the remaining flow.
 - **Diffusion Action Head:** A diffusion policy takes the predicted remaining task flow (`zt`), the current state (`st`), and proprioception (`ρt`) as input to output a sequence of robot actions (6-DOF end-effector pose and 1-DOF gripper state). Diffusion policies are known for their ability to model complex, multi-modal action distributions.

- **Training Data:** Simulated exploration data collected using a UR5e robot with predefined random heuristic actions across rigid, articulated, and deformable object environments. This "task-less" exploration data enables the policy to learn the general relationship between actions and resulting object movements.

By integrating these two components, Im2Flow2Act creates a cohesive system that acquires skills from heterogeneous data sources without requiring costly real-world robot training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Im2Flow2Act was evaluated on four diverse real-world tasks involving rigid (pick-and-place, pouring), articulated (drawer opening), and deformable (cloth folding) objects. The evaluation was conducted in two settings: demonstration-conditioned execution (using object flow extracted from a single human/sphere demonstration) and language-conditioned execution (using flow generated by the network from a task description). Crucially, *no real-world robot data was used for training*.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Flow Effectively Bridges Different Data Sources:** Im2Flow2Act consistently outperformed all baselines in both simulation (Table 1) and real-world environments (Table 2). In real-world language-conditioned execution, it achieved success rates of 90% (Pick&Place), 80% (Pouring), 85% (Drawer), and 70% (Cloth). The minimal performance drop from simulation to real-world (average 15%) validates object flow as a robust interface for connecting cross-embodiment and cross-environment data sources. Visualizations confirmed the policy's ability to follow generated flows across object types (Figure 4).

2. **Learning-Based Policy is Necessary for Translating Flow to Actions:** When compared to heuristic-based policies (e.g.,), Im2Flow2Act's learned policy demonstrated superior performance, especially for articulated and deformable objects (e.g., 95% vs. 30% for Drawer in sim demo-conditioned, and 70% vs. 0% for Cloth in sim demo-conditioned). While heuristics performed reasonably for rigid objects in simulation, they struggled significantly in real-world scenarios due to pose estimation errors and lack of impedance control (frequently triggering emergency stops on the UR5e). This highlights that a learned policy is essential for translating potentially noisy flow information into accurate and safe robot actions.

3. **Object-Centric Flow is Crucial for Cross-Embodiment Learning:** The authors directly compared their object-centric flow design with ATM, which uses grid flow. Im2Flow2Act significantly outperformed ATM (average 30% higher success rate in language-conditioned simulation, Table 1). The reason for ATM's poor performance with cross-embodiment data is that its grid flow heavily captures the embodiment's motion. When trained on human/sphere demonstrations and then deployed on a robot, the visual input becomes out-of-distribution for the flow generation, leading to noisy predictions. Im2Flow2Act's object-centric approach, by design, ignores embodiment motion, making it robust to different manipulators. Ablations using uniform grid flow with Im2Flow2Act also showed significant performance drops, reinforcing the superiority of object-centric flow.

4. **Temporal Alignment Module is Necessary for Unstructured Exploration Data:** Ablation studies showed that removing the temporal alignment module (`No alignment` baseline) led to less smooth robot execution in simulation and a significant performance drop in the real world (e.g., 55% vs. 95% for Pick&Place in real demo-conditioned, Table 2). Without alignment, the policy struggles to find analogous trajectories in its diverse, unstructured simulation data when faced with potentially different human demonstration speeds or execution patterns. The alignment module allows the policy to accurately track task progress and condition on the *remaining* task flow, leading to smoother and more effective interpolation.

5. **Long-Horizon and Multi-Object Tasks:** An additional experiment (Appendix B.1) demonstrated Im2Flow2Act's capability on a long-horizon, multi-object task ("Put the blue cube into the drawer"). It achieved 90% success in demonstration-conditioned and 85% in language-conditioned settings, showcasing its ability to generate and follow complex, multi-stage object flows.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Im2Flow2Act represents a significant advancement in robot learning, primarily by demonstrating a highly scalable framework that eliminates the need for expensive real-world robot training data. Its key contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Democratization of Robot Learning:** By relying on readily available human demonstration videos and cost-effective simulated robot play data, Im2Flow2Act lowers the barrier to entry for developing and deploying robotic manipulation skills. This could accelerate the pace of research and application in robotics, making complex skills accessible to more researchers and industries.
- **Robustness to Domain Gaps:** The core innovation of using object flow as an intermediate, embodiment-agnostic representation effectively bridges the long-standing embodiment and sim-to-real gaps. This is a crucial step towards creating "generalist" robots that can learn from diverse data sources and adapt to various environments.
- **Efficiency in Data Utilization:** The framework intelligently leverages the strengths of different data types: human videos for high-level task understanding (what to do with the object) and simulated robot data for low-level control (how the robot moves the object). This multi-modal data integration strategy is highly data-efficient.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work paves the way for several exciting avenues:
 - **Scaling to More Complex Tasks:** The framework's ability to handle rigid, articulated, and deformable objects suggests its potential for even more complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks.
 - **Integration with Large-Scale Models:** The successful use of pre-trained diffusion models for flow generation highlights the power of leveraging large-scale vision and language models in robotics.
 - **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** The interpretability of flow-based representations could simplify task specification and human oversight of robot behaviors.

**Limitations and Future Work (as acknowledged by the authors):**

- **2D Flow Ambiguity for 3D Actions:** Using 2D flow can be ambiguous for representing precise 3D actions, particularly z-axis movements (depth) and out-of-plane rotations (e.g., screwing). This suggests a natural extension to exploring 3D flow representations.
- **Simulating Deformable Objects:** The significant performance drop for cloth folding in the real world compared to simulation highlights the ongoing challenge of accurately simulating deformable object dynamics. Improving deformable object simulation or developing more robust sim-to-real transfer techniques for such objects is a critical area for future work.
- **Dependency on Camera Calibration and Visibility:** The system assumes a calibrated camera viewpoint and that all relevant actions and object states are visible within the camera's field of view.
- **Dexterous Manipulation:** While effective for manipulation, using flow as an action abstraction might overlook fine-grained action details necessary for highly dexterous tasks like in-hand manipulation.
- **Object Detection Reliance:** The flow generation model relies on accurate object detection, which could be further enhanced by more advanced segmentation models.
- **Applicability Scope:** The method is best suited for "manipulation tasks" where object state changes are reflected in object motion, potentially limiting its application to tasks like pressing a button on a touchscreen where object motion is minimal.

Despite these limitations, Im2Flow2Act provides a robust and scalable paradigm for robot learning, demonstrating a viable path toward enabling robots to acquire a wide range of real-world skills from accessible, diverse data sources without the prohibitive cost of real-robot training.
