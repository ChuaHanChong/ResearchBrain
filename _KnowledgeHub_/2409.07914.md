# InterACT: Inter-dependency Aware Action Chunking with Hierarchical Attention Transformers for Bimanual Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The InterACT framework from UC Davis and UC Berkeley enhances bimanual robotic manipulation by integrating hierarchical attention transformers that explicitly model inter-dependencies between dual-arm actions and multi-modal inputs. This approach consistently achieves higher success rates across various simulated and real-world tasks compared to the ACT baseline, particularly for movements requiring precise coordination.

## Problem

- Bimanual robotic manipulation tasks are challenging due to the need for intricate coordination and precise timing between two robotic arms.
- Existing transformer-based imitation learning policies, such as ACT, effectively manage temporal dependencies within action sequences but lack explicit mechanisms to capture the complex inter-dependencies between dual-arm joint states and visual features.
- Robustly executing high-precision bimanual tasks with learning-based methods, especially on low-cost hardware, remains a significant hurdle.

## Method

- InterACT introduces a Hierarchical Attention Encoder to process multi-modal inputs (joint states, visual features) using interleaved segment-wise and cross-segment self-attention, leveraging CLS tokens to summarize segment-specific and inter-segment information.
- A Multi-arm Decoder with parallel paths for each arm processes the enriched encoded states, enabling each arm's action prediction to be informed by its own state, the other arm's summarized state, and the visual environment.
- A crucial Synchronization Block integrates intermediate outputs from both arm decoders using self-attention, ensuring shared contextual information to enhance inter-arm coordination before final action generation.

## Results

- InterACT consistently outperformed the ACT baseline across all nine evaluated simulated and real-world bimanual manipulation tasks, with Diffusion Policy failing to perform with the given limited demonstrations.
- In simulated tasks, InterACT achieved an 82% success rate for the 'Transfer' subtask in Transfer Cube (compared to ACT's 50%) and a 44% success rate for 'Insert' in Peg Insertion (compared to ACT's 20%), showing substantial gains in coordination-heavy phases.
- Ablation studies confirmed that removing key components like CLS tokens, the cross-segment encoder, or the synchronization block led to significant performance drops, for example, 'Peg Insertion (Insert)' success rate fell from 44% to 22% without CLS tokens, demonstrating their critical role in enabling coordinated actions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Bimanual tasks possess a hierarchical structure where effective coordination requires explicit modeling of both independent arm movements (intra-segment dependencies) and their interactions (inter-segment dependencies).
> - Hierarchical attention mechanisms, successfully employed in NLP for long document processing, can be adapted to robot policy attention layers to capture complex multi-modal and multi-agent dependencies for improved bimanual control.
> - Dedicated architectural components like CLS tokens for segment summarization, a cross-segment encoder for multi-modal integration, and a synchronization block for inter-arm communication are vital for achieving robust and highly coordinated bimanual actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bimanual manipulation, involving two robotic arms coordinating to perform tasks, represents a significant challenge in robotics. Historically, approaches to bimanual manipulation relied on classical control methods, which required precise modeling, extensive calibration, and expensive hardware. While effective for structured environments, these methods often lacked adaptability to dynamic and varied scenarios.

The field has seen a paradigm shift with the rise of learning-based techniques, notably Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL). RL has enhanced adaptability and robustness by allowing robots to learn from environmental interactions. Imitation Learning, by leveraging human demonstrations, has emerged as a particularly promising avenue for teaching robots complex, dexterous tasks without requiring explicit reward engineering or extensive simulation, thereby enabling the execution of intricate actions.

A significant recent advancement in this space is the ALOHA framework and its associated Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) algorithm. ALOHA demonstrated that low-cost hardware systems, when combined with effective imitation learning, could achieve high-precision manipulation tasks previously thought to require expensive setups. ACT specifically addressed the compounding error problem in imitation learning by predicting sequences ("chunks") of actions rather than single steps, thereby reducing the effective task horizon and improving robustness.

InterACT builds directly upon this foundation, aiming to overcome a critical limitation of existing transformer-based imitation learning policies like ACT: the explicit modeling of *inter-dependencies* between the two arms and multimodal inputs (joint states and visual features). While ACT effectively handles temporal dependencies within an action sequence, it does not inherently possess mechanisms to deeply understand and leverage the intricate coordination required *between* the arms.

The paper posits that bimanual tasks have a hierarchical structure, where independent arm movements are managed at a lower level, but high-level coordination requires information exchange. This insight leads them to leverage hierarchical attention mechanisms, a concept that has proven highly effective in other domains like Natural Language Processing (NLP) for processing long documents and capturing dependencies across different segments of text. The novelty of InterACT lies in tailoring and applying this advanced hierarchical attention architecture to the *policy's attention layer* in bimanual robotic manipulation, a departure from prior hierarchical approaches in robotics that typically decompose tasks into sub-tasks. By doing so, InterACT seeks to enable more coordinated, precise, and robust bimanual actions, pushing the capabilities of low-cost, learning-based robot systems even further.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel imitation learning framework, named InterACT (Inter-dependency aware Action Chunking with Hierarchical Attention Transformers), specifically designed to enhance bimanual manipulation performance. The core motivation stems from the inherent complexity of coordinating two robotic arms, a challenge that existing learning-based methods, including transformer-based policies like ACT, have not fully addressed in terms of explicitly modeling inter-dependencies.

The key motivations and objectives can be summarized as:
- **Addressing Complex Coordination:** Bimanual tasks demand high precision and intricate coordination between two robotic arms, which remains a significant hurdle for robust task execution.
- **Explicitly Modeling Inter-dependencies:** Current transformer-based policies, while effective for sequential action prediction, often lack explicit mechanisms to capture the complex relationships and inter-dependencies between dual-arm joint states and visual inputs. InterACT aims to fill this gap.
- **Leveraging Hierarchical Attention:** The authors hypothesize that hierarchical attention mechanisms, inspired by their success in NLP for long document processing, can effectively capture both intra-segment (within a single arm's data or visual input) and inter-segment (between arms, or between an arm and visual input) dependencies.
- **Enhancing Robustness and Precision:** By explicitly modeling these inter-dependencies, the goal is to develop a policy that can generate more coordinated, precise, and ultimately more robust bimanual actions, leading to higher success rates in diverse manipulation tasks.
- **Building on Existing Strengths:** InterACT is motivated to extend the successes of the ACT framework, particularly its ability to mitigate compounding errors through action chunking, by adding a crucial layer of inter-dependency awareness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

InterACT is an imitation learning framework that builds upon the ACT model by integrating hierarchical attention mechanisms. It consists of two main components: a Hierarchical Attention Encoder and a Multi-arm Decoder.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Attention Encoder

The encoder is responsible for processing multi-modal inputs (joint states of each arm and visual features) and capturing both intra-segment and inter-segment dependencies.
- **Input Processing:** Raw joint positions are embedded into single tokens via a linear layer. Visual features are extracted from RGB images using ResNet18 backbones, flattened into sequences of visual feature tokens. Joint sequences from both arms and the visual feature sequence are concatenated.
- **Segments and CLS Tokens:** "Segments" refer to independent groups of data inputs (e.g., Arm1 joint states, Arm2 joint states, visual features). Classification tokens (CLS tokens), similar to BERT, are prepended to each segment. These CLS tokens are designed to summarize and capture segment-specific information during attention processing. Positional embeddings are added to all tokens to maintain sequence order.
- **Segment-Wise Encoder:** This component captures *intra-segment* dependencies. It processes each segment individually using a self-attention mechanism. Each token within a segment (including its CLS token) can attend to all other tokens within that same segment. This allows the CLS token to condense information and dependencies specific to that modality (e.g., how different joints of Arm1 relate, or spatial relationships within the visual input).
- **Cross-Segment Encoder:** This component captures *inter-segment* dependencies. After the segment-wise encoder, the CLS tokens from all segments (Arm1, Arm2, visual) are extracted. The cross-segment encoder then processes these CLS tokens using self-attention. By focusing on these condensed representations, it efficiently integrates information across different modalities and arms, reducing computational overhead while understanding their relationships.
- **Interleaved Stacking:** Multiple layers of segment-wise and cross-segment encoders are stacked in an interleaved manner. This allows the model to progressively refine its understanding of both local (intra-segment) and global (inter-segment) relationships.

#### 3.2. Multi-arm Decoder

The decoder generates synchronized action predictions for both arms, leveraging the enriched encoded states from the Hierarchical Attention Encoder.
- **Parallel Decoder Paths:** The Multi-arm Decoder comprises two parallel paths of decoder blocks, one for each arm. This design allows for independent action processing while enabling coordination.
- **Input to Decoders:** The encoded states from the Hierarchical Attention Encoder serve as context. Target tokens, initialized with positional embeddings, are the starting point for generating action sequences.
- **Arm Specific Decoders:**
 - **Arm1 Decoder** receives its own arm's segments, the *other arm's* CLS tokens (Arm2 CLS tokens), and the visual feature segments (including visual CLS tokens).
 - **Arm2 Decoder** similarly receives its own arm's segments, Arm1 CLS tokens, and visual feature segments.
 - Each layer within these decoders uses a cross-attention mechanism between target tokens and the contextual inputs, allowing each arm's action prediction to be informed by its own state, the other arm's summarized state, and the visual environment.
- **Synchronization Block:** This is a crucial mechanism for enhancing inter-arm coordination. Before the final output, the intermediate outputs from both parallel arm decoders are concatenated. This combined output is then fed into a synchronization block, which uses a self-attention mechanism to integrate shared information between the two arms. The output of this block is then split and passed back to the respective arm decoders for final action generation. This ensures that both arms leverage a combined, coordinated context.

#### 3.3. Training and Evaluation

- **Imitation Learning:** InterACT is trained end-to-end using an imitation learning framework adapted from ACT.
- **Data Collection:** High-quality human demonstrations are collected via a teleoperation system (modified ALOHA 2 setup), capturing joint positions and RGB images at 50Hz.
- **Preprocessing:** Collected data is preprocessed to extract joint states and visual features (using ResNet18), tokenize them, and add CLS tokens and positional embeddings.
- **Action Chunking:** The model predicts sequences of actions (chunks) rather than single steps, addressing compounding errors.
- **Temporal Ensemble:** A temporal ensemble method is employed during evaluation to improve consistency and robustness by weighing predictions over multiple time steps.
- **Evaluation:** Performance is measured by success rates on various simulated and real-world bimanual manipulation tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on three simulated tasks and six real-world tasks. Four tasks (Transfer Cube, Peg Insertion, Slide Ziploc, Thread Velcro) were adapted from the ACT benchmark, and five new tasks (Slot Insertion (Sim), Insert Plug (Real), Click Pen (Real), Sweep (Real), Unscrew Cap (Real)) were introduced by the authors to further test coordination. A dataset of 50 human demonstrations was collected for each task.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison with Baseline (ACT)

InterACT was compared against the ACT model and, initially, Diffusion Policy. Diffusion Policy performed poorly with only 50 demonstrations, failing all tasks, and was therefore excluded from further detailed comparison. The primary comparison focused on ACT, which is already a strong baseline, significantly outperforming other policies like BC-ConvMLP, BeT, and VINN in bimanual manipulation.

The results (Table 1) consistently demonstrate **superior performance of InterACT over ACT across all nine simulated and real-world tasks.**
- **Simulated Tasks:** InterACT showed significant improvements, particularly in subtasks requiring high coordination and precision. For instance, in "Transfer Cube," InterACT achieved an 82% success rate for the "Transfer" subtask compared to ACT's 50%. In "Peg Insertion," InterACT reached 44% for "Insert" versus ACT's 20%. The newly introduced "Slot Insertion" task, which critically relies on precise dual-arm coordination, saw InterACT achieve a 100% success rate for "Insert," outperforming ACT's 88%.
- **Real-world Tasks:** Similar trends were observed. In "Insert Plug," InterACT achieved 42% for "Insert" compared to ACT's 30%. While some tasks showed closer performance (e.g., "Grasp" subtask often high for both), InterACT generally maintained a lead, especially in the more challenging, coordination-heavy phases of tasks.

These results validate the effectiveness of InterACT's hierarchical attention framework in improving coordination and precision for bimanual manipulation, leading to a more robust solution in both simulation and real-world scenarios.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted on simulated tasks (Table 2) to quantify the contribution of InterACT's key novel components: CLS tokens, the cross-segment encoder, and the synchronization block.

- **Impact of CLS Tokens:** Removing CLS tokens had no significant impact on simpler tasks like "Transfer Cube." However, in more complex tasks like "Peg Insertion" and "Slot Insertion" (specifically for coordination-heavy "Insert" phases), success rates dropped when CLS tokens were omitted. For example, "Peg Insertion (Insert)" dropped from 44% to 22%. This suggests CLS tokens are crucial for aggregating and summarizing segment information, particularly when high precision and synchronization are required. Attention weight analysis (Figure 2) further showed spikes in CLS token attention during coordinated phases, confirming their role in facilitating precise bimanual actions.
- **Impact of Cross-segment Encoder:** Removing the cross-segment encoder, which is responsible for integrating information across different segments (arms and visual inputs), significantly decreased performance in complex coordination tasks. For "Peg Insertion (Insert)," success dropped from 44% to 24%. This highlights the critical role of explicitly capturing inter-segment dependencies for accurate and coordinated actions.
- **Impact of Synchronization Block:** The removal of the synchronization block, which shares contextual information between the two arm decoders, led to a substantial performance drop across all coordination-heavy tasks. "Transfer Cube (Transfer)" fell from 84% to 54%, and "Peg Insertion (Insert)" dropped from 44% to 30%. This firmly establishes the necessity of the synchronization block for achieving coordinated actions between the arms.

The ablation studies conclusively demonstrate that all proposed components—CLS tokens, cross-segment encoder, and synchronization block—are vital for InterACT's superior performance, especially in tasks demanding high coordination between robotic arms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The InterACT framework represents a significant step forward in the field of bimanual robotic manipulation, particularly for imitation learning approaches utilizing low-cost hardware. Its main contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Enhanced Bimanual Coordination:** By explicitly modeling inter-dependencies between dual-arm joint states and visual inputs through hierarchical attention, InterACT overcomes a key limitation of prior transformer-based policies like ACT. This leads to more precise and synchronized actions, crucial for complex bimanual tasks.
- **Improved Robustness and Success Rates:** The consistent outperformance of InterACT over the strong ACT baseline, across a variety of simulated and real-world tasks (including newly introduced, challenging ones), demonstrates its superior robustness and reliability. This is particularly valuable for deploying robots in less structured environments or for tasks with high success rate requirements.
- **Leveraging NLP Advancements for Robotics:** InterACT effectively adapts hierarchical attention mechanisms, proven successful in NLP for understanding long-range dependencies, to the unique challenges of multi-modal robotic control. This cross-domain application opens new avenues for leveraging sophisticated neural network architectures developed in one field to solve problems in another.
- **Validation of Key Architectural Components:** The detailed ablation studies not only confirm the overall effectiveness of InterACT but also rigorously validate the individual contributions of its novel components: CLS tokens for summarizing segment information, the cross-segment encoder for integrating multi-modal dependencies, and the synchronization block for enabling inter-arm communication. This provides valuable insights for future research in designing multi-agent or multi-arm policies.
- **Applicability to Low-Cost Robotics:** By building upon the ALOHA/ACT ecosystem, InterACT demonstrates that advanced coordination capabilities can be achieved without requiring prohibitively expensive, high-fidelity robotic systems. This democratizes access to complex manipulation capabilities, making them more attainable for broader research and application.

Despite its significant advancements, the paper acknowledges limitations, primarily concerning the difficulty of hyperparameter tuning for the number of CLS tokens and encoder blocks, which currently lacks clear guiding principles and depends on subjective measures of "coordination level." This suggests an area for future research, possibly involving auxiliary loss terms for explicit coordination or more quantitative definitions of coordination.

Future work will explore integrating additional modalities such as language or sensory feedback, and addressing the hierarchical nature of tasks for better decomposition. InterACT provides a flexible foundation for these expansions, demonstrating that its attention mechanisms can manage increased complexity and data integration. Overall, InterACT paves the way for more dexterous, adaptable, and robust bimanual robotic systems capable of tackling increasingly complex real-world manipulation challenges.
