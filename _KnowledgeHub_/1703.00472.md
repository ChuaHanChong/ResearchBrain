# Reinforcement Learning for Pivoting Task

> [!summary] Summary
> A pivoting task, rotating a grasped tool between two parallel-gripper fingers to a target angle, is learned by model-free RL (TRPO) in a simple custom simulator built from two-link under-actuated dynamics plus a Coulomb-viscous friction model, with randomized actuation delays and friction noise injected. The policy commands gripper acceleration and finger distance, transfers zero-shot to a Baxter robot: 93% success on the trained tool, 83% on an unseen tool.

## Problem

- Prior pivoting methods rely on accurate object models and precise friction measurement, quantities intractable to estimate for widely available robots lacking tactile sensing.
- Existing pivoting controllers assume fast gripper feedback, fixed gripper pose, or vertical-plane motion reaching only lower-potential-energy configurations, so they generalize poorly.
- Model-free continuous control needs millions of steps, heavy hyper-parameter search, and suffers forgetting across target angles, making direct hardware training infeasible.

## Method

- A custom simulator encodes the gripper-plus-tool as an ==under-actuated two-link planar arm==, with normal force expressed as linear fingertip deformation over commanded finger distance.
- Friction torque combines ==Coulomb static bound== and viscous plus Coulomb sliding terms, with a zero-velocity neighborhood where friction cancels net torque to avoid stick-slip singularities.
- ==Robustness training== injects up to 10% randomized arm and finger actuation delay plus 10% friction noise; **TRPO** optimizes a 32-16 network on the angle-error MDP.

## Results

- On Baxter, the simulator-trained policy reached the goal in **28 of 30** trials with the modeled tool and **25 of 30** with an unmodeled tool.
- Despite only **10%** Coulomb-friction noise during training, the policy kept about **40%** goal-reaching when tested at **250% to 500%** changed friction coefficients.
- DDPG training repeatedly stalled and was abandoned; TRPO converged with a two-hidden-layer network, reaching targets in roughly **5s** for tool 1, **10s** for tool 2.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An approximate analytic simulator plus randomized delay and friction noise can suffice for zero-shot transfer, removing the need for precise friction identification.
> - Encoding symmetry into state, using signed tool-to-target angle error, lets one small network cover many target angles and reduces forgetting.
> - Robustness margins extend well beyond the injected noise range, but degrade gracefully; unmodeled objects and untracked slippage remain the main failure sources.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the field of robotics, specifically addressing problems in extrinsic dexterity and in-hand manipulation. Extrinsic dexterity involves reorienting an object using interaction with environmental forces or, as in this case, manipulating an object between a robot's fingers without external contact. The "pivoting task," which involves rotating an object between a gripper's fingers to achieve a desired angle, is a specific challenge within this domain. This capability is relevant to many robotic tasks requiring object interaction and tool use, as it allows for proper object posing relative to the gripper.

Traditional approaches to in-hand manipulation often rely on complex multi-fingered robotic hands, customized grippers, or strategies that involve releasing and re-grasping the object. However, many commercially available robots, such as Baxter, PR2, and Yumi, are equipped with simpler parallel grippers. This necessitates the development of re-grasping or in-hand manipulation strategies that do not depend on the advanced degrees of freedom of complex hands. Prior work in pivoting has often exploited gravity, contact surfaces, or precisely controlled torques, frequently relying on accurate physical models of the object and precise measurements of friction, which are difficult to obtain in real-world scenarios. Many of these methods also assume specific motion planes or fast sensory feedback.

The present work introduces deep reinforcement learning (RL) as a paradigm for solving the pivoting task. Recent advancements in model-free continuous control algorithms, such as Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG), have demonstrated success in simulated robotic tasks. However, applying these algorithms directly to real robots faces significant challenges:
1. **Data Efficiency:** Deep RL algorithms often require thousands to millions of training episodes, making direct learning on physical hardware prohibitively time-consuming and resource-intensive.
2. **Hyper-parameter Sensitivity:** Extensive hyper-parameter tuning (e.g., exploration rate, reward shaping, network architecture) is often necessary, which is difficult on real systems.
3. **Generalization:** Policies learned on a specific robot and tool might not generalize well to different setups or objects.
4. **"Forgetting":** Neural networks can exhibit "catastrophic forgetting" when trained sequentially on different tasks (e.g., reaching different target angles), hindering learning progress.

To mitigate these challenges, some research has explored pre-training in simulation, followed by fine-tuning on real hardware or using progressive neural network architectures. This paper proposes an approach to learn policies robust enough in simulation to be directly applicable to real robots, without requiring further on-hardware training. This aims to make model-free RL more accessible for real-world robotic tasks where precise physical models are unavailable and direct hardware training is impractical.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop an approach for learning a robust policy capable of solving the pivoting task for a robotic gripper, specifically enabling the rotation of an object between two fingers to a desired target angle.

This objective is motivated by several considerations:

- **Necessity for In-Hand Manipulation:** Many robotic tasks require interaction with objects and tool use, making the ability to precisely reorient items within the gripper crucial. Current robotic systems often utilize parallel grippers, lacking the multi-fingered dexterity of more complex hands. Relying on re-grasping (releasing and picking up the object) is often inefficient. Therefore, developing strategies for in-hand manipulation with simpler grippers is important.
- **Limitations of Prior Pivoting Approaches:** Existing methods for pivoting typically rely on accurate physical models of the object, precise friction properties, and often require rapid sensory feedback and gripper response times. These assumptions are frequently difficult or impossible to meet in practical robotic setups, especially when dealing with unknown or varying objects. The precise estimation of friction coefficients, for example, is particularly challenging.
- **Challenges of Direct Reinforcement Learning on Hardware:** Applying powerful model-free deep reinforcement learning algorithms directly to physical robots is often impractical. The high sample complexity (thousands to millions of training episodes) renders real-time learning on hardware prohibitively time-consuming. Moreover, learning on a specific robot and tool might result in policies that lack generalization capabilities. The issue of "forgetting" in neural networks, where learning one target angle might degrade performance on others, further complicates real-time on-robot learning.
- **Addressing Simulation-to-Real Mismatch:** The disparity between simulated environments and real-world physics (e.g., unmodeled dynamics, imprecise parameters like friction, hardware delays, and errors in actuation) is a significant obstacle for policies trained solely in simulation. The goal is to develop a simulation-based training procedure that intrinsically accounts for these mismatches, enabling the learned policy to generalize effectively to a real robot.
- **Leveraging Model-Free RL Capabilities:** The flexibility of non-linear control policies, particularly those represented by deep neural networks learned through model-free RL, is appealing for complex manipulation tasks. By developing a robust simulation-to-real transfer method, the authors aim to leverage these powerful learning algorithms without being constrained by their sample inefficiency on real hardware. This also implies not needing to rely on sample-efficient but potentially less flexible model-based learning algorithms that make strict assumptions about system dynamics.
- **Practical Robot Constraints:** The problem also considers practical limitations of commercial robots, such as limited control frequency for gripper fingers and reliance on readily available vision systems with finite frame rates, which contribute to the uncertainty of system dynamics and state estimation.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between advanced reinforcement learning algorithms and the practical constraints of real-world robotics, allowing robust in-hand manipulation skills to be learned in simulation and directly transferred to robots with parallel grippers, even with uncertainties in object properties and hardware performance.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The approach developed in this work involves learning robust policies for the pivoting task through a custom simulator, specifically designed to account for discrepancies between simulation and real-world robotic hardware.

**A. Overall Learning Procedure:**
1. **Custom Simulator Construction:** A simplified simulator is built using fundamental dynamics equations for the system, without requiring precise estimation of all parameters (e.g., friction).
2. **Robustness Injection:** To prepare for simulation-to-real transfer, the simulator incorporates randomized delays for actuation and noise in estimated friction values.
3. **Model-Free Deep Reinforcement Learning:** A model-free policy search algorithm is trained within this augmented simulated environment.
4. **Hardware Deployment:** The learned policy is then directly deployed and evaluated on a real robot (Baxter) without further on-robot training.

**B. Modeling for Simulation:**
The simulator models the system as an under-actuated two-link planar arm, where a 1-DOF parallel gripper (first link) holds a tool (second link) that pivots around a contact point.

- **Dynamic Model:**
 - The system includes a first link (gripper) with angle `φ_grp` and a second link (tool) with angle `φ_tl`.
 - The dynamic equation (Equation 1) relates the angular accelerations (`¨φ_grp`, `¨φ_tl`), angular velocity (`˙φ_grp`), tool's moment of inertia (`I`), mass (`m`), distance of center of mass from pivot (`r`), length of the first link (`l`), gravity acceleration (`g`), and torsional friction (`τ_f`).
 - The gravity component `g` is adjusted based on the pitch angle of the robotic manipulator, assuming no roll, affecting motion in one direction.

- **Friction Model:**
 - Friction at the contact point between gripper fingers and the tool is crucial for controlling rotational motion and is regulated by gripper finger distance.
 - **Static Friction (`τ_s`):** When the tool is stationary (`˙φ_tl = 0`), Coulomb friction is used: `|τ_s| ≤ γf_n`, where `γ` is the static friction coefficient and `f_n` is the normal force.
 - **Kinetic Friction (`τ_f`):** When the tool is moving (`˙φ_tl ≠ 0`), friction is modeled as a combination of viscous and Coulomb friction: `τ_f = −μ_v ˙φ_tl − μ_c f_n sgn(˙φ_tl)`. `μ_v` and `μ_c` are viscous and Coulomb friction coefficients.
 - **Singularity Alleviation:** To prevent numerical singularities when switching between static and kinetic friction, a smoothing approach is used where friction torque equals the net torque within a small velocity neighborhood.
 - **Normal Force Estimation:** Since tactile sensors are often unavailable, the normal force `f_n` is expressed as a linear function of the distance `d_fing` between the fingers: `f_n = k(d_0 − d_fing)`, where `k` is a stiffness parameter and `d_0` is the initial contact distance.

**C. Learning Robust Policies using a Simulator:**
- The simulator is kept simple and fast.
- **Noise Injection:** To account for simulation-to-real mismatch, up to 10% randomized delays are added for arm and finger actions, and 10% noise is applied to friction values (e.g., `kμ_c`). This aims to train policies that are robust to uncertainties in tool properties and robot execution.

**D. Pivoting Task as a Markov Decision Process (MDP):**
- **State Space (S):** `s_t = [φ_tl − φ_tgt, ˙φ_tl, φ_grp, ˙φ_grp, d_fing]`. The difference `φ_tl − φ_tgt` is used for generalization due to motion symmetry.
- **Action Space (A):** `a_t = {¨φ_grp, d_fing}`. `¨φ_grp` is rotational acceleration of the arm, and `d_fing` is finger distance. For hardware limitations, controlling the direction of change in `d_fing` might be advantageous.
- **Transition Probabilities:** Implicitly handled by the model-free approach; the learner does not explicitly access `P(s'|s, a)`.
- **Reward Function (R):** `r(t) = −|φ_tl − φ_tgt| / φ_RNG` (normalized negative absolute angle difference). A bonus of +1 is awarded when the tool is within the goal region (close to `φ_tgt` and stable velocity).
- **Discount Factor (α):** `α = 0.99` for an infinite horizon discounted MDP, implying an average task duration of 100 steps.

**E. Policy Search using Reinforcement Learning:**
- **Algorithm:** Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) was selected after DDPG frequently stalled in initial experiments.
- **TRPO Overview:** TRPO is designed to optimize large non-linear control policies (e.g., neural networks) by ensuring monotonic improvement during training. It iteratively solves an optimization problem (Equation 5) that maximizes expected reward while constraining the new policy to be sufficiently close to the old one using a KL-divergence limit (`δ`). This prevents large policy updates that could destabilize learning.
- **Rationale for TRPO in Simulation:** While theoretically well-motivated, TRPO's practical approximations and sample inefficiency can be challenging for direct robot learning. Therefore, training in a simulated environment, where large numbers of samples are feasible, is explored.

**F. Implementation Details (Simulation):**
- An OpenAI Gym environment for the pivoting task MDP was created.
- The `rllab` implementation was used as a basis for TRPO.
- Policy and Q-function approximators were fully connected neural networks with 2 hidden layers (32 and 16 nodes).
- Training episodes involved randomly selected initial and target angles from `[-π/2, π/2]` for 100 steps each.
- Simulated tool parameters (Tool 1) included `μ_v = 0.066` and `kμ_c = 9.906`.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presented several findings from both simulation-based training and hardware experiments on a Baxter robot.

**A. Learning Robust Policies in Simulation:**
- **Algorithm Choice:** Initial experiments indicated that Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) consistently achieved satisfactory learning progress, while Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) frequently stalled. Consequently, TRPO was selected for all subsequent experiments.
- **Network Architecture:** A fully connected neural network with two hidden layers (32 and 16 nodes) for both policy and Q-function approximation was found to be sufficient for solving the task. Larger networks (up to 3 hidden layers) were also tested but did not yield substantial improvements.
- **Training Progress:** The training process, visualized in Figure 3, demonstrated that as the number of training iterations increased, the average reward obtained per episode improved, and the average number of steps required to reach the goal region (defined as ±3° of the target angle) decreased. This indicated successful policy learning within the simulated environment.
- **Robustness to Friction Mismatch (Simulation):** A key finding was the policy's robustness to significant variations in friction properties. Although the simulation injected only up to 10% noise into the `kμ_c` (Coulomb friction) parameter during training, the policy, after 20,000 training iterations, still achieved a 40% success rate when tested on simulated settings where `kμ_c` was varied from 250% to 500% of its training value. This suggests that the learned policy can generalize well to objects with unprecisely known or varying friction coefficients, a critical aspect given the difficulty of accurately estimating friction in real systems.

**B. Experiments on Hardware with Baxter Robot:**
- **Hardware Setup:** Experiments were conducted on a Baxter robot equipped with one of its 7-DOF arms and deformable fingertips. The gripper's control frequency was limited, with an observed delay of 80ms for fingers to reach a desired distance. The tool's angle (`φ_tl`) was estimated using a color-based marker tracking system with a Kinect 2 RGB-D camera operating at 30 fps. The experiments were performed in a horizontal plane, negating gravitational influence (`g=0`), thus relying solely on inertial forces generated by commanded actions. The distance between the actuated joint and the pivoting point was 0.35 m.
- **Tool Parameters:** Two different tools (Tool 1 and Tool 2) with varying materials and physical properties (inertia, mass, center of mass distance, initial contact distance `d_0`) were used. Tool 1's parameters were used (with noise) in the simulator during training. Tool 2's parameters, including its friction properties, were unknown to the policy during training.
- **Performance:** A total of 30 trials were conducted for each tool, cycling through a sequence of target angles (0°, 45°, 0°, −60°, 30°, 5°, 0°).
 - **Tool 1 (known parameters):** Achieved a success rate of approximately 93% (28 out of 30 trials reached the goal). One trial resulted in a dropped tool, and one trial did not reach the goal.
 - **Tool 2 (unknown parameters):** Achieved a success rate of approximately 83% (25 out of 30 trials reached the goal). Two trials resulted in dropped tools, and three trials did not reach the goal.
- **Generalization to Unknown Objects:** The policy demonstrated robust performance even when manipulating Tool 2, which had different physical properties and unknown friction coefficients compared to the training object. While performance was slightly better with Tool 1, the success rate with Tool 2 was still substantial, indicating good generalization capabilities.
- **Trajectory Analysis:** Example trajectories (Figure 5) showed that the target angle was reached faster when using Tool 1 (approximately 5 seconds) compared to Tool 2 (approximately 10 seconds). This difference is attributed to the policy being trained with parameters more closely matching Tool 1.
- **Robustness to Tracking Inaccuracies:** Averaged results (Figure 6) indicated that the policy was able to recover and continue adjusting the tool even if initial reports of reaching the goal region were later corrected by the tracking system due to measurement inaccuracies. Drops were infrequent and typically occurred after several rounds of experiments without re-adjusting the tool.

These results collectively demonstrate that the proposed training procedure in a simple, noisy simulator can yield policies robust enough for successful deployment on a real robotic system, even when faced with significant uncertainties in object properties and hardware limitations.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes a significant contribution to the field of robotics by demonstrating a practical methodology for learning robust in-hand manipulation policies through reinforcement learning in simulated environments, addressing several long-standing challenges in transferring simulated skills to real robots.

The primary significance lies in showing that:
- **Feasibility of Model-Free RL for Real-World Robotics:** The work provides evidence that advanced model-free reinforcement learning algorithms, which are typically data-intensive, can be effectively utilized for real-world robotic tasks by pre-training in a simple simulator. This bypasses the prohibitive time and cost associated with direct on-hardware training for millions of episodes.
- **Robustness to Simulation-to-Real Mismatch:** The proposed training procedure, which involves injecting noise and delays into a custom simulator built on approximate dynamics, successfully mitigates the "simulation-to-real" gap. This is crucial because it means that high-fidelity, computationally expensive simulators, or extremely precise parameter estimation (especially for hard-to-model properties like friction), are not strictly necessary for learning effective control policies.
- **Generalization Across Object Properties:** A major impact is the demonstration of policy generalization. The learned policy successfully pivoted not only the object whose parameters were approximately known during training but also an object with substantially different inertia, mass, shape, and unknown friction properties. This ability to generalize to unseen objects significantly enhances the practical applicability of robotic manipulation systems.
- **Reduced Reliance on Accurate Physical Models:** By achieving robustness to imprecisely estimated or unknown parameters, particularly friction coefficients, the approach reduces a major barrier in robotic manipulation. This is highly relevant as accurate friction modeling remains a complex problem in robotics.
- **Enabling Dexterous Manipulation with Simpler Grippers:** The work focuses on the pivoting task with a parallel gripper, which is common in many industrial and research robots. This offers a path to achieving more dexterous manipulation capabilities without requiring complex, multi-fingered robotic hands, thereby broadening the applicability of existing robotic platforms.
- **Practicality for Robot Constraints:** The methodology implicitly handles common robot hardware limitations such as actuation delays and limited sensor frame rates, leading to policies that are effective despite these practical constraints.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. It could accelerate the deployment of reinforcement learning in various robotic applications requiring in-hand manipulation, such as assembly, tool use, and object reorientation in unstructured environments. By making model-free RL more accessible for real robots, it opens avenues for learning complex, adaptive behaviors that might be difficult to program manually. Future work outlined by the authors, such as combining pivoting with sliding for more general dexterous manipulation, fine-tuning on hardware for even higher performance, or extending to non-rigid objects using approximate simulators, further highlights the foundational nature and broad implications of this research.
