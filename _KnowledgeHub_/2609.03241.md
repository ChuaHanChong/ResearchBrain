# FlowBalance: Verifier-Grounded Self-Improvement from On-Policy Reasoning Experience

> [!summary] Summary
> FlowBalance is a self-improvement method for reasoning LLMs that learns a normalized distribution over complete responses. A frozen privileged-hindsight view of the same policy scores sampled tokens; a verifier's group advantage gates the sign of that guidance, and profiled trajectory balance fits the resulting Gibbs target with one log-partition estimate per rollout group. It beats GRPO, OPSD, RLSD and FlowRL on math reasoning at both 4B and 8B scale.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards grounds training reliably but supplies one terminal reward for responses spanning thousands of tokens, leaving each decision under-informed.
- Dense same-model guidance is cheap but untrustworthy: it can confidently score a trajectory the verifier rejects, turning the model's own error into supervision.
- Outcome-only distributional methods translate terminal evidence into response probability, yet cannot exploit fine-grained evidence along a sampled reasoning path, and direct self-distillation shortens reasoning.

## Method

- **Privileged-hindsight self-guidance**: a frozen policy snapshot conditioned on the training solution scores already-sampled tokens, ==clipped log-probability gains== against the reference averaged into a trajectory score.
- **Outcome-calibrated energy**: the verifier's ==stopped group-relative advantage== sets guidance direction, retained when the advantage is positive, reversed when negative, disabled when the group shows no preference.
- **Profiled trajectory balance**: the energy exponentially tilts the reference policy, and a squared ==trajectory-balance residual== with one profiled ==log-partition== per rollout group fits the normalized target.

## Results

- Five-benchmark average **64.26** on Qwen3-4B and **67.61** on Qwen3-8B, beating FlowRL by **1.04** and **1.76** points, GRPO by **1.95** and **2.12**, OPSD by **10.14** and **26.45**.
- On Qwen3-8B it hits **0.5** AIME24 validation accuracy in roughly **100** steps versus **143** for GRPO, and stays near peak across **400** steps while GRPO degrades after step **180**.
- Correct-only Simpson strategy diversity on AIME24 is **0.2194** versus **0.1017** for GRPO and **0.1456** for RLSD; raising the guidance coefficient from **1** to **3** drops the average from **67.61** to **65.95**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense self-generated signals are safe to use only when a trustworthy sparse verifier fixes their direction; sign gating converts false confidence into a corrective push.
> - More dense supervision is not better: stronger guidance coefficients degraded accuracy, so the gain comes from calibration rather than from signal strength.
> - Fitting a normalized distribution over complete responses, not a local per-token loss, preserves multiple correct strategies and avoids the response-length collapse of direct self-distillation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a core challenge in the post-training phase of reasoning models: enabling a model to improve effectively from its own generated experience. This self-improvement process, an "inner loop" of policy updates based on on-policy trajectories, is recognized as a central promise but also as inherently fragile.

Current approaches to reasoning model self-improvement typically fall into two categories, each with distinct limitations:

1. **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**: Methods in this category, such as those employing group-based policy optimization (e.g., GRPO), provide reliable feedback by using external verifiers for terminal outcomes. However, this supervision is often sparse, with a single final reward assigned to a long sequence of tokens. This makes it challenging to attribute credit or blame to specific intermediate steps in a complex reasoning path, leading to inefficient learning for fine-grained improvements. Distributional methods like FlowRL attempt to convert this terminal evidence into probabilities over complete responses but still rely solely on outcome-level signals.

2. **Dense Same-Model Guidance / On-Policy Self-Distillation**: These approaches leverage a "privileged-hindsight" view of the model itself, which has access to additional training-time context (e.g., a reference solution) to score sampled tokens. This signal is dense (token-level) and inexpensive, as it does not require larger external models or additional response generation. However, this form of self-guidance carries risks. The privileged view might develop "false confidence," favoring plausible but incorrect trajectories, shortening reasoning, suppressing beneficial exploration, or concentrating updates around narrow, local solution modes. This can lead to self-confirmation loops where the model reinforces its own erroneous preferences.

FlowBalance positions itself at the intersection of these two areas, aiming to combine the reliability of sparse verifiable outcomes with the density of self-guidance while mitigating the respective drawbacks. It seeks to provide a principled method for learning a normalized response distribution that is both grounded in verified outcomes and informed by dense, outcome-calibrated self-guidance. This approach contrasts with existing methods that either rely solely on sparse outcomes (like GRPO or FlowRL) or combine guidance with RL objectives without explicit outcome calibration or distributional normalization (like RLSD or OPSD). The paper's contribution lies in defining a novel distributional target that explicitly addresses the challenge of integrating these signals to promote effective, stable, and diverse self-improvement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust self-improvement method, named FlowBalance, for reasoning models. This method aims to learn an optimal normalized distribution over complete responses by intelligently combining sparse, reliable verifier feedback with dense, but potentially imperfect, self-guidance derived from the model's own on-policy experience.

The motivation for FlowBalance stems from addressing critical limitations inherent in existing self-improvement paradigms for long-horizon reasoning tasks:

1. **Bridging Sparse Reliability and Dense Utility**: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) provides dependable, objective feedback on terminal outcomes. However, this feedback is often sparse, delivering a single reward for a lengthy reasoning trajectory. This sparsity makes it challenging for the model to learn fine-grained improvements along the reasoning path, as credit assignment across hundreds or thousands of tokens is difficult. Conversely, dense self-guidance, obtained by having a privileged view of the model score its own sampled tokens, offers rich, token-level signals. While inexpensive and comprehensive, this dense guidance can be unreliable, potentially reinforcing erroneous reasoning if the privileged view develops "false confidence" or favors paths that ultimately lead to failure. FlowBalance is motivated by the need to integrate these two types of signals in a way that preserves the reliability of verifier grounding while exploiting the information density of self-guidance, without allowing the latter to lead to self-confirmation of errors.

2. **Preventing Self-Confirmation and Narrowing of Solution Modes**: A significant concern with uncalibrated self-guidance is the risk of self-confirmation. If the model's privileged view incorrectly assigns high confidence to a faulty reasoning step or an ultimately incorrect trajectory, and the policy updates based on this, it can inadvertently reinforce erroneous behaviors. This can lead to a policy that overconcentrates on a narrow set of reasoning strategies, even if some of them are suboptimal or flawed. Furthermore, such ungrounded guidance can shorten reasoning paths, suppress uncertainty-driven exploration, and compromise the policy's ability to discover or maintain diverse, correct strategies. FlowBalance is specifically motivated to counter this by calibrating self-guidance with verifier outcomes, ensuring that dense signals refine the *direction* established by the verifier rather than overriding it or reinforcing mistakes.

3. **Promoting Diverse and Robust Reasoning**: For complex tasks like mathematical reasoning, a truly effective self-improvement system should not merely increase the mean reward but also foster a diverse set of correct reasoning strategies. Over-sharpening one locally preferred trace can make a model brittle to variations in problems or suppress alternative, equally valid, or even more robust approaches. The motivation includes developing an update mechanism that moves probability mass towards correct reasoning while preserving useful alternative strategies and avoiding the collapse into a single, potentially fragile, solution mode.

In essence, the paper asks: *Given on-policy reasoning experience, sparse verified outcomes, and dense but imperfect self-guidance, what normalized response distribution should the next policy learn?* FlowBalance aims to answer this by defining a novel trajectory energy that combines these signals in an outcome-calibrated manner and then fitting the resulting normalized distribution using trajectory balance, thereby facilitating robust, stable, and diverse self-improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FlowBalance introduces a verifier-grounded self-improvement method that operates as an inner loop for policy updates, designed to learn a normalized distribution over complete reasoning responses. The approach integrates sparse terminal rewards with dense, internal self-guidance through a calibrated energy function, optimized via profiled trajectory balance.

#### 3.1. The FlowBalance Cycle

The method follows a five-step self-improvement cycle (depicted in Figure 1 of the paper):
1. **On-Policy Reasoning Experience Generation**: The current policy (π_θ) samples a group of reasoning trajectories (rollout group) for a given prompt (x).
2. **Verifier Feedback**: An external verifier provides sparse, terminal feedback in the form of rewards (R) for each trajectory. These rewards are used to compute a *stopped group-relative advantage* (A).
3. **Privileged-Hindsight Self-Guidance**: A frozen snapshot of the current policy (π_θ-) is used as a privileged-hindsight view (π_H). This view conditions on training-only context (c, e.g., a reference solution) to score the already sampled tokens, generating dense token-level log-probability gains. These gains are aggregated into a *trajectory-level self-guidance gain* (G_H).
4. **Outcome Calibration (Sign Gating)**: The trajectory-level self-guidance gain (G_H) is calibrated using the verifier-derived group advantage (A) through a sign-gating mechanism. This determines whether the guidance is retained, reversed, or disabled.
5. **Distribution Learning via Profiled Trajectory Balance**: The resulting outcome-calibrated self-guidance, combined with the verifier advantage and a reference policy (π_ref), defines a *reference-supported Gibbs target distribution*. This target is internalized into the trainable policy (π_θ) using profiled trajectory balance. The frozen snapshot is then refreshed to repeat the cycle.

#### 3.2. On-Policy Reasoning Experience

For each training iteration, the current policy parameters (θ) are snapshotted as θ-. A group of N responses {y^(i)} is sampled from this frozen rollout policy π_θ-(·|x). A rule-based verifier assigns a terminal reward R_i = R(y^(i); x) based on correctness. The *stopped group-relative advantage* A_i is computed as (R_i - μ_R(x)) / (σ_R(x) + ϵ), where μ_R and σ_R are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the rollout group. These rewards, group statistics, and sampled trajectories are treated as constants (stopped gradients) for the policy update.

#### 3.3. Privileged-Hindsight Self-Guidance

A training-only context (c) is used to create a privileged-hindsight view π_H(·|s_t, c) = π_θ-(·|x, c, y_<t), which observes 'c' after a response has been sampled. This contrasts with the rollout view π_roll(·|s_t) = π_θ-(·|x, y_<t), which does not see 'c'. The hindsight view scores the sampled tokens, and the *clipped token-level hindsight gain* δ_H,t(y; x, c) = clip(log π_H(y_t|s_t, c) - log π_ref(y_t|s_t), -B, B) is computed. These gains are averaged over the trajectory length T to produce the *trajectory-level self-guidance gain* G_H(y|x, c) = (1/T) Σ_t δ_H,t. This G_H acts as a stopped trajectory feature and no gradient propagates through π_H or G_H.

#### 3.4. Outcome-Calibrated Self-Improvement Target

FlowBalance defines its core *energy function* E_FlowBalance(y|x, c) as:
E_FlowBalance(y|x, c) = η_A * A(y) + β_G * G_H(y|x, c) * sgn(A(y))
where η_A and β_G are non-negative coefficients.
The `sgn(A(y))` term implements the outcome calibration:
- If A(y) > 0 (verified success), positive G_H reinforces the trajectory.
- If A(y) < 0 (verifier-rejected failure), positive G_H is *reversed* (-G_H), preventing self-confirmation of a confidently scored failure.
- If A(y) = 0 (no outcome preference), the dense branch (G_H) is disabled.

This energy function, along with a fixed *reference policy* (π_ref, a copy of the initial checkpoint), defines an *unnormalized target distribution* ep_FlowBalance(y|x,c) = π_ref(y|x) exp(E_FlowBalance(y|x,c)/τ), where τ is a temperature parameter. The *normalized target distribution* p*_FlowBalance is then obtained by dividing ep_FlowBalance by its partition function Z_FlowBalance(x,c). The reference policy ensures support and controls drift, while the energy term specifies relative preference.

#### 3.5. Trajectory Balance as a Normalized Self-Update

The policy (π_θ) is updated by minimizing the squared *trajectory-balance residual* (∆_TB). The residual for a sampled response y^(i) is:
∆_TB(y^(i); x, c) = τ log Z_FlowBalance(x, c) + τ log (π_θ(y^(i)|x) / π_ref(y^(i)|x)) - E_FlowBalance(y^(i)|x, c).
When this residual is zero, the policy's probability ratios for any pair of trajectories match those specified by the target distribution (Eq. 17). The main loss function is L_FlowBalance(θ) = E_((x,c)~D) [ (1/(2N)) Σ_i ∆_TB(y^(i); x, c)^2 ]. Gradients are only taken through log π_θ(y^(i)|x). The paper primarily uses the complete-response implementation but mentions subtrajectory balance for denser objectives.

#### 3.6. Profiled Optimization over Rollout Groups

The partition function log Z_FlowBalance(x, c) is not learned but *profiled* directly from the rollout group. For each response y^(i), an estimate of log Z_i(x, c) can be derived. The final profiled log Z_FlowBalance(x, c) is the average of these individual estimates within the group. Gradients are stopped through this estimate. This profiling method preserves all N-1 independent within-group probability contrasts, retaining rich relative evidence from the sampled responses.

#### 3.7. Theoretical Analysis Highlights

The paper's theoretical analysis establishes several properties for FlowBalance's target distribution:
- **Within-Group Contrast Preservation (Proposition 1)**: Profiling the partition function retains all relative probability contrasts among sampled responses.
- **Conservative Minimum-Change Update (Proposition 2)**: The FlowBalance target represents the unique minimum reverse-KL displacement from the reference policy among group distributions that achieve its expected composite energy level.
- **Monotonic Verifier Control (Proposition 3)**: Increasing the verifier coefficient (η_A) monotonically increases the target's expected verifier reward, ensuring the verifier remains an explicit control.
- **Correction Against False-Positive Self-Guidance (Proposition 4)**: For a verified success and a rejected response, sign gating converts positive self-guidance on the rejected response into a probability-ratio correction favoring the verified response, preventing self-reinforcement of failures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluate FlowBalance's performance, stability, and diversity on mathematical reasoning tasks using Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B language models, comparing it against GRPO, OPSD, RLSD, and FlowRL.

#### 4.1. Mathematical Reasoning Accuracy

- **Overall Performance**: FlowBalance achieved the highest aggregate average accuracy across five mathematical benchmarks (AIME24, HMMT25, Minerva, MATH500, OlympiadBench) on both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B backbones.
 - On Qwen3-4B, FlowBalance scored a 64.26% average, outperforming GRPO (62.31), OPSD (54.12), RLSD (59.55), and FlowRL (63.22). It notably improved over GRPO by 1.95 points, RLSD by 4.71 points, and FlowRL by 1.04 points.
 - On Qwen3-8B, FlowBalance reached a 67.61% average, outperforming all baselines and achieving the best mean on every individual benchmark. It surpassed GRPO by 2.12 points, OPSD by 26.45 points, RLSD by 3.49 points, and FlowRL by 1.76 points.
- **Specific Benchmarks**: FlowBalance showed particularly strong gains on AIME24 Pass@16, where diverse correct strategies are beneficial. The consistent, albeit sometimes smaller, gains on Pass@1 benchmarks like MATH500 and OlympiadBench suggest that the distributional update does not trade broad accuracy for sampling-heavy metrics.
- **OPSD Performance**: Direct OPSD exhibited significantly lower performance, particularly on Qwen3-8B, which the authors attribute to potential issues like suppression of epistemic verbalization and shortening of reasoning paths.

#### 4.2. Training Dynamics

- **Training Acceleration**: On Qwen3-8B, FlowBalance reached 0.5 AIME24 validation accuracy in approximately 100 steps, which is 1.43 times faster than GRPO, which required around 143 steps.
- **Training Stability**: FlowBalance maintained near-peak accuracy for over 400 training steps, demonstrating stability over extended training periods. In contrast, GRPO experienced a sharp degradation in performance after approximately 180 steps.
- **Response Length**: FlowBalance consistently maintained longer reasoning trajectories compared to direct OPSD, which rapidly collapsed to significantly shorter responses, supporting the hypothesis that ungrounded local imitation can lead to shortcut behaviors.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

Ablations on the verifier coefficient (η_A) and self-guidance coefficient (β_G) on Qwen3-8B explored the impact of the FlowBalance energy components.
- **Verifier Coefficient (η_A)**: Increasing η_A from 5 to 15 (with β_G=1) monotonically improved the aggregate average accuracy from 65.65% to 67.61%, suggesting the importance of strong verifier grounding.
- **Self-Guidance Coefficient (β_G)**: Increasing β_G from 1 to 3 (with η_A=15) led to a decrease in average accuracy from 67.61% to 65.95%. This result supports the claim that the benefit comes from *calibrated* self-guidance rather than simply strengthening the dense signal, as excessive, uncalibrated emphasis on self-guidance can be detrimental.

#### 4.4. Correct-Strategy Diversity

- **LLM-Judged Diversity**: An LLM-judged AIME24 diagnostic, using Simpson strategy diversity, found that FlowBalance exhibited higher correct-only semantic strategy diversity (0.2194) compared to GRPO (0.1017) and RLSD (0.1456). This indicates that FlowBalance encourages the policy to explore and retain a broader range of genuinely different correct solution strategies, rather than converging on a single template.
- **Case Studies**: Illustrative case studies, such as AIME24 Problem 23, demonstrated that FlowBalance generated distinct mathematical approaches (e.g., a hidden 4x5x8 box embedding) where GRPO used a more standard method (e.g., Cayley-Menger determinant). Additional examples in the appendix confirmed this, showing different mathematical representations (e.g., envelope vs. multiple-root reasoning, meridian-section vs. implicit-normal geometry).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FlowBalance contributes to the field of large language model (LLM) reasoning by addressing the complexities of policy self-improvement from on-policy experience. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized in several key areas:

1. **Robust Self-Improvement Paradigm**: The work introduces a principled framework for combining sparse, reliable external feedback with dense, internal self-guidance. By explicitly calibrating self-guidance with verifier outcomes through a sign-gating mechanism, FlowBalance mitigates the risk of self-confirmation and the amplification of false confidence, which are known pitfalls in direct self-distillation or ungrounded guidance methods. This leads to a more robust and reliable inner-loop update for reasoning models.

2. **Enhanced Performance and Stability**: FlowBalance demonstrates improved mathematical reasoning accuracy across various benchmarks and model scales (Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B). Beyond aggregate performance, it also exhibits faster convergence to target accuracy thresholds and superior training stability over extended periods compared to strong baselines like GRPO. This indicates that its distributional update mechanism is not only effective but also efficient and resilient.

3. **Preservation of Strategy Diversity**: A notable contribution is the empirical evidence that FlowBalance fosters higher correct-only semantic strategy diversity. This is critical for complex reasoning tasks, where multiple valid solution paths exist. By avoiding the collapse into a single reasoning mode, FlowBalance can lead to more flexible and generalizable reasoning capabilities, allowing the model to tackle a broader range of problem variations. This contrasts with methods that might inadvertently narrow the solution space.

4. **Strong Theoretical Grounding**: The method is supported by theoretical analysis establishing several desirable properties of its target distribution. These include the preservation of within-group probability contrasts, characterization as a minimum reverse-KL displacement from the reference at its attained energy level, monotonic verifier control over target reward, and an exact correction mechanism against false-positive self-guidance on rejected responses. These guarantees provide a strong foundation for the algorithmic design.

5. **Addressing Key Challenges in LLM Training**: FlowBalance offers a solution to the long-standing challenge of effectively integrating different forms of supervision (sparse external rewards vs. dense internal signals) for self-improving LLMs. Its approach can potentially be applied or adapted to other long-horizon, complex tasks beyond mathematical reasoning, including agentic behavior, multimodal reasoning, or scientific discovery where detailed reasoning paths are crucial.

The immediate impact is providing a more effective method for fine-tuning LLMs for mathematical reasoning. Longer-term, FlowBalance's principles could inform the design of future self-evolving AI systems, particularly when combined with outer-loop mechanisms for task generation or curriculum learning, ensuring that policy updates lead to genuinely improved, diverse, and robust reasoning capabilities rather than reinforcing learned errors or suboptimal strategies.
