# Towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning for Real-World Robotic Manipulation with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> ARCHIE presents a pipeline that autonomously generates reward functions and success criteria for reinforcement learning agents directly from natural language task descriptions. This system enables one-shot training of robotic manipulation skills for real-world applications by implementing a strict mathematical formalization for LLM-generated rewards, achieving successful sim-to-real transfer for complex tasks on an ABB YuMi robot.

## Problem

- Manually designing effective dense reward functions for real-world robotic reinforcement learning (RL) is complex, time-consuming, and difficult to scale.
- Existing LLM-based reward generation methods often require iterative refinement loops and multiple training stages, leading to high computational costs and reduced autonomy.
- Translating high-level human goals into precise, machine-interpretable success and failure criteria for RL environments is challenging due to inherent ambiguity.

## Method

- ARCHIE proposes a one-shot pipeline where GPT-4 generates Python code for reward functions and explicit task termination conditions (success/failure) directly from natural language descriptions.
- A novel dense reward formalization is enforced, ensuring the terminal reward upon successful task completion is at least one order of magnitude greater than cumulative shaping rewards to stabilize learning.
- A modified ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== algorithm trains policies in simulation, leveraging the LLM-defined success and failure conditions to guide episode termination and learning.

## Results

- ARCHIE's formalized rewards consistently and successfully solved all benchmark tasks, demonstrating significantly higher and more stable success rates compared to unformalized LLM-generated rewards.
- The pipeline successfully trained policies that completed all 10 complex robotic manipulation tasks (both single-arm and bi-manual) on an ABB YuMi robot, outperforming human-engineered distance-based baselines and direct GPT-4 code policies.
- Policies trained entirely in simulation using ARCHIE's method were successfully demonstrated on the physical ABB YuMi robot in real-world settings, validating effective sim-to-real transfer for learned skills.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models (LLMs) are effective at interpreting high-level human instructions and generating appropriate components for reward functions and task criteria.
> - A strict mathematical formalization of LLM-generated reward functions is crucial for ensuring stable and efficient RL policy learning, preventing issues like numerical instability and suboptimal local optima.
> - Policies trained exclusively in simulation with ARCHIE's LLM-generated and formalized rewards can reliably transfer to physical robots without complex safe RL methods or real-world fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Visual Language Models (VLMs) have significantly broadened the capabilities of robotic systems. These models now enable high-level semantic motion planning, allowing robots to interpret complex natural language instructions and translate them into actionable plans. Concurrently, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for enabling agents to autonomously learn and optimize complex behaviors through direct interaction with their environment. While LLMs excel in high-level reasoning and language understanding, RL is crucial for acquiring nuanced, low-level physical skills.

However, a critical bottleneck in deploying RL for real-world robotics is the arduous process of designing effective reward functions. Sparse rewards, while easy to define, present significant exploration challenges for RL agents. Dense rewards, which guide exploration more effectively, demand intricate and often manual engineering, leading to issues of numerical instability and misalignment with true task objectives. This manual effort limits the scalability and accessibility of RL for non-expert users.

The current research landscape has seen various attempts to automate reward function design using LLMs. Notable works like Eureka leverage LLMs (specifically GPT-4) to generate dense reward functions from natural language task descriptions. However, these methods often necessitate iterative "reward reflection," where the LLM evaluates an agent's performance and refines the reward function over multiple training stages. Other approaches also follow similar iterative self-refinement loops, sometimes requiring annotated demonstrations for real-world tasks. While these methods demonstrate promising results in simulation and some real-world applications (e.g., Eureka in locomotion, in dexterous manipulation), the need for multiple training cycles increases overall training time, computational resources, and human intervention, hindering their practicality for widespread real-world deployment.

The work presented in this paper, ARCHIE, distinguishes itself by proposing an unsupervised and *one-shot* pipeline for generating deployable robot skills. Unlike its predecessors, ARCHIE aims to eliminate the iterative reward refinement process by introducing a novel formalization of the reward function and directly integrating task success criteria generation within the LLM's capabilities. This approach directly addresses the limitations of multi-stage training, positioning ARCHIE as a more efficient and practical solution for translating human-readable text into robotic skills without extensive expert feedback or prolonged training cycles. By focusing on a single, automated training stage, ARCHIE pushes the boundaries towards truly autonomous skill acquisition in complex human-informed environments, marking a significant step forward in the quest for more accessible and resource-efficient robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an **Autonomous Reinforcement Learning for Complex Human-Informed Environments (ARCHIE)** pipeline. This pipeline aims to automatically generate reward functions and success criteria for RL agents directly from natural language task descriptions, thereby enabling the unsupervised training of robotic manipulation skills for real-world applications.

The motivation behind ARCHIE stems from several critical challenges currently faced in the field of robotic RL:

1. **Complexity of Reward Design:** While RL offers significant potential for learning complex behaviors, the manual design of effective dense reward functions for real-world tasks is exceedingly difficult and time-consuming. Poorly designed rewards can lead to suboptimal policies, slow learning, or even failure to converge.
2. **Limitations of Existing LLM-based Reward Generation:** Prior research utilizing LLMs for reward generation often requires iterative refinement loops (e.g., reward reflection), where the LLM adjusts the reward function based on feedback from multiple RL training attempts. This multi-stage process is computationally expensive, time-consuming, and still demands a degree of human oversight or extensive data, thereby limiting its "autonomy" and practicality.
3. **Ambiguity in Task Definition:** Translating high-level human goals into precise, machine-interpretable success and failure criteria for RL environments is a non-trivial task. This ambiguity can hinder robust policy learning and evaluation.
4. **Efficiency and Resource Consumption:** The long training times associated with traditional RL and iterative LLM-based approaches result in high energy and computational resource consumption. A more streamlined, one-shot process is crucial for broader applicability and sustainability.
5. **Bridging Sim-to-Real Gap with Minimal Intervention:** The ultimate goal is to enable robots to learn in simulation and transfer these skills to the real world with minimal human intervention or specialized safe RL methods, especially for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks.

By addressing these motivations, ARCHIE seeks to create a fully automated, efficient, and robust method for transforming human intent, expressed in natural language, into deployable robot skills, thereby democratizing access to advanced robotic capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ARCHIE pipeline is structured into two main phases: Initialization and Autonomous Skill Learning, designed to translate human language task descriptions into deployable robot skills in a single, automated process.

**A. Initialization Stage:**
The first stage focuses on setting up the environment and processing user input.

- **Simulation Generation:** While manual setup is an option, ARCHIE includes a methodology for automatic simulation generation. This involves using Deep Learning models like **YoloWorld** for open-vocabulary object detection and **NanoSAM** for object segmentation from 3D camera images and depth planes. These tools allow the system to extract point cloud representations of objects, compute their coordinates relative to the robot frame, and populate the simulated environment with corresponding 3D models. The LLM assists this process by inferring necessary text descriptors for object detection from the user's task description.
- **Natural Language to Rewards:** This core component leverages the code-writing capabilities of **GPT-4**. The user provides a natural language task description, crucially including explicit geometric definitions for **success and failure conditions**. GPT-4 is then prompted to generate a new Python class that extends a predefined base environment class. This base class provides fundamental functionalities like simulation control, robot kinematics, and APIs for contact/collision checks and pose readings. The generated class must redefine the reward function and, uniquely, implement the task's termination conditions (success and failure). This explicit inclusion of termination conditions directly addresses ambiguity and guides the agent's exploration by penalizing failure.
 The prompt design is carefully structured, comprising: an introduction outlining the LLM's task; the detailed robot's task description with geometric constraints; a "Coding Context" block providing the base Python class and documentation, alongside explicit instructions for implementing the dense reward formalization; and an "RL Context" offering general advice and examples for reward function design.

**B. Autonomous Skill Learning Stage:**
The second stage utilizes the LLM-generated code to train an RL agent.

- **Dense Reward Formalization:** This is a cornerstone of ARCHIE's novelty. Unlike previous iterative approaches, ARCHIE imposes a strict formalization on the LLM-generated reward function to ensure stability and efficiency from the first attempt. The reward `r'` is defined as a sum of multiple shaping terms `r_k` (generated by the LLM) and a **terminal reward term `R_F`**, multiplied by a binary success classifier `Φ(s_t+1)`.
 `r'(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) = Σ_k r_k(s_t, a_t) + R_F(s_t, a_t) * Φ(s_t+1)`
 The crucial aspect is the definition of `R_F` as `10 * T * max(Σ_k+ r_k+(s,a), 1)`, where `T` is the maximum episode length and `r_k+` are positive bonus shaping terms. This ensures that the terminal reward upon successful task completion is at least **one order of magnitude greater** than the cumulative shaping rewards achievable during a trajectory. This robust weighting prevents the agent from getting stuck in local optima or high-rewarding non-terminal states, a common issue with unbalanced reward components. This formalization is enforced as a *hard programming constraint* in the GPT-4 prompt, making ARCHIE a "one-shot" process, bypassing the need for iterative reward reflection.
- **Policy Learning:** A modified **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)** algorithm is employed for policy training in simulation. Modifications include running `N` parallel simulated environments to accelerate exploration and delayed actor updates (similar to TD3) to reduce computational burden. The LLM-defined success `Φ` and failure `Γ` conditions are used to terminate episodes, guiding the learning process. The state space encompasses end-effector poses, velocities, gripper joint states, and object poses, while the action space consists of end-effector reference linear/angular velocities and gripper joint velocity. The policies are trained entirely in simulation, relying on the availability of accurate kinematic and dynamic robot models, with successful demonstration on a real robot setup without the need for safe RL methods, implying effective sim-to-real transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of ARCHIE was conducted in two phases: first, on benchmark environments to demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed reward formalization, and second, on complex robotic manipulation tasks using an ABB YuMi robot.

**A. Benchmark Environments:**
To isolate the impact of the dense reward formalization, simplified 2D (grasp and lift, grasp and slide, place) and 3D (constrained cube pushing) simulation tasks were used. ARCHIE's performance was compared against a baseline where GPT-4 generated rewards without the proposed formalization (referred to as "Eureka (step 0)").
- **Result:** Agents trained with ARCHIE's formalization consistently and successfully solved all benchmark tasks, demonstrating significantly higher and more stable success rates. In contrast, agents trained with the unformalized, purely GPT-4-generated rewards ("Eureka (step 0)") exhibited much less consistency and struggled to converge.
- **Key Insight:** This directly validates that the explicit terminal reward and the guaranteed magnitude difference effectively stabilize policy learning. It mitigates issues arising from numerically unbalanced shaping terms, a common challenge with raw LLM-generated rewards, preventing agents from getting trapped in suboptimal, high-shaping-reward states.

**B. Robotic Manipulation Tasks:**
The methodology was then applied to 10 distinct, more complex robotic manipulation tasks on an ABB YuMi collaborative robot, including both single-arm (e.g., CubeSlide, VialGrasp, CentrifugeInsertion) and bi-manual operations (e.g., BimanualPenInTape, BimanualBoxLift, BimanualHandover). Comparisons were made against manually defined, purely distance-based reward functions (a common human-engineered baseline) and direct "Code Policies" generated by GPT-4 for control.
- **Result (ARCHIE vs. Baselines):**
 - **ARCHIE:** The agents trained using rewards generated by ARCHIE successfully completed *all 10 robotic manipulation tasks*, achieving high success rates in most cases. While some learning instability was observed (attributed to potential reward function discontinuities), the overall performance was robust.
 - **Human-Engineered Distance-Based Baseline:** This baseline only managed to solve a *few of the tasks*, and even then, with *low success rates*, highlighting the limitations of simple, generic reward structures for complex behaviors.
 - **GPT-4 Code Policies:** Direct control policies generated by GPT-4 *failed to solve any of the selected tasks*, underscoring LLMs' limitations in generating precise low-level control code without specialized interfaces or extensive training.
- **Real-World Demonstration:** Critically, policies trained *exclusively in simulation* using ARCHIE's generated rewards were successfully demonstrated on the physical ABB YuMi robot in laboratory settings. This "sim-to-real" transfer without the need for complex safe RL methods or real-world fine-tuning speaks volumes about the robustness and practical utility of the generated skills.
- **Conclusion:** The results strongly indicate that LLMs are highly effective in interpreting high-level human instructions and translating them into appropriate reward components. However, the *mathematical formalization* introduced by ARCHIE is essential to structure these components effectively, ensuring stable and successful RL policy learning. This effectively leverages LLMs' reasoning capabilities for reward design, while allowing RL to handle the low-level skill acquisition.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ARCHIE pipeline represents a significant leap forward in the field of autonomous reinforcement learning for robotics, offering several critical advancements and potential impacts:

1. **Democratization of Robot Learning:** By automating the complex and expert-intensive process of reward function design and task criteria definition, ARCHIE lowers the barrier for non-experts to program robots. Users can now simply describe a task in natural language, and the system autonomously generates the necessary learning framework, paving the way for wider adoption of RL in diverse applications.

2. **Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability:** The "one-shot" nature of ARCHIE, which avoids iterative reward reflection and multiple training stages, dramatically reduces the overall training time, computational resources, and energy consumption. This makes RL-based skill acquisition more efficient and scalable, enabling faster development cycles for new robotic capabilities and reducing the environmental footprint of extensive experimentation.

3. **Robustness of LLM-Generated Rewards:** The introduction of a strict reward formalization, which ensures the terminal reward is significantly higher than accumulated shaping rewards, is a crucial methodological contribution. This hard programming constraint directly addresses the issue of unbalanced reward components and numerical instability often observed in unconstrained LLM-generated rewards. The demonstrated success in benchmark and complex real-world tasks validates this approach as a robust method for leveraging LLMs in reward design.

4. **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The successful demonstration of policies trained purely in simulation on an ABB YuMi collaborative robot for various single-arm and bi-manual manipulation tasks highlights the immediate practical relevance of ARCHIE. This ability to achieve reliable sim-to-real transfer without requiring specialized safe RL methods or real-world fine-tuning is a major step towards deploying LLM-assisted autonomous learning in industrial and service robotics. The collaboration with ABB Corporate Research further underscores this practical orientation.

5. **Bridging High-Level Cognition and Low-Level Control:** The findings reinforce the complementary strengths of LLMs and RL. While LLMs excel at understanding human intent and generating high-level semantic components for tasks, they struggle with precise, low-level control. ARCHIE effectively harnesses the LLM's high-level reasoning to define effective reward signals, which then guide RL algorithms to master the intricate low-level physical interactions required for manipulation.

Looking ahead, ARCHIE opens several exciting avenues for future research. Integrating real-world feedback and human preferences could further refine LLM-generated rewards, leading to even more nuanced and adaptable robotic behaviors. Incorporating safe RL strategies during sim-to-real transfer could enhance robustness for applications with higher safety criticality. Moreover, extending the methodology to more complex multi-robot or multi-agent scenarios could unlock entirely new possibilities for advanced automation. Ultimately, ARCHIE significantly contributes to the vision of truly autonomous robots that can learn and adapt complex skills with minimal human intervention, fundamentally transforming robot programming paradigms.
