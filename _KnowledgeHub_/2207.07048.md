# Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis in ML-based Science

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper surveys data leakage in machine-learning-based science and finds it in 17 fields, affecting 329 papers. It offers a taxonomy of 8 leakage types and proposes model info sheets, a reporting template that makes authors justify the absence of leakage. In civil war prediction, every paper claiming complex ML beats logistic regression fails to reproduce because of leakage.

## Problem

- Data leakage is a known cause of ML errors, but it had not been studied comprehensively in scientific applications, so mitigations for it remain understudied.
- Mitigation strategies built for modeling contests and engineering applications often do not carry over to ML-based science, where models are used as evidence for scientific claims.
- Existing checklists and model cards do not address leakage, and most checklists are field-specific, so reporting standards for ML-based science are underspecified and errors go uncaught before publication.

## Method

- Literature survey of 20 papers that report pitfalls across 17 fields, from which the authors derive a ==taxonomy of 8 leakage types==, from textbook errors to open research problems.
- **Model info sheets**, inspired by ==model cards==, require researchers to give precise arguments that a model used for a scientific claim has ==no leakage==. They cover every leakage type found in the survey.
- ==Reproducibility case study== on civil war prediction. A systematic search yields 124 papers, narrowed to 12 with ==train-test splits== and full code and data. Errors are corrected and the data re-analyzed.

## Results

- The survey finds leakage in all **17** fields, collectively affecting **329** papers. Some fields have every reviewed paper affected, such as **62 of 62** in radiology and **30 of 30** in computer security.
- Errors appear in **4 of the 12** civil war papers, exactly the 4 claiming complex ML beats logistic regression. Corrected, ML models do no better, and Wang's AUC gap drops from **0.14** to **0.01**.
- Model info sheets would detect leakage in **all 4** papers, though none could have been caught by reading them. **9 of 12** papers lack significance tests, and one test AUC of **0.85** has a 95% interval of **0.66 to 0.95**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Impressive ML results in scientific fields should be treated as suspect until leakage is ruled out. Reading a paper alone cannot reveal these errors, so code and data checks matter.
> - Requiring authors to argue for the absence of each leakage type shifts the burden of proof and makes errors detectable, which is more effective than generic checklists.
> - Complex models often add little over simple baselines once errors are fixed, so always compare against a strong simple baseline and report uncertainty, especially on small test sets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Civil-war forecasting offers a clear test of how machine learning is used in science. A researcher compares a complex model with a familiar statistical baseline, Logistic Regression (LR), and sees a striking advantage. That comparison only means something if the test data stand apart from model-building and represent the future or population the claim concerns. The authors reviewed papers claiming that complex ML models outperform LR in civil-war prediction and found leakage in all four of them. After correction, the complex models generally did not do better.

Four columns compare claims, errors, impacts, and discussion for Muchlinski et al., Colaresi and Mahmood, Wang, and Kaufman et al. The charts above the columns contrast reported with corrected model results, using different metrics and comparisons.

### 2. A score can learn the wrong lesson

Data leakage is a spurious relationship between predictors and the target. It is introduced by data collection, sampling, or preprocessing, and it inflates performance because it will not hold in the distribution the scientific claim concerns.

The authors' taxonomy describes several forms. In one, the training and test data are not cleanly separated, as when imputation is performed using training and test data together. In another, the predictors are proxies for the outcome, or they are unavailable at the time of the outcome.

A test set can also be cleanly separated and still fail to represent the scientific distribution. Temporal order, repeated people or units, or biased sampling can make the evaluation unlike the population or future the paper claims to address.

These errors can be hidden in code and data even when a paper's description looks sound. The authors' survey compiles 20 review papers across 17 fields, collectively affecting 329 papers. The authors call this total a lower bound, and they say it is not a systematic meta-review.

### 3. Make the scientific claim auditable

The authors propose **model info sheets**, a reporting tool inspired by model cards. Each sheet asks researchers to make three arguments:

- The test data stayed separate during preprocessing, modeling, and evaluation.
- Each feature is legitimate for the task.
- The test set represents the distribution of scientific interest, including relevant time windows and sampling.

These written arguments could help authors catch errors. They could also give reviewers a more focused way to question modeling decisions when reproducing code is too time-consuming. The sheets are a proposal and do not guarantee detection. Their claims cannot be verified without computational reproducibility, and subtle leakage may escape authors or reviewers without ML expertise.

### 4. The civil-war stress test

The authors' search produced 124 papers. Of these, 15 met their focus and train/test criteria, and 12 of those shared complete code and data. The authors examined those 12 for errors and reanalyzed the data after correcting the errors they identified. They found leakage in four papers, the same four that claimed complex-model superiority over LR.

The leakage mechanisms differed across the four papers:

- joint or reused imputation;
- temporal leakage from shuffled cross-validation;
- outcome-proxy features.

The Wang (2019) paper gives the clearest numerical case. The metric is AUC, the area under the ROC curve, which measures ranking discrimination across thresholds. In Wang, the reported AUC difference between complex ML and LR falls from 0.14 to 0.01 after correction. When the errors are corrected, the complex models did not perform substantively better than LR in the reviewed studies.

This is evidence about the reviewed papers. It does not show that complex models never outperform LR. The authors also caution that the corrected Muchlinski results still rely on imputing a test set with over 95% missing values.

Density plots of the `agexp` predictor for peace and war: (a) original Hegre et al. data ignoring missing values; (b) the imputed dataset used for training; (c) observations added during imputation; (d) the out-of-sample test set. Imputation changes the distributions, and the test-set peace peak is close to the imputed-training peak.

### 5. Make claims inspectable

The paper catalogues leakage failures and shows that correcting them removed or sharply reduced the claimed advantage in the reviewed civil-war papers. It then proposes sheets that turn the link between score and claim into explicit arguments. Their effectiveness still depends on reproducible code and data and adequate ML expertise.
