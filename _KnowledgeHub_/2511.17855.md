# QuickLAP: Quick Language-Action Preference Learning for Semi-Autonomous Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> QuickLAP introduces a Bayesian framework that fuses physical corrections and natural language feedback to infer human preferences for semi-autonomous agents. The system achieves lower reward inference error and improves subjective measures of human-robot collaboration by addressing the ambiguities inherent in single-modality feedback.

## Problem

- Physical corrections from humans are precise but often ambiguous, leading to "feature coupling" where it's difficult for robots to infer specific reward features.
- Natural language feedback can convey high-level intent but is frequently vague or underspecified, lacking the physical grounding needed for concrete robot actions.
- Existing human-robot interaction methods often treat feedback modalities in isolation or struggle with real-time online adaptation to combine their complementary strengths.

## Method

- **QuickLAP** employs a ==Bayesian framework== to jointly interpret physical corrections and natural language, factorizing the posterior distribution over preference parameters.
- A ==dual-LLM== (Large Language Model) approach processes language: an Attention LLM identifies relevant features, and a Preference LLM determines desired shifts and associated confidence.
- It models language as a noisy observation of a latent reward shift and incorporates an attention-modulated ==conditional prior==, enabling a closed-form, element-wise ==MAP update== for real-time adaptation.

## Results

- In simulated robot manipulation and autonomous driving tasks, QuickLAP significantly reduced reward-weight error (NMSE) by **over 2x** compared to physical-only baselines.
- User studies showed that non-expert users rated QuickLAP's robot behaviors as significantly more "understandable" (**p=.023**) and "collaborative" (**p=.029**) than those learned from physical-only feedback.
- QuickLAP achieved significantly lower NMSE (**0.46**) in non-expert user studies, demonstrating that it facilitated teaching functionally better behaviors compared to Language Only (**0.57**) and physical-only (**0.58**) approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing physical feedback (for grounded precision) with language feedback (for semantic clarity) enables more accurate and less ambiguous inference of user preferences than either modality alone.
> - Providing physical context (e.g., feature differences from physical corrections) is crucial for LLMs to effectively map abstract language commands to specific, actionable robot feature changes.
> - Adaptive weighting mechanisms, based on language-derived attention masks and confidence scores, allow the system to robustly handle noisy, ambiguous, or even contradictory multimodal human input.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of human-robot interaction (HRI) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), specifically focusing on how robots can infer human preferences to adapt their behavior in semi-autonomous settings. Traditional IRL methods often learn reward functions from physical demonstrations or corrections. However, as noted in the related work, these physical inputs are inherently ambiguous; a correction might influence multiple features simultaneously, making it difficult to discern the user's specific intent. For example, a physical steering nudge could indicate a desire to avoid an obstacle, change lanes, or adjust speed, without specifying the primary motivation.

Concurrently, advancements in natural language processing, particularly large language models (LLMs), have enabled robots to interpret verbal commands and goals. Existing approaches in language-guided robot learning often rely on extensive paired datasets of trajectories and language, or treat language instructions as precise, self-contained inputs. These methods frequently lack online adaptation capabilities or fail to interpret language in the context of concurrent physical actions, which can be crucial for resolving ambiguities inherent in language itself (e.g., vague commands like "Stay away!").

QuickLAP addresses the limitations of both physical-only and language-only approaches by proposing a unified Bayesian framework for real-time multimodal feedback. It aims to overcome the ambiguity of physical corrections by leveraging the semantic clarity of language, while simultaneously grounding abstract language in physical actions to resolve vagueness. This positions QuickLAP as an advancement in online, interactive reward learning that integrates complementary human feedback modalities, moving beyond methods that treat them in isolation or require extensive offline training. The work builds upon prior research in learning from physical human feedback (e.g., Bajcsy et al., Jain et al.) and the increasing use of language models in robot control, seeking to combine their strengths in a principled manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for QuickLAP stems from the observation that neither physical nor language feedback alone provides a complete signal for robots to accurately infer human preferences.
- **Physical corrections** (e.g., kinesthetic nudges, steering adjustments) are precise and physically grounded, offering immediate, reactive adjustments. However, they are often opaque in intent, leading to ambiguity regarding which specific reward features the human desires to change. A single physical intervention might inadvertently affect multiple correlated features, making it challenging for the robot to isolate the user's true preference. This can result in "feature coupling," where an intended correction to one aspect (e.g., speed) unintentionally alters another (e.g., lateral position).
- **Natural language** feedback (e.g., "Stay away from the cones!") can explicitly convey high-level goals and underlying intent, clarifying what matters to the user. However, language can be vague, underspecified, or context-dependent, often lacking the physical grounding required to guide specific robot behavior independently. Without corresponding physical actions, language alone might not fully convey *how* the robot should act or the precise magnitude of a desired change.

The **key objective** of QuickLAP is to introduce a framework that effectively fuses these two complementary modalities – physical corrections and natural language – to infer user reward functions in real time. The core insight is to treat language not merely as a description of preferences, but as a probabilistic observation that clarifies the user's latent reward state, modulating how concurrent physical corrections should be interpreted.

Specifically, QuickLAP aims to achieve the following:
- **Resolve ambiguity**: By combining physical precision with linguistic clarity, QuickLAP seeks to overcome the inherent ambiguities of each modality when used in isolation. Language clarifies the "why" behind a physical correction, while the physical action grounds the "what" of a verbal command.
- **Enable rapid, real-time online learning**: The framework is designed for efficient, closed-form updates of reward parameters, allowing the robot to adapt quickly to user feedback during task execution. This is critical for interactive scenarios where user preferences may change or need refinement on the fly.
- **Enhance robustness**: The probabilistic nature of QuickLAP aims to make the system robust to noisy, ambiguous, or even contradictory feedback from either channel, allowing it to adapt decisively when signals are clear and remain stable when inputs are uncertain.
- **Improve human-robot collaboration**: By more accurately inferring user intent, QuickLAP intends to lead to robot behaviors that are more understandable, predictable, and collaborative from the user's perspective, thereby improving the overall human-robot interaction experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

QuickLAP (Quick Language–Action Preference learning) is presented as a Bayesian framework designed for online reward inference by jointly interpreting physical and language feedback.

**Problem Formulation:**
The robot operates under deterministic dynamics, optimizing a linear reward function $R_θ = θ^⊤Φ(ξ)$, where $Φ(ξ)$ aggregates task-relevant features (e.g., distance to obstacles, speed) over a trajectory ξ, and θ represents the relative importance of these features. The robot uses Model Predictive Control (MPC) to generate trajectories based on its current estimate $\hat{θ}_t$. A human provides feedback through physical corrections ($ξ_H$) and/or natural language utterances ($l_t$), leading to a corrected trajectory. The robot's goal is to infer the human's true preference weights ($θ^⋆$) online.

**Learning from Physical Corrections:**
Following prior work, physical corrections are modeled using a Boltzmann noisily-rational model. The likelihood of observing the human's corrected trajectory $ξ_H$ given the robot's trajectory $ξ_R$ and the true preference θ is approximated as:
$P(ξ_H | ξ_R, θ) \approx exp(θ^⊤(Φ(ξ_H) - Φ(ξ_R)) - λ∥ξ_H - ξ_R∥^2)$
This model reflects that humans prefer trajectories with higher rewards and incur effort for large corrections. In a physical-only setting, a simple MAP update adjusts $θ$ in the direction of the feature difference $∆Φ = Φ(ξ_H) - Φ(ξ_R)$.

**QuickLAP's Bayesian Framework:**
QuickLAP factorizes the posterior distribution over preference parameters $P(θ | ξ_H, ξ_R, l)$ as:
$P(θ | ξ_H, ξ_R, l) \propto P(ξ_H | ξ_R, θ) \cdot P(l | ξ_H, ξ_R, θ) \cdot P(θ)$
This requires defining the language likelihood $P(l | ξ_H, ξ_R, θ)$ and a conditional prior $P(θ | \hat{r})$.

**A. Learning from Language Input:**
Language interpretation is decomposed into two components using a dual-LLM framework, processing the utterance in context (which includes $∆Φ$, current $\hat{θ}_t$, and environment description):
1. **Attention Language Model ($LM_{att}$)**: This model identifies which features are relevant to the human's utterance, producing an attention mask $r \in \{0, 1\}^d$. Each component $r_i$ indicates whether feature $ϕ_i$ is relevant. This mask influences the conditional prior.
2. **Preference Language Model ($LM_{pref}$)**: This model determines how attended features should change, generating a confidence vector $m \in^d$ and a shift vector $μ \in R^d$. The shift $μ$ represents the suggested magnitude and direction of the update, and $m$ indicates the LLM's certainty.

**Language Likelihood:**
Instead of directly modeling $P(l | ξ_H, ξ_R, θ)$, which is intractable, QuickLAP introduces a latent proxy variable $μ_t = θ - θ_t$, representing the reward shift the utterance is intended to convey. The $LM_{pref}$ output $(\mu, m)$ is treated as a noisy observation of this latent shift. This leads to a Gaussian likelihood for language:
$P(μ_t | θ, θ_t) = N(μ_t; θ - θ_t, diag(σ^2_L(m_t)))$
Here, $σ_L$ modulates the standard deviation as a function of confidence $m_t$, such that high confidence leads to small variance (strong influence of language) and low confidence leads to large variance (reduced influence). This allows factorization across dimensions.

**B. Conditional Prior:**
The attention mask $r$ from $LM_{att}$ is incorporated into a conditional Gaussian prior $P(θ | \hat{r}_t)$. The prior variance (or inverse precision $\Lambda_{prior,i}$) for each feature $θ_i$ depends on $\hat{r}_{t,i}$. For features with low attention ($\hat{r}_{t,i} \rightarrow 0$), precision is high (small variance), anchoring $θ_i$ to its current estimate $θ_{t,i}$. For features with high attention ($\hat{r}_{t,i} \rightarrow 1$), precision is lower (higher variance), allowing $θ_i$ to adapt more freely.

**C. Full Posterior and MAP Update:**
By substituting the physical likelihood, language likelihood, and conditional prior into the log-posterior and setting its gradient with respect to $θ$ to zero, a closed-form MAP update rule is derived. This update is applied element-wise for each feature $i$:
$\hat{θ}_{t+1,i} = \hat{θ}_{t,i} + \frac{σ^2_{L,i} ∆Φ_i + μ_{t,i}}{\Lambda_{prior,i} σ^2_{L,i} + 1}$

This equation shows a Kalman-style fusion. A scalar gain $κ_i(m_{t,i}, \hat{r}_{t,i})$ adaptively weights the contributions of the physical correction ($∆Φ_i$) and the language-suggested shift ($μ_{t,i}$). If language confidence is high, the update is dominated by $μ_{t,i}$. If language confidence is low, more weight is given to $∆Φ_i$. If attention for a feature is low, the update for that feature is suppressed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated QuickLAP through simulated experiments in robotic manipulation and autonomous driving, followed by user studies.

**Simulated Experiments:**
Two domains were used:
- **Robot Manipulation (Robosuite)**: An object transport task in a "Two-Block Environment" with obstacles and a region of indifference. The task inherently features coupling between obstacle avoidance and path efficiency.
- **Autonomous Driving (InterACT Lab Driving Simulator)**: Four scenarios of increasing complexity (2-lane Cone, 2-lane Cone + Puddle, 3-lane Cone + Puddle + Car, 4-lane Cone + Puddle + Cars), designed to test disambiguation of trade-offs among multiple penalized features.

**Baselines:**
1. **Physical-Only (pHRI)**: Uses only physical corrections for reward updates.
2. **Masked Physical Update (Masked pHRI)**: Incorporates the language-derived attention mask ($r$) into the conditional prior but relies solely on physical corrections for update magnitude.
3. **QuickLAP Language Only**: Uses the language-derived shift ($μ$) and confidence ($m$) for updates, with LLM access to physical intervention context, but removes the physical correction's direct contribution to the update magnitude.
4. **No-Context Baseline (Ablation, Appendix)**: LLM receives only language utterance without physical context.

**Evaluation Metric:** Normalized Mean Squared Error (NMSE) between learned preference weights ($\hat{θ}$) and ground-truth user preferences ($θ^⋆$).

**Simulated Results:**
- **Robot Manipulation**: QuickLAP (0.0444 $\pm$ 0.0052) and QuickLAP Language Only (0.0427 $\pm$ 0.0043) significantly reduced reward-weight error (by over 2x) compared to pHRI (0.1018 $\pm$ 0.0001) and Masked pHRI (0.1000 $\pm$ 0.0002). This indicated language's effectiveness in resolving ambiguity. In this controlled setting, the language-only variant performed similarly to the full QuickLAP model.
- **Driving Scenario**: QuickLAP consistently achieved lower NMSE across all four environments. For example, in the CP environment, QuickLAP (0.0761 $\pm$ 0.1090) showed a reduction of over 50% compared to Masked pHRI (0.3034 $\pm$ 0.0236) and pHRI (0.5941 $\pm$ 0.0013).
- **Convergence**: QuickLAP demonstrated faster convergence than baselines, stabilizing below 0.25 NMSE by the third intervention, while baselines remained above 0.40. This suggests QuickLAP efficiently integrates feedback.
- **No-Context Ablation (Appendix)**: The "No-Context" baseline, where the LLM received language without physical context, performed significantly worse, confirming that physical grounding is essential for the LLM to map abstract language to specific feature changes.

**User Evaluations:**
Two user studies were conducted:
- **Pilot Study (N=12 expert users)**: Robotic manipulation scenario using a 3D SpaceMouse. QuickLAP and QuickLAP Language Only outperformed pHRI in NMSE, similar to simulation. This indicated robustness to real-world expert inputs.
- **Non-Expert User Study (N=15 non-expert users)**: Driving scenario using a physical steering wheel.
 - **User-reported Constructs (Likert Scale)**: QuickLAP was rated significantly more "understandable" (p=.023) and "collaborative" (p=.029) than pHRI. No significant difference was found for "ease of use" or "predictability," though empirical trends favored QuickLAP.
 - **Overall Preference (Ranking)**: Participants significantly preferred QuickLAP over QuickLAP Language Only (p=.048) and pHRI (p=.022).
 - **Performance Error (NMSE)**: QuickLAP achieved significantly lower NMSE (0.46) than both Language Only (0.57, p=.015) and pHRI (0.58, p=.040). This demonstrated that QuickLAP facilitated teaching functionally better behaviors.
- **Robustness to Real-world Inputs (Discussion)**: The user study highlighted QuickLAP's robustness to realistic challenges like contradictory inputs (e.g., verbal command to "stay away" while physically moving towards an obstacle) and imprecise language (e.g., mispronouncing "cone" as "coin"). QuickLAP's confidence-weighted formulation helped prevent incorrect reward shifts in these cases.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

QuickLAP represents a significant step in human-robot interaction by providing a principled, real-time Bayesian framework for fusing multimodal human feedback.
- **Improved Reward Learning Accuracy**: By combining the precision of physical corrections with the semantic clarity of natural language, QuickLAP consistently achieves lower reward inference error compared to single-modality or heuristic multimodal baselines. This leads to robots learning user preferences more accurately and efficiently.
- **Enhanced Interpretability and Collaboration**: The user studies indicate that QuickLAP improves subjective aspects of human-robot interaction, making robot behavior more understandable and fostering a greater sense of collaboration. This is crucial for developing intuitive and effective interactive robot systems.
- **Robustness to Ambiguity**: The framework's adaptive weighting mechanism, based on language-derived attention masks and confidence scores, allows it to handle ambiguous, noisy, or even contradictory feedback from either modality. This robustness is critical for real-world deployments where human input is often imperfect.
- **Online and Real-time Adaptation**: QuickLAP's closed-form update rule enables rapid, real-time adaptation of robot behavior during interaction, which is essential for dynamic and interactive tasks where preferences may evolve or need immediate refinement.
- **Generalizability**: While demonstrated in robotic manipulation and autonomous driving, the underlying Bayesian framework and dual-LLM approach are presented as generalizable to other domains where users can provide both physical "show" and verbal "tell" feedback, such as assistive robotics or collaborative drones.
- **Foundational for Future HRI**: This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions, including exploring more modalities (gaze, gesture, facial expressions), improving LLM calibration and grounding, and dynamically generating reward features in complex, underspecified environments. It highlights the practical necessity of physical grounding for language models in real-world HRI scenarios, even when language models themselves provide rich contextual understanding.

Overall, QuickLAP advances the state-of-the-art in interactive robot learning by addressing fundamental challenges of ambiguity and completeness in human feedback, paving the way for more intelligent, adaptive, and user-centric robotic systems.
