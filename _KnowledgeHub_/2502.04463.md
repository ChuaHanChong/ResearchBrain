# Training Language Models to Reason Efficiently

> [!summary] Summary
> Carnegie Mellon University researchers developed an online reinforcement learning method to train large language models to produce shorter, more efficient chain-of-thought rationales while largely maintaining accuracy. The approach enables up to a 50% reduction in inference tokens with less than a 5% drop in accuracy for a 7B model on math reasoning tasks, offering a controllable trade-off via a tunable length penalty.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) employing chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting for complex reasoning tasks incur high inference costs due to long generated rationales, increasing latency and computational load.
- The economic feasibility, user experience, and environmental sustainability of these powerful reasoning models are hampered by their substantial operational costs.
- Existing efficiency techniques primarily focus on system-level optimizations or model compression, without directly addressing the efficiency of the reasoning process itself.

## Method

- An online reinforcement learning (RL) framework is introduced, fine-tuning pre-trained reasoning LLMs using ==Proximal Policy Optimization== (PPO) and a REINFORCE Leave One Out (RLOO) advantage estimator.
- The RL objective is modified with a tunable length penalty α, which proportionally reduces the reward for longer outputs, normalized per-prompt to account for problem difficulty.
- The method trains a family of models from a single base, allowing precise control over the trade-off between inference compute and model performance via the α hyperparameter.

## Results

- On math benchmarks, the **7B** reasoning model achieved up to a **50%** reduction in generated tokens while incurring less than a **5%** decrease in accuracy, outperforming supervised fine-tuning and direct preference optimization baselines.
- The method demonstrated difficulty-adaptive token reduction, showing an **83%** token saving on easier tasks like GSM8K compared to a **27%** saving on harder tasks like AIME2024.
- Training is computationally efficient, requiring approximately 20 hours on 4-8 GH200 GPUs for fine-tuning, making it accessible for academic resources.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The novel per-prompt normalized length penalty allows models to adapt reasoning depth, reducing verbosity for easier problems while maintaining necessary detail for harder ones.
> - Avoiding normalization of the token-level advantage function in RL training is crucial for the length penalty to effectively control output length without severely degrading accuracy.
> - The approach provides theoretical guarantees, under idealized conditions, ensuring both accuracy preservation and the generation of the shortest possible correct response.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental challenge emerging from the rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs): the high inference cost associated with their growing capabilities, particularly in complex reasoning tasks. The broader research landscape for LLMs is characterized by several key trends:

- **Scaling Laws and Diminishing Returns:** Initial breakthroughs in LLM performance were largely driven by scaling model size and training data (Kaplan et al., 2020). However, this approach is encountering diminishing returns and exorbitant computational costs, necessitating alternative strategies for capability improvement.
- **Emergence of Reasoning Models (Chain-of-Thought):** To overcome the limitations of direct output, frontier reasoning models like OpenAI's o1, Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking Experimental, and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) have adopted "chain-of-thought" (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022). These models generate intermediate tokens to "think" step-by-step before producing a final answer, leading to unprecedented problem-solving abilities, especially in mathematical reasoning.
- **The Inference Cost Problem:** While powerful, these reasoning models incur substantial deployment costs. The generation of long CoTs increases inference latency, memory footprint (due to KV cache growth), and computational load (quadratic attention mechanism in transformers). This makes their economic feasibility, user experience, and environmental sustainability significant concerns.

This work positions itself directly against this "inference cost problem" of large reasoning models. It is complementary to existing efforts in LLM efficiency, which can be broadly categorized as:
- **System-level techniques:** Methods like speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) or optimized batching engines (vLLM, Kwon et al., 2023a) accelerate inference at the infrastructure level.
- **Model-based techniques:** Approaches such as weight pruning (Liu et al., 2018) and quantization (Lin et al., 2024) reduce the model's computational footprint.

Unlike these, Arora and Zanette's work focuses on making the *reasoning process itself* more efficient by teaching models to generate *shorter, yet accurate* CoTs. This approach is orthogonal to and can be combined with existing efficiency methods.

The paper also acknowledges several concurrent and related works:
- **Overthinking Phenomena:** Chen et al. (2024) investigate "overthinking" in LLMs and propose mitigation using preference data.
- **Length Penalties in RL:** Kimi k1.5 (Team et al., 2025) reports using a length penalty in online RL, similar in principle but without the explicit tunable parameter for a family of models.
- **Offline RL for CoT Pruning:** O1-Pruner (Luo et al., 2025) uses an offline RL objective to minimize tokens while maintaining accuracy.
- **Controlling Compute/Length:** Aggarwal & Welleck (2025) train models to follow exact token constraints, while Qu et al. (2025) frame compute minimization as a meta-RL problem. Xia et al. (2025) prune tokens based on semantic importance.
- **Prompt Engineering for Length:** Earlier works explored prompt engineering (Nayab et al., 2025; Han et al., 2024) or conditional training (Kang et al., 2024) to elicit shorter CoTs, but the authors note that distilled reasoning models struggle to follow explicit length constraints without further training (as shown in Appendix F).

By proposing a simple yet effective online reinforcement learning approach with a tunable length penalty, Arora and Zanette contribute a method that directly optimizes the trade-off between reasoning depth and inference cost, distinguishing itself through its direct RL-based training and controllable output.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **train large reasoning models to reason efficiently** by minimizing unnecessary computational overhead while largely maintaining accuracy. This overarching goal is driven by several critical motivations:

1. **Addressing High Inference Costs:** Current frontier reasoning models, leveraging long chain-of-thoughts (CoTs), achieve impressive problem-solving capabilities. However, this comes at a substantial deployment cost due to increased generation length, which quadratically affects the attention mechanism and linearly increases the KV cache for transformer architectures. This makes serving these powerful models economically challenging, potentially leading to operating at a loss.
2. **Improving Economic Feasibility:** For LLMs to be widely adopted and commercially viable, their inference costs must be reduced. High costs limit accessibility and profitability, even for well-resourced organizations.
3. **Enhancing User Experience:** Longer generations inherently lead to higher latency, negatively impacting user responsiveness and overall experience. Reducing inference computation directly translates to faster response times.
4. **Promoting Environmental Sustainability:** The massive computational demands of LLMs contribute significantly to carbon emissions. Making models more efficient aligns with environmental responsibility by reducing energy consumption.
5. **Developing Controllable Efficiency:** The authors aim to create a method that allows developers to precisely control the trade-off between inference compute and model performance. This means deriving a *family* of reasoning models from a single base model, each offering different levels of efficiency, controlled via a single, intuitive hyperparameter. This flexibility is crucial for adapting models to various deployment scenarios with differing latency and accuracy requirements.
6. **Simplicity and Accessibility:** A key motivation is to achieve these efficiency gains with a method that is computationally efficient for training (viable with "modest academic resources") and simple to implement (a "couple of lines of changes" to standard RL implementations).

In essence, the research seeks to unlock the full potential of large reasoning models by making their advanced capabilities accessible and sustainable, moving beyond merely scaling model size to optimizing the efficiency of the reasoning process itself.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of Arora and Zanette's methodology lies in leveraging **reinforcement learning (RL) with a modified reward function** to encourage large reasoning models to produce correct solutions using shorter chain-of-thoughts.

#### 3.1. Modified Reward Function

The standard RL objective for reasoning models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1) aims to maximize accuracy: `E[1{y = y⋆(x)}]`, where `y` is the model's output (including CoT) and `y⋆(x)` is the correct answer. The authors introduce a length penalty to this objective:
`E[1{y = y⋆(x)}(1 - αf(LEN(y)))]`

- **`α` (alpha):** This is a tunable scalar coefficient, ranging from `[0, 1)`, that controls the strength of the length regularization. When `α = 0`, the objective reverts to standard accuracy maximization. Increasing `α` penalizes longer responses more heavily.
- **`f(LEN(y))`:** This monotonic function of the response length `LEN(y)` quantifies the penalty. To make the regularization effective and fair across problems of varying difficulty, `f` is designed as:
 `f(LEN(y)) = σ((LEN(y) - MEAN(x)) / STD(x))`
 - **Per-Prompt Normalization:** `MEAN(x)` and `STD(x)` are the per-prompt mean and standard deviation of correct response lengths. This is crucial because a naturally long response for a hard problem should not be penalized as much as an unnecessarily long response for an easy problem. These statistics are estimated dynamically from rollouts during online training.
 - **Sigmoid Function (`σ`):** The sigmoid soft-clips the normalized length, ensuring the overall reward remains bounded between `` and that correct responses, even if long, are always preferred over incorrect ones (since `1 - αf(LEN(y))` will always be `> 0` if `α < 1`).

#### 3.2. Optimization with Reinforcement Learning

Since the objective function involves auto-regressive sampling and is non-differentiable, policy gradient methods are employed.
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** A widely used algorithm for stable policy updates in RL.
- **REINFORCE Leave One Out (RLOO) Advantage Estimator:** Instead of maintaining a separate value network (which adds computational and implementation complexity), the authors use a Monte Carlo-based advantage estimator (Kool et al., 2019). The advantage for a trajectory `y_i` (response to prompt `x`) is estimated as `R(y_i, x) - (1/(n-1)) * Σ_{j≠i} R(y_j, x)`, where `R` is the trajectory return. This simplifies the RL setup while still being effective.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Guarantees

Under simplified assumptions (tabular representation for the LLM, and coverage – existence of a correct answer for every prompt), the authors provide population-level optimality guarantees:
- **Accuracy Preservation (Proposition 4.3):** The model maximizing the length-penalized objective (`θ⋆_eff`) will achieve 100% accuracy, just like the accuracy-maximizing model (`θ⋆`), in this idealized setting.
- **Shortest Correct Solution (Proposition 4.4):** The model will, for any given prompt, generate the shortest possible correct response. These proofs are detailed in Appendix E.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Base Models:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo et al., 2025), chosen for being open-weight reasoning models.
- **Training Data:** 3.2k math prompts from the Numina Math dataset (LI et al., 2024), filtered for numerical answers.
- **Evaluation:** Models are evaluated on three math benchmarks of varying difficulty: GSM8K (grade-school math), MATH500 (standard hard math), and AIME2024 (competition-level math). Robustness is also checked on CommonSenseQA and Logical Deduction from BIG-Bench (non-math datasets). Metrics include average pass rate@k and average token usage.
- **Computational Efficiency of Training:** The method requires only about 100 RL steps (~200 gradient updates), taking approximately 20 hours on specified GPU setups (4 GH200 GPUs for 1.5B, 8 GH200 GPUs for 7B). This is highlighted as highly efficient for fine-tuning.

#### 3.5. Baselines

To benchmark their method, the authors compare against:
- **Generation Cutoff:** Simply truncating responses at a maximum token limit during inference.
- **Rejection Sampling + SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning):** Generating multiple solutions, selecting the shortest correct ones, and then SFTing the model on these curated efficient solutions.
- **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization):** Using preferred pairs (shortest correct vs. longest correct) to fine-tune the model.
- **O1-Pruner (Luo et al., 2025):** A concurrent offline RL method for reasoning pruning.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a direct and controllable approach to efficiently train reasoning models, supported by theoretical grounding and rigorous empirical validation against strong baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that the proposed method effectively trains large reasoning models to reason efficiently, offering a controllable trade-off between accuracy and inference cost.

1. **Effective and Controllable Efficiency-Accuracy Trade-off:**
 - The method successfully navigates the token-accuracy curve, allowing the derivation of a family of models with varying efficiency levels controlled by the single hyperparameter `α` (Figure 1, Figure 6).
 - For the 7B model, a **50% reduction in tokens** can be achieved with **less than a 5% reduction in accuracy**.
 - Specific examples: On the MATH dataset, `α = 0.1` reduced token usage by 36% (from ~4000 to ~2600 tokens) with only a 2.2% accuracy loss. On AIME, `α = 0.2` reduced token usage by 27% (from ~13,000 to ~9,000) with a 4% accuracy drop.

2. **Superior Performance to Baselines:**
 - The proposed online RL training method consistently yields more performant models for the same token usage compared to SFT and DPO baselines (Figure 1, Figure 5).
 - The method also outperforms O1-Pruner, achieving higher accuracy at lower token costs for comparable `α` values (Table 13).
 - The naive "Generation Cutoff" baseline performs poorly, showing that simply truncating output without training is ineffective.

3. **Difficulty-Adaptive Token Reduction:**
 - The reduction in response length is more significant for easier problems than for harder ones. For example, the 7B model with `α = 0.2` achieved an 83% token saving on GSM8K (easier) but only 27% on AIME2024 (harder) (Figure 3).
 - This phenomenon is hypothesized to be due to reasoning models exhibiting a "larger relative spread" (more wasteful reasoning patterns) on simpler problems compared to complex ones, where longer chains-of-thought are often genuinely necessary (Table 17, Appendix I). A qualitative example (1+1 question) vividly illustrates how the original model generates pages of reasoning for a simple task, which the trained model condenses into a few tokens (Appendix B.1).

4. **Effects on CoT Faithfulness and Macro-Behaviors:**
 - CoT compression generally leads to a slight decrease in faithfulness (i.e., less explicit verbalization of hints), but the compressed models remain significantly more faithful than non-reasoning instruction-tuned models (Table 1). This aligns with previous findings (Chua & Evans, 2025).
 - Qualitatively, compressed CoTs are less verbose while retaining semantic content (Appendix B.2).
 - Quantitatively, the number of "macro-behaviors" (verification, backtracking, exploration) in the CoT generally decreases as `α` increases (Table 2), indicating a more streamlined and direct reasoning process.

5. **Ablation Study Insights:**
 - **Advantage Normalization:** Standard practice of normalizing the token-level advantage function can severely impair performance when combined with a length penalty. This is because it unintentionally neutralizes the `α` parameter's effect on reward differentiation, leading to excessive token reduction and sharp accuracy drops (Figure 4). The authors' choice *not* to normalize the advantage function is critical for their method's success.
 - **Training a Base Model:** A preliminary investigation on a base model for a simple Countdown task showed that while length-regularized RL slows down convergence and reduces peak performance compared to vanilla RL, it learns more token-efficient solutions for equivalent performance levels. This suggests the method is most effective as a post-training fine-tuning approach on already capable reasoning models (Appendix J).

6. **Unexpected `α=0` Length Reduction:**
 - An interesting observation was that even with `α=0` (no explicit length penalty), a reduction in response length was noted. This was attributed to a known bias in the RLOO implementation in popular open-source libraries like OpenRLHF, which unintentionally favors shorter correct sequences (Liu et al., 2025c). This bias was confirmed to disappear upon applying a proposed fix (Table 16), but it does not affect the overall applicability of their `α`-controlled method.

In summary, the main findings highlight the method's effectiveness in achieving significant inference cost reductions with minimal accuracy trade-offs, its ability to adapt reasoning length to problem difficulty, and its robustness compared to alternative approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work by Arora and Zanette, "Training Language Models to Reason Efficiently," carries significant implications and potential impacts across several dimensions:

1. **Enabling Practical Deployment of Advanced Reasoning LLMs:** The most immediate and critical impact is making sophisticated reasoning capabilities of LLMs economically viable for widespread deployment. By substantially reducing inference costs, the method helps overcome a major barrier to the adoption of frontier models in real-world applications. This can lead to increased accessibility for a broader range of organizations and users, fostering innovation across various industries.

2. **Improved User Experience and Responsiveness:** Lower inference costs directly translate to reduced latency. This means users will experience faster response times from LLMs, improving interactivity and overall user satisfaction in applications like intelligent assistants, educational tools, and complex problem-solving platforms.

3. **Advancing Environmental Sustainability in AI:** The computational burden of LLMs has significant environmental implications. By minimizing unnecessary token generation, this method directly contributes to reducing the energy consumption and carbon footprint associated with LLM inference, aligning AI development with global sustainability goals.

4. **Granular Control over Compute-Performance Trade-offs:** The ability to derive a *family* of reasoning models with varying efficiency levels via a single, intuitive hyperparameter (`α`) is a powerful feature. This allows developers to fine-tune models to specific operational requirements, balancing strict latency budgets against desired accuracy levels, without needing to retrain entirely new models or resort to heuristic-based solutions.

5. **Adaptive Resource Allocation for Reasoning:** The discovery that the trained models inherently adapt their reasoning length based on problem difficulty is a particularly promising finding. This suggests a paradigm shift where a single, efficiently trained model can intelligently allocate computational resources: being concise for simple queries and expanding its chain-of-thought only when necessary for challenging problems. This adaptive intelligence could negate the need for multiple specialized models for different task complexities, simplifying model management and deployment strategies.

6. **Simplicity and Accessibility of the Method:** The authors emphasize the method's simplicity ("a couple of lines of changes") and computational efficiency during training (viable with "modest academic resources"). This low barrier to entry for fine-tuning existing reasoning models means that a wider range of researchers and practitioners can adopt and build upon this technique, accelerating further advancements in efficient AI.

7. **Foundational Contribution to Efficient AI Research:** This work establishes a clear and effective method for integrating efficiency directly into the reasoning process of LLMs through targeted RL training. It opens new avenues for research into how models "think" and how that thinking can be optimized, potentially inspiring further innovations in areas like dynamic compute allocation, sparse reasoning, and more nuanced control over model behavior.

While acknowledging limitations like the non-precise targeting of specific generation lengths, the overall significance of this work lies in its practical utility for deploying powerful, cost-effective, and environmentally responsible AI solutions. It represents a crucial step towards making advanced LLM reasoning capabilities both scalable and sustainable for the future.
