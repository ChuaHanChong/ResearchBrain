# Diff-DAgger: Uncertainty Estimation with Diffusion Policy for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Virginia developed Diff-DAgger, an interactive learning framework that leverages a diffusion policy's intrinsic loss to accurately estimate uncertainty in robotic manipulation tasks. It achieves a 39.0% higher F1 score in task failure prediction and reduces DAgger iteration wall-clock time by 7.8 times compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Traditional interactive imitation learning methods like robot-gated DAgger struggle with highly expressive, multi-modal policies (e.g., diffusion models) due to inefficient uncertainty estimation.
- Ensemble-DAgger, a common approach, misinterprets multi-modal decision points (where multiple valid actions exist) as high uncertainty, leading to unnecessary expert queries and inefficient data collection.
- Existing DAgger methods are computationally expensive, especially when used with complex policies, hindering scalability and slowing down the learning process.

## Method

- **Diff-DAgger** utilizes the ==diffusion policy's own training objective== (diffusion loss) as a direct and reliable indicator of uncertainty during robot rollouts.
- It employs a ==dynamic α-quantile threshold== for the diffusion loss, along with a ==patience mechanism==, to distinguish between true out-of-distribution states requiring expert help and in-distribution multi-modal states.
- The framework integrates this uncertainty estimation into the standard ==DAgger iterative loop==, collecting expert demonstrations for genuinely uncertain states and efficiently updating the single diffusion policy.

## Results

- **Diff-DAgger** achieved an average of **39.0%** higher F1 score and accuracy in predicting task failures across simulated visuomotor tasks compared to baseline methods like Ensemble-DAgger and Thrifty-DAgger.
- It consistently outperformed baselines in task completion rates, showing a **13.7%** improvement in state-based tasks and a **25.2%** improvement in visuomotor tasks with the same amount of expert data.
- The method reduced the average policy update time by a factor of **7.8x** (to **0.54** hours per iteration) in visuomotor pushing, with substantial reductions in training (**5.1x**), threshold setting (**48x**), and inference (**3.8x**) times.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The intrinsic loss of a diffusion policy can serve as a robust and computationally efficient proxy for uncertainty, effectively identifying out-of-distribution states in complex, multi-modal robotic tasks.
> - Existing ensemble-based uncertainty estimation methods are fundamentally ill-suited for multi-modal policies as they conflate multi-modal distributions with high uncertainty, leading to inefficient expert queries.
> - An intelligent, model-inherent uncertainty estimation mechanism enables more efficient use of expert demonstrations and significantly reduces the wall-clock time required for interactive learning iterations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of imitation learning, robotic manipulation, and generative models, specifically diffusion models. It addresses a critical challenge in training autonomous robots: effectively learning from expert demonstrations while being robust to out-of-distribution (OOD) states and reducing the burden of human supervision.

**Imitation Learning (IL)** is a fundamental paradigm for robotic control, allowing robots to learn complex tasks by observing expert demonstrations. However, traditional offline imitation learning, also known as **behavior cloning (BC)**, suffers from the **covariate shift problem**. This occurs when the robot's policy deviates from the expert's, leading to states not present in the training data, causing compounding errors and failures.

To mitigate this, **interactive imitation learning** methods, such as **DAgger (Dataset Aggregation)**, were introduced. DAgger iteratively refines a policy by allowing an expert to intervene when the robot encounters novel or uncertain states, collecting new data, and retraining the policy. A key variant is **robot-gated DAgger**, where the robot autonomously decides when to seek expert help, thereby scaling learning across multiple robots and reducing continuous human oversight. The effectiveness of robot-gated DAgger hinges on the robot's ability to accurately estimate its own uncertainty.

Recent advancements in **generative models**, particularly **diffusion policies**, have shown impressive results in handling multi-modal action distributions in robotic manipulation. Diffusion policies, by modeling the distribution of action sequences, can learn diverse strategies for a given task, mitigating compounding errors and achieving state-of-the-art performance in many complex tasks.

However, the authors identify a critical gap: existing robot-gated DAgger methods struggle to integrate with highly expressive, multi-modal policies like diffusion models. Specifically, widely adopted **Ensemble-DAgger** approaches, which estimate uncertainty based on the disagreement (action variance) among an ensemble of policies, often misinterpret multi-modal decision points (where multiple valid actions exist) as high uncertainty. This leads to unnecessary expert queries, inefficient data collection, and slower learning. This problem is exacerbated when tasks inherently admit multiple successful solutions, making it difficult for ensemble methods to distinguish between true OOD states and valid in-distribution multi-modal states.

Diff-DAgger bridges this gap by proposing a novel uncertainty estimation mechanism that is intrinsically compatible with diffusion policies. It leverages the diffusion model's own training objective (diffusion loss) as a reliable indicator of uncertainty, thereby overcoming the limitations of ensemble-based methods in multi-modal scenarios. This work represents a significant step towards enabling scalable and efficient interactive learning for robots equipped with powerful, multi-modal generative policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind Diff-DAgger stems from the recognition of both the strengths and weaknesses of current state-of-the-art approaches in robotic imitation learning. While diffusion policies have shown remarkable capabilities in generating diverse and effective actions for complex manipulation tasks, they, like all behavior cloning methods, are susceptible to out-of-distribution (OOD) failures. Interactive learning methods like robot-gated DAgger are designed to combat these OOD failures by actively querying expert assistance. However, existing robot-gated DAgger strategies, particularly those relying on policy ensembles, prove inefficient when coupled with expressive, multi-modal policies. They frequently misclassify valid multi-modal decision points within the expert's data distribution as states of high uncertainty, leading to redundant expert interventions and hampering learning efficiency.

Given this context, the authors set forth the following key objectives for Diff-DAgger:

1. **Develop an efficient and effective robot-gated DAgger algorithm:** The primary goal is to create a system that allows robots to autonomously and accurately decide when to seek expert help, specifically designed to leverage the inherent properties of diffusion policies.
2. **Accurately estimate uncertainty in multi-modal environments:** A central objective is to differentiate between true out-of-distribution states (where expert intervention is crucial) and in-distribution multi-modal states (where multiple valid actions exist, and the policy should ideally proceed confidently). This aims to rectify the misinterpretations of existing ensemble-based methods.
3. **Improve task failure prediction:** The proposed system should demonstrably enhance the robot's ability to predict its own impending failures, leading to more timely and relevant expert queries.
4. **Increase task completion rates:** By making more intelligent query decisions, the method should lead to a policy that achieves higher task success rates with a fixed number of expert interventions, maximizing the utility of valuable expert data.
5. **Reduce wall-clock time for DAgger iterations:** A practical objective is to significantly decrease the computational overhead associated with DAgger's iterative training and data collection cycles, making the learning process faster and more scalable. This is particularly important for integrating computationally intensive models like diffusion policies.

Ultimately, the authors aim for Diff-DAgger to pave the way for efficiently incorporating expressive yet data-hungry policies into interactive robot learning settings, thereby unlocking the full potential of advanced generative models in real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Diff-DAgger introduces a novel uncertainty estimation mechanism embedded within the robot-gated DAgger framework, specifically tailored for diffusion policies. The core idea is to leverage the diffusion policy's intrinsic loss function as a robust indicator of uncertainty.

**A. Diffusion Policy Overview:**
The method builds upon the **Diffusion Policy**, which formulates action generation as a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM). This involves iteratively denoising a Gaussian prior to generate structured action sequences. This approach is known for its stable training, ability to handle multi-modal action distributions, and state-of-the-art results in behavior cloning for robot manipulation.
The key to Diff-DAgger is the diffusion policy's **loss function**:
$L_{\pi}(o, a, \epsilon, t) = \|f(\epsilon, a, t) - f_{\theta}(o, \sqrt{\alpha_t}a + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\epsilon, t)\|^2$
Here, $o$ is the observation sequence, $a$ is the action sequence, $\epsilon$ is Gaussian noise, $\alpha_t$ is a time-dependent factor, and $t$ is the denoising timestep. The function $f_{\theta}$ is the diffusion model that predicts the noise, and the loss measures the mean squared error between the prediction target and the model's prediction given the noised data.

**B. OOD Detection via Diffusion Loss (Diff-DAgger's Query System):**
During robot rollout, instead of using ensemble disagreement, Diff-DAgger estimates the uncertainty of the current action plan by calculating the diffusion policy's loss value for the given observation ($o$) and its own generated action ($a_r$).
1. **Expected Diffusion Loss Calculation:** To establish a baseline for uncertainty, the expected diffusion loss, denoted as $\bar{L}(o, a, \pi)$, is calculated for each data point $(o, a)$ in the initial training dataset. This is an expectation over noise vectors ($\epsilon$) and diffusion timesteps ($t$) using the policy $\pi$. In practice, this is approximated by sampling a batch of noise and timesteps and computing the average loss.
2. **Uncertainty Thresholding:** A robot-generated action $a_r$ at a state $o_r$ is deemed "out-of-distribution" if its computed diffusion loss, $L(o_r, a_r, \pi_r)$, exceeds an $\alpha$-quantile threshold. This threshold is dynamically set based on the distribution of expected diffusion losses observed in the *current training dataset*.
3. **Patience Mechanism:** To prevent spurious queries from brief fluctuations and reduce false positives, an expert intervention is only triggered if the diffusion loss consecutively violates the $\alpha$-quantile threshold for $K$ timesteps.

**C. Data Aggregation and Policy Update (DAgger Iteration):**
Diff-DAgger follows the standard DAgger iterative loop:
1. **Initialization:** The robot policy $\pi_r$ is initially trained on a set of $N_i$ expert demonstrations.
2. **Rollout and Query:** The robot executes actions in the environment. At each timestep, it performs inference to generate an action ($a_r$) and then calculates the diffusion loss associated with this action. If the loss exceeds the set threshold for $K$ consecutive timesteps, the robot queries an expert.
3. **Expert Intervention:** Upon a query, or if the robot times out without querying or completing the task, an expert intervenes from the current state and completes the task. The expert's state-action trajectory is recorded and added to the growing dataset ($D_{exp}$).
4. **Task Reset:** After task completion (by robot or expert), the environment is reset to a new random initial configuration.
5. **Policy Update:** After every $N_d$ expert interventions have been added to the dataset, a policy update cycle is triggered. This involves:
 - **Policy Training:** Retraining the diffusion policy $\pi_r$ for a fixed number of epochs on the entire aggregated dataset $D_{exp}$.
 - **Threshold Setting:** Re-calculating the baseline expected diffusion losses from the updated $D_{exp}$ and setting the new $\alpha$-quantile threshold for OOD detection for the next rollout phase.

**D. Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated across diverse tasks:
- **Simulation Tasks:** Stacking, Pushing (with 1 or 2 experts for multi-modality), and Plugging in ManiSkill.
- **Real-world Tasks:** Stacking and Loading using a Franka Research 3 robot.
- **Experts:** Rule-based RRT motion planners, PPO-trained RL agents (single or multi-modal policies), and human teleoperators.
- **Observation Types:** Both state-based (proprioception, ground-truth object poses) and image-based (R3M ResNet18 encoder with spatial softmax, proprioception) are tested.
- **Baselines:** Diff-DAgger is compared against:
 - **Behavior Cloning (BC):** Offline learning from initial expert data.
 - **Ensemble-DAgger:** Uses an ensemble of five diffusion policies and calculates action variance for uncertainty.
 - **ThriftyDAgger:** A state-of-the-art robot-gated DAgger method incorporating risk measures, building upon ensemble principles.
- **Model Architecture:** All methods utilize a U-Net policy head with FiLM conditioning, a standard architecture for diffusion policies.

By integrating diffusion loss directly into the query system, Diff-DAgger aims to efficiently and accurately identify true OOD states, thereby enabling more effective interactive learning with highly expressive robot policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Diff-DAgger consistently demonstrates superior performance across all evaluation metrics, highlighting its effectiveness in handling multi-modal data distributions, improving task outcomes, and enhancing learning efficiency.

**A. Predictability of Robot Query on Task Failure (RQ1):**
The ability of Diff-DAgger to accurately predict task failures is a cornerstone of its design. Evaluated in simulation by constructing a confusion matrix between robot queries and task outcomes (success/failure), Diff-DAgger significantly outperforms baselines:
- **F1 Score Improvement:** Diff-DAgger achieves an average of 39.0% higher F1 score and 39.0% greater accuracy in predicting task failure across simulated visuomotor tasks compared to other methods. Specifically, it shows a 42.7% higher F1 and 38.4% higher accuracy compared to Ensemble-DAgger, and 22.6% higher F1 and 32.8% higher accuracy compared to Thrifty-DAgger.
- **Reduced False Negatives:** Crucially, Diff-DAgger incurs far fewer *false negatives* (robot failing to query when a failure is imminent) across tasks, particularly in multi-modal scenarios like "Pushing (2 Experts)". Ensemble-DAgger and Thrifty-DAgger struggle here, often exhibiting high false negative rates, indicating they are insensitive to true OOD situations when multi-modal "in-distribution" data is present.
- **Qualitative Observation (Fig. 5):** In the plugging task, Diff-DAgger proactively queries the expert early when the charger slips, leading to a successful intervention. In contrast, Thrifty-DAgger queries only after the object is dropped, and Ensemble-DAgger fails to query entirely, resulting in a timeout. This highlights Diff-DAgger's superior sensitivity to critical failure points.

**B. Task Performance (RQ2):**
Diff-DAgger achieves higher task completion rates given the same amount of expert demonstration data:
- **Consistent Outperformance:** Diff-DAgger consistently outperforms all baselines in task completion rates across state-based and image-based observations, including real-world tasks.
- **Significant Improvement:** It achieves an average of 13.7% improvement over the second-best method in state-based tasks and a more substantial 25.2% improvement in visuomotor tasks (which include real-world scenarios).
- **Baseline Weakness:** Notably, Ensemble-DAgger and Thrifty-DAgger sometimes perform worse than basic Behavior Cloning. The authors attribute this to their misinterpretation of multi-modal data, leading to inefficient or misleading expert queries that can degrade the quality of the collected dataset.
- **Emergent Recovery Behavior (Fig. 6):** Policies trained with Diff-DAgger collected data exhibit an interesting emergent behavior: recovery from initial failures. For example, a robot might misalign a cube, fail the first stacking attempt, but then make a second successful attempt. This kind of robust, adaptive behavior is typically not observed with offline behavior cloning.

**C. Wall-Clock Time for DAgger Iterations (RQ3):**
Diff-DAgger significantly reduces the computational time required for DAgger iterations:
- **Overall Efficiency:** In the visuomotor pushing task (a representative example), Diff-DAgger reduces the average policy update time by a factor of 7.8, bringing it down to just 0.54 hours per iteration.
- **Component-wise Reduction:**
 - **Training Time:** 5.1x reduction compared to Ensemble-DAgger, and 6.4x compared to Thrifty-DAgger. This is primarily due to using a single policy instead of an ensemble.
 - **Threshold Setting Time:** A dramatic 48x reduction compared to Ensemble-DAgger and 53x compared to Thrifty-DAgger. Diff-DAgger efficiently calculates expected diffusion losses by passing a single batch per training data point, whereas ensemble methods require full inference across multiple policies on the entire dataset.
 - **Inference Time:** 3.8x reduction compared to Ensemble-DAgger and 3.9x compared to Thrifty-DAgger, again benefiting from a single policy.
- **ThriftyDAgger Comparison:** Thrifty-DAgger, which requires additional data collection and training for its risk estimation component, is computationally more expensive than Ensemble-DAgger, making Diff-DAgger's time savings even more pronounced against this SOTA baseline.

In summary, Diff-DAgger provides a more accurate, efficient, and robust interactive learning framework for robotic manipulation by smartly leveraging the inherent properties of diffusion policies to estimate uncertainty in complex, multi-modal environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Diff-DAgger represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic imitation learning, particularly for systems employing highly expressive generative policies like diffusion models. Its contributions have several important implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Scalable and Efficient Interactive Learning with Expressive Policies:** The most immediate significance is that Diff-DAgger effectively addresses the long-standing challenge of integrating powerful, multi-modal policies (e.g., diffusion policies) into interactive learning frameworks. By providing an efficient and accurate uncertainty estimation mechanism, it allows robots to leverage the full representational power of these policies without being bogged down by inefficient expert queries, thereby making large-scale, robot-gated DAgger more practical.
2. **Improved Robot Autonomy and Safety:** By achieving significantly higher accuracy in predicting task failures and drastically reducing false negatives, Diff-DAgger leads to more reliable robot autonomy. Robots are better equipped to identify situations where they are truly out of their learned distribution and require human assistance. This proactive querying prevents critical failures, reduces the risk of undesirable states, and enhances the overall safety and trustworthiness of robot deployments, especially in real-world scenarios.
3. **Maximizing Expert Data Utility:** The method's ability to achieve higher task completion rates with the *same number of expert demonstrations* is crucial. Expert interventions, whether from humans or sophisticated planners, are often costly and time-consuming. By making more intelligent query decisions, Diff-DAgger ensures that each expert intervention is genuinely useful for policy improvement, accelerating learning and reducing the overall data-collection burden.
4. **Novel Uncertainty Estimation Paradigm:** The work introduces a novel paradigm for uncertainty estimation in imitation learning, moving beyond traditional ensemble methods. By using the diffusion policy's own loss function as a proxy for OOD detection, Diff-DAgger offers a more direct and computationally efficient approach that is intrinsically aligned with the model's learning objective. This could inspire similar approaches for other generative models in decision-making contexts.
5. **Emergent Robust Behaviors:** The observation of emergent recovery behaviors in Diff-DAgger trained policies (e.g., a robot re-attempting a failed stack) indicates that the interactive learning process, when guided by an intelligent query system, can lead to more robust and adaptive policies than purely offline methods. This suggests a path toward policies that are not just proficient but also resilient to minor errors.
6. **Paving the Way for Future Research:** The paper explicitly acknowledges trade-offs between policy performance and acceleration choices. This opens up avenues for future research into optimizing diffusion policy training and inference, exploring different prediction types, and refining the OOD detection thresholding. As computing power continues to advance, the foundation laid by Diff-DAgger will become even more impactful, enabling even more complex and data-hungry policies to be efficiently incorporated into interactive robot learning.

In conclusion, Diff-DAgger not only solves a specific and critical problem in robot-gated DAgger but also offers a broader vision for how advanced generative models can be seamlessly integrated into interactive learning frameworks to create more capable, efficient, and reliable robotic systems.
