# NavGSim: High-Fidelity Gaussian Splatting Simulator for Large-Scale Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> NavGSim is a navigation simulator that renders large indoor scenes with hierarchical 3D Gaussian Splatting and derives collision geometry by analytically slicing Gaussian ellipsoids at fixed heights into 2D occupancy maps. A Python API exposes rendering, planning, and collision checking. A Uni-NaVid-based VLA fine-tuned on 4.5K NavGSim trajectories reaches 98.3% simulated and 69.2% real-world success on annotated targets.

## Problem

- Navigation demands high-fidelity scenes spanning whole floors, unlike locomotion or manipulation, so simulator realism must scale across hundreds of square meters.
- Mesh-based navigation simulators like Habitat and AI2-THOR render reconstructed or synthetic meshes with limited visual realism, and handcrafted game-engine scenes cost enormous artist effort.
- Prior large-scale Gaussian Splatting reconstruction work targets view synthesis only, offering no collision geometry for complex occluded indoor layouts, so policies cannot be trained inside it.

## Method

- ==Hierarchical 3D Gaussian Splatting== backbone: tree of spatial chunks trained independently then merged, giving level-of-detail rendering and multi-GPU reconstruction of floor-scale scenes.
- **GS-Slice** collision simulation: horizontal planes every 10 cm analytically intersect each 3D Gaussian, yielding 2D Gaussian slices whose union forms a 2D occupancy map for planning.
- **NavGSim**-API plus an adapted ==Uni-NaVid== VLA that replaces discrete action tokens with an MLP head regressing continuous (x, y, theta) trajectories, trained on shortest-path plus two ==DAgger== rounds.

## Results

- Simulated navigation reaches **98.3%** average success and **0.91 m** distance-to-goal on **nine** annotated targets, and generalizes to unseen Table and Sofa goals at **80.8%** average.
- Swapping hierarchical for vanilla 3DGS drops rendering to **23.221** PSNR from **25.396** and collapses real-world success from **69.2%** to **13.7%**, an **81%** relative decline.
- Pretraining plus two DAgger rounds pushes success past **90%**, while training from scratch improves slowly and plateaus far lower; slice error stays within **5 cm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rendering fidelity is the dominant sim-to-real bottleneck for navigation policies: modest PSNR gains translated into a several-fold difference in real-world success rate.
> - A probabilistic radiance representation can double as collision geometry; analytic height slicing of Gaussians avoids meshing while keeping occupancy error centimeter-scale.
> - Photorealism alone is insufficient; on-policy DAgger correction remains necessary to control compounding error in long-horizon continuous-trajectory navigation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of realistic simulation environments is recognized as a fundamental requirement in robot learning, particularly for training embodied AI agents. This paper addresses a challenge within this domain, focusing on navigation tasks. Traditional robot learning simulators, such as Gazebo, AI2-THOR, and Habitat, have provided platforms for various applications including locomotion, manipulation, and navigation. However, the existing approaches for navigation simulation often face limitations.

Current navigation simulators frequently rely on mesh-based environments, either reconstructed from real-world scans or synthetically generated. While these methods offer varying levels of interactivity and scale, they often compromise on visual fidelity. Creating large-scale, photorealistic environments using conventional game engines like Unreal or Unity requires substantial manual effort from artists, which limits their applicability for rapid iteration in research.

In parallel, advancements in 3D reconstruction and rendering, particularly with the emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), have demonstrated capabilities for generating high-quality visual renderings from real-world data. 3DGS represents scenes using a collection of 3D Gaussian kernels, allowing for real-time rendering and facile reconstruction from camera and LiDAR data. Recent research has begun to explore the integration of 3DGS into navigation tasks to enhance fidelity and scalability.

NavGSim positions itself within this evolving landscape by integrating hierarchical 3D Gaussian Splatting (H3DGS) with specific mechanisms for navigation-centric simulation. It aims to bridge the gap between photorealistic scene representation (provided by 3DGS) and the functional requirements of robot navigation (collision detection, path planning) in large-scale environments. By doing so, NavGSim seeks to offer a simulator that combines the visual realism previously lacking in mesh-based systems with the scalability and physical interaction necessary for training robust navigation policies, particularly for tasks spanning extensive indoor spaces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop NavGSim, a Gaussian Splatting-based simulator capable of generating high-fidelity, large-scale environments specifically for robot navigation tasks. This objective is motivated by several challenges observed in the field of robot learning and simulation:

Firstly, **rendering realism** is a significant concern. Existing navigation simulators, which predominantly rely on reconstructed or synthetic meshes, frequently produce visual outputs that do not accurately represent real-world environments. This visual discrepancy can create a "sim-to-real gap," making it difficult for policies trained in simulation to transfer effectively to physical robots. The motivation is to provide a visually rich simulation environment that closely mimics reality, thereby improving the generalizability of learned robot policies.

Secondly, **physical simulation accuracy and scalability** pose challenges, particularly in navigation tasks that require robots to traverse extensive areas, such as multiple rooms or entire floors. Simulating realistic collisions and determining navigable areas in such complex, large-scale environments is computationally intensive and difficult with traditional methods. The authors are motivated to develop a method that can efficiently extract precise collision information directly from the Gaussian Splatting representation, enabling accurate path planning and collision checking across vast spaces.

Thirdly, the **ease of use and accessibility** for researchers is a critical motivational factor. Developing custom simulation environments and integrating them with robot learning frameworks often involves complex engineering efforts. The authors aim to provide a comprehensive and user-friendly API that supports the entire workflow, from custom scene reconstruction and robot configuration to policy training and evaluation, thereby lowering the barrier for researchers to conduct embodied AI experiments.

Finally, a key objective is to **validate the effectiveness of NavGSim** in facilitating robot learning. This involves training a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model using data collected within the NavGSim environment and evaluating its performance in both the simulator and real-world settings. The motivation behind this evaluation is to demonstrate that NavGSim can enhance a VLA model's scene understanding and enable it to handle diverse navigation queries effectively, ultimately proving the simulator's utility for research in generalizable robot navigation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NavGSim is structured as a modular and scalable simulator, designed for high-fidelity, large-scale navigation tasks. Its architecture integrates photo-realistic rendering, path planning, and collision detection, all accessible through a Python API.

The core of NavGSim's rendering capability relies on a **Hierarchical 3D Gaussian Splatting (H3DGS) engine**. This engine, based on prior work in H3DGS, models indoor scenes as a tree-based structure. Leaf nodes within this hierarchy contain dense sets of anisotropic Gaussians, capturing fine-grained visual details, while internal nodes aggregate these details into coarser representations. This hierarchical organization enables Level-of-Detail (LOD) rendering, allowing for dynamic balancing of visual fidelity and computational efficiency during simulation. To manage large scenes (hundreds to thousands of square meters), a divide-and-conquer strategy is employed: input image sets are divided into spatial chunks, optimized independently using multiple GPUs, and then merged into a global hierarchy.

For **Gaussian Splatting-based navigation collision simulation**, NavGSim introduces a multi-slice Gaussian projection method. This technique addresses the challenge of extracting traversable regions and detecting collisions from the probabilistic nature of 3D Gaussian fields. It involves defining a series of horizontal slicing planes at fixed height intervals (e.g., every 10 cm within the robot's height range) parallel to the floor. For each 3D Gaussian component, its intersection with these planes is analytically computed, yielding 2D Gaussian slices. These 2D slices collectively form a dense, two-dimensional occupancy map that serves as a geometric prior for collision checking and path planning algorithms. The method computes the 2D Gaussian slice parameters (center and covariance) from the 3D Gaussian's properties at a given height, including a decay term that influences the slice size based on distance from the 3D Gaussian's z-center.

To facilitate research, NavGSim provides **comprehensive APIs** for various functionalities. These include tools for custom scene reconstruction from posed RGB images, robot configuration, policy training, and evaluation. The API supports multi-GPU development and is designed to be compatible with existing navigation simulator functionalities.

The **evaluation of NavGSim** involved training and testing a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model. An existing VLA model, Uni-NaVid, was adapted to output continuous trajectories rather than discrete actions.
- **VLA Architecture:** The modified VLA model processes a sequence of egocentric RGB frames and natural language instructions. An EVA-CLIP vision encoder extracts visual features, which are then compressed using an online token merging technique to manage computational load across short- and long-term visual observations. These visual tokens, along with language tokens embedded by a Vicuna-7B Large Language Model (LLM), are concatenated and fed into the LLM. The LLM's final hidden state is then passed through an MLP-based action head to predict a continuous trajectory (x, y, θ).
- **Data Collection:** A dataset of 4,500 navigation trajectories was collected within NavGSim. This involved manually annotating 9 semantic landmarks as navigation goals. Initial "expert" trajectories were generated by a shortest-path planner. To mitigate compounding errors during policy inference, the Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) algorithm was employed, iteratively augmenting the dataset with agent-generated trajectories and expert supervision for corrective learning over two rounds.
- **Experimental Setup:** An office factory environment spanning approximately 700 m² was reconstructed using a handheld LiDAR-RGB camera and the H3DGS pipeline. The VLA model was trained for 320 GPU-hours on 16 NVIDIA H800 GPUs, utilizing supervised learning, DAgger-augmented data, and additional video question-answering data for generalization.
- **Real-World Deployment:** The trained VLA agent was deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot equipped with an Intel RealSense D455 RGB camera. Real-time visual data and language instructions were streamed to a remote NVIDIA A100 server running the VLA model. The model's predicted trajectory waypoints (x, y, θ) were then converted into robot control signals (at 8 Hz) by the robot's onboard planner. A velocity modulation model was implemented for smooth deceleration and stopping near targets, and radar data was used for obstacle avoidance.
- **Metrics:** Performance was evaluated using Success Rate (SR), defined as stopping within 1.5 meters of the target, and Distance to Goal (DTG).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of NavGSim demonstrated its capabilities across simulated and real-world environments, particularly in enhancing the performance and sim-to-real transferability of VLA models.

**Performance in Simulation and Real-World Environments:**
Experiments showed that the VLA model trained within NavGSim (utilizing H3DGS) achieved high success rates (SR) in simulation. For annotated targets, the average SR was 98.3%, with a low average Distance to Goal (DTG) of 0.91 meters. Performance on specific targets like "Television" and "Laboratory" reached 100% SR. The model also exhibited generalization to novel, unannotated targets, achieving an average SR of 80.8% and DTG of 3.23 meters for "Table" and "Sofa."

Crucially, the model trained in NavGSim demonstrated transferability to real-world environments. Despite the inherent sensor noise and domain discrepancies, the VLA model maintained an average SR of 69.2% for annotated targets and 69.6% for novel targets in physical deployment. This indicates that the high-fidelity simulation environment facilitated the learning of robust navigation policies that generalize to the real world.

**Comparison of Rendering Quality and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
A direct comparison was made between the H3DGS-based NavGSim and a simulator built on original (non-hierarchical) 3DGS. In terms of rendering quality (Table II), H3DGS outperformed standard 3DGS, as indicated by higher PSNR (25.396 vs. 23.221), lower LPIPS (0.274 vs. 0.426), and higher SSIM (0.866 vs. 0.789). This superior rendering quality correlated with significantly better sim-to-real transfer.

While the VLA model trained on standard 3DGS performed reasonably in its simulated environment (average SR 80.8%), its real-world performance dropped substantially to an average SR of 13.7%, representing an 81% decrease. In contrast, the model trained on H3DGS (NavGSim) exhibited a more robust transfer, with average real-world SR remaining high (69.2%), suggesting that the enhanced visual fidelity of NavGSim contributes to closing the sim-to-real gap.

**Influence of Training Strategy:**
The research investigated the impact of training strategies, comparing models trained with and without pre-training. Models that incorporated pre-training demonstrated accelerated learning curves and achieved higher final success rates, exceeding 90% SR after two rounds of DAgger. Conversely, models trained without pre-training exhibited slower performance improvement and plateaued at considerably lower success rates. This finding highlights the importance of pre-training in improving data efficiency, reducing compounding errors, and fostering more effective long-term navigation policy learning.

**Trajectory Characteristics:**
The dataset collected within NavGSim comprised diverse trajectory lengths, reflecting the large-scale nature of the environments. The average navigation trajectory length across different target categories was 22.26 meters, with individual targets ranging from 16.80 meters ("Laboratory") to 30.98 meters ("Water Dispenser").

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

NavGSim represents an advancement in the field of robot learning and simulation by addressing limitations of existing platforms, particularly concerning visual fidelity and scalability for navigation tasks.

The primary significance of NavGSim lies in its ability to **generate high-fidelity, large-scale, and photorealistic simulation environments** using hierarchical 3D Gaussian Splatting. This approach overcomes the visual realism shortcomings of traditional mesh-based simulators and the manual effort associated with creating large scenes in game engines. By providing a more realistic visual experience, NavGSim contributes to reducing the **sim-to-real gap**, as demonstrated by the improved transferability of VLA models trained within its environment to real-world robots. This implies that policies learned in NavGSim are more likely to perform effectively in physical deployments, accelerating the development and testing cycles for embodied AI.

The introduction of the **Gaussian Splatting-based slice technique for collision simulation** is a notable methodological contribution. This method allows for the direct extraction of navigable areas and efficient collision detection from the continuous and probabilistic 3D Gaussian representation. This technical innovation provides a foundation for realistic physical interaction within visually rich 3DGS environments, which is crucial for navigation tasks.

NavGSim's **comprehensive API** supports the entire pipeline of embodied AI research, from custom scene reconstruction to robot configuration, policy training, and evaluation. This user-friendly interface lowers the entry barrier for researchers, enabling them to construct and experiment with customized navigation simulators for diverse applications. The support for multi-GPU development further ensures its scalability for large-scale research.

The demonstrated success of a VLA model trained in NavGSim, showing **enhanced scene understanding and effective handling of diverse navigation queries**, indicates the simulator's potential to facilitate the development of more capable and generalizable robot navigation systems. This could lead to advancements in areas such as vision-and-language navigation, object goal navigation, and embodied question answering, where robust environmental understanding is paramount.

However, the authors acknowledge certain limitations, such as the time-consuming pre-processing pipeline for large scenes and the lack of dynamic editing capabilities. Addressing these aspects in future work could further enhance NavGSim's utility and flexibility. Despite these limitations, NavGSim provides a valuable tool for the research community, enabling the construction of simulation environments that combine high-quality scene reconstruction with effective embodied learning, thereby fostering progress towards scalable and generalizable robot navigation.
