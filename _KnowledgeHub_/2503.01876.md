# Uncertainty Comes for Free: Human-in-the-Loop Policies with Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper proposes a human-in-the-loop diffusion policy that decides when to request operator assistance by measuring uncertainty in its own denoising process. It fits a Gaussian Mixture Model over sampled denoising vectors to capture inter-mode divergence and intra-mode variance, requiring no extra training and enabling both selective intervention and targeted fine-tuning with minimal human effort.

## Problem

- Human-in-the-loop deployment typically requires continuous human monitoring and intervention, which is labor-intensive and impractical when scaling to many robots.
- Prior uncertainty-aware HitL methods like ThriftyDAgger and HULA require extra training procedures or online model updates, adding computational overhead and latency at deployment.
- Existing intervention-budgeting and OOD-detection approaches do not exploit the multi-modal structure of demonstration data, causing naive variance metrics to misfire on multi-modal action distributions.

## Method

- ==Denoising-based uncertainty metric==: samples end-effector poses near the current pose, feeds them through the **diffusion policy**'s noise prediction network, and collects the resulting denoising vector field for analysis.
- ==Gaussian Mixture Model== uncertainty estimation: fits GMMs of varying mode counts to the vector field via maximum likelihood, then combines ==inter-mode cosine divergence== and ==intra-mode variance== into a single uncertainty score.
- Uncertainty-gated HitL deployment and fine-tuning: triggers operator teleoperation when uncertainty exceeds a fixed 95th-percentile threshold, then fine-tunes the diffusion policy on collected intervention data mixed 50/50 with pretraining data.

## Results

- On three simulated tasks the method needed far fewer human assistance steps for **100%** success than baselines, e.g. **5.4** vs **21.2-54.0** for Cup Stacking and **16.9** vs **30.2-55.7** for Lift-sim.
- On four real robot tasks the method achieved **100%** success using human assistance for only about **8.3%** of time steps on average, versus **48.8-111.9** steps needed for full-trajectory fine-tuning.
- HitL fine-tuning on the Lift-real task raised test success rate from **0.16** (zero-shot) to **0.63** using only **80** intervention samples, outperforming full-trajectory fine-tuning's **0.31** with **132** samples.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Denoising uncertainty can be extracted as a free byproduct of an already-trained diffusion policy, avoiding extra models or training just to gate human intervention.
> - Multi-modal action distributions require GMM-based divergence rather than naive output variance, since single-mode variance can mask true uncertainty in multi-modal regions.
> - A single fixed quantile threshold generalized across distinct task types without per-task calibration, suggesting the metric captures a task-agnostic notion of policy confidence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of robotic autonomy, specifically focusing on Human-in-the-Loop (HitL) operation and policy learning. HitL paradigms are increasingly adopted in robotics to manage scenarios where fully autonomous systems may encounter unforeseen "corner cases," data distribution shifts, or incomplete state observability, leading to potential failures. By enabling human operators to intervene, HitL aims to improve task success rates and reliability.

However, a significant challenge in current HitL implementations is the labor-intensive nature of continuous human monitoring and frequent interventions. This can be impractical and non-scalable, particularly for deploying multiple robots or operating over extended periods.

In parallel, advancements in policy learning, especially with generative models like diffusion models, have demonstrated improved robustness in complex manipulation tasks. Diffusion policies, in particular, have shown proficiency in imitation learning by capturing multi-modal action distributions. This work leverages the inherent properties of diffusion models to address the limitations of existing HitL systems.

Prior work in HitL and Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL) has explored various forms of human feedback (e.g., interventions, preferences, rankings) and strategies for budgeting expert queries. Some methods rely on model ensembles or explicit risk metrics learned during training or deployment, which can introduce computational overhead or require specific training procedures. Other approaches use metrics like action consistency or diffusion loss. A common challenge for these methods is the online tuning or selection of appropriate intervention thresholds. This paper distinguishes itself by proposing an uncertainty metric derived "for free" from the diffusion policy's generative process, aiming to minimize computational overhead and simplify threshold selection for efficient, selective human assistance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-driven approach for generating Human-in-the-Loop (HitL) policies that allows robotic agents to autonomously determine when to request human assistance. This objective is motivated by several challenges and limitations in current robot deployment paradigms:

1. **Reducing Human Labor:** Current HitL operations often necessitate continuous human monitoring or frequent interventions, which are labor-intensive and impractical for large-scale or long-duration robot deployments. The goal is to reduce this burden by enabling robots to operate autonomously when capable and only seek human input when truly necessary.
2. **Addressing Policy Failures:** While modern policy learning methods have enhanced robustness, robots can still fail due to issues such as data distribution shifts (e.g., novel objects, changed lighting), incomplete state observability, or ambiguity in action choices from under-specified tasks. The aim is to leverage human expertise to overcome these specific failure modes.
3. **Leveraging Diffusion Models' Capabilities:** The authors specifically chose diffusion models as their policy class due to two perceived advantages: their demonstrated robust performance in imitation learning, and their iterative denoising generative process, which offers a mechanism to gain insight into the agent’s decision-making uncertainty. The motivation is to exploit this generative process to derive an uncertainty metric without additional dedicated training.
4. **"Free" Uncertainty Estimation:** A key motivation is to compute an uncertainty-based metric directly from the noise prediction model learned during the diffusion policy training process. This implies that uncertainty estimation should not require training any additional models, impose minimal runtime cost, and thus be a "free" byproduct of existing training.
5. **Eliminating Training-Phase Human Interaction:** To further reduce the operator burden, the method is designed to avoid the need for expert intervention or interaction during the training phase. The agent operates without explicit knowledge of the effects of human assistance, relying on the assumption that such assistance will be effective for task completion.
6. **Enabling Efficient Data Collection for Fine-tuning:** A secondary, yet significant, objective is to utilize the same uncertainty metric for efficient data collection. By identifying states where the policy is uncertain and human assistance is provided, targeted data can be gathered. This data can then be used to fine-tune the diffusion policy, potentially improving its autonomous performance more efficiently than collecting full-trajectory demonstrations.
7. **Robust and Generalizable Intervention Thresholds:** The authors aim for a method that does not require per-task calibration of intervention thresholds. They propose using a fixed quantile-based threshold, estimated from a validation set, across diverse tasks to underscore the robustness and generality of the approach.

In summary, the core motivation is to create a more practical, scalable, and human-efficient HitL system by endowing robots with the ability to introspect their uncertainty, request assistance judiciously, and adapt through targeted learning from these interventions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates human-in-the-loop operation with diffusion policies by deriving an uncertainty metric directly from the policy's generative process. The approach consists of three main steps: training a diffusion policy, deploying it with uncertainty-based intervention, and optionally fine-tuning the policy with collected human assistance data.

**A. Diffusion Policies Background**
The method utilizes Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) as the policy class. These models generate actions (a_0) by iteratively denoising a sequence of noisy actions, starting from Gaussian noise (a_K). The core component is a learned noise prediction model, ε_θ(o, a_k, k), which estimates the noise to be removed at each denoising step *k*, given the robot observation *o* and the current noisy action *a_k*. The authors assume policies operate in task-space control, outputting absolute end-effector poses, allowing the predicted noise vectors to be interpreted as directions toward intended end-effector poses.

**B. Denoising-based Uncertainty Metric**
To quantify uncertainty, the method leverages the vector field encoded by the noise predictions during the generative process.
1. **Sampling Denoising Vectors:** Given an observation *o_t* at time *t*, a set of potential end-effector poses *A_s_t* is sampled within a fixed radius *r* of the current end-effector pose.
2. **Generating the Vector Field:** Each sampled pose *a_s_t* from *A_s_t* is fed into the policy's noise prediction model (at denoising step *k=0*) to obtain a set of denoising vectors *V_s_t* = {ε_θ(o_t, a_s_t, 0) | a_s_t ∈ A_s_t}. These vectors represent the policy's local "beliefs" about the next action.
3. **Addressing Multi-modality with GMMs:** Recognizing that diffusion policies can capture multiple distinct action modes (e.g., different ways to achieve a goal), a simple variance of *V_s_t* might not adequately represent uncertainty. To account for this, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) are used. The collected denoising vectors *V_s_t* are fitted with *N* GMMs, each with a different number of modes. The best-fit GMM is selected using maximum likelihood estimation.
4. **Uncertainty Calculation:** The final uncertainty metric, Uncertainty(*o_t*), is computed using two components from the best-fit GMM:
 - **Inter-mode divergence (D(*V_s_t*)):** This measures the divergence between the means (*g_i*) of the different modes in the GMM. It is calculated as 1 minus the cosine similarity between each pair of mode means, averaged across all pairs. This component captures how distinct or conflicting the policy's multi-modal action preferences are.
 - **Intra-mode variance (Var_g(*V_s_t*)):** This measures the weighted average variance within each mode of the GMM, where the weights *p(v_i)* correspond to the probability of vector *v_i* belonging to its respective mode. This component captures the entropy or spread of actions within each distinct mode.
 - The total uncertainty is a weighted sum: Uncertainty(*o_t*) = D(*V_s_t*) + αVar_g(*V_s_t*), where α is a scaling constant balancing the two components.

**C. Uncertainty-based Intervention and Policy Fine-tuning**
The computed uncertainty metric is then integrated into the robot's deployment pipeline (Algorithm 1):
1. **Deployment Decision:** At each time step *t*, the robot calculates Uncertainty(*o_t*). If this value exceeds a pre-defined *threshold*, the robot requests human assistance.
2. **Human Intervention:** Upon request, a human operator takes control of the robot for a pre-specified number of steps (*m*, e.g., 4 steps) via teleoperation. The observation and human action pairs {o, a_human} generated during this intervention are saved.
3. **Autonomous Operation:** If the uncertainty is below the threshold, the robot executes an action *a_t* autonomously using its diffusion policy.
4. **Policy Fine-tuning (Optional):** The collected intervention data (*D_ft*) is used to fine-tune the diffusion policy. To mitigate catastrophic forgetting of the original skills, fine-tuning mini-batches are composed of a 50% split from *D_ft* and 50% from the initial pretraining dataset (*D_train*). This process implicitly focuses learning on states where the agent previously exhibited high uncertainty.

**D. Hyperparameter Settings**
- **Uncertainty Threshold:** Empirically set at the 95% quantile of the uncertainty values derived from a held-out validation set, designed to be robust across tasks.
- **Sampling Radius *r*:** The action space dimensions are normalized to. A radius of 0.05 was found to offer a balance between local and global uncertainty estimation.
- **Scaling Constant α:** Small values (e.g., 0.01-0.1) are generally favored, emphasizing inter-mode divergence as a primary indicator of critical uncertain states, while still retaining the intra-mode variance for single-mode scenarios.

This methodology provides a system where uncertainty estimation is an inherent part of the diffusion policy's operation, enabling selective and efficient human intervention, and facilitating targeted policy improvement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the proposed method across both simulated and real-world environments, considering three categories of deployment challenges: data distribution shift, incomplete state observability, and incorrect choice of action modes. The evaluation focused on the efficiency of human-robot interaction (measured by intervention frequency for 100% success) and the impact of human assistance and fine-tuning on task performance.

**A. Experimental Scenarios and Baselines**
- **Simulated Tasks:**
 - **Lift-sim (Distribution Shift):** Robot lifts unseen objects after training on a single object.
 - **Cup Stacking (Partial Observability):** Robot grasps and places cups, sensitive to misalignment and observation views.
 - **Open Drawer (Action Multi-modality):** Robot opens one of three drawers with under-specified task instructions.
- **Real Robot Tasks (4 tasks):** Lift-real, Ramekin Stacking, 3-Mode Pushing, Nut Pick-and-Place, mirroring the simulated challenge categories.
- **Baselines:** ThriftyDAgger, Diff-DAgger, and HULA-offline (an adapted offline RL method). Other methods (Sentinel, diffusion output variance) were explored in the Appendix and found less suitable.

**B. Efficiency of Human Interactions**
The primary finding is that the proposed method significantly reduces the need for human intervention while achieving 100% task success, demonstrating higher efficiency compared to baselines.
- **Simulated Environments (Table I):**
 - For Lift-sim, the method required an average of 16.9 (±4.5) human assistance steps, substantially lower than ThriftyDAgger (33.5), Diff-DAgger (30.2), and HULA-offline (55.7).
 - In Cup Stacking, it needed only 5.4 (±1.0) steps, outperforming baselines which required 21.2 to 54.0 steps.
 - For Open Drawer, 8.0 (±1.9) steps were needed, compared to 16.0-21.7 for baselines.
 - These numbers represent a fraction of the average full trajectory length (e.g., 76.6 steps for Lift-sim).
- **Real Robot Environments (Table II):** The method achieved 100% success rates by requesting human help for approximately 8.3% of the time steps on average across the four real-world tasks. This is a small fraction compared to the full trajectory lengths (e.g., 7.2 steps vs. 80.0 for Lift-real).
- **Qualitative Observations:** Interventions occurred in critical states:
 - Lift-sim/real: When the gripper neared unseen objects for grasping.
 - Cup/Ramekin Stacking: During alignment phases, especially when visual occlusion was present.
 - Open Drawer/3-Mode Pushing: When deciding which action mode to pursue (e.g., which drawer to open), with human guidance steering the policy to the intended target.
- **Baseline Performance:** ThriftyDAgger showed reasonable uncertainty estimation but suffered from less effective autonomous behavior, leading to more interventions. Diff-DAgger, despite using diffusion models, required more assistance. HULA-offline generally performed the worst, potentially due to challenges with sparse rewards in an offline RL setting.

**C. Fine-tuning Performance**
The data collected during human interventions, triggered by high uncertainty, proved to be highly effective for policy fine-tuning.
- **Targeted Data Value (Figure 4 Left, Table IV):** The method consistently achieved higher autonomous success rates with a smaller amount of fine-tuning data compared to baselines.
- For the Lift-real task, fine-tuning with 80 intervention steps (collected via the uncertainty metric) improved the success rate from 16% (zero-shot) to 63%. This outperformed fine-tuning with 132 steps of full-trajectory data, which only reached 31% success. This indicates that the uncertainty metric effectively identifies valuable data points for policy improvement.
- Diff-DAgger showed the best fine-tuning improvements among baselines, attributed to its use of diffusion-based policies.

**D. Uncertainty Metric Robustness and Hyperparameter Effects**
- **Threshold Selection (Figure 4 Right):** Setting the uncertainty threshold at the 95% quantile of a validation set consistently led to 100% success rates with minimized human intervention across different scenarios. This indicates the metric's robustness and consistency in identifying critical states.
- **Sampling Radius *r* (Table V):** A normalized sampling radius of 0.05 was identified as optimal for Lift-sim, balancing local and global uncertainty. Deviations led to less precise estimations and more interventions.
- **Scaling Constant α (Table VI):** Small α values (0.01-0.1) were found to be more effective, emphasizing inter-mode divergence as a strong indicator of uncertainty, while still integrating intra-mode variance for single-mode scenarios.

**E. Comparison with Other Metrics (Appendix A)**
- **Sentinel:** Performed poorly due to assigning high inconsistency to valid but "reversed" motions and suffering from high latency in detecting failures, making it unsuitable for real-time HitL.
- **Diffusion Output Variance:** Was unstable and produced false negatives, as it did not adequately account for multi-modality in action distributions, assigning low variance during critical multi-modal failure states.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that the proposed uncertainty metric, derived from diffusion policy denoising, is effective in enabling robots to selectively request assistance, leading to more efficient human-robot collaboration and targeted policy improvement through fine-tuning, across various challenging robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a method for Human-in-the-Loop (HitL) robot policies that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on robotics research and deployment:

1. **Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability of Human-Robot Interaction:** The most direct impact is the reduction of human burden in supervising robotic systems. By enabling robots to proactively identify states of high uncertainty and selectively request assistance, the method eliminates the need for constant human monitoring or reactive interventions. This increases the efficiency of human operators and makes the deployment of larger fleets of semi-autonomous robots more scalable and practical.

2. **"Free" and Practical Uncertainty Estimation:** The integration of uncertainty estimation directly into the generative process of diffusion policies is a key innovation. This means that uncertainty can be calculated without additional model training or significant runtime overhead, rendering it a "free" byproduct of policy execution. This practicality removes a common barrier to implementing uncertainty-aware behaviors in real-world robotic systems.

3. **Improved Policy Performance and Robustness:** The method demonstrably improves policy performance and task success rates, particularly in challenging scenarios characterized by data distribution shifts, partial observability, or task under-specification leading to action multi-modality. By judiciously incorporating human expertise at critical junctures, the robot can successfully navigate situations that would otherwise lead to failure.

4. **Targeted and Efficient Data Collection for Adaptation:** The uncertainty metric serves as an effective mechanism for identifying states where the policy struggles, thus pinpointing valuable opportunities for data collection. This enables a more efficient fine-tuning process, as data is specifically gathered from high-impact states rather than through general, often redundant, full-trajectory demonstrations. This accelerates the robot's ability to adapt to new environments, objects, or task variations with minimal human effort.

5. **Generality and Broad Applicability:** The method's demonstrated consistency with a fixed, quantile-based uncertainty threshold across diverse simulated and real-world tasks suggests a high degree of generality and robustness. This reduces the need for extensive, task-specific calibration, making the approach applicable to a wider range of robotic manipulation problems.

6. **Foundation for Future Human-Robot Collaboration:** This work establishes a foundation for more sophisticated and intuitive human-robot collaborative paradigms. By understanding its own uncertainty, a robot could potentially convey its intent or reasoning to a human operator, leading to more transparent and effective human guidance. The authors specifically mention future work extending this approach to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which could pave the way for interpretable feedback mechanisms and more natural human-robot communication regarding uncertainty.

In essence, this research provides a practical, efficient, and robust framework for enabling robots to introspect their capabilities, leverage human intelligence selectively, and continuously improve through targeted learning, thereby advancing the state of the art in semi-autonomous robot deployment.
