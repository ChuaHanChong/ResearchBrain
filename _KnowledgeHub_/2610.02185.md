# Decoding Looped Transformers Better for (Almost) Free

> [!summary] Summary
> LoopCD is a training-free contrastive decoding method for looped Transformers. It treats an early recurrent iteration as a built-in weak predictor and pushes the final prediction further away from it, either in logit space (one extra output pass) or in hidden space (no overhead). It lifts Ouro-2.6B-Thinking's AIME 2024 pass@1 from 61.88% to 73.33%, and at half the loops it still matches full-depth accuracy while cutting forward FLOPs by 22.5-48.2%.

## Problem

- Standard looped-Transformer decoding keeps only the final recurrent state and discards intermediate iterations, even though the shared output head can decode them into valid next-token distributions.
- Contrastive decoding in ordinary language models needs an aligned weak predictor, usually an auxiliary smaller model, a perturbed context, or an intermediate layer, which adds cost and alignment mismatch.
- Halving recurrent iterations to save inference compute costs 0.17 to 1.29 points of multiple-choice accuracy, and no existing method recovers that loss without retraining.

## Method

- **LoopCD-Logits**: runs both the first and final recurrent states through the ==coda== and ==LM head==, then guides with z_R + omega(z_R - z_1), at the cost of one extra output pass.
- **LoopCD-Hidden**: extrapolates hidden states as h_R + omega(h_R - h_1) before the coda layers, so the coda and LM head run once and output-pass overhead is zero.
- ==Adaptive margin-gated strength==: scales omega per token by one minus the top-two probability margin, so guidance goes to contested tokens and is withheld from confident decisions.

## Results

- Adaptive LoopCD-Logits lifts Ouro-2.6B-Thinking from **61.88%** to **73.33%** on AIME **2024** pass@1 and from **49.58%** to **56.88%** on AIME **2025**; Ouro's mean math pass@1 rises **5.27**-7.33 points.
- LoopCD-Hidden raises Huginn HumanEval pass@1 from **22.56%** to **31.71%** at R=32 with no added output pass, and every model family's seven-benchmark multiple-choice mean improves.
- At half the recurrent iterations, LoopCD matches or beats the full-depth unguided baseline in all six settings while saving **22.5**-48.2% of forward FLOPs; a first-iteration reference gives the largest gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Weight-shared recurrence gives a free, aligned sequence of predictors from weak to strong; continuing along the refinement direction adds accuracy without auxiliary models or any training.
> - Gains track how often the reference disagrees with the final prediction, and they come from re-ranking close, low-confidence decisions, not from temperature-like rescaling; confident predictions barely change.
> - Autoregressive generation tolerates about half the guidance strength that likelihood scoring does because errors compound, and noise-initialized recurrent states corrupt early hidden-space references.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Place in the broader research landscape

Looped Transformers reuse a shared block of Transformer layers for multiple recurrent iterations. This allows a model to increase its effective computational depth without adding a distinct set of parameters for every layer. Recent work has applied recurrence to latent reasoning and test-time computation, with models such as Ouro, Huginn, and Parcae designed to make use of iterative internal processing. A related line of research explores how to improve these models through training changes or different inference schedules.

This paper addresses a less explored opportunity: using intermediate recurrent states during decoding. Standard generation uses the final state and discards earlier ones, even though each state can produce a prediction for the same input prefix. The authors interpret an early state as a weaker prediction and the final state as a stronger one, then apply contrastive decoding to extrapolate from the early prediction toward the final prediction.

The approach relates to contrastive decoding, which improves a strong predictor by contrasting it with a weaker reference. Existing methods may require a second model, a modified prompt, or an intermediate layer in a conventional Transformer. LoopCD instead uses the recurrent trajectory already available in a looped model. The paper distinguishes this temporal contrast—between completed recurrent iterations—from spatial contrast between layers. Its analysis argues that intermediate states *within* a recurrent iteration may not be reliable prediction points, whereas states at iteration boundaries can be decoded through the model’s established output path. The method also shares a general guidance principle with classifier-free guidance and autoguidance in diffusion models: use the difference between weaker and stronger predictions to adjust the final output.

### 2. Objectives and motivation

The central objective is to improve the quality or computational efficiency of looped Transformer decoding without further training, adding an auxiliary model, or increasing the number of recurrent iterations. The authors begin from an architectural observation: each iteration applies shared parameters to the same input prefix, producing successive representations in a common model. Those intermediate representations are normally unused at inference, but they can be turned into aligned predictions.

The proposed method is intended to exploit the direction of refinement across recurrence. If an early prediction favors one answer less than the final prediction does, the difference between them can indicate which alternatives gained support as the model computed further. Extrapolating in that direction may change the ranking of close candidates while leaving confident decisions largely unaffected.

The work pursues two related goals. First, it tests whether this contrast improves model performance when recurrent depth is held constant. Second, it asks whether the gains can compensate for reducing the number of recurrent iterations, thereby maintaining full-depth accuracy with less inference computation. The paper also compares applying contrast in output-logit space with applying it in hidden-state space, because these options have different computational costs and may behave differently when the architecture has layers after its recurrent block.

### 3. Methodology and approach

#### 3.1. Contrastive guidance from recurrent states

Let \(h_1\) be the state after the first recurrent iteration and \(h_R\) the state after the final iteration. The paper treats \(h_1\) as the weaker reference and \(h_R\) as the stronger prediction. Both concern the same prefix, and the model’s existing output layers can map either state to a token distribution.

LoopCD has two variants:

- **LoopCD-Logits** passes both states through the relevant output layers, producing logits \(z_1\) and \(z_R\). It forms guided logits as \(z' = z_R + \omega(z_R-z_1)\), where \(\omega\) controls guidance strength. This requires an extra pass through the layers after the recurrent block to decode the reference state.
- **LoopCD-Hidden** combines the states before the output layers: \(h' = h_R + \omega(h_R-h_1)\). It then applies the output layers once. This avoids an extra output pass, although subsequent layers can attenuate or alter the hidden-state contrast.

When \(\omega=0\), either form returns the unguided final prediction. Guidance is training-free and does not add recurrent iterations.

#### 3.2. Guidance strength and reference selection

The authors evaluate fixed guidance strength and, for LoopCD-Logits, an adaptive rule. The adaptive rule scales guidance using the margin between the two most probable tokens in the final prediction: when the leading candidates are close, guidance increases toward a specified maximum; when one candidate is clearly ahead, guidance is reduced. The intention is to focus changes on uncertain decisions and avoid disturbing settled ones. The hidden-state variant is not evaluated with this adaptive rule because computing the required final prediction for the uncertainty estimate would require an extra output pass.

The reference is generally the first completed iteration for logit guidance. For hidden-state guidance, reference selection depends on model dynamics. In Huginn, whose recurrent state begins from Gaussian noise, the first hidden state can contain noise that the output layers would otherwise suppress. The authors therefore use later states for some hidden-state evaluations.

#### 3.3. Models and evaluation

The study evaluates four families: Ouro, Huginn, Parcae, and Looped-Qwen3. Their recurrence designs differ. Ouro repeats its decoder stack, Huginn and Parcae place a recurrent block between fixed layers, and Looped-Qwen3 applies damped recurrence to a frozen layer window.

Evaluation covers mathematical reasoning, code generation, and multiple-choice question answering. Mathematical benchmarks include AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and OlympiadBench; code tests include HumanEval and MBPP with standard and extended tests; multiple-choice evaluation includes seven benchmarks such as ARC, MMLU, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, PIQA, and SciQ. Guided and baseline runs use matched checkpoints and prompts, and full-depth comparisons match recurrent iteration counts. The compute analysis reports theoretical forward FLOPs for a 512-token prefill, rather than measured wall-clock latency.

### 4. Main findings and results

#### 4.1. Accuracy at full recurrent depth

LoopCD improves performance across the evaluated model families and task types, though the gains vary by benchmark and configuration. The largest highlighted mathematical-reasoning result is for Ouro-2.6B-Thinking: adaptive LoopCD-Logits raises AIME 2024 pass@1 from 61.88% to 73.33%, an increase of 11.45 percentage points. It also increases AIME 2025 pass@1 from 49.58% to 56.88% and OlympiadBench pass@1 from 64.05% to 67.29%. The Ouro-Thinking configurations generally improve both pass@1 and pass@10 on the reported mathematical tests.

Code-generation results also show substantial gains in some settings. For Huginn at 32 iterations, LoopCD-Hidden raises HumanEval pass@1 from 22.56% to 31.71%, a 9.15-point increase. For the same model and depth, the reported four-column average across HumanEval and MBPP base and extended tests rises by 5.13 points. Logit guidance also improves code results in several configurations, although results are not uniformly positive in every individual column.

Across the seven multiple-choice benchmarks, each evaluated model’s mean score improves under LoopCD-Logits with both fixed and adaptive strength. The mean changes are generally modest compared with some reasoning and code gains, and individual benchmark scores sometimes decline. The hidden-state variant improves the mean for its reported configurations and can be competitive with, or better than, logit guidance, particularly on code generation and in architectures with a shallow output coda.

#### 4.2. Reduced recurrent depth and explanatory analyses

The paper reports six half-depth comparisons on the multiple-choice suite. In each, LoopCD at half the recurrent iterations matches or exceeds the corresponding full-depth unguided model’s seven-benchmark mean. Depending on the model and guidance variant, the reported reduction in forward FLOPs ranges from 22.5% to 48.2%. This result depends on the architecture: logit guidance has a greater cost when substantial output layers must be run again, while hidden-state guidance adds negligible output-pass cost.

The authors’ analyses provide evidence for their weak-to-strong interpretation. The first recurrent prediction trails the final prediction by 4.0 to 23.7 points in seven-benchmark mean accuracy across the tested models. Prediction distributions generally become more similar as recurrence proceeds, and LoopCD gains tend to be larger when the reference disagrees with the final prediction. In one analysis, guidance gains concentrate on the least-confident fifth of questions, with little or no gain on the most-confident fifth.

The paper also finds that guidance strength must be controlled. Multiple-choice scoring tolerates a comparatively broad range, while autoregressive generation is sensitive to excessive guidance: changes to early generated tokens can compound, causing performance to fall at higher strengths. The adaptive logit rule is intended to limit that risk by applying more guidance to ambiguous predictions. Results on GSM8K are mixed, and the paper reports that LoopCD can reduce pass@1 while increasing pass@10 for Looped-Qwen3 on some mathematical evaluations. Thus, improvements depend on the task, decoding setup, and guidance configuration.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

LoopCD demonstrates a way to make intermediate computation in recurrent language models useful at decoding time. Its practical attraction is that it requires no additional training data or auxiliary model, and hidden-state guidance can reuse the standard output pass. The half-depth results suggest that guidance may help trade some recurrent computation for prediction quality, although the reported FLOP reductions are analytic estimates and do not establish equivalent real-world latency savings.

The study also contributes a distinction relevant to model design and analysis: completed recurrent iterations may provide meaningful weak-to-strong prediction pairs, while states inside an iteration may not be stable exits. This supports further investigation of how recurrence trajectories can inform decoding and compute allocation.

The findings should be interpreted within the study’s scope. Evaluations cover four model families and established benchmarks, and guidance strengths are selected through screening sweeps. Some tasks show mixed outcomes, and generation is particularly sensitive to the settings. Further work would be needed to assess robustness across additional models, tasks, prompting and sampling conditions, and deployment hardware. Even with these qualifications, LoopCD offers a simple inference-time method for using recurrent states that standard decoding discards, with potential value both for improving outputs and for reducing recurrent compute.
