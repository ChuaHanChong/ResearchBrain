# Improved GelSight Tactile Sensor for Measuring Geometry and Slip

> [!summary] Summary
> A redesigned fingertip GelSight optical tactile sensor replaces the semi-specular gel membrane with a Lambertian aluminum-powder coating and adds collimated tri-color LED arrays on a 3D-printed hexagonal semitransparent tray, giving uniform illumination and accurate photometric-stereo height maps. Marker motion plus object-texture tracking detects translational and rotational slip, raising closed-loop grasp success to 89% over 37 everyday objects.

## Problem

- Prior fingertip GelSight used acrylic light guides giving non-uniform illumination, so estimated surface normals carried position-dependent errors and depth maps stayed coarse and grainy.
- Photometric stereo works poorly on the semi-specular membrane earlier designs required, and color-to-normal mapping became ambiguous beyond about 30 degrees of surface slope.
- Earlier sensors needed hand-tuned, hard-to-reproduce assembly, and prior slip work tested only flat, weakly textured objects without real-time robot grasping trials.

## Method

- New illumination: ==collimated 30-degree-lens LEDs== in 2x4 red/green/blue arrays mounted at 71 degrees on a 3D-printed semitransparent hexagonal tray, plus a dome-shaped ==Lambertian aluminum-silicone membrane==.
- Calibration builds a ==lookup table from RGB to surface normals== by pressing a 3.96 mm ball at many positions and averaging tables, then reconstructs height maps by ==photometric stereo==.
- ==Slip detection== combines relative translation/rotation between ==object texture and gel markers== in a 120x120 window with a ==peripheral-to-maximum marker displacement ratio== thresholded at 0.8, plus ==contact-area monitoring==.

## Results

- Surface-normal pitch regression improved from R-squared **0.557** to **0.818** versus ground truth, with linear color-to-normal response extended from **5-25 degrees** to **5-60 degrees**.
- Across calibration positions, the new sensor's worst R-squared roughly equals the old sensor's best, with no low-R-squared tail, showing no blind spots; reconstructed coins and watch chains resolve fine features.
- Slip prediction over **37 objects** scored **79%** on 147 successful grasps, **84%** on 116 slip cases, **60%** on 52 border cases; closed-loop regrasping succeeded **88/99** times (**89%**), averaging **2.3 attempts**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fixing the optics upstream, uniform collimated light on a Lambertian surface, buys more measurement accuracy than post-hoc algorithmic correction of a badly lit specular membrane.
> - Slip cues grounded in physics definitions, relative object-versus-skin displacement, generalize across unknown geometry, weight and material without per-object priors or training.
> - Caution: the method degrades on smooth, textureless objects held with low force, where weak shear signal and absent texture leave slip undetectable until it is severe.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research contributes to the field of robotic tactile sensing, an essential component for robots to effectively interact with physical environments. Tactile sensors provide critical information about contact, force, and object properties, enabling robots to perform tasks such as grasping, manipulation, and object identification.

Historically, tactile sensors have evolved from basic contact detection to devices capable of measuring various physical signals including force, vibration, and deformation. Optical tactile sensors represent a significant subcategory, utilizing cameras to capture deformations and infer contact information with high spatial resolution. Early optical sensors focused on reconstructing 2D shapes, detecting contact points, or estimating force distributions by tracking markers or observing surface changes.

The GelSight sensor, developed by Adelson and colleagues, emerged as an optical tactile sensor specifically designed for high-resolution 3D topography measurement of contact surfaces. Previous iterations, such as the Fingertip GelSight, have demonstrated utility in robotic tasks like object recognition, fine manipulation, and even medical diagnosis. However, these earlier GelSight designs faced challenges including limitations in geometric measurement accuracy, non-uniform illumination, and complex, non-standardized fabrication processes.

Simultaneously, the broader robotics community has long pursued robust slip detection as a means to achieve stable grasping. Conventional slip detection methods often rely on accelerometers, vibration sensors, or force/torque sensors to monitor changes indicative of incipient or actual slip. While previous work by Yuan et al. explored using GelSight for slip detection by tracking marker displacements, a comprehensive and generalized approach robust to diverse object properties and integrated with real-time robotic control remained an ongoing challenge.

This paper addresses these interconnected issues by introducing a redesigned GelSight sensor that aims to enhance geometric accuracy and simplify fabrication. It then applies this improved sensor to develop and validate a generalizable slip detection system for robotic grasping, thereby advancing both tactile sensor technology and autonomous manipulation capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were twofold: first, to develop an improved design for the GelSight tactile sensor that overcomes identified limitations of previous versions, particularly the Fingertip GelSight sensor described by Li et al. Second, to demonstrate the utility of this new sensor for robust and generalized slip detection in robotic grasping.

The motivation for improving the GelSight sensor stemmed from several challenges associated with the prior Fingertip GelSight:
1. **Inaccurate 3D Geometry Reconstruction:** The previous sensor used a semi-specular reflective surface and an illumination system that often resulted in non-uniform light distribution. This led to errors in surface normal estimation and, consequently, limited precision in reconstructing the 3D height map of contacted objects. The goal was to achieve a more precise and accurate representation of object geometry.
2. **Complex Fabrication Process:** The existing sensor required intricate manual adjustment of its components, making its fabrication difficult, time-consuming, and hard to reproduce consistently. A key motivation was to simplify and standardize the manufacturing process to facilitate wider adoption and research.
3. **Limitations in General Slip Detection:** While previous GelSight research explored slip detection, there was a need for a more generalizable and robust method applicable to a wide variety of objects without requiring prior knowledge of their specific geometry, material, or surface characteristics. Human slip perception, often based on skin stretch and relative motion, provided inspiration for developing more universal slip detection strategies.

To address these motivations, the research aimed to:
- Redesign the sensor's illumination system to provide uniform light suitable for a Lambertian reflective surface, thereby improving the quality of 3D height map reconstruction.
- Develop a sensor frame and components that could be easily fabricated, for example, using 3D printing, to reduce manufacturing complexity and increase reproducibility.
- Maintain a compact form factor suitable for integration into robot grippers.
- Develop slip detection methods based on the fundamental definitions of slip (relative displacement) and human perception (skin stretch/deformation), making them universally applicable to various objects. This would allow robots to better monitor grasp states and increase success rates with unknown or complex objects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research involved a multi-stage methodology encompassing the design, fabrication, calibration, evaluation, and application of the improved GelSight tactile sensor.

**Sensor Design and Fabrication:**
The new GelSight sensor was designed with several key modifications. The illumination system was re-engineered using three arrays of red, green, and blue LEDs, each with a small collimating lens (30° viewing angle). These arrays were tightly arranged and mounted on a 3D-printed, semi-transparent hexagonal plastic tray. The LED mounts were tilted at 71° to the sensor surface to ensure uniform illumination across the sensing area and to create a significant variance in reflection for different surface normals, which is beneficial for photometric stereo. The semi-transparent tray also contributed to light homogenization.

A significant change involved replacing the semi-specular membrane with a Lambertian surface. This was achieved by coating the silicone gel, which already contained black markers, with a thin layer of aluminum powder mixed in a silicone base. This modification simplified fabrication and used non-toxic materials. The gel surface itself was designed with a dome shape to further improve reflection uniformity.

A laser-cut transparent hexagonal acrylic window served as a support plate for the gel. The hollow tray beneath this window was filled with silicone material to match the refractive index of the sensing gel, thus eliminating internal reflections and potential imaging artifacts. For imaging, a compact camera (Logitech C310) was mounted 35 mm above the sensor, providing a wide field of view. The entire sensor frame and protective cover were designed to be 3D-printable using robust materials, standardizing the fabrication process and making it accessible. The design also ensured compatibility with standard robot grippers, specifically fitting the WSG parallel gripper.

**Calibration for Geometry Measurement:**
Before operation, the sensor underwent a calibration process to create a lookup table mapping RGB color values to surface normals. This involved pressing a standard ball (3.96 mm diameter) against the elastomer gel. The camera captured images of the color changes induced by the gel's deformation. The known geometry of the ball allowed for the calculation of the ground truth surface normal for each pixel. A program then automatically generated a lookup table. To account for slight spatial variations in illumination, this calibration was performed at multiple positions across the sensor surface, and the final lookup table was averaged to minimize noise.

**Evaluation of Geometry Measurement:**
The performance of the new GelSight sensor in measuring geometry was quantitatively and qualitatively compared against the previous Fingertip GelSight sensor (Li's sensor) across four metrics:
1. **Gradient vs. Color Change:** The relationship between the surface normal pitch angle and the corresponding color change was analyzed to assess the linearity and effective range of surface normal encoding.
2. **Mapping Accuracy of Lookup Table:** The precision of surface normal measurements was evaluated by comparing estimated pitch and yaw angles from the sensor against ground truth values obtained from a standard calibration ball. The R² coefficient was used as a statistical measure of accuracy.
3. **Spatial Difference of Illumination:** The uniformity of measurement accuracy across the sensor surface was assessed by analyzing the distribution of R² values obtained from calibrations performed at various locations. This helped identify potential "blind spots" or areas of reduced accuracy.
4. **Quality of 3D Shape Reconstruction:** Qualitative comparisons were made by reconstructing 3D depth maps of various test objects, including ball arrays, a human finger, a watch chain, and a quarter coin, and visually comparing the clarity, smoothness, and detail retention of the reconstructions from both sensors.

**Slip Detection Methodology:**
The improved GelSight sensor was integrated with a UR5 6DOF robotic arm and a WSG 50 parallel gripper for real-time slip detection. The slip detection algorithm utilized three complementary cues:
1. **Relative Displacement between Object Texture and Markers:** For objects with visible surface textures, a 120x120 pixel window centered on the area with the largest color intensity difference (indicating high curvature or distinct texture) was analyzed. The translation and rotation of both the object's texture (derived from grayscale intensity changes) and the black markers embedded in the gel were tracked between consecutive video frames. Slip was inferred if a significant difference in their relative motion (translational or rotational) exceeded a predefined threshold or if cumulative relative motion was substantial.
2. **Tracking Marker Motion Distribution in the Contact Area:** For objects with smooth surfaces or large curvatures where texture tracking was less effective, slip was detected by analyzing the deformation pattern of the gel. The ratio (r) was calculated between the maximum moving distance of markers in the "approximate peripheral area" (identified by areas of largest color change) and the maximum moving distance of any marker within the contact patch. If this ratio fell below a threshold (0.8 in experiments), it indicated that the peripheral area was slipping relative to the more stable central contact area, signaling incipient or actual slip.
3. **Contact Detection and Change in Contact Area:** Initial contact was detected by comparing the current image's color intensity to a baseline image with no contact. During lifting, a significant decrease in the overall contact area was interpreted as an indicator of severe slip. For flat, smooth objects, the overall motion of markers was also used to infer changes in normal force.

**Robotic Slip Detection Experiments:**
Two sets of experiments were conducted to evaluate the slip detection capabilities:
1. **Slip Prediction:** The robot grasped and slowly lifted 37 common household objects with diverse properties (size, shape, material, texture). Each object was subjected to 7-10 grasp attempts with varying gripping forces. GelSight video data was recorded, and human observation was used to determine the ground truth of slip occurrence (successful grasp, slip occurred, or border case). The algorithm's accuracy in classifying these outcomes was then quantified.
2. **Grasp Control with Slip Detection:** This experiment involved a closed-loop control system. The robot attempted to grasp 33 objects. If the GelSight sensor detected slip during the lifting phase, the robot autonomously released the object, placed it down, and then re-attempted the grasp at the same position with a 1.2x higher contact threshold (i.e., increased gripping force). This iterative process continued until a stable grasp was achieved. The success rate of achieving stable grasps and the average number of attempts were recorded.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Evaluation of Geometry Measurement**
The new GelSight sensor demonstrated quantifiable improvements in its ability to measure 3D geometry:

- **Gradient vs. Color Change:** The new sensor exhibited a more linear relationship between the surface normal pitch angle and the corresponding color change, extending from approximately 5 to 60 degrees. This linearity provided a more reliable and less ambiguous mapping compared to Li's sensor, which showed linearity only between 5 and 25 degrees and ambiguous mapping beyond 30 degrees.
- **Mapping Accuracy of Lookup Table:** The accuracy of surface normal measurement was improved. The R² value for estimating surface normal pitch angles was 0.818 for the new sensor, a notable increase over Li's sensor's 0.557. For yaw angles, both sensors achieved high R² values, with the new sensor showing a marginal improvement, indicating a more precise estimation of orientation.
- **Spatial Difference of Illumination:** The new sensor achieved greater illumination uniformity across its sensing surface. The probability distribution of R² values for pitch angles showed that the majority of measurements from the new sensor were more accurate than the best measurements from Li's sensor. Critically, the new sensor's distribution lacked the low R² "tail" observed in Li's sensor, indicating the absence of spatially localized areas of poor measurement accuracy ("blind spots"). Similar uniform improvements were observed for yaw angles.
- **Quality of 3D Shape Reconstruction:** Qualitative analysis of 3D depth maps revealed superior reconstruction quality. The new sensor produced smooth, clear depth maps that accurately preserved the shapes and fine features of objects, such as the distinct feather patterns on a quarter coin. In contrast, Li's sensor generated grainy, often deformed 3D reconstructions with lower signal-to-noise ratios, particularly degrading the quality of features towards the edges of the contact area due to non-uniform illumination.

**B. Robotic Slip Detection**
The GelSight sensor, integrated into a robotic grasping system, showed effectiveness in slip detection and control:

- **Slip Prediction:** Across 315 trials involving 37 diverse objects, the slip prediction algorithm demonstrated considerable accuracy:
 - **Successful cases (no slip):** 79% were correctly identified.
 - **Slip cases (slip occurred):** 84% were correctly identified.
 - **Border cases (grasp barely stable):** 60% were correctly identified as 'no slip'.
 Failure analysis indicated that approximately 28% of slip detection failures occurred when objects had flat, smooth surfaces and gripping forces were very small, leading to insufficient shear force for marker motion analysis or lack of texture for tracking. Another 28% of slip case failures were attributed to significant rotational slip on flat objects, suggesting limitations in the current marker rotation analysis.
- **Grasp Control with Slip Detection:** In 99 closed-loop grasp experiments with 33 objects, the robot achieved a stable grasp in 88 instances, resulting in an 89% success rate. The robot performed an average of 2.3 grasp attempts per object (ranging from 1 to 6 attempts) by iteratively increasing the gripping force upon slip detection. Failure cases (11%) generally occurred when the initial gripping forces were too low to provide sufficient contact information for effective slip measurement. Human observation corroborated most of the detected slip events.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an advancement in the field of robotic tactile sensing, offering a new GelSight sensor design that significantly improves geometric measurement accuracy and simplifies the fabrication process. The adoption of a Lambertian surface and optimized, uniform illumination allows for more precise and reliable 3D shape reconstruction, which is a fundamental requirement for many advanced manipulation tasks. The emphasis on a 3D-printable frame and standardized components makes the sensor more accessible, reproducible, and scalable for deployment in various research and industrial settings.

The robust slip detection capability, demonstrated across a diverse set of objects and without requiring prior object knowledge, represents a substantial contribution to autonomous robotic manipulation. By providing real-time feedback on grasp stability, the GelSight sensor enables robots to dynamically adjust gripping forces, thereby preventing grasp failures and increasing the success rate of interacting with unknown or complex objects. This capability is critical for robots operating in unstructured environments where object properties cannot be precisely predetermined.

The enhanced GelSight sensor holds potential for a range of applications beyond stable grasping, including:
- **Object Recognition:** More accurate 3D geometry and texture information could enable finer-grained object differentiation and recognition.
- **Precise Manipulation:** The ability to sense minute surface details and deformations could facilitate tasks requiring high dexterity, such as assembly of small components or handling delicate objects.
- **Quality Control/Inspection:** Detecting subtle surface imperfections or variations through high-resolution tactile imaging.
- **Medical Robotics:** Potential applications in diagnostics, building on previous work in lump detection.

Overall, this work contributes to making robots more perceptive and adaptive, moving closer to human-level tactile intelligence and enabling more reliable and autonomous interaction with the physical world.
