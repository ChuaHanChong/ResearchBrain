# Learning from Runtime Feedback through Failure-Bank Self-Evolution for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> FailBank is a four-stage self-evolving framework that turns runtime safety feedback into lasting improvement of a vision-language-action (VLA) policy. A fixed CBF safety teacher watches rollouts and logs corrections without executing them. Outcome-aware admission keeps useful corrections, plus successful uncorrected actions as quiet anchors. A guarded LoRA update then fits the accumulated bank. On VLA-Arena, success rises 8.5 and 6.9 points while policy-induced cost falls 35.6% and 23.8%.

## Problem

- Runtime shields such as the CBF-based AEGIS correct individual unsafe actions but leave the nominal VLA policy unchanged, so the same action disagreements recur over consecutive control steps.
- This persistent policy-shield mismatch lets repeated projections cut immediate contact cost while pulling the gripper away from targets in constrained regions, causing timeouts and shield collapse on harder tasks.
- Collecting supervision while the shield executes distorts the rollout distribution, so the logged failures mostly reflect failures the shield caused rather than the nominal policy's own failure modes.

## Method

- ==Observe-only CBF teacher==: the policy always executes its nominal action, while a fixed CBF teacher using privileged simulator geometry logs ==counterfactual translational corrections== and trigger flags at each step.
- ==Outcome-aware admission== and weighting: rollout outcomes score each CBF-triggered record into weights of 0, 0.25, 0.60 or 1.00, while successful uncorrected actions become fixed-weight ==quiet anchors==, all accumulated into a growing ==failure bank==.
- ==Guarded LoRA update==: each round fits a fresh adapter from the base checkpoint with a weighted first-action ==flow-matching loss==, accepted only if held-out flow loss and first-action drift stay within fixed limits.

## Results

- Across **ten** VLA-Arena static-obstacle tasks, mean success rises from **66.0%** to **74.5%** on pi0.5 and from **47.5%** to **54.4%** on pi0, while policy-induced cost falls from **47.76** to **30.76** and from **10.62** to **8.09**.
- Against AEGIS shielding, success improves from **49.1%** to **74.5%** on pi0.5 and from **44.9%** to **54.4%** on pi0 at slightly higher cost; with data collected only on Level 1 mango, Level 2 mean success still rises from **50.5%** to **59.4%**.
- With the shield in the loop, **88.9%** of failure records are shield-induced; quiet anchors hold success at **89%** while cutting cost from **20.88** to **16.52**; over **five** rounds, success peaks at round **2** and cost bottoms at round **3**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Safety corrections are worth more as training targets than as execution-time overrides: distilling the shield into the policy removes the need for it at deployment and avoids repeated mismatch.
> - Collect supervision on the learner's own rollout distribution, because executing the teacher contaminates failure data with failures the teacher caused, while observe-only counterfactual labels keep outcomes attributable to the policy.
> - More accumulated feedback does not help monotonically, since success and cost reach their best values in different rounds, so iterative self-improvement needs drift guards and anchors that preserve already-good behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In a VLA-Arena simulation task, a robot must grasp an onion and place it in a bowl among protected bottles. A runtime shield can redirect an unsafe-looking action without teaching the underlying policy. Repeated redirection may therefore protect the immediate motion but obstruct the task. FailBank takes a different approach. It lets a safety teacher *propose* a correction while the policy remains in control. It then screens and accumulates those proposals into guarded policy updates. The shield's correction becomes a candidate lesson rather than an executed detour. The measurements are in VLA-Arena simulation, not a real-robot deployment. In one matched Level-2 replay, the runtime safety layer AEGIS repeatedly redirects the gripper near hazard bottles and times out. FailBank's policy enters the same intervention-prone region with its shield running only in observe-only mode, and it places the onion in the bowl. This replay is illustrative and is not evidence of how often shield collapse occurs.

One matched replay. Top: AEGIS makes 80 projections over 300 controller steps and times out at environment step 309. Bottom: FailBank with the shield off. Its observe-only CBF flags 33 of 180 controller steps and executes none of those corrections. The policy places the onion at environment step 189. This is one replay, not a general frequency or causal estimate.

### 2. From intervention to a lesson

The teacher is a control barrier function (CBF), a rule for identifying and projecting actions away from a defined unsafe region. The loop has four stages.

1. **Record.** The current policy proposes an action. The fixed CBF teacher uses privileged simulator geometry to record a counterfactual correction, but the environment executes the policy's nominal action.
1. **Screen.** After the rollout, the framework attaches the outcome and admits or discards records. A retained correction uses the teacher proposal as a target. A successful uncorrected action can be retained as a "quiet anchor" with the nominal action as target. Outcome-aware weighting rates the usefulness of triggered records, but it does not establish the causal effect of a correction that was never executed.
1. **Accumulate.** The bank accumulates admitted records across rounds.
1. **Update.** Each update fits a fresh low-rank adaptation (LoRA) adapter from the base model. The adapter is accepted only if a held-out guard on flow loss and first-action drift passes; otherwise the prior collecting policy is kept.

At deployment, the teacher and privileged geometry are absent.

The four-stage loop. The environment executes the policy's nominal action, while the teacher's correction is only a counterfactual proposal. The accepted policy, not the teacher, collects the next round.

### 3. Does the feedback survive the test?

The benchmark is VLA-Arena's ten-task static-obstacle suite, with five tasks at Level 1 and five at Level 2. Success rate (SR) is the percentage of trials meeting the task-completion predicate within the episode limit. $\text{CC}^{\text{policy}}$ is the trial-average sum of step costs after subtracting cost already present in the initial state.

On π₀.₅, FailBank raises the unweighted mean SR from 66.0% to 74.5%. It lowers mean $\text{CC}^{\text{policy}}$ from 47.76 to 30.76, a descriptive reduction of 35.6%. The same direction of change is reported on π₀. The paired tests for ten-task mean $\text{CC}^{\text{policy}}$ are not significant on either backbone (p = 0.085 for π₀.₅; p = 0.13 for π₀).

AEGIS shows a success–cost tradeoff in the reported means. On π₀.₅, it lowers mean $\text{CC}^{\text{policy}}$ more than FailBank (29.01 versus 30.76), while its mean SR is lower (49.1% versus 74.5%). On π₀, AEGIS likewise has lower mean cost (6.27 versus 8.09) and lower mean SR (44.9% versus 54.4%).

A Level-1 mango ablation tests whether collecting with the shield in the loop gives the same evidence. Across 46 paired episodes, steering rescued five otherwise failing episodes but turned eight otherwise successful episodes into failures. Shield-induced failures accounted for 88.9% of failure records in the shield-in-loop bank.

### 4. A correction that carries forward

FailBank turns observe-only runtime feedback into supervision for improving future policy behavior. The evidence is limited to static-obstacle VLA-Arena tasks and two flow-matching backbones. The authors identify dynamic obstacles and other action formulations as limitations.
