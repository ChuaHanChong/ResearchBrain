# Position: Agentic Evolution is the Path to Evolving LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces "Agentic Evolution" as a new paradigm for Large Language Model (LLM) systems to autonomously adapt and improve in dynamic real-world environments. This framework, called A-EVOLVE, enables LLMs to continuously diagnose failures, strategically update their capabilities through verified, modular artifacts, and consistently enhance task performance, allowing smaller models to surpass larger static counterparts.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with the "train-deploy environment gap," leading to performance degradation in dynamic, open-ended real-world scenarios.
- Existing adaptation methods lack "strategic agency" to intelligently diagnose failures and decide what, when, and how to change, often risking catastrophic forgetting or accumulating unverified experience.
- Centralized retraining for continuous adaptation faces challenges with privacy constraints and high computational costs, especially for recurrent failures.

## Method

- Introduces ==Agentic Evolution==, defining it as a deployment-time ability for an LLM system to autonomously improve its capabilities through interaction without direct human intervention.
- Develops **A-EVOLVE**, a framework where an explicit ==evolver agent== diagnoses failures, plans targeted updates to a persistent, editable "artifact state" (knowledge, tools, validation assets), and commits them via a ==governance gate==.
- Separates system operation into a "Solve Phase" for task execution and an "Evolve Phase" for asynchronous capability improvement, ensuring dedicated compute for adaptation and relying on goal-oriented, autonomous, and compositional principles.

## Results

- **A-EVOLVE** achieved substantial improvements in task goal completion (TGC) and average passed tests (APT) across various LLM solver backbones, e.g., Claude Haiku 4.5 with A-EVOLVE reached **64%** TGC compared to **32%** for the vanilla solver.
- Smaller solver models augmented with **A-EVOLVE** matched or exceeded the performance of larger vanilla models; for instance, Claude Haiku 4.5 with A-EVOLVE outperformed a vanilla Claude Sonnet 4.
- Performance consistently improved with increased evolution-time compute (evolution steps) and larger evolver model sizes, with the `Verify` component identified as critical for preventing regressions and ensuring stable long-term adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating evolution as an agentic decision-making problem, rather than a fixed pipeline, allows for strategic, goal-directed, and autonomous adaptation to address underlying failure causes.
> - System improvements can be made through structured, modular, and verifiable artifacts, which amortize expensive reasoning into reusable, persistent capabilities.
> - A new "evolution-scaling hypothesis" suggests that the capacity for adaptation scales predictably with the compute allocated to the evolution process, offering a new axis for AI development complementary to training and inference compute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has primarily been driven by scaling along two axes: increasing training-time compute (encompassing pre-training and post-training phases) and scaling inference-time compute through sophisticated reasoning chains. While these scaling paradigms have led to remarkable capabilities, a fundamental challenge persists when deploying LLMs in open-ended, real-world environments: the "train-deploy environment gap." Static models, trained on finite data, are inherently limited in their ability to anticipate the infinite variability of real-world scenarios, shifting APIs, and evolving constraints. Consequently, purely static models are prone to degradation and failure under prolonged deployment.

Existing approaches to address this deployment-time adaptation largely fall into two categories:
1. **Parametric Evolution:** Methods like test-time training or online fine-tuning update the LLM's parametric backbone (weights). While capable in principle, these updates are characterized as opaque, difficult to govern, and carry a risk of catastrophic forgetting under distribution shifts.
2. **Non-parametric Heuristic Evolution:** These methods modify the system's non-parametric state, such as by appending textual memories or optimizing prompts using fixed rules. These approaches are often lightweight but treat evolution as a storage or search problem. As experience accumulates, memory can saturate with noisy, unverified text, leading to diminishing returns.

The paper argues that a common limitation across these existing methods is the absence of "strategic agency" in the evolution process itself. The update rules employed are typically static and heuristic, lacking the ability to reason about failures, diagnose underlying causes, decide *what* to change, *when* to change it, and *how* to adapt the update strategy as deployment conditions evolve. This deficiency results in either blind modification of weights without semantic accountability or indiscriminate accumulation of experience without understanding relevance or long-term utility.

This work positions itself by proposing a new scaling axis—evolution—defined as the deployment-time ability of an LLM system to autonomously improve its capabilities through interaction, without direct human intervention. The central contribution is to elevate evolution from a fixed pipeline to an "autonomous evolver agent," thereby introducing "agentic capability" into the adaptation process. This paradigm aims to address the inherent limitations of static LLMs and non-agentic adaptation methods by enabling a more deliberate, goal-directed, and scalable approach to sustained real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the fundamental limitation of static Large Language Models (LLMs) when deployed in dynamic, open-ended real-world environments. This limitation, termed the "train-deploy environment gap," implies that models trained on finite datasets cannot exhaustively account for the infinite variety of real-world inputs, evolving interfaces, and changing constraints, leading to inevitable performance degradation over time.

The key motivations for this work are multifold:

1. **Bridging the Train-Deploy Gap:** The authors contend that static optimization over fixed distributions is inherently insufficient for prolonged deployment. Adaptation must occur *in situ*—during deployment—to ensure continued functionality and improvement. This necessitates an "evolution" capability, where the LLM system continuously learns and improves based on accumulated experience.
2. **Addressing Privacy and Governance Constraints:** Many real-world deployment settings involve sensitive user feedback, proprietary data, or private interactions. Centralized logging for global retraining in such scenarios is often impractical or violates privacy regulations. Agentic evolution, by enabling local, governed improvements through persistent artifacts, offers a mechanism for adaptation without requiring the leakage of private or sensitive data.
3. **Amortizing Computational Costs:** While increased inference-time compute (e.g., through reasoning chains) can help solve novel instances, it is deemed inefficient for recurrent failures. If an LLM system repeatedly struggles with a particular capability (e.g., parsing a new file format or handling a brittle API), continuously "thinking longer" each time is suboptimal. The motivation here is to enable the system to "learn once and reuse the solution," thereby amortizing expensive reasoning into cheap, persistent capabilities. This contrasts with purely reactive inference-time solutions.
4. **Introducing Strategic Agency into Adaptation:** A critical motivation arises from the perceived limitations of existing adaptation methods. Both parametric (e.g., fine-tuning) and non-parametric heuristic (e.g., memory accumulation) approaches are seen as lacking the "strategic agency" needed to intelligently diagnose failures, determine *what* aspects of the system require change, and adapt their own update strategies. The authors argue that treating evolution as a mechanical procedure rather than an agentic decision-making problem is the root cause of these limitations.
5. **Establishing a Scalable Path for Sustained Adaptation:** The paper aims to provide a framework for LLM systems that can reliably adapt over indefinite deployment horizons. It hypothesizes an "evolution-scaling hypothesis," suggesting that the capacity for adaptation scales with the compute allocated to the evolution process. This introduces a new scaling axis, complementary to existing training-time and inference-time compute, with the objective of providing a predictable and systematic path toward sustained, open-ended adaptation.

In summary, the core objective is to shift the paradigm of LLM deployment from static inference to continuous, autonomous, and governed adaptation through an agentic evolution process, thereby enabling LLM systems to maintain and improve capabilities in dynamic real-world environments sustainably and scalably.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces "Agentic Evolution" as a new paradigm for LLM adaptation, moving beyond static training and non-agentic deployment-time adjustments. It is instantiated through a general framework called A-EVOLVE, built upon a defined system model, core principles, and structural components.

**System Model:**
An LLM system is modeled as a composite policy $\pi_t = (\pi_{\theta,t}, \pi_{S,t})$, where:
- $\pi_{\theta,t}$ is the parametric backbone (e.g., LLM weights).
- $\pi_{S,t}$ is a non-parametric, persistent, and editable "artifact state." This state encompasses elements like tools, skills, workflows, structured knowledge, and validation assets, which condition the system's behavior across episodes.

**Definition of Evolution:**
Evolution is defined as a cross-episode update process that converts accumulated interaction evidence ($\text{Obs}_{1:t}$) into lasting behavioral change:
$(\pi_{\theta,t+1}, \pi_{S,t+1}) \leftarrow F_{\text{Evolve}}(\pi_{\theta,t}, \pi_{S,t}, \text{Obs}_{1:t})$.
Crucially, in agentic evolution, $F_{\text{Evolve}}$ is instantiated as an explicit "evolver agent"—a goal-directed, autonomous optimizer. This evolver agent proposes structured candidate updates ($\Delta_t$) and makes an explicit commit decision ($c_t \in \{0, 1\}$) via a "governance gate" ($C$), which typically involves automated verification (e.g., unit tests) or human-in-the-loop review. Updates are treated as conditional decisions, proposed, evaluated, and either committed or rejected.

**Three Core Principles of Agentic Evolution:**

1. **Goal-Oriented Principle (What to Change):** The evolver actively diagnoses deployment failures, attributes them to underlying causes (e.g., missing tools, brittle logic, interface mismatches), and targets specific components of the persistent artifact state ($\pi_S$) for modification. This aims to achieve durable performance gains rather than transient fixes.
2. **Autonomy Principle (When to Change):** The evolver governs the adaptation process itself. Instead of following a fixed update schedule, it selects relevant evidence, determines if a failure is actionable, and explicitly decides whether to commit or reject candidate updates. This allows evolution compute to be allocated strategically.
3. **Compositional Principle (How to Change):** Improvements are realized through structured, modular artifacts (e.g., executable tools, reusable workflows, validation tests). These artifacts are produced by decomposed decision processes within the evolver and integrated only after verification, allowing for amortization of reasoning into reusable capabilities.

**A-EVOLVE Framework:**
A-Evolve is a general, implementation-agnostic framework that instantiates these principles through three structural components:

1. **Persistent Artifact State ($\pi_S$):** Elevated to a first-class, editable interface to system capability. It is composed of:
 - **Knowledge Registry ($K_t$):** Stores structured or textual artifacts like schemas, workflows, interface contracts, and exemplars. Artifacts are addressable and versioned for targeted edits.
 - **Tool Registry ($T_t$):** Contains executable functions (scripts, API wrappers) with explicit input–output signatures and associated tests. Provides deterministic action primitives at solve-time and diagnostic instruments at evolve-time.
 - **Validation Registry ($V_t$):** Contains governance assets such as unit tests, regression suites, and human review hooks. Crucially, $V_t$ grounds the commit decision $c_t$, ensuring only verified updates are committed.

2. **Solve–Evolve Loop:** Explicitly separates task execution from capability improvement.
 - **Solve Phase:** For input $x_t$, the solver executes task $\tau_t = \text{Solve}(\pi_t, x_t)$. The artifact state $\pi_{S,t}$ is read-only.
 - **Evolve Phase:** Triggered asynchronously or when sufficient evidence accumulates. The evolver analyzes $\text{Obs}_{1:t}$ and proposes a conditional update $\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t \oplus (c_t \cdot \Delta_t)$. This separation ensures dedicated compute for each phase.

3. **The Evolver ($F_{\text{Evolve}}$):** Implemented as four cooperating functions:
 - **Diagnose($\text{Obs}_{1:t}, \pi_{S,t}$):** Identifies actionable failure modes and their causes, producing an update objective $g_t$. May invoke tools from $T_t$.
 - **Plan($g_t, \pi_{S,t}$):** Translates $g_t$ into an explicit edit plan specifying target artifacts, operators (add, patch, refactor, prune), and ordering.
 - **Update($p_t, \pi_{S,t}$):** Executes the plan, synthesizing concrete artifact changes and assembling a candidate update $\Delta_t$.
 - **Verify($\Delta_t, V_t$):** Evaluates $\Delta_t$ against $V_t$ and returns the commit decision $c_t \in \{0, 1\}$.

**Evolution-Scaling Hypothesis:**
The paper proposes that the capacity for adaptation—the achievable performance frontier ($P^*(\text{C}_{\text{evolve}}, \pi_0)$)—scales strictly with the compute allocated to the evolution process ($\text{C}_{\text{evolve}}$). More evolution-time compute is hypothesized to enable more accurate diagnosis, consideration of more candidate updates, synthesis of more robust artifacts, and stronger verification, leading to systematic performance improvement.

**Experimental Approach:**
The methodology is evaluated on the AppWorld benchmark, which features executable environments and unit tests for goal verification. Experiments compare A-Evolve against non-evolving baselines and non-agentic evolution methods (APE for prompt evolution, AWM for workflow memory). Ablation studies are conducted to assess the contribution of each A-Evolve component (Diagnose, Analysis tools, Plan, Verify). The evolution-scaling hypothesis is tested by varying evolution steps (as a proxy for $\text{C}_{\text{evolve}}$) and the size/capability of the evolver model. Metrics used are Task Goal Completion (TGC) and Average Passed Tests (APT), with fixed solve-time and evolve-time compute budgets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical studies focused on three key questions: the effectiveness of agentic evolution, the feasibility and reliability of its core components, and the validation of the evolution-scaling hypothesis.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness: Agentic vs. Non-agentic Evolution

- **Significant Performance Gains:** A-Evolve consistently demonstrated substantial improvements in task performance across various LLM solver backbones (Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4, Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5, Gemini 3 Flash). For instance, with Claude Haiku 4.5, A-Evolve achieved 64% Task Goal Completion (TGC) and 84.31% Average Passed Tests (APT), compared to 32% TGC and 51.16% APT for a Vanilla (non-evolving) solver, 30% TGC and 56.00% APT for APE (prompt evolution), and 46% TGC and 65.76% APT for AWM (workflow memory).
- **Capability Multiplier Effect:** The results indicated that agentic evolution can function as a "capability multiplier." Smaller solver models, when augmented with A-Evolve, could match or exceed the performance of larger vanilla models. For example, Claude Haiku 4.5 with A-Evolve achieved 64% TGC, which surpassed the 42% TGC of a vanilla Claude Sonnet 4. This suggests that persistent procedural refinement through evolution can rival gains typically achieved by simply scaling up the base model.
- **Qualitative Distinction in Adaptation:** Case studies revealed that non-agentic baselines like AWM primarily capture surface-level patterns and store raw, often irrelevant or hallucinated, textual trajectories. In contrast, A-Evolve performed active diagnosis to identify underlying failure causes, synthesizing insights into persistent, verified artifacts (tools, knowledge schemas) rather than raw text. This allowed A-Evolve to replace fragile, probabilistic regeneration with robust, governed capability invocation, leading to more efficient task completion (e.g., 8 steps for an A-Evolved agent vs. 29 steps for an AWM agent in a specific task).

#### 4.2. Feasibility: Reliability of Agentic Evolution Loop (Ablation Studies)

Ablation studies investigated the contribution of A-Evolve's core components (Diagnosis, Analysis tools, Planning, Verification) to overall performance and loop reliability.

- **Interdependence of Components:** The full A-Evolve framework achieved the best performance across solvers. Removing any single component led to performance degradation, although all ablated variants still outperformed the vanilla solver. This indicates that the modules play complementary roles and highlights the importance of their end-to-end coupling.
- **Critical Role of Verification:** The `Verify` component (A-Evolve/V) was identified as a critical stabilizer. Removing verification led to the most significant performance degradation among all ablations. Committing updates without validation allowed brittle or overfit patches to accumulate, causing regressions and unstable long-horizon behavior.
- **Specific Failure Modes:**
 - **Without Diagnosis (A-Evolve/D):** The evolver acted blindly, producing superficial patches rather than addressing root causes.
 - **Without Analysis Tools (A-Evolve/A):** Updates relied on single-trajectory inference, capturing local symptoms but missing systematic patterns across episodes.
 - **Without Planning (A-Evolve/P):** The evolver failed to coordinate interdependent edits, leading to incoherent updates (e.g., modifying tools without updating corresponding schemas).
 - **Without Verification (A-Evolve/V):** Defective artifacts (e.g., tools with syntax errors) were committed, polluting the context and degrading established capabilities.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Evolution Scaling

- **Scaling Evolution Compute (Evolution Steps):** Varying the number of evolution steps (a proxy for evolution-time compute, C_evolve) from 1 to 12 demonstrated a consistent trend. A-Evolve's performance (TGC and APT) improved monotonically as evolution compute increased, while the AWM baseline quickly plateaued. This provides empirical support for the evolution-scaling hypothesis, indicating that additional evolution compute translates into systematically higher attainable performance. The results also suggested high efficiency, with even minimal evolution steps yielding substantial gains.
- **Impact of Evolver Size:** Increasing the evolver model size (from Claude Haiku 4.5 to Sonnet 4.5 to Opus 4.5) also led to monotonically increasing TGC and APT. This confirmed that evolver capacity is a key contributor to C_evolve. Larger evolvers were qualitatively observed to produce higher-quality diagnoses that identified structural capability gaps, rather than superficial symptoms. They synthesized more robust, generalizable artifacts that passed verification more reliably (e.g., Sonnet 4.5 evolver had 2 verification failures vs. 8 for Haiku 4.5 evolver across 50 tasks), converting compute into durable capability more effectively and reducing optimization noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for "Agentic Evolution" that positions it as a path for the sustained and open-ended adaptation of Large Language Models (LLMs) in real-world, dynamic environments. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Addressing Fundamental Limitations of LLM Deployment

The primary significance lies in proposing a method to overcome the "train-deploy gap," a critical bottleneck for deploying intelligent systems in open-ended environments. By moving beyond static model capacity and fixed update rules, agentic evolution enables LLMs to autonomously adapt to unforeseen changes, thereby improving their reliability and longevity in practical applications. This paradigm shift suggests that the ability to evolve is as crucial as static model capability for real-world utility.

#### 5.2. Introducing a New Scaling Axis for AI Development

The "evolution-scaling hypothesis" introduces a third scaling axis—evolution-time compute—complementary to existing training-time and inference-time computation. This framework posits that systematically allocating resources to the evolution process can predictably increase the capacity for adaptation. This perspective offers a structured approach to engineering adaptation, suggesting that continuous investment in the evolver's capabilities can lead to sustained performance improvements over indefinite deployment horizons.

#### 5.3. Enhanced System Reliability and Governance

A-Evolve's emphasis on structured, modular artifacts (knowledge, tools, validation tests) and a governed update process (via a verify gate) provides several benefits. It allows for targeted, interpretable modifications rather than opaque weight updates, improving accountability and making system behavior easier to audit and debug. The explicit verification mechanism mitigates risks of introducing harmful or brittle behaviors, leading to more stable and reliable long-term deployment.

#### 5.4. Privacy and Computational Efficiency Benefits

By enabling local adaptation through persistent artifacts, agentic evolution can reduce the need for transmitting sensitive user data to central servers for global retraining, thereby enhancing user privacy and data sovereignty. Furthermore, the amortization of expensive inference-time reasoning into reusable, persistent tools and knowledge offers a more computationally sustainable path for intelligence in dynamic environments compared to repeatedly "thinking harder" for every new problem instance.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Agentic Systems

This work contributes to the broader research direction of "agentic AI" by formalizing the evolution process as an agentic decision-making problem. It provides a concrete framework (A-Evolve) that can serve as a starting point for developing more sophisticated self-evolving agent systems capable of higher levels of autonomy and self-improvement.

#### 5.6. Risks and Mitigations

The authors acknowledge inherent risks associated with empowering systems to modify their own behavior, such as potential for capability drift or optimization towards unaligned objectives. To mitigate these, the A-Evolve framework explicitly incorporates a validation gate, requiring automated verification (e.g., regression tests) before updates are committed. The focus on explicit, interpretable artifacts (code, tools, skills) further allows for human auditing and rollback, which is a key differentiator from opaque parameter updates. Continued refinement of these verification standards is proposed as crucial for ensuring safe and aligned autonomous evolution.

#### 5.7. Future Research Directions

The paper outlines three key areas for future work:
- **Benchmarks:** Development of new benchmarks that explicitly capture the train–deploy gap, featuring high-entropy, non-stationary environments, and metrics beyond task success that measure the durability and reuse of evolved artifacts.
- **Frameworks:** Further refinement and improvement of diagnosis, planning, and verification mechanisms within evolution frameworks to enhance the efficiency of converting evolution-time compute into stable improvements.
- **Theory:** Development of a foundational theory of agentic evolution, including formalizing evolution as optimization over combinatorial program spaces, establishing separation results for agentic versus non-agentic heuristics, and bounding regret relative to idealized fine-tuning.
