# RAT: Adversarial Attacks on Deep Reinforcement Agents for Targeted Behaviors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed RAT, a framework for targeted adversarial attacks on Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents, utilizing human preferences to define precise behavioral targets. This method achieves superior performance in manipulating DRL agents into specific, desired actions and contributes to enhancing agent robustness through adversarial training.

## Problem

- Existing adversarial attacks on DRL agents primarily focus on reducing cumulative rewards, lacking the precision to induce specific, targeted behaviors.
- Prior targeted attack methods are rigid, requiring explicit pre-definition of target states or policies, and often necessitate access to the victim's internal reward function.
- A universal, efficient, and flexible method was needed to induce complex, human-aligned behaviors in DRL agents for both vulnerability assessment and robustness improvement.

## Method

- RAT is introduced as a comprehensive framework, comprising an intention policy, an adversary, and a weighting function, all trained via a bi-level optimization scheme.
- Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) is leveraged to learn an intention policy from human preference labels, defining flexible and expressive target behaviors without reward engineering.
- An adversary is trained to apply small perturbations to observations, forcing the victim agent to mimic the intention policy, with a weighting function optimizing the attack by prioritizing significant states.

## Results

- RAT significantly outperformed existing evasion attack methods in Meta-world robotic tasks, consistently achieving higher attack success rates in inducing specific and opposite behaviors.
- The method successfully demonstrated the vulnerability of advanced offline DRL agents, such as Decision Transformers, by manipulating them into highly specific, human-preferred behaviors in MuJoCo locomotion tasks.
- RAT-based adversarial training methods (RAT-ATLA, RAT-WocaR) improved agent robustness against various attacks, outperforming existing methods and confirming RAT's effectiveness as a strong attacker even against these robust agents.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human preferences offer a flexible and expressive mechanism to define complex, targeted behaviors for adversarial attacks, overcoming limitations of rigid reward functions or predefined targets.
> - A bi-level optimization approach, coupling an intention policy with a state-weighting adversary, can efficiently achieve precise behavioral manipulation in DRL agents by mitigating distribution drift.
> - A powerful targeted adversary not only exposes vulnerabilities but also provides a crucial tool for adversarial training, thereby enhancing DRL agent robustness and contributing to AI safety.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), Adversarial Machine Learning, and AI Safety. DRL has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in mastering complex tasks across various domains, including robotics and gaming. However, a significant and growing body of research has highlighted the vulnerability of DRL agents to adversarial attacks, raising serious concerns for their deployment in safety-critical applications.

**Existing Adversarial Attacks on DRL:**
Historically, adversarial attacks on DRL agents have predominantly focused on *evasion attacks* that aim to reduce the agent's cumulative reward by perturbing state observations. Methods like FGSM, SA-MDP, SA-RL, and PA-AD fall into this category. While effective at degrading performance, these attacks are often too generic to precisely manipulate an agent into *specific, targeted behaviors*. Some prior work has explored "targeted attacks" to steer agents towards predefined states or mimic a target policy. However, these methods suffer from rigidity, requiring explicit pre-definition of target states or policies, and often necessitate access to the victim's training reward function, which is often impractical in real-world scenarios.

**The Gap RAT Addresses:**
The core limitation in the existing landscape is the absence of a universal, efficient, and flexible method for *targeted behavior attacks* that can induce specific, complex, and human-aligned behaviors in DRL agents. Traditional reward functions, while useful for task completion, are often insufficient to capture nuanced safety requirements or desired behavioral outcomes. For example, simply minimizing a robot's reward doesn't necessarily prevent it from performing a specific unsafe action, such as colliding with an obstacle, if the reward function doesn't explicitly penalize that exact behavior.

**Introduction of Human Preferences:**
This paper addresses this gap by introducing **Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL)** as a mechanism to define the *target behavior*. PbRL has emerged as a promising approach for aligning AI agents with human intentions without requiring explicit reward engineering. By leveraging human preferences, RAT can define a target behavior more expressively and flexibly than traditional methods.

**Robustness and AI Safety:**
Beyond just attacking, the work contributes to the broader field of DRL robustness. By developing a powerful targeted attack, RAT simultaneously provides a benchmark for evaluating the resilience of DRL agents and a tool for *adversarial training* to enhance their robustness. This dual contribution is vital for the development of trustworthy and safe AI systems, particularly in embodied AI and robotics, where unexpected or malicious behavior can have severe consequences. The paper explicitly states its motivation stemming from concerns in "high-risk or safety-critical situations," firmly placing it within the AI safety research paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel and effective method for conducting **universal, targeted behavior attacks** on Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Overcoming Limitations of Reward-Based Attacks:** Existing adversarial attacks on DRL agents primarily focus on reducing cumulative rewards. However, rewards are often too generic to effectively encode complex safety requirements or to precisely manipulate agents into *specific* desired (or undesired from a victim's perspective) behaviors. The authors highlight that solely reducing rewards can lead to suboptimal attack strategies in safety-critical scenarios where precise behavioral control is paramount.
2. **Addressing Rigidity of Prior Targeted Attacks:** Previous attempts at targeted attacks often relied on predefined target states or policies. This rigidity makes them inflexible and difficult to apply in diverse or complex scenarios where such precise definitions are challenging to provide. Furthermore, these methods frequently require access to the victim's internal training reward function, which is impractical in many real-world black-box attack scenarios.
3. **Enabling Expressive Behavior Manipulation:** The authors are motivated to create an adversary capable of inducing *complex, human-aligned behaviors* rather than just general performance degradation. This necessitates a more expressive target for the adversary, which they propose to achieve through human preferences.
4. **Assessing and Enhancing DRL Robustness:** By developing a "universal and strong adversary," the research aims to provide a critical tool for evaluating the robustness of DRL agents against sophisticated manipulations. A secondary, but equally important, motivation is to leverage this strong adversary for *adversarial training* to develop more resilient DRL policies, thereby contributing directly to AI safety and reliability.
5. **Exposing Vulnerabilities in Advanced DRL Models:** The motivation extends to demonstrating the vulnerability of advanced DRL architectures, including offline RL agents like Decision Transformers, which are increasingly powerful and widely deployed. This helps in understanding the security landscape of cutting-edge AI.

In essence, the paper seeks to shift the paradigm of adversarial attacks in DRL from generic performance degradation to precise, intention-driven behavioral control, thereby serving both offensive (attack) and defensive (robustness improvement) goals in the pursuit of safer and more reliable AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **RAT (AdveRsarial Attacks against DRL agents for Targeted behaviors)**, is a comprehensive framework designed for universal, targeted behavior attacks. It comprises three synergistically trained components: an intention policy, an adversary, and a weighting function, all orchestrated within a bi-level optimization scheme.

#### 3.1. Learning the Intention Policy (πθ)

The core idea for achieving "targeted behaviors" is to define these targets using human preferences, thereby avoiding rigid, pre-defined objectives or reliance on the victim's reward function.

- **Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL):** RAT leverages PbRL to train an *intention policy* (πθ) that explicitly aligns with human preferences. This process eliminates the need for manual reward engineering.
- **Reward Model (brψ):** Instead of a ground-truth reward, a reward model (brψ) is learned from human preference labels. Humans provide feedback by comparing pairs of agent trajectories (segments σ0, σ1) and indicating which is preferred.
- **Bradley-Terry Model:** A preference predictor based on the Bradley-Terry model (Equation 1) determines the probability of one segment being preferred over another, proportional to its exponential return.
- **Cross-Entropy Loss:** The reward model (brψ) is optimized using a cross-entropy loss (Equation 2) to align its predictions with human preferences.
- **Policy Optimization (SAC):** Once the reward model is trained, the intention policy (πθ) is optimized using a standard DRL algorithm, specifically Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) (Haarnoja et al. 2018). The Q-function (Qϕ) is optimized to minimize the Bellman residual (Equation 3) using rewards provided by brψ, and the policy (πθ) is updated to minimize a policy loss (Equation 4).
- **Flexible Behavioral Target:** This intention policy (πθ) serves as a dynamic, flexible, and adaptive behavioral target that the adversary aims to make the victim mimic.

#### 3.2. Learning the Adversary (πα) and Weighting Function (hω)

The adversary's goal is to manipulate the victim agent's policy (πν) to behave like the intention policy (πθ) by applying small perturbations to its observations. A weighting function is introduced to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of this manipulation.

- **Threat Model:** The adversary (πα) perturbs the observed state *s* into *˜s* = *s* + ∆ * ϵ, where ∆ is a Gaussian noise with ℓ∞(∆) < 1, and ϵ is a small, bounded attack budget (L∞ norm). The victim then acts based on *˜s*, while the true environment state remains unchanged. This setup is formulated as a Rewarded State-Adversarial Markov Decision Process (RSA-MDP).
- **Bi-level Optimization:** The training of the adversary (πα) and the weighting function (hω) is formulated as a bi-level optimization problem (Equation 5):
 - **Inner Loop (Adversary πα):** Given the intention policy (πθ) and weighting function (hω), the adversary's parameters (α) are optimized to minimize the *re-weighted Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence* between the perturbed victim policy (πν∘α) and the intention policy (πθ) (Equation 6). This makes the victim's behavior align with the target intention. The weighting function hω(s) assigns importance weights to states, reflecting their significance for the attack.
 - **Outer Loop (Weighting Function hω):** The weighting function's parameters (ω) are optimized to improve the adversary's performance. This is done by minimizing a meta-level validation loss (Jπ) (Equation 7), which measures the performance of the perturbed policy (πν∘α) with respect to the learned Q-function of the intention policy. This effectively identifies crucial states that, when re-weighted, enhance the adversary's ability to manipulate the victim.
- **Practical Implementation:** A one-step gradient update approximates the inner loop optimization (Equation 8), and the gradient of the outer loss with respect to ω is computed using the chain rule (Equation 9), enabling efficient training.
- **Combined Behavior Policy (for Data Collection):** To mitigate distribution shift between the intention policy and the perturbed policy, and to ensure effective data collection for training, a "combined behavior policy" is used (Appendix A.1). This policy switches between the perturbed policy (πν∘α) and the intention policy (πθ) at a randomly sampled point within an episode, balancing exploration and exploitation.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides theoretical guarantees for RAT's convergence. Theorem 4.1 demonstrates that the gradient of the outer loss converges to zero at a rate of O(1/√T), ensuring that the weighting function learns optimally. Theorem 4.2 proves that the inner loss also converges to critical points, ensuring the adversary's parameters converge toward optimal manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of RAT across various robotic simulation tasks in Meta-world and continuous locomotion tasks in MuJoCo demonstrates its superior performance and versatility.

1. **Universal Targeted Behavior Attack on DRL Agents (Meta-world):**
 - RAT significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art evasion attack methods (SA-RL, PA-AD, and a Random baseline) in inducing specific, human-desired behaviors.
 - **Manipulation Scenario:** In tasks where the goal was to make a robot grasp objects at distant, non-target locations, RAT consistently achieved higher attack success rates. Even when baselines were provided with *ground-truth rewards* (oracle versions), RAT often surpassed their performance, highlighting its ability to learn effective adversaries based purely on human preferences. PA-AD struggled notably in these tasks.
 - **Opposite Behaviors Scenario:** In tasks requiring the victim agent to perform the *opposite* of its trained skill (e.g., closing a window instead of opening it), RAT again demonstrated exceptional performance and clear advantages over all baselines. This confirms RAT's effectiveness across a broad spectrum of tasks and its capability to efficiently learn adversaries aligned with human preferences.
 - The low success rates of SA-RL and PA-AD in some tasks are attributed to *distribution drift*, which RAT mitigates through its intention policy and weighting function.

2. **Manipulation on Sequence Model Agents (Decision Transformer, MuJoCo):**
 - RAT successfully demonstrated the vulnerability of advanced *offline Reinforcement Learning* agents, specifically Decision Transformers.
 - On MuJoCo tasks (Half Cheetah, Walker), RAT manipulated these agents into executing highly specific, human-preferred, and often unusual behaviors (e.g., a Cheetah running backward or performing a 90-degree push-up; a Walker standing on one foot or dancing).
 - These results emphasize that RAT is effective against diverse DRL architectures and highlights significant vulnerabilities in embodied AI systems, pushing for further research into their robustness.

3. **Robust Agents Training and Evaluation:**
 - RAT's utility extends beyond mere attack; it can be used to *enhance agent robustness* through adversarial training.
 - Two new robust training methods were introduced: **RAT-ATLA** (alternately training an agent and a RAT attacker) and **RAT-WocaR** (estimating and minimizing the reward of the intention policy without explicit attacker learning).
 - Both RAT-ATLA and RAT-WocaR significantly improved agent robustness against various attack methods, outperforming existing methods like PA-ATLA and ATLA-PPO in several tasks.
 - Crucially, RAT itself was shown to be a stronger attacker even against these newly robust agents, underscoring its general effectiveness in challenging environments.

4. **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Contribution of Each Component:** The *intention policy (πθ)* was identified as a pivotal component, significantly boosting the attack success rate by mitigating distribution drift between the victim's behavior and the desired target behavior. The *weighting function (hω)* also contributed positively, and the "combined behavior policy" was shown to be important.
 - **Effects of the Weighting Function:** Visualization using t-SNE demonstrated that the weighting function effectively differentiates trajectories based on their quality, assigning higher weights to more successful trajectories, thereby optimizing the adversary's learning.
 - **Impact of Feedback Amount:** Increasing the number of human preference labels generally improved RAT's performance, leading to stronger adversaries and more stable attack success rates, affirming the importance of adequate feedback.
 - **Impact of Attack Budgets:** As expected, the performance of all methods, including RAT, improved with an increase in the adversarial budget (ϵ), indicating that greater perturbation allowance generally leads to more effective attacks.
 - **Quality of Learned Reward Functions:** The learned reward functions from human preferences were found to align well with the true reward functions across tasks, validating the efficacy of the PbRL component in capturing human intentions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "RAT: Adversarial Attacks on Deep Reinforcement Agents for Targeted Behaviors" carries substantial significance and potential impact across several domains:

1. **Advancement in Adversarial DRL and AI Safety:** RAT introduces a novel and more sophisticated form of adversarial attack, moving beyond generic reward reduction to *targeted behavioral manipulation*. This is a critical development for AI safety because real-world threats often involve inducing specific unsafe or undesirable actions rather than just degrading overall performance. By providing a tool to precisely control an agent's behavior according to human preferences (or an attacker's nefarious goals), RAT helps illuminate deeper vulnerabilities in DRL systems, especially those deployed in safety-critical applications like autonomous driving, medical robotics, or industrial control.

2. **Enhanced DRL Robustness and Defense Mechanisms:** The paper demonstrates that a strong attack framework like RAT can also be instrumental in building more robust DRL agents. By integrating RAT into adversarial training regimens (RAT-ATLA, RAT-WocaR), the authors show that policies can be made more resilient against a wider range of attacks. This dual-use nature – acting as both a powerful attacker and a defense enhancer – is invaluable for iteratively improving the trustworthiness and reliability of AI systems. Understanding how to target specific behaviors allows for more focused and effective defensive strategies.

3. **Broader Applicability to Advanced AI Models:** The successful attack on Decision Transformer agents, prominent in offline RL and capable of sequence modeling, highlights the widespread vulnerability of even cutting-edge DRL architectures. As AI systems become more complex and multimodal (e.g., LLM-based agents, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models), the ability to craft targeted behavioral attacks against them becomes increasingly important for identifying and mitigating new security risks. The paper explicitly points to this as a future direction, underscoring the foresight of the proposed methodology.

4. **Improved Alignment with Human Intentions:** The integration of Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) is a significant methodological contribution. It allows for the definition of targeted behaviors in a flexible, expressive manner that aligns with complex human preferences, without the need for difficult and often incomplete reward engineering. This approach has implications not just for adversarial attacks but also for general agent design, promoting AI systems that are more genuinely aligned with human values and safety constraints.

5. **Catalyst for Future Research:** By demonstrating effective targeted behavioral attacks and their application in robustness training, RAT is expected to spur further research in several key areas:
 - **More Sophisticated Attack Vectors:** Exploring other perturbation types or attack surfaces beyond state observations.
 - **Proactive Defense Strategies:** Developing methods that can predict and preemptively defend against targeted attacks.
 - **Formal Verification for Behavioral Robustness:** Leveraging targeted attacks to inform the development of formal methods to guarantee certain behavioral properties of DRL agents.
 - **Human-in-the-Loop AI Safety:** Improving how human preferences can be leveraged more efficiently for both attack definition and defense.

In conclusion, RAT provides a crucial missing piece in the adversarial DRL puzzle, allowing researchers and practitioners to define and execute targeted behavioral manipulations. This capability is not merely an academic exercise but a vital step towards ensuring the safety, reliability, and ethical deployment of DRL and other advanced AI systems in an increasingly complex world.
