# Can Causal Models Enhance Robot Navigation? Online Causal Adaptation for Real-Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Chalmers University of Technology integrated a pre-trained causal model into a real robot's navigation stack, enabling online adaptation that improved navigation performance in complex obstacle avoidance and cornering scenarios and demonstrated strong agreement between model-predicted competence and human perception.

## Problem

- Conventional robot behavior generation methods (hand-coded or learned) often lack transparency, making decision-making difficult to interpret and hindering self-adaptation.
- Limited practical exploration of integrating causal models with existing robot systems for real-world navigation tasks.
- Uncertainty about whether causal components offer measurable benefits, can integrate with existing control policies, or be reused across different contexts without retraining.

## Method

- A pre-trained, simplified ==Causal Bayesian Network (CBN)== model, originally developed for social navigation, was integrated into a physical PAL TIAGo robot's ==ROS2 nav2== stack.
- The causal model was employed in two modes: ==offline evaluation== to assess the competence of recorded trajectories and ==online adaptation== to actively intervene and suggest alternative velocity commands.
- Online adaptation utilized ==counterfactual reasoning== to identify actions leading to higher predicted competence when the default navigation's competence fell below a ==competence threshold==.

## Results

- Offline evaluation revealed a strong positive correlation (**Spearman's ρ = 0.80**) between predicted competence and **Goal-directed Progress**, and substantial to near-perfect agreement (**Cohen's κ = 0.88**) with human annotations.
- Online adaptation in obstacle scenarios showed superior performance for the causal-adapted method, yielding higher **Goal-directed Progress (0.976 vs. 0.870)** and significantly lower **Path Irregularity (0.054 vs. 1.414)** compared to the baseline.
- In cornering scenarios, the causal method demonstrated improved **Goal-directed Progress (0.940 vs. 0.724)** and reduced **Stop Ratio (0.107 vs. 0.474)**, achieving substantially higher predicted competence (**65.22% vs. 25.0%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Causal models, even when adapted from simulated social navigation, can successfully transfer to and enhance general real-world robot navigation tasks without requiring retraining.
> - The model's 'competence' prediction strongly correlates with quantitative navigation performance metrics and shows high agreement with human perception of robot navigation quality.
> - Causal adaptation measurably enhances navigation performance, particularly in complex scenarios like obstacle avoidance and cornering, acting as an enhancement layer for existing navigation systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robot behaviors are often generated through either hand-coded policies, which implicitly incorporate expert knowledge about causal structures, or policies learned from examples, typically via associative learning. While both approaches enable robot functionality, they frequently result in decision-making processes that are difficult to interpret, either by external observers (in the case of black-box models) or by the robot itself (limiting introspection and self-adaptation).

Causal representation and reasoning offer an alternative by guiding robot behavior with explicit causal models, which can be either manually specified or learned. Historically, causal models in robotics have been employed for failure analysis and correction across various levels of the robot decision-making hierarchy, including robot configuration parameters, components, plans, individual skills, and long-horizon tasks. Recent advancements have also demonstrated the utility of causal models in human-centered and multi-agent scenarios, where they can lead to more understandable and modifiable behaviors through causal interventions and counterfactual reasoning.

Despite these developments, the deployment of causal models with existing robot systems in real environments, particularly for navigation, remains an area with limited exploration. Studies in human-centered robotics using causal models often do not examine their performance under reduced conditions, where some causal variables might be less critical. This gap raises fundamental questions about whether causal components offer measurable benefits over established robot systems, if they can be integrated with existing control policies rather than replacing them, and whether causal models developed for specific contexts can be reused in other settings without requiring retraining.

This paper addresses these questions by focusing on robot navigation, investigating the practical integration of a pre-trained causal model into a real-robot system. The work differentiates itself from much of the related literature, which often focuses on causal inference during learning or offline decision selection, primarily in simulation, and typically does not explicitly connect human perception or social competence with online robot behavior adaptation. Existing work in causal reasoning for behavior adaptation often pertains to manipulation tasks or failure correction rather than continuous navigation. While human perception of robot navigation has been studied as an evaluation factor, it is rarely utilized to directly guide online adaptation of navigation behaviors. This research therefore contributes to bridging the gap between theoretical causal modeling and its practical, online application in real-world robot navigation, particularly concerning human-perceived competence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent limitations of conventional robot behavior generation methods. Both hand-coded and learned policies often lack transparency, making it challenging to understand the underlying rationale for a robot's decisions or for the robot itself to adapt its behavior through introspection. This opacity hinders the development of more flexible, robust, and trustworthy autonomous systems.

Causal models are proposed as a solution to these issues, as they enable robots to predict the consequences of their actions through explicit causal structures. This capability promises to yield more interpretable and adaptable robot behaviors. However, the practical challenge lies in integrating these causal models with existing robot systems, such as navigation stacks, and validating their performance in real-world environments.

Against this background, the paper outlines several key objectives and research questions:

1. **Assessing Measurable Benefits:** To investigate whether integrating causal-based components can provide tangible benefits over standard, well-established robot navigation systems, which often perform adequately in many scenarios.
2. **Integration vs. Replacement:** To determine if causal models can be effectively combined with existing robot control policies to enhance their performance, rather than necessitating a complete replacement of the default navigation frameworks. This objective explores a modular and incremental approach to robotic intelligence.
3. **Model Reusability and Transferability:** To examine whether causal models, specifically those developed and trained in one context (e.g., social navigation in simulation), can be successfully reused in different real-robot settings without requiring extensive retraining. This addresses the efficiency and generalizability of causal modeling approaches.

To address these objectives, the authors specifically aim to study whether a robot can genuinely benefit from incorporating a causal model into its decision-making process to improve navigation task performance. The investigation is conducted through a two-pronged approach:
- **Offline Evaluation:** Utilizing the causal model as an evaluation module to assess the competence of recorded real-robot navigation trajectories and correlate this prediction with quantitative navigation performance metrics and human annotations.
- **Online Adaptation:** Employing the causal model as an online adaptation module that actively monitors the predicted competence of the robot's default navigation behavior and intervenes by suggesting alternative velocity commands when the predicted competence falls below a defined threshold.

The overarching goal is to demonstrate that causal models, particularly those developed for behavioral interpretation, can be successfully integrated into and enhance real-robot navigation systems, offering a path towards more interpretable and flexible robotic autonomy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the utility of a pre-trained causal model for enhancing robot navigation in real-world scenarios. The core approach involves integrating a causal model, originally developed for social navigation, into a physical robot's navigation stack, testing its capabilities in both offline evaluation and online adaptation modes.

**Causal Navigation Model:**
The work utilizes a simplified Causal Bayesian Network (CBN) model, derived from a more comprehensive social navigation model previously described in. This CBN represents knowledge about robot behavior and human perception using a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG).
- **Variables:** The model incorporates five observation variables describing robot motion over a fixed temporal window: robot-goal distance change (D), heading error change (H), accumulated rotation (R), initial orientation (O), and human pose change (∆). Additionally, it includes two human perception variables: human-perceived robot intention (I) and robot competence (C).
- **Simplification:** For this study, the variables ∆ (human pose change) and I (human-perceived intention) are omitted from the model as the navigation scenarios do not explicitly involve human interaction, focusing solely on the robot's perceived competence (C). The removal of these child nodes does not affect the conditional probability tables of the remaining parent nodes.
- **Training:** The model was initially trained using trajectory data collected in a virtual environment with a simulated robot. Trajectory data were discretized using K-means clustering, where cluster centroids represent characteristic motion patterns.

**Causality-Based Offline Evaluation Module:**
This module is designed to assess the quality of recorded robot navigation trajectories.
- **Trajectory Processing:** Collected trajectories are divided into temporal windows of 8 seconds each. Within each window, robot motion is uniformly sampled into 40 states, consisting of the robot's pose (position and orientation) and velocity. This temporal resolution aligns with the causal model's design and training data.
- **Navigation Metrics:** Each trajectory segment is evaluated using four quantitative social navigation metrics:
 - **Path Irregularity (PI):** Measures accumulated excess rotation beyond the minimum required, normalized by path length, indicating trajectory smoothness.
 - **Stop Ratio (SR):** The fraction of time steps where the robot remains approximately static, capturing halting or hesitation. A threshold of 0.006m for step length differentiates stationary behavior from motion.
 - **Goal-directed Progress (GP):** The fraction of time steps where the distance to the goal decreases, evaluating progress towards the objective.
 - **Path Efficiency (PE):** The ratio of the straight-line distance between initial and final robot positions to the actual path length, indicating trajectory directness.
- **Human-Model Competence Agreement:** To validate the model's competence predictions, a subset of trajectory segments is manually annotated by humans for perceived competence. The model's binary competence labels (derived by thresholding its predicted competence probability P_M(C|T^(k)_i) > τ) are then compared with human annotations using Cohen's kappa coefficient (κ) to measure agreement.
- **Correlation Analysis:** Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) is calculated between the model-predicted competence probability and the quantitative trajectory metrics to assess the monotonic association.

**Causality-Based Online Adaptation Module:**
This module enables the causal model to actively guide the robot's navigation behavior in real-time.
- **Intervention Mechanism:** The module integrates with the robot's default navigation component (ROS2 nav2). It continuously monitors the action (velocity) proposed by the default navigation.
- **Counterfactual Reasoning:** If the causal model predicts that the default action's competence P_M(C|s_t, do(˜a_t)) falls below a predefined threshold τ, the module initiates a contrastive search. This search uses counterfactual reasoning on the causal model to identify an alternative action (velocity a*_t) that would lead to a higher predicted perceived competence.
- **Action Execution:** The causally-derived velocity a*_t is applied to the robot for a short duration (1 second) before a new prediction is made.
- **Safety Constraints:** The linear and angular velocities generated by the causal model are constrained within [-0.1m/s, 0.1m/s] and [-0.1rad/s, 0.1rad/s] respectively, to prevent abrupt motions and potential collisions.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Robot Platform:** A PAL TIAGo robot, equipped with an omnidirectional mobile base, was used. For consistency with common scenarios, motions were simulated to emulate a differential-drive platform.
- **Environment:** Experiments were conducted in a physical research lab and surrounding corridors, using a pre-defined map.
- **Scenarios:**
 - **Offline Evaluation:** 60 goal-following trajectories were collected using the default nav2 stack, equally divided into: (i) straight-line motion without obstacles, (ii) turning around a corner, and (iii) motion involving obstacles (including temporary human obstruction during data collection, although humans were not explicitly modeled in the simplified causal model for adaptation).
 - **Online Adaptation:** 30 navigation trajectories were collected, 10 for each scenario type: straight-line, cornering, and obstacle avoidance. A baseline was established by running the robot through the same scenarios using only the default nav2 stack.
- **Parameters:** A window duration of 8 seconds with 40 samples per window was used. For online adaptation, a competence threshold (τ) of 0.5 was selected for causal interventions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the causal navigation model encompassed both offline trajectory analysis and online navigation adaptation experiments.

**A. Offline Trajectory Evaluation**

**a) Correlation Analysis between Predicted Competence and Social Navigation Metrics:**
Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) was calculated between the model-predicted competence probability and the defined social navigation metrics (Goal-directed Progress, Path Efficiency, Stop Ratio, Path Irregularity).
- **Positive Behaviors:** Metrics indicating positive navigation behavior, such as Goal-directed Progress (GP) and Path Efficiency (PE), showed positive correlations with predicted competence. Specifically, GP had a strong increasing correlation (ρ = 0.80), and PE exhibited a moderate increasing correlation (ρ = 0.60). This suggests the model is capable of identifying situations where the robot is effectively moving towards its goal.
- **Negative Behaviors:** Metrics associated with suboptimal behavior, namely Stop Ratio (SR) and Path Irregularity (PI), demonstrated negative correlations. SR had a strong decreasing correlation (ρ = -0.84), indicating that higher competence predictions correspond to less stopping behavior. PI showed a weak decreasing correlation (ρ = -0.32), implying that higher competence is associated with smoother, less irregular paths.
These results indicate that the causal model's competence prediction aligns with quantitative measures of navigation performance, effectively differentiating between competent and suboptimal robot motions.

**b) Human-Model Competence Agreement:**
The agreement between the model-predicted competence and human annotations was assessed using Cohen's kappa coefficient (κ) across various probability thresholds (τ).
- **Overall Agreement:** The study observed substantial to near-perfect agreement, with κ = 0.88 at a threshold of τ = 0.6 for all trajectory types. This indicates a strong consistency between the probabilistic output of the model and human perception of robot navigation competence.
- **Scenario-Specific Agreement:**
 - For trajectories without obstacles (straight-line), agreement remained high across thresholds, reaching κ = 1.00 at τ = 0.6.
 - For obstacle and corner scenarios, agreement was high at moderate thresholds (e.g., τ = 0.5 and τ = 0.6). However, for corner trajectories, a sharper degradation in agreement was observed at higher thresholds. This degradation is attributed to the increased complexity of cornering, which often necessitates larger orientation adjustments and occasional halts, leading to lower predicted competence by the model even if the human perceives the overall navigation as competent.
The findings suggest that the learned competence representation in the causal model is consistent with human judgment across diverse navigation contexts, and provide validated thresholds for online adaptation.

**B. Online Navigation Adaptation**

**a) Quantitative Evaluation:**
The causal-adapted method was compared against the default nav2 baseline in 30 online navigation trajectories (10 per scenario type), using identical starting and goal poses. The competence threshold for causal intervention was set at τ = 0.5.
- **Frequency of Causal Model Application:** The causal model intervened more frequently in complex scenarios. On average, it was active 9.6 times per trajectory in cornering scenarios and 9.3 times per trajectory in obstacle scenarios, compared to 4.9 times per trajectory in straight-line scenarios (Table IV). This suggests the model identified more opportunities for improvement in complex situations.
- **Performance Comparison (Table V):**
 - **Obstacle Scenario:** The causal-adapted method consistently outperformed the baseline across all metrics. It yielded higher Goal-directed Progress (0.976 vs. 0.870), higher Path Efficiency (0.966 vs. 0.869), lower Stop Ratio (0.077 vs. 0.118), and significantly lower Path Irregularity (0.054 vs. 1.414). The predicted competence (P_c) was also higher (87.88% vs. 74.07%).
 - **Corner Scenario:** The causal method demonstrated improvements in GP (0.940 vs. 0.724), SR (0.107 vs. 0.474), and PI (0.142 vs. 0.0), indicating more stable and less hesitant behavior. However, it showed a slight reduction in Path Efficiency (0.876 vs. 0.972). The predicted competence was substantially higher (65.22% vs. 25.0%).
 - **Straight-line Scenario:** In this simpler scenario, the causal method resulted in a slight degradation of performance compared to the baseline, which already performed near-optimally. GP was slightly lower (0.991 vs. 0.992), PE was slightly lower (0.979 vs. 0.981), and SR and PI increased. The predicted competence was lower (81.48% vs. 92.85%).
These results indicate that causal adaptation is particularly effective in enhancing navigation performance in complex scenarios, such as obstacle avoidance and cornering, where the default navigation system might struggle. In simpler scenarios where the baseline performs optimally, the causal adaptation provided limited benefit and could even introduce minor degradations.

**b) Qualitative Observations:**
The online experiments confirmed the potential for social causal models to augment existing robot navigation frameworks, suggesting an enhancement rather than replacement strategy for complex or dynamic situations. However, limitations were also observed; in some instances, the causal model-driven navigation led to behaviors deemed unsafe, such as moving too close to walls. This is attributed to the model's current lack of explicit encoding for environmental variables (e.g., obstacles or free space), a factor that could be addressed in future work by integrating safety filters based on environmental information like costmaps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates the practical utility of integrating causal models into real-robot navigation systems, addressing several key challenges in autonomous robotics.

Firstly, the study provides empirical evidence that a causal model, initially developed for assessing human-perceived competence in simulated social navigation, can be successfully repurposed and deployed in a physical robot for general navigation tasks without requiring retraining. This highlights the **reusability and transferability** potential of such models, suggesting that causal representations can generalize across different deployment contexts and reduce the need for extensive re-training or redesign.

Secondly, the work establishes that "competence," as predicted by the causal model, serves as a **meaningful proxy for evaluating and improving a robot's navigation performance**. The strong positive correlation with metrics like goal-directed progress and path efficiency, and negative correlation with path irregularity and stop ratio, validates its alignment with quantitative measures of effective navigation. Furthermore, the high agreement with human annotations confirms that the model's internal representation of competence is consistent with human perception, which is crucial for human-robot interaction and trust.

Thirdly, the research demonstrates that **causal models can measurably enhance navigation performance**, particularly in complex scenarios such as cornering and obstacle avoidance. The online adaptation module's interventions led to superior navigation metrics and higher predicted competence compared to the default ROS2 navigation stack in these challenging situations. This suggests that causal adaptation can provide a valuable layer of intelligence to existing, well-established navigation frameworks, enabling more robust and adaptable behaviors where traditional systems may fall short. In simpler, near-optimal scenarios, while the benefits were limited, the approach offers a path for targeted improvements where they are most needed.

Fourthly, this work proposes a **framework for enhancing, rather than replacing, existing robot navigation systems**. By utilizing the causal model as an online adaptation module that intervenes only when predicted competence is low, the robot can leverage the strengths of both traditional reactive navigation and introspective causal reasoning. This hybrid approach offers a practical pathway towards more flexible and interpretable robot behaviors without discarding years of development in classical navigation stacks.

Finally, the study opens several avenues for future research, including investigating the impact of robot embodiment on perceived competence through large-scale user studies, exploring automated causal model learning techniques to identify relevant variables, and enriching causal models with environmental information (e.g., static and dynamic obstacles) to address safety concerns and expand applicability to more complex everyday scenarios. The findings also encourage comparisons with other navigation methods, such as learning- or optimization-based approaches, to further benchmark the efficacy of causal adaptation.
