# Now You See That: Learning End-to-End Humanoid Locomotion from Raw Pixels

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed an end-to-end framework enabling humanoid robots to achieve robust locomotion across diverse terrains using raw depth images. The approach, which includes a comprehensive depth sensor simulation and vision-aware distillation, attained a 98.9% success rate on the RDT-Bench benchmark and 97.8% in real-world deployments on two different humanoid platforms, notably traversing extended staircases.

## Problem

- The significant sim-to-real gap in vision-based humanoid locomotion, where complex real-world depth sensor noise and imperfections are not accurately modeled in simulation.
- The difficulty of training a single, unified control policy for humanoid robots to adapt robustly to a wide array of heterogeneous terrains (e.g., stairs, gaps, platforms) due to conflicting learning objectives.
- Limitations of existing LiDAR-based methods in terms of sensor frequency, latency, and susceptibility to odometry drift, which hinder dynamic maneuvers.

## Method

- A comprehensive, high-fidelity depth sensor simulation pipeline that synthesizes realistic stereo camera imperfections, including fusion artifacts, depth-dependent noise, optical distortions, and calibration uncertainties.
- A two-stage training approach combining a privileged policy (trained with idealized height scans) and a deployment policy (trained with augmented depth images) via vision-aware behavior distillation.
- A multi-critic and multi-discriminator reinforcement learning architecture with terrain-specific reward shaping to enable a single policy to master diverse locomotion behaviors for different terrain types.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved a **98.9%** average success rate on the RDT-Bench simulation benchmark, outperforming all baselines with a **27.9%** higher success rate than prior work.
- In real-world deployment, the policy demonstrated an overall success rate of **97.8%** (88/90 trials) on a full-sized humanoid robot, including **100%** success on extended staircases and wide gaps without real-world fine-tuning.
- Preliminary cross-platform validation on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot showed a **100%** success rate on the stair ascending task, indicating generalizability across different hardware configurations and depth cameras.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Realistic depth sensor simulation, particularly stereo fusion artifacts and depth-dependent noise, is critical for bridging the sim-to-real gap and achieving robust vision-based locomotion.
> - A multi-critic and multi-discriminator architecture significantly improves the ability of a single policy to learn and adapt to diverse terrain types by providing stable, terrain-specific policy gradients.
> - Vision-aware behavior distillation, incorporating denoising and feature regularization objectives, is essential for transferring knowledge from privileged observations to noisy raw depth images effectively.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied intelligence aims to develop autonomous robots capable of navigating and interacting with diverse environments. A prominent challenge within this domain is achieving robust locomotion for humanoid robots, particularly when relying on vision-based perception. Unlike quadrupedal robots, which benefit from inherent static stability and can tolerate moderate control errors, humanoid robots operate within inherently unstable regimes. This characteristic demands precise and synchronized coordination between their perception and action systems.

Current approaches to legged locomotion typically fall into two categories: LiDAR-based and vision-based methods. LiDAR-based systems often construct elevation maps by fusing height measurements with odometry data. While successful in both quadrupedal and humanoid platforms, these methods are limited by sensor frequency, inherent latency, and the susceptibility to odometry drift, which can lead to cumulative errors and affect footholds, particularly during dynamic maneuvers.

Vision-based methods, utilizing depth cameras, offer higher-bandwidth ego-centric perception. However, their application to humanoid platforms has been restricted by the "sim-to-real gap." This gap arises from the inability of traditional simulation environments to accurately model the complex noise patterns, artifacts, and calibration uncertainties present in real-world depth sensors, leading to degraded performance upon hardware deployment.

Beyond perception, training a single, unified control policy that can adapt to a wide array of heterogeneous terrains—ranging from high platforms and wide gaps to continuous staircases—remains a complex problem. Standard reinforcement learning techniques often struggle to reconcile the diverse reward landscapes and distinct dynamic requirements of different terrain types, sometimes necessitating the training of multiple specialized policies, which increases developmental overhead.

This research aims to advance the state of vision-based humanoid locomotion by directly addressing these two core challenges: mitigating the sim-to-real gap in perception and enabling a single policy to master diverse terrain scenarios. The authors position their work as an end-to-end vision-based framework that integrates comprehensive noise modeling, supports long-term deployment, facilitates fine-grained locomotion, and enables extreme parkour, distinguishing it from prior methods that may lack one or more of these capabilities, as highlighted in the paper's comparative analysis (Table I).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end framework that enables humanoid robots to achieve robust, vision-driven locomotion across diverse and challenging terrains directly from raw depth images. This objective is driven by two fundamental issues identified in current humanoid locomotion research:

1. **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap and Perception Noise:** Real-world depth sensors are subject to various imperfections, including stereo matching artifacts, noise profiles that vary with depth, optical distortions, and calibration uncertainties. Existing simulation models frequently oversimplify these factors, resulting in a significant discrepancy between simulated and real-world sensor data. This "sim-to-real gap" negatively impacts the performance of policies, especially for fine-grained locomotion tasks where centimeter-level accuracy is required for stable bipedal movement. The motivation is to create a simulation environment that realistically replicates these sensor imperfections, thereby improving the transferability of policies from simulation to physical robots and ensuring robust performance on fine-grained tasks.

2. **Enabling a Unified Policy for Diverse Terrain Adaptation:** Training a single locomotion policy to handle a broad spectrum of terrains, from extreme obstacles such as high platforms and wide gaps to precise challenges like ascending and descending long staircases, is inherently difficult. This difficulty stems from the conflicting learning objectives associated with different terrain types; for instance, the optimal gait and reward structure for climbing stairs differs significantly from that for dynamically crossing a gap. The motivation is to overcome these conflicting objectives to develop a single, unified policy capable of autonomously adapting its behavior across heterogeneous environments without the need for manual mode switching or the compilation of multiple specialist policies.

To achieve these objectives, the research introduces several specific contributions:
- **Realistic Depth Sensor Simulation:** The development of a high-fidelity depth sensor simulation pipeline is proposed. This pipeline is designed to capture and reproduce realistic stereo camera imperfections, including matching artifacts and calibration uncertainties, thereby narrowing the perception gap between simulation and the real world.
- **Vision-Aware Behavior Distillation:** A distillation framework is designed to transfer locomotion knowledge effectively. This framework combines latent space alignment with noise-invariant auxiliary tasks, facilitating the transfer of learned behaviors from a privileged policy (trained with ideal height map observations) to a deployment policy that operates on noisy raw depth images.
- **Multi-Critic and Multi-Discriminator Terrain Learning:** To enable versatile terrain adaptation within a single policy, the research integrates terrain-specific reward shaping with a multi-critic and multi-discriminator learning architecture. This approach utilizes dedicated networks to learn the distinct dynamics and motion priors appropriate for each type of terrain, allowing for unified control.
- **Cross-Platform Validation:** The framework's robustness and generalizability are to be validated through extensive real-world experiments on multiple humanoid platforms equipped with different stereo depth cameras.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for end-to-end vision-based humanoid locomotion employs a two-stage training pipeline. The first stage focuses on training a "privileged policy" using idealized height scan observations, leveraging specialized reinforcement learning. The second stage then distills this privileged policy into a "deployment policy" that operates directly on raw depth images, incorporating a comprehensive depth augmentation pipeline and vision-aware distillation techniques for robust sim-to-real transfer.

#### 3.1. A. Realistic Depth Sensor Simulation

Recognizing that real-world depth cameras exhibit structured imperfections not typically simulated, the authors introduce a comprehensive augmentation pipeline. This pipeline sequentially applies eight operators to raw depth images, aiming to synthesize realistic sensor artifacts that bridge the sim-to-real gap.

1. **Stereo Depth Fusion:** This process simulates the characteristic hole patterns observed in occluded and textureless regions of real stereo camera outputs. It computes disparity-based correspondence from left and right camera views and applies a consistency check. Pixels failing this check are marked invalid, replicating real-world fusion artifacts.
2. **Depth-Dependent Noise:** Real depth measurements exhibit noise that increases nonlinearly with distance. This effect is modeled with a quadratic scaling function, where coefficients are randomly sampled per environment to account for inter-device variations.
3. **Structured Noise Patterns:** Spatially correlated noise patterns, resulting from environmental interference or sensor artifacts, are simulated using multi-octave Perlin noise. This noise is applied with a depth-dependent amplitude, replicating the multi-scale nature of real sensor interference.
4. **Optical Distortions:** Lens aberrations are modeled through a randomized 3x3 convolution operation. The weights of the convolution kernel are randomly sampled, introducing local geometric distortions across the image plane.
5. **Calibration Uncertainties:** To simulate systematic measurement errors from manufacturing variations or imperfect calibration, the pipeline randomizes depth scaling (symmetrically around 1.0), perturbs camera intrinsics (aperture scaling), and adds extrinsic offsets to camera position and orientation. Additionally, random pixel failures (setting depth to zero or max) are introduced with a low probability.
6. **Preprocessing:** Before being fed to the policy, depth values are clipped to a valid sensing range (0.3 to 2.0 meters), normalized to, and spatially cropped (from 30x40 to 24x32 pixels) to mitigate peripheral distortion artifacts. An observation delay, sampled from 2 to 4 frames, simulates processing pipeline latency. Table II provides a detailed summary of these operations and their parameters.

#### 3.2. B. Privileged Reinforcement Learning

The initial training stage develops a "teacher" or "privileged policy" that operates under ideal observation conditions to efficiently learn diverse locomotion behaviors.

1. **Height Scan Observations:** The privileged policy receives dense geometric information as input, represented by a 21x33 grid of height samples obtained from ray casting over a 1.6m x 1.0m ego-centric window. This idealized terrain representation serves as the target for later vision-based transfer.
2. **Terrain-Specific Reward Shaping:** Terrains are partitioned into three categories: (1) stairs and platforms, (2) gap crossing, and (3) rough terrain. Specialized reward components are formulated for each category to encourage appropriate motion strategies. For example, an exponential velocity tracking reward promotes precise speed regulation for stairs, while a directional velocity tracking reward allows for dynamic acceleration during gap crossing. A feet contact height reward is employed for stairs and platforms to encourage foot placement on flat, stable surfaces.
3. **Multi-Critic and Multi-Discriminator Architecture:** To manage the diverse reward landscapes and dynamics, the framework utilizes three distinct critic networks and three discriminator networks, each specialized for one of the terrain categories. While the critic networks share a common backbone for efficient parameter sharing, they maintain separate output heads for terrain-specific value estimation. During training, the appropriate critic and discriminator are selected based on the current terrain label, providing the actor with tailored policy gradients and style rewards.
4. **Motion Priors:** Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) are integrated to guide the learning process towards natural and stylized locomotion. Separate motion datasets are collected for each terrain category, and discriminators operate on torso-centric observations to provide terrain-appropriate style rewards.
5. **Advanced Domain Randomization:** To ensure the robustness of the privileged policy and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, comprehensive randomization is applied to various robot dynamics parameters. This includes contact properties (static/dynamic friction, restitution), body properties (mass scaling, center of mass offset, joint offsets), actuator dynamics (armature scaling, stiffness/damping), and external disturbances (push velocity, IMU bias) (Table III).

#### 3.3. C. Vision-Aware Behavior Distillation

The second stage involves transferring the knowledge from the privileged policy to a "student" or "deployment policy" that operates directly on augmented depth images. This DAgger-style distillation process allows the student policy to learn from the teacher's supervision while interacting with the environment.

1. **Behavior Cloning (L_behavior):** The primary objective of distillation is to minimize the discrepancy between the actions of the deployment policy (which receives augmented depth images and proprioceptive observations) and the privileged policy (which receives clean height scans). This is formulated as an L2 loss on actions.
2. **Denoising Objective (L_denoise):** To promote robust feature extraction in the presence of sensor noise, a denoising objective is introduced. This loss enforces consistency between the latent representations generated by the depth encoder for clean depth images and their augmented, noisy counterparts, encouraging the encoder to learn noise-invariant features.
3. **Feature Regularization (L_kl):** A KL divergence loss is applied to regularize the depth encoder's latent features. This objective minimizes the difference between the empirical batch-wise feature distribution and a standard normal prior, which helps prevent representation collapse and encourages the learning of diverse features.
4. **Combined Objective (L_total):** The total distillation loss is a weighted sum of the behavior cloning loss, denoising objective, and KL regularization loss. This combined objective balances direct imitation of the teacher's policy with mechanisms to enhance robustness against sensor noise and stabilize feature learning. The output of the trained depth encoder then replaces the height scan embedding in the student policy, enabling vision-based deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated its approach through extensive simulation on the RDT-Bench and real-world deployment on two distinct humanoid platforms.

#### 4.1. A. Experimental Setup

The authors created the Real-World Depth Transfer Benchmark (RDT-Bench) to evaluate sim-to-real transfer capabilities under realistic perception conditions. RDT-Bench uses a CycleGAN model, trained on unpaired real-world and simulated depth images, to augment simulated depth observations with realistic noise patterns during evaluation. The policy's performance was evaluated on four terrain configurations: stairs (ascending and descending, 15 cm step height, 30 cm step depth), a 0.45 m wide gap, and a 0.40 m high platform. Each configuration was tested over 1024 parallel environments for 100 episodes.

Baseline methods included: Humanoid Parkour Learning, a No Augmentation baseline, Partial Augmentation, Standard Domain Randomization (DR), a Single Critic/Discriminator variant of the proposed method, Direct Reinforcement Learning (RL), and Behavior Cloning Only. Metrics used for evaluation were Success Rate (SR), Average Power (P), and Power Degradation Ratio (PDR).

#### 4.2. B. Main Results

The proposed method achieved an average success rate of 98.9% on RDT-Bench, consuming 27.7 × 10^1 W of power with a 5.8% power degradation ratio. This performance surpassed all baselines across all evaluated terrains and metrics (Table IV).

- **Perception Transfer:** The importance of realistic depth simulation was underscored by the "No Augmentation" baseline, which attained only a 43.0% SR with a 70.9% PDR. In contrast, the complete augmentation pipeline contributed to the proposed method's 98.9% SR and 5.8% PDR, indicating robust and efficient control despite perception noise.
- **Comparison with Prior Work:** The proposed method outperformed "Humanoid Parkour Learning", which achieved a 71.0% SR and a 30.9% PDR. This represented a 27.9% improvement in success rate and a 25.1% reduction in PDR.
- **Architectural Contributions:** The "Single Critic/Disc." baseline achieved an 82.0% SR and a 20.8% PDR. The multi-critic and multi-discriminator architecture of the proposed method demonstrated higher performance with 98.9% SR and 5.8% PDR. "Direct RL" exhibited the highest PDR (58.1%), suggesting that the two-stage privileged distillation pipeline is an integral component for effective vision-based control.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

1. **Augmentation Pipeline Analysis (Table V):** Removing the "Stereo Fusion" component resulted in the most significant performance decrease, lowering SR to 90.4% and increasing PDR to 17.5%. "Depth-Dependent Noise" and "Calibration Uncertainties" also contributed to performance gains. The study indicated that each component of the comprehensive depth augmentation pipeline contributed to policy robustness and control smoothness.
2. **Multi-Critic Architecture (Table VI):** The multi-critic/discriminator approach consistently improved performance compared to the single-critic/discriminator baseline. Improvements ranged from +14.9% to +22.1% in SR and -13.5% to -18.2% in PDR across different terrain categories, suggesting that terrain-specific value functions and discriminators lead to more stable gradient estimation.
3. **Distillation Objectives (Table VII):** The denoising objective (L_denoise) contributed a 5.5% SR improvement, promoting consistent latent representations. KL regularization (L_kl) provided a 2.8% SR improvement by preventing representation collapse. Behavior cloning alone achieved an 86.0% SR with 17.7% PDR, indicating the necessity of auxiliary objectives for robust vision-based deployment.

#### 4.4. D. Latent Space Analysis

A t-SNE visualization of the depth encoder's latent space (Figure 6) demonstrated that augmented depth observations from distinct terrain types (stair ascending, stair descending, gap, platform ascending, platform descending, rough terrain) formed well-separated clusters. This suggested that the encoder successfully extracts discriminative, terrain-specific geometric features despite the presence of realistic sensor noise.

#### 4.5. E. Real-World Deployment

The policy was deployed on a full-sized humanoid robot equipped with an Orbbec Gemini 336L depth camera, running onboard at 50 Hz without real-world fine-tuning.

- **Test Scenarios:** The robot was tested on outdoor stairs (ascending and descending), outdoor platforms, extended staircases (30+ steps), and wide gaps.
- **Quantitative Results (Table VIII):** The policy achieved an overall success rate of 97.8% (88/90 trials), with perfect performance (100% SR) on five out of six test conditions. The extended staircase test validated the policy's long-horizon stability.
- **Failure Analysis:** A performance disparity was observed between stair ascending (100% SR) and descending (86.7% SR). This was attributed to gravitational acceleration amplifying minor errors during descent, making them harder to recover, and the partial occlusion of target footholds by step edges, which exacerbates stereo matching artifacts in critical areas.
- **Cross-Platform Generalization:** Preliminary validation on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, equipped with an Intel RealSense D435i depth camera, showed a 100% success rate on the stair ascending task without platform-specific adaptation (Table XVI, Figure 7). This result suggests that the learned depth representations possess a degree of generalizability across different hardware configurations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for vision-based humanoid locomotion that addresses key challenges in robot perception and control. The integration of a realistic depth sensor simulation pipeline is a notable contribution toward bridging the sim-to-real gap. By accurately modeling various real-world sensor imperfections—including stereo fusion artifacts, depth-dependent noise, optical distortions, and calibration uncertainties—the framework aims to improve the transferability of policies from simulated to physical humanoid robots, potentially reducing the need for extensive and costly real-world data collection and fine-tuning.

The proposed multi-critic and multi-discriminator reinforcement learning architecture, combined with terrain-specific reward shaping, enables a single, unified policy to learn and execute robust locomotion strategies across a diverse range of terrains. This capability for versatile terrain mastery, encompassing both extreme obstacles (e.g., high platforms, wide gaps) and fine-grained tasks (e.g., ascending and descending long staircases), represents an advancement over methods that may rely on specialized policies or struggle with conflicting learning objectives in heterogeneous environments. The demonstration of a single policy handling a variety of challenges without manual mode switching highlights its adaptability.

Furthermore, the vision-aware behavior distillation approach, which integrates latent space alignment and noise-invariant auxiliary tasks, facilitates efficient knowledge transfer from ideal, privileged observations to noisy, vision-based inputs. This distillation process is crucial for enabling practical, end-to-end vision-driven control on physical hardware.

The validation of the framework through comprehensive simulation benchmarks (RDT-Bench) and successful real-world deployment on two distinct humanoid platforms, including long-duration operations like extended staircase traversal, underscores its practical applicability and generalizability. The preliminary cross-platform results suggest that the learned depth representations are transferable across different sensor modalities and robot morphologies.

The potential impact of this work includes:
- **Accelerated Robotics Development:** By improving the reliability of sim-to-real transfer, this research could accelerate the development and deployment of advanced humanoid locomotion controllers.
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** Humanoid robots equipped with such robust policies could operate more autonomously in complex and unstructured real-world environments, relevant for applications in logistics, disaster response, and assistance.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework establishes a foundation for continued research in robust perception and control for legged robots, particularly in addressing remaining challenges such as highly dynamic maneuvers and intricate interactions with unknown terrains.

Despite its advancements, the research identifies limitations, particularly in the relative robustness of stair descent compared to ascent. This observation suggests avenues for future investigation, such as incorporating higher-resolution near-field depth sensing or developing more sophisticated predictive foot placement strategies, to mitigate issues related to gravitational error amplification and visual occlusions during critical maneuvers.
